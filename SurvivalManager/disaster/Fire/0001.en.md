# 1. When a Fire Breaks Out - the Order of Things

1. Alert people - Shout "Fire!" and hit the alarm. If an alarm wakes you, wake the others first instead of going to check whether it is real.
2. Decide: fight it or leave - A small fire can be put out with an extinguisher or a bucket of water. Once the flames have grown, you leave.
3. Get out - Take the stairs, stay low, and head for ground level or the roof. Never use the elevator.
4. Call 119 - once you are somewhere safe. Do not lose escape time to the phone call.
5. Count heads - Gather at the spot you agreed on in advance and check everyone is there. If someone is missing, tell the firefighters when they arrive.

> Touch the door handle with the back of your hand. If it is warm or hot, the fire is on the other side - do not open it. The same goes if smoke is seeping under the door.

> Smoke and heat rise. Keep as low as you can, feel along the wall, and wrap yourself in a wet towel or blanket. Wear a fire escape hood if you have one; otherwise cover your mouth and nose with a wet cloth.
> Most fire deaths are caused not by flames but by carbon monoxide and other toxic gases.

# 2. When You Cannot Get Out

1. Move to the room farthest from the flames and smoke, close the door, and seal the gaps with wet towels.
2. Call 119, give your exact location and situation, and ask for rescue.
3. At the window, wave a flashlight or a light-colored towel so rescuers can see where you are.
4. If your building has a refuge area, a light-weight partition, a descending escape hatch, or a descending life line, go there.
   - A light-weight partition is a thin wall shared with the neighboring unit - you can kick it through and cross over.

※ Apartments permitted before October 1992 have none of these escape features. Know your route in advance, and consider having escape equipment installed.

# 3. Judging Your Situation

| Situation | What to do |
| --- | --- |
| Your home, front door usable | Alert everyone → close the door behind you and take the stairs, staying low → alarm and 119 |
| Your home, front door unusable | Go to the refuge area, partition, or escape hatch → if there is none, close the door, seal the gaps, call 119 for rescue |
| Neighbor's fire, no smoke coming in | Close the windows and stay put, watching how it develops → call 119, follow the announcements |
| Neighbor's fire, smoke coming in | If the corridor and stairs are clear, close your door and take the stairs / if there is smoke in the corridor, close the door, seal the gaps, then move to the refuge area |

> Never assume someone else has already called it in. And keep watching for reignition after the fire looks out.

# 4. Extinguishers, Hydrants, and Descending Devices

## 4-1. Fire Extinguisher
Indoors, work with your back to the door so your escape route is behind you.

1. Bring the extinguisher, grip the body, and pull the safety pin.
2. Take the nozzle and move in close to the fire.
3. Squeeze the handle hard.
4. Sweep so the powder covers the fire evenly.

## 4-2. Fire Hydrant (two-person team)
1. A opens the hydrant cabinet and checks the hose is coupled to the nozzle.
2. B runs the hose out to the fire without kinks, then takes the nozzle and braces for the water.
3. A turns the valve, confirms water is flowing, then runs over to help B hold the hose.
4. Turn the nozzle tip to adjust the flow and fight the fire.

## 4-3. Descending Life Line
1. Hook the device onto the bracket and lock it.
2. Push the bracket out of the window and throw the line down.
3. Put the belt on at chest height and tighten it.
4. Walk your hands down the wall and descend slowly.

## 4-4. If Your Clothes Catch Fire
1. Stop. Running feeds the flames and makes you breathe smoke.
2. Cover your face with both hands.
3. Drop to the floor.
4. Roll side to side to smother the fire.

# 5. Classes of Fire and How to Put Them Out

| Class | What burns | How to fight it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A - ordinary | Wood, paper, textiles - leaves ash | Water-based: cooling and smothering |
| B - flammable liquids | Oil, gas, other flammable liquids and gases - leaves no ash | Foam or powder: smothering |
| C - electrical | Switchgear, wiring | No water. CO₂, halon, powder |
| D - metal | Sodium, potassium, titanium, magnesium and the like | No water. Cover with dry sand or dry diatomite |
| K (F) - cooking oil | Cooking oils, animal and vegetable fats | Wet chemical or powder. Water makes it erupt |

> Never put water on an oil fire or an electrical fire. The same holds for metal fires - some metals react with water and generate their own heat.
