# Electric Shock

> The rule that matters most: touching a person who is still in contact with the current makes you the second casualty.

1. Cut the power - trip the breaker, or pull the plug if you can reach it. Know where your breaker panel is before you ever need it.
2. Separate with an insulator - if you cannot cut the supply, use a non-conductive object (a dry wooden stick, rubber gloves, plastic) to push the person clear.
3. Move them to safety - away from electrical equipment and from water.
4. Assess and treat - tap both shoulders to check for a response; if there is no consciousness or breathing, call 119 and begin CPR at once.
5. Treat burns - do not break the blisters. Cool under running water for at least 10 minutes, then cover loosely with clean cloth.

> Electrical injury can look minor on the surface while internal damage and arrhythmia appear later. Even if the person recovers consciousness, they must be seen at a hospital.

# Electrical Fires

> Water on an electrical fire electrocutes you and spreads the flames. Cut the power first, always.

1. Cut the power - trip the breaker or pull the plug.
2. Fight it early - while the flames are small, use an ABC dry-powder or CO₂ extinguisher.
3. Decide to leave - if you cannot put it out, evacuate at once. Skip the elevator, stay low, cover your nose and mouth with a wet towel, and take the stairs.

# Battery and Electric Vehicle Fires

## 1. Warning signs
- Abnormal heat from the battery area.
- A strange sweet or acrid smell.
- Smoke from under the floor of an electric vehicle.
- A battery warning light on the dashboard.
- Unusual noises during charging.

## 2. Fire while charging
1. Stop charging - unplug the connector. If you cannot, cut power at the charger.
2. Get away - put at least 30 meters between you and the vehicle, and take cover behind something solid.

## 3. Fire while driving
1. Pull over somewhere safe, switch off, and set the parking brake.
2. Everyone gets out immediately. Belongings are not worth a life.
3. Move at least 30 meters away and take cover.
4. Warn the people around you - especially other owners if you are in an underground garage.

## 4. What to understand
- An EV battery fire cannot be put out with a household extinguisher.
- At the early smoking stage, flooding it with water to bring the temperature down can help - but once heavy smoke is pouring out, do not approach; evacuate.
- The pack takes hours to cool fully, and it can reignite in the meantime.
- These vehicles carry a high-voltage battery: during a fire, do not touch the metal bodywork.

# Power Outages

1. Work out the scope - is it just your home, or the whole area? Rolling blackouts usually last under an hour per area.
2. Get light - use a flashlight or lantern. Candles are a serious fire risk and are not recommended.
3. Unplug - pull appliance plugs to protect them from the surge when power returns.
4. After restoration - plug things back in one at a time, with gaps between.
5. Check the equipment - if the breaker or switch is faulty, call an electrical contractor; if the equipment is fine, call KEPCO (123).
6. If you are stuck in an elevator - read out the seven-digit elevator number inside the car to 119. Do not try to force the doors.

## Safety and hygiene during an outage
- Food stays reasonably safe for about four hours if you keep the refrigerator closed. Discard perishables that have been above 4 °C for more than two hours.
- After a full day without power, discard refrigerated medication unless the label says otherwise.
- Households that depend on powered life-sustaining medical equipment should arrange backup power and a relocation plan in advance.
- Run generators and charcoal grills outdoors only, well away from windows - carbon monoxide kills.
- Never use a gas range or oven to heat the house.

# Preventing Electrical Accidents

## 1. Wiring and equipment
- Do not fasten cables with nails or staples.
- Fuses and breakers must always be the correct rating.
- Use cable sized for the load, and replace anything aged or damaged.
- Run wiring in conduit to protect it from heat and impact.
- Inspect concealed wiring - in ceilings, for instance - periodically.
- Route cable so it does not touch metalwork or damp structures.
- Fit an earth-leakage breaker on each circuit.

## 2. Habits
- No octopus wiring. Every cable has a fixed current it can carry.
- Unplug appliances after use.
- Do not leave heating mats and similar devices running for long periods.
- Check the thermostats on heaters regularly for faults.
- Give hot devices such as incandescent lamps room to shed heat, and keep combustibles away.
- Tighten connections so they cannot work loose.
- Clear dust and metal filings from distribution boards regularly, and keep furniture and combustibles away from them.

## 3. Everyday safety
- Never touch a plug or switch with wet hands.
- Pull the plug body, not the cord - otherwise the copper inside breaks.
- In homes with small children, use covered outlets.
- Stay away from anything marked "danger" or "high voltage."
- Do not climb utility poles or poke at wires with a stick.
- Stay away from downed power lines - and from the puddles and metal they are touching.

# Power Supply Alert Levels

## 1. Watch / Caution (reserve margin 4.5 GW – 2.5 GW)
- Industry and public agencies implement demand management and ready their emergency generators.
- Homes, businesses, and offices cut unnecessary consumption.
- Minimize lighting and unplug devices to eliminate standby draw.
- Avoid peak hours - 10 a.m. to noon in winter, 2 p.m. to 5 p.m. in summer.
- Keep indoor temperatures at 28 °C or above for cooling, 18 °C or below for heating.
- Defer heavy loads: vacuum cleaners, washing machines, irons, office equipment.
- Locate emergency exits and evacuation routes in advance.

## 2. Alert / Serious (reserve margin below 2.5 GW)
- The government issues civil defense alerts and emergency broadcasts, and prepares or implements emergency curtailment and rolling blackouts.
- Shut down everything but essential lighting and equipment.
- Avoid using elevators - an outage can come without notice.
- Facilities where an outage causes losses - fish farms, aquariums, hospitals - secure backup power.
- At home, ready a flashlight, emergency food, water, and a radio.
- Fit an uninterruptible power supply (UPS) to sensitive equipment.
- Avoid running heaters, space heating, and air conditioning simultaneously.
