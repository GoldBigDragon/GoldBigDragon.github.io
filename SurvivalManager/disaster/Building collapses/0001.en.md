# Warning Signs - Leave Now

- The floor cracks or sinks.
- Windows and doors suddenly warp and become hard to open or close.
- Columns bow, or finish materials such as marble begin falling off in pieces.
- Web-like cracks appear around columns, or a floor slab sags sharply.
- Column bases push upward, or the middle of the floor dips.
- Ground subsidence continues, or stone walls and retaining walls crack or bulge.
- Walls or floors make a cracking sound like ice breaking.
- A structure under demolition catches fire, or its steel members are exposed to flame.
- Local or national authorities issue a warning broadcast after a major flood or earthquake.

> The moment you notice any of these: shut off the gas and power, put on shoes, and take the stairs straight out of the building.

---

# Prepare in Advance

- Know where the escape equipment is - descending device, rope, flashlight.
- Know the evacuation route out of the building. Stairs, never the elevator.
- Pick a refuge space in case you cannot get out: under a sturdy table, beside a load-bearing wall, or in the stairwell.

---

# During a Collapse - Getting Out

- Use the stairs. An elevator can trap you when the power fails.
- Glass, signage, and roof tiles fall - shield your head with whatever you carry and move calmly.
- Help children, elderly people, people with disabilities, and pregnant women evacuate first, with a companion.
- If there is no time to reach a designated shelter, get to a nearby safe area and call 119 or 112 for rescue.
- After a major earthquake, aftershocks can bring on a second collapse - stay away from buildings and embankments.
- In continuing heavy rain or snow, recovery and resupply may be slow. Ration what you have.
- Do not let rumors drive your decisions.

# If You Are Trapped Inside

## 1. If escape is possible
- Elevator lobbies and stairwells have load-bearing walls and are comparatively safe. Work your way out through them.
- Do not use broken stairs or elevators.
- Look for a descending device, rope, or flashlight.
- Leave obstacles alone where you can. When you must move one, expect further collapse.
- A healthy adult who knows the building should lead.
- Protect your head and face with a coat, blanket, newspaper, cardboard box, or bag as you move.
- Give the injured what first aid you can, then bring them out with you.

## 2. If escape is not possible
- Take a low position beside a solid wall without windows, next to a column, or under a sturdy table and wait for rescue.
- The air may be full of dust - cover your mouth and nose with cloth to reduce what you breathe in.
- Tap on a wall or pipe at regular intervals. It costs far less energy than shouting.
- When you hear rescuers calling, answer calmly - do not shout unnecessarily.
- Shine a light or call out only when you are sure someone is near.
- If gas may be leaking, do not light a match, lighter, or stove. Use a flashlight only.
- If you are pinned, move your fingers and toes frequently to keep circulation going.
- Look for food and water in a refrigerator or nearby, and ration it to last as long as possible.
- Thinking of your family and continuing to search for a way out keeps your energy and morale up.

## 3. Managing your phone's battery
- Power it on for about 10 minutes every six hours or on a set schedule: check for signal, text 119, try sending your GPS location - then switch off again.
- If turning it fully off feels too risky, use airplane mode so it stops draining the battery hunting for a tower.
- Turn the screen brightness all the way down.
- The phone's flashlight drains the battery very fast - use it as a signal only when you hear rescuers.
- Batteries discharge faster when cold, so keep the phone inside your clothing when not in use.
- But if you hear rescue activity - footsteps, drilling, shouting - turn it on immediately regardless of schedule and signal.

# If You Are Outside a Collapse Site

- Further collapse and gas explosions are real risks: do not enter the building or approach the scene.
- Move to an open area at a safe distance.
- Passing nearby, protect your head with a bag, cushion, or book against falling debris and glass.
- Keep access routes clear so rescue work is not obstructed.
- If you know of missing people or buried facilities, tell responders specifically where.
