# 7. Heat-Related Illness

## 7-1. Common Steps
1. Move the person out of the heat at once - into shade, indoors, or underground.
2. Wet the body and fan it to bring the temperature down.
3. Mix salt and sugar into water to replace fluids and electrolytes.

## 7-2. Telling the Three Apart

| | Consciousness | Sweating | Temperature | Severity |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Heat cramps | Normal | Heavy | Normal or slightly raised | Low |
| Heat exhaustion | May be slightly reduced | Heavy | Above 37°C | Moderate |
| Heat stroke | Confused or absent | May be absent or present | Above 40°C | Critical |

## 7-3. Heat Cramps
Signs - conscious, sweating heavily, with cramps in the calves, abdomen, hands, or feet.
> Cramps plus dizziness plus vomiting may mean it is progressing to heat exhaustion.

1. Stop the activity immediately.
2. Move somewhere cool and rest.
3. Take water with salt and sugar.
4. Stretch the cramping muscle slowly, with massage.

## 7-4. Heat Exhaustion
Signs - temperature above 37°C, heavy sweating with damp skin, headache, dizziness, vomiting, fainting.
> If they become confused, the temperature keeps rising, or sweating decreases, it is turning into heat stroke.

1. Move into shade immediately.
2. Wipe the whole body with a wet towel, then use airflow for evaporative cooling.
   - Work especially on the neck, armpits, and groin - where the large vessels run.
3. Only if conscious, give the salt-and-sugar solution in small, repeated sips.
4. Lie them down and raise only the legs slightly against shock.
5. If they vomit, turn them on their side to keep the airway clear.

## 7-5. Heat Stroke - Life-Threatening
Signs - temperature above 40°C, reduced consciousness, confusion, seizures from central nervous system dysfunction, hot dry skin (though sweating may still be present).

> Cooling comes before the hospital.
> Immerse them in water or cover them with wet cloth and fan, concentrating on the neck, armpits, and groin to drop the temperature fast. Do not stop cooling during transport.

1. Begin cooling as the first priority.
2. If conscious, lie them down immediately and adjust position according to their breathing.
3. If conscious, give water in small, repeated sips.
4. Watch breathing and pulse continuously; if either stops, start CPR immediately.

Never do this
- Delaying the cooling
- Forcing them to drink
- Giving antipyretics - they do nothing for the hyperthermia of heat stroke

# 8. Snake Bite

> Snake venom spreads mainly through the lymphatic system, not the bloodstream.
> Injection → lymph vessels → lymph nodes → bloodstream → whole body
> That is why the more you move, the faster it spreads. The goal is not to remove the venom but to slow it down.

## 8-1. What to Do
1. Get away from the snake at once.
2. Call 119.
3. Lie the person down and calm them to slow the heart rate. That alone slows the spread.
4. If you can, carry them on a stretcher so they do not move.
   - If you are far out and isolated, forcing a move can be more dangerous - waiting for rescue may be better.
5. Rinse the bite gently under running water. Do not rub it.
6. Wrap the entire limb with a pressure bandage, starting at the bite, and immobilize it with a splint.
   - Loose enough to slide a finger under - not tight, not slack.
7. Keep the bite slightly below or level with the heart.
8. If conscious and not vomiting, give small sips of water.
9. Maintain body heat to prevent shock.
10. Remove rings, watches, and tight clothing. Swelling will turn them into tourniquets.

## 8-2. Never Do This
- Sucking out the venom by mouth
- Cutting the wound open
- Binding tightly with a tourniquet
- Applying an electric shock
- Putting ice directly on it
- Drinking alcohol
- Letting the person run or move about
- Applying bean paste or cigarette ash

## 8-3. Signs to Treat as a Venomous Bite
Any one of these means treat it as venomous.

| Local signs | Systemic signs |
| --- | --- |
| Severe pain · rapid swelling · blistering · tissue death | Dizziness · vomiting · blurred vision · difficulty breathing |

# 9. Bee and Wasp Stings

> Most stings end as local pain, but some progress to severe systemic allergic shock (anaphylaxis).
> So watching for a whole-body reaction matters more than removing the sting.

## 9-1. What to Do
1. Get away from where the bees are, quickly. Bees use pheromones to call in more attacks.
2. Remove the sting as fast as you can - fingernail, card, tweezers, whatever is at hand.
   - The venom sac attached to the sting keeps pumping, so speed matters more than method.
3. Lie them down and raise the legs to prevent hypotensive shock.
   - But if they are struggling to breathe, raise the upper body slightly.
4. Wash the site with soap and clean water.
5. Wrap ice in cloth and apply it. Never directly.
6. Do not scratch, however much it itches - infection risk.

## 9-2. Anaphylaxis - Act Immediately

These signs mean life-threatening
- Swelling of the lips, tongue, or throat
- Hives spreading across the body
- Shortness of breath
- Hoarse voice
- Reduced consciousness
- Falling blood pressure
- Cyanosis - lips, tongue, and fingertips turning blue

What to do
1. Lie them down and raise the legs immediately.
2. Inject epinephrine (EpiPen) into the thigh at once.
3. If unconscious, turn them on their side to keep the airway open.
4. Keep checking breathing and pulse, and start CPR if needed.

## 9-3. Prevention and Prohibitions
Prevention
- Do not wear perfume.
- Do not wear bright colors.
- If they attack, running far and fast is the most effective response.

Never
- Apply bean paste or cigarette ash
- Swat at bees to drive them off
- Delay removing the sting
- Put ice directly on the site
