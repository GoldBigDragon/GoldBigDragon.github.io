## 2. Adhesives from Trees
### 2-1. Pine Resin
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Boiling pine resin collected from pine trees to drive off volatile components leaves a resin that hardens solid. However, if used as is, it cracks like glass after hardening. Because resin molecules are firmly fixed in place without sliding past one another, applying force causes it to crack instead of stretch, and that crack spreads instantly across the entire layer.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Therefore, when utilizing resin, an adhesive is created as a composite material composed of three ingredients. Resin performs the binding task, fillers block cracks from spreading, and plasticizers provide flexibility.

| Component | Representative Material | Function |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Resin | Refined pine resin, pine nut or fir resin | Holds two surfaces together as it hardens |
| Filler | Fine charcoal powder, wood ash, dry dung powder, clay powder | Blocks cracks from spreading and dissipates stress |
| Plasticizer | Beeswax, animal fat, vegetable oil | Intercalates between molecules to create room for sliding |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The operating principle of fillers is identical to adding gravel to concrete. When a advancing crack encounters a hard particle, it changes direction or stops. The more particles there are, the more complex the path the crack must take becomes, requiring correspondingly more energy to break.

### 2-2. Mixing Proportions
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The widely used standard mix by volume is as follows:

| Material | Volume Ratio |
|:---|---:|
| Refined Pine Resin | 5 |
| Fine Charcoal Powder | 1–2 |
| Beeswax or Oil | 0.5 or less |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This ratio is merely a starting point and must practically be adjusted according to the season of use and application. This is because the properties of the hardened adhesive depend heavily on temperature.

| Situation | Adjustment Direction | Reason |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Winter, cold regions | Increase plasticizer for softness | Resin becomes more brittle at lower temperatures |
| Summer, hot regions | Reduce plasticizer and increase filler for hardness | Bonded joints melt and run at high temperatures |
| Arrowheads, handle securing | Shift toward hardness | Shape retention is more important than impact absorption |
| Waterproofing, filling gaps | Shift toward softness | Must not crack even when base materials shift |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Checking whether the mixture is correct is simple. Pressing the hardened mass with a fingernail should leave a faint impression for it to be appropriate. If it makes no impression at all, it is too hard and prone to cracking; if the fingernail sinks right in, it is too soft to hold load.

### 2-3. Harvesting and Refining
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The basic method for gathering resin is collecting hardened lumps that have flowed from injured pine trees. Because slashing deep wounds into living trees kills them, it is better to look for resin-rich fatwood—wood hardened by resin saturation in dead pines, stump roots, or spots where branches broke off. Fatwood itself is also excellent tinder.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Gathered resin contains tree bark, pine needles, dirt, and insects. Melting it over low heat and straining it through coarse cloth leaves clean resin, and driving off volatile components during this process improves its hardening properties.

> **When heated, pine resin emits flammable vapors and behaves identically to an oil fire if ignited. Melting it in a pot over direct flame causes it to ignite immediately if it boils over, and splashing water on it will splatter burning resin in all directions.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Therefore, double-boiling—floating a smaller vessel inside a large pot filled with water—is the standard rule for melting. If double-boiling is difficult, place it over very low heat, fill the vessel only one-third full, keep a lid and wet cloth nearby, and cover with the lid to cut off oxygen if it catches fire.

### 2-4. Birch Tar
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Birch bark contains a high amount of a substance called betulin. Heating the bark to roughly 300–400°C in the absence of air decomposes this substance, yielding a dark, sticky tar. It can be extracted through dry distillation similar to charcoal making, with the difference being that a liquid is extracted rather than a solid.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The simplest dry distillation method is the double-vessel technique. Pack bark tightly into an upper vessel, make a small hole at the bottom, invert it, and seat it snugly against the mouth of a lower vessel buried in the ground. Seal the seam completely with soil or clay. Then, cover only the upper vessel with fire and embers to heat it; tar produced from the bark drops down through the hole and collects in the bottom vessel.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The reason for burying the lower vessel in the ground is that the soil cools that portion. If collected tar heats up again, it simply burns away, leaving nothing behind. Conversely, if the temperature of the upper vessel does not rise sufficiently, the bark merely scorches without yielding tar. The success of this method depends on maintaining a hot upper vessel and a cold lower vessel.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Unlike pine resin, birch tar is naturally somewhat flexible and far more water-resistant. Mixing in a small amount of filler immediately creates an adhesive; using it as is yields a waterproofing agent for boats, barrels, and roofs, as well as a wood preservative. However, because it softens and runs on hot days, additional filler must be added in summer.
