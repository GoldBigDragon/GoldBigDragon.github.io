## 3. Adhesives from Animals
### 3-1. Collagen
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;A protein called collagen is present in leather, sinew, bone, fish skin, and swim bladders. Collagen has a structure in which three thin strands are twisted together like a rope, and thanks to this twisting, sinew withstands pulling forces exceptionally well.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;When supplied with water and proper heat, the twists unravel, scattering into individual strands. This state is gelatin, and while dissolved in hot water, it remains a liquid. However, as the temperature drops, the scattered strands intertwine again to form a network that traps water, and this network solidifies to hold two surfaces together.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;All properties of animal glue stem from the single fact that this process is reversible. Reapplying heat and water unravels the network, making repair and disassembly possible; for the same reason, it is vulnerable to water and heat.

---
### 3-2. Glue Is Destroyed the Moment It Boils
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Although heat is required to convert collagen into gelatin, excessive heat chops the long gelatin chains into fragments. Because shortened chains cannot form a dense network even after cooling, the mixture becomes a thin broth rather than glue.

| Temperature | What Happens |
|:---|:---|
| 60–70°C | Twists unravel slowly. Takes longer, but causes minimal damage |
| 70–85°C | The most suitable range for extraction |
| Above 85°C | Chains begin breaking and strength drops rapidly |
| Near boiling point | Chains are chopped short, losing nearly all adhesive strength |

> **Hide glue must never be boiled. From the moment bubbles begin rising to the surface, the glue is deteriorating, and once chopped, chains cannot be restored.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Therefore, double-boiling is the absolute rule both during extraction and application. Floating the glue vessel inside a large pot filled with water keeps the inner temperature from exceeding 100°C even if the outer water boils. Adding lye obtained from wood ash or salt to raise the boiling point is a completely counterproductive measure and provides no help.

---
### 3-3. Manufacturing Hide Glue
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The most important step in preparation is degreasing. Grease ruins the wetting of adhesives and turns rancid over time, weakening the bonded joint. Soaking hides for several days in lye leached from wood ash removes hair along with grease; afterward, they must be rinsed thoroughly in running water to remove alkali. Sinews should be dried, hammered, and shredded into strands, while bones are crushed finely and boiled once to skim off grease.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;After soaking the prepared materials in water for about a day to swell, maintaining a low temperature for several hours turns the liquid sticky. Pour off this liquid, add fresh water, and repeat the same process two or three times. Because the first batch is the strongest and subsequent extractions grow progressively weaker, storing them separately according to application is preferable.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;While slow simmering over low heat is the standard method for concentration, a freeze-thaw method is far more advantageous in winter. When glue solution freezes, only the water forms ice crystals and separates; discarding the water after thawing concentrates the glue without applying any heat.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Completed hide glue must be dried thoroughly. Because wet glue is a mass of protein, it rots and grows mold within a few days. Spreading it thin to solidify, cutting it into pieces, and drying them completely allows storage for years; whenever needed, rehydrate only the necessary amount in water and melt it via double-boiling. The target consistency is roughly that of thick honey; if too thick, the layer becomes excessively heavy, and if too thin, it lacks strength.

---
### 3-4. The Two Sides of Hide Glue
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Joints bonded with animal glue separate again when exposed to warm wet towels or steam. This property is a decisive advantage for objects made under the premise of future repair. The reason musical instruments and furniture have used animal glue for centuries is not because of raw strength, but because broken parts can be detached and replaced with new components.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;That same property becomes a weakness on the flip side. It cannot be used on items placed outdoors, in damp areas, or where water makes contact. Furthermore, because the hardened gelatin network absorbs and releases water according to ambient humidity, changing its volume, changing seasons cause micro-movements in the joint that accumulate and ultimately cause separation.

| Property | Advantageous Case | Disadvantageous Case |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Melts with heat | Enables repair and disassembly | Joints loosen in hot environments |
| Dissolves in water | Brushes and containers can be washed with water | Cannot withstand rain or moisture |
| Responds to humidity | Moves together with wood | Alternating dry and wet conditions causes separation |
| Extremely hard when dry | Creates strong bonds with a thin layer | Vulnerable to impacts |

### 3-5. Isinglass (Fish Glue)
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Glue obtained from the swim bladders of fish such as croaker or sturgeon is called isinglass (fish glue). Because the swim bladder is a nearly pure collagen membrane, it contains little grease or impurities, melting at a lower temperature to yield a much clearer and stronger glue.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;What makes isinglass special is its ability to form an extremely thin adhesive layer. As established previously, thinner adhesive layers are stronger; therefore, a glue that spreads thin without breaking is inherently advantageous. In composite bows, when layering horn, wood, and sinew together, this is the glue that withstands the immense shear forces exerted across the layers while the bow flexes.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The manufacturing method is identical to other animal glues, but targets a lower temperature. Dry the swim bladder, soak it in water, and melt it slowly around 60°C. The difficulty in obtaining raw materials is its sole yet significant limitation.
