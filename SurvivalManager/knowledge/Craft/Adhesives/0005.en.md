## 4. Other Adhesives and Selection Criteria
### 4-1. Casein Glue
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Milk contains a protein called casein. In an acidic environment, this protein loses its electrical equilibrium, coagulating and settling into clumps; this is the exact phenomenon seen when milk spoils and curdles. Adding vinegar or sour fruit juice produces the same result.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Filtering the curdled clumps through cloth, rinsing with water multiple times to remove sourness, and squeezing firmly yields casein curd. Mixing this with slaked lime causes calcium to bridge the protein chains, creating a network that is virtually insoluble in water. This is the crucial difference from animal glues: casein glue does not re-dissolve once cured, rendering it quite resistant to water.

| Ingredient | Ratio | Remarks |
|:---|---:|:---|
| Casein Curd | 5 | Drained of moisture by squeezing |
| Slaked Lime | 1 | Made by adding water to quicklime |
| Water | As needed | Until a thick porridge-like consistency is reached |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Because mixed casein glue begins to set within a few hours, prepare only the amount needed for the day. The curd itself lasts long when dried into powder for storage. Its limitations are that it is not completely waterproof, grows mold if exposed to moisture for long periods, and its alkaline content leaves dark stains on woods like oak.

### 4-2. Starch Paste
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;When starch extracted from rice, wheat, potatoes, or acorns is dissolved in water and heated, the starch granules absorb water, swell, and burst, unraveling their molecular chains. These tangled, sticky chains form starch paste, which dries by penetrating between the fibers of paper or fabric.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The advantage is that it does not damage base materials. When applied to paper or cloth, it neither stiffens nor discolors them and can be easily removed with water. On the other hand, it cannot be used for joints subjected to heavy loads like wood or bone, dissolves immediately in water, and serves as food for mold and insects. Adding a small amount of alum or salt slightly delays mold growth.

### 4-3. Resins, Rubber, Urushi (Lacquer), and Bitumen
| Material | Source / Extraction | Properties and Cautions |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Natural Rubber | White latex from rubber trees, milkweed, or dandelions | High elasticity and water resistance, but low bonding strength |
| Urushi (Lacquer) | Sap from the urushi tree | Cures in humidity and warmth, providing extremely high full waterproofing. Pre-cured sap causes severe skin inflammation |
| Bitumen | Natural asphalt, oil seeping from outcrops | Outstanding waterproofing, but flows when hot and shatters when cold |
| Beeswax | Honeycombs | Weak adhesive power; used for waterproofing and as a plasticizer |
| Tree Sap | Fir, spruce, cherry trees | Used in the same manner as pine resin; differs in curing speed |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Urushi exhibits the highest performance among natural materials, but is the most dangerous to handle. Contact with pre-cured sap causes severe rashes and blisters days later, and depending on constitution, can affect the respiratory tract. When harvesting and applying, handle it in well-ventilated areas to avoid exposing bare skin or inhaling vapors generated during curing.

### 4-4. Property Comparison and Selection by Application
| Adhesive | Water Resistance | Heat Resistance | Strength | Reversibility | Storability |
|:---|:---|:---|:---|:---|:---|
| Pine Resin Glue | Medium | Low | Medium | High | Very Good |
| Birch Tar | High | Low | Medium | High | Very Good |
| Animal Glue | Low | Low | High | High | Good when dried |
| Isinglass (Fish Glue) | Low | Low | Very High | High | Good when dried |
| Casein Glue | High | Medium | High | None | Good as powder |
| Starch Paste | Very Low | Medium | Low | High | Poor |
| Urushi (Lacquer) | Very High | High | High | None | Good when sealed |
| Bitumen | Very High | Very Low | Low | High | Very Good |

| Application | Recommended Adhesive | Reason for Choice |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Securing Arrowheads and Fletching | Pine resin glue + Sinew wrapping | Can be repaired on-site as long as fire is available in the field |
| Indoor Woodwork Joinery | Animal glue | Strong, with the ability to detach and repair later |
| Laminated Structures like Bows | Isinglass | Endures shear forces while keeping layers extremely thin |
| Waterproofing Boats, Tubs, Roofs | Bitumen, Birch tar | Repels water and does not crack even when base materials move |
| Paper and Cloth | Starch paste | Does not stiffen or discolor materials |
| Pottery and Dish Repair | Urushi, Pine resin glue | Cures while filling gaps |
| Water-Exposed Joints | Casein glue, Urushi | Will not re-dissolve after curing |

### 4-5. When Binding Is Stronger Than Bonding
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;No adhesive obtained from nature is as strong as modern synthetic adhesives. Therefore, traditional methods do not entrust all load-bearing force to the adhesive alone.

> **Forces are borne by the wrapped and bound fibers, while the adhesive secures those fibers from unraveling and seals out water. The standard of natural material joining is not choosing between bonding and binding, but overlapping both.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This approach is strong for three reasons. First, sinew or plant fibers possess exceptional tensile strength, bearing the tensile loads exerted on the joint. Second, wet sinew shrinks significantly as it dries, self-tightening the wrapped area. A clamping mechanism that applies continuous pressure during curing is effectively built into the joint itself. Third, the wrapped fibers cover the edges of the joint, eliminating the starting point for peel stress—the weakest load mode.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The typical sequence is applying a thin coat of adhesive first, wrapping sinew or fibers tightly over it, and finally applying another coat of adhesive to cover the fibers. Wrapping should be done in crisscrossing patterns rather than a single direction to withstand torsional forces. Mechanical joinery like mortises, tenons, wedges, and pegs are integrated under the same logic. The goal is constructing a framework that does not immediately collapse even if the adhesive fails.
