## Introduction
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Where objects fail is usually not in the middle of a material, but where two materials meet. An axe head separated from its handle, a leaking wooden tub, or a detached shoe sole are all problems caused not by a lack of material, but by the disappearance of the force holding them together. Repairing and bonding things is needed far more often than making new ones, and without adhesives, you will repeatedly find yourself unable to do anything despite holding two perfectly good parts in your hands.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The holding force of an adhesive works only at extremely close distances; therefore, adhesive strength is generated only when the adhesive spreads evenly over a surface and makes actual physical contact. On greasy surfaces, dusty surfaces, or surfaces so smooth that there is nothing to catch on, any adhesive will yield the same poor result. In other words, preparing a clean and roughened surface is more crucial than choosing the adhesive material itself.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This document is not meant for memorizing recipes for making adhesives, but for understanding why pine resin alone cracks as soon as it hardens, why mixing charcoal powder makes it tougher, why hide glue must not be boiled, and why arrowheads are wrapped with sinew rather than attached with glue alone. Understanding the underlying principles allows you to adjust proportions even when the materials in your hands differ from those written in this document.
