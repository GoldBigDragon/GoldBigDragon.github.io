## Introduction
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This document covers the principles of adhesion, how to make each adhesive and its properties, and how to choose one for a given use.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;An adhesive works only where it actually touches the surface. No adhesive sticks well to greasy, dusty, or smooth surfaces, so before choosing an adhesive, make the surface rough and clean.

---

## 1. Adhesion
### 1-1. Principles
- Mechanical interlocking: Liquid adhesive seeps into tiny gaps and pores in the surface, then hardens and grips it. In porous materials such as wood, leather, bone, paper, and fired pottery, this accounts for most of the bond strength.
- Intermolecular attraction: This acts only when adhesive molecules and surface molecules are within nanometers of each other. On smooth surfaces such as glass and metal, this force is the whole of the bond strength.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Both principles work only if the adhesive actually touches the surface.

---
### 1-2. Surface Condition
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;If an adhesive beads up on the surface like water droplets, the contact area shrinks and the bond weakens. An adhesive sticks well when it spreads thinly; this property is called wetting. The main factor that spoils wetting is grease.

- Grease: prevents wetting.
- Dust: forms a weak layer between the adhesive and the surface. The adhesive sticks to the dust, and the dust merely sits on the surface, so the joint comes apart at that layer.
- Old wood surfaces: their components have oxidized and the pores are crushed shut. Plane or scrape off a fresh face just before gluing.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Re-planing and roughening the surface often raises bond strength more than changing the type of adhesive.

---
### 1-3. Failure Modes
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Where the joint comes apart shows the cause.

| Failure mode | Where it came apart | Cause → remedy |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Interfacial failure | Adhesive remains on one surface only | Contaminated surface, poor wetting → improve surface preparation |
| Cohesive failure | Adhesive remains on both surfaces, about half on each | The adhesive itself is weak, or the layer is thick → adjust the mix and thickness |
| Substrate failure | Wood or bone is torn out | The bond succeeded. No improvement needed |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Once the joint is stronger than the material, there is no reason to change the adhesive further.

---
### 1-4. Bond Line Thickness
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The thinner the adhesive layer, the stronger it is. A thick layer shrinks as it hardens and keeps internal stress; it is more likely to have bubbles and cracks, and under load it splits inside the layer.

- Do not fill a wide gap with adhesive. Fill it with a piece of the same material or a wedge, and use the adhesive only to join the surfaces thinly in between.
- Press the two surfaces together and hold them. Clamping squeezes out the adhesive to thin the layer and keeps the surfaces from separating while it hardens.

---
### 1-5. Load Direction and Strength
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Even with the same adhesive and area, the strength of the joint can differ by more than 10 times depending on the direction of the force.

| Load | How the force is applied | Joint strength |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Shear | Slides the bonded faces sideways along each other | Strongest |
| Compression | Presses the bonded faces together | Strong |
| Tension | Pulls perpendicular to the bonded faces | Medium |
| Cleavage | Opens one side like a wedge | Weak |
| Peel | Strips it off from one end | Weakest |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In peeling, the force is not spread over the whole bonded area but concentrated on a single line at the leading edge of the peel, so widening the area does not help. Lap the joint instead of butting it, and wrap or press down the edges so that nothing can lift, which removes the place where peeling would start. This is the first reason lashing (wrapping and binding) is always used together with natural adhesives.

## 2. Adhesives from Trees
### 2-1. Pine Resin
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Boiling pine resin to drive off the volatile components leaves a hard resin. Resin alone breaks like glass once it hardens. The resin molecules cannot slide past each other, so under load it does not stretch but cracks, and the crack spreads through the whole piece instantly. The resin is therefore used as a composite mixed with a filler and a plasticizer.

| Component | Typical materials | Role |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Resin | Refined pine resin, resin of Korean pine or fir | Hardens and grips the two surfaces |
| Filler | Fine charcoal powder, wood ash, dried dung powder, clay powder | Stops cracks from advancing and spreads the stress |
| Plasticizer | Beeswax, animal fat, vegetable oil | Leaves room for the molecules to slide, giving flexibility |

---
### 2-2. Mixing Ratios
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The standard mix is below. It is only a starting point; the properties of the hardened adhesive depend strongly on temperature, so adjust it to the season and use.

| Material | Ratio by volume |
|:---|---:|
| Refined pine resin | 5 |
| Fine charcoal powder | 1-2 |
| Beeswax or oil | 0.5 or less |

| Situation | Adjustment | Reason |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Winter, cold regions | Add more plasticizer to make it softer | Resin is more fragile at low temperatures |
| Summer, hot regions | Reduce plasticizer and add more filler to make it harder | At high temperatures the joint flows |
| Fixing arrowheads and handles | Harder | Holding shape matters more than impact |
| Waterproofing, filling gaps | Softer | The material must not crack even when it moves |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Check the mix by pressing the hardened lump with a fingernail.

- Leaves a slight dent: suitable
- Does not dent at all: too hard and brittle
- The nail sinks in easily: too soft to bear a load

---
### 2-3. Collecting and Refining
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Collect lumps of resin that have flowed from wounded pines and hardened. Cutting deep wounds into a living tree to squeeze out the resin kills it. Take it from broken branch scars, felled stumps, and dead pines that have become hard with soaked-in resin (fatwood). Fatwood can also be used as kindling.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Collected resin contains bark, needles, soil, and insects. Melt it over low heat and strain it through a coarse cloth to leave clean resin. This process also drives off the volatile components, which improves how it hardens.

> **Caution:** Heated resin gives off flammable vapor, and when it catches fire it burns like an oil fire. If it boils over in a pot on an open flame it ignites at once, and splashing water on it scatters burning resin in all directions.

- Use a double boiler as a rule. Float a small container in a large pot of water to melt it.
- If a double boiler is difficult, put it over very low heat and fill the container only one third full. Keep a lid and a wet cloth nearby.
- If it catches fire, cover it with the lid to cut off oxygen.

---
### 2-4. Birch Bark Tar
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Birch bark is rich in betulin. Heating the bark to about 300-400 °C with no air reaching it (dry distillation) breaks down the betulin, and a black, sticky tar flows out. It is the same dry-distillation method as for charcoal, but the product is a liquid, not a solid.

**Double-container method**

1. Pack the upper container tightly with bark and make a small hole in it.
2. Turn the upper container upside down and fit it onto the mouth of the lower container buried in the ground.
3. Seal the seam completely with soil or clay.
4. Cover only the upper container with fire and charcoal to heat it. The tar drips down through the hole and collects.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Success depends on keeping the top hot and the bottom cold.

- Why the lower container is buried: the soil cools it. If the collected tar gets hot again, it burns away.
- If the upper part is not hot enough, the bark only scorches and no tar comes out.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Unlike pine resin, birch bark tar stays fairly flexible and is far more water-resistant. Mixed with a little filler, it becomes an adhesive; used as it is, it serves as a waterproofing agent for boats, barrels, and roofs and as a wood preservative. It softens and runs on hot days, so add more filler in summer.

## 3. Adhesives from Animals
### 3-1. Collagen and Gelatin
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Hides, tendons, bones, fish skin, and swim bladders contain collagen. Collagen is a structure of three fine strands twisted like a rope, and this twist is what lets tendons withstand pulling forces.

1. Given water and moderate heat, the twist unwinds and the collagen scatters into single strands (gelatin). While it is dissolved in hot water, it is a liquid.
2. As the temperature falls, the strands tangle again into a net that traps water, and this net hardens and grips the two surfaces.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This process is reversible. Applying heat and water again loosens the net, so repair and disassembly are possible, and for the same reason it is weak against water and heat.

---
### 3-2. Heating Limits
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Too much heat breaks the long chains of gelatin. Shortened chains cannot form a dense net when they cool, so instead of glue you get a thin broth. Broken chains cannot be restored.

| Temperature | What happens |
|:---|:---|
| 60-70 °C | The twist unwinds slowly. It takes a long time but causes little damage |
| 70-85 °C | Most suitable for extraction |
| Above 85 °C | The chains begin to break and the strength drops quickly |
| Near boiling | The chains are cut short and almost all adhesive power is lost |

> **Caution:** Never boil hide glue. Once bubbles start to rise on the surface, it is already weakening.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Do both extraction and use in a double boiler. If you float the glue container in a large pot of water, the inside never exceeds 100 °C even when the outer water boils. Raising the boiling point with lye or salt works the other way, so do not use it.

---
### 3-3. Making Hide Glue
**Preparing the material: removing fat**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Fat spoils wetting and turns rancid over time, weakening the joint.

- Hides: soak them for a few days in lye made by steeping ash in water to remove hair and fat. Rinse thoroughly under running water to remove the alkali.
- Tendons: dry them, pound them, and tear them into strands.
- Bones: crush them small and boil them once, skimming off the fat that floats to the top.

**Extraction**

1. Soak the prepared material in water for about a day.
2. Hold it at a low temperature for several hours, and pour off the broth when it turns sticky.
3. Pour water over the same material again and repeat 2-3 times.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The first broth is the strongest and each later batch is weaker, so store them separately according to use.

**Concentrating**

- Reduce it slowly over low heat.
- In winter, freezing and thawing is advantageous. When frozen, only the water forms ice crystals and separates, so if you pour off the water after thawing, the concentration rises without heating.

**Drying and storage**

- Always dry the finished glue. Wet glue is a lump of protein and rots and molds within a few days.
- Spread it thin to set, cut it into pieces, and dry it completely, and it keeps for several years.
- Each time, soak only the amount you need in water and melt it in a double boiler.
- The standard consistency is thick honey. If too thick, the layer becomes thick; if too thin, it has no strength.

---
### 3-4. Advantages and Disadvantages
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;A hot wet towel or steam makes the joint come apart again. The reason animal glue has been used on instruments and furniture for hundreds of years is not its strength but that you can remove just the broken part and glue on a new one.

| Property | Advantage | Disadvantage |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Melts with heat | Repair and disassembly are possible | The joint loosens in hot places |
| Dissolves in water | Brushes and containers wash clean with water | Weak against rain and humidity |
| Responds to humidity | Moves together with the wood | Its volume changes with humidity, so the joint moves slightly with the seasons and, if repeated, opens up |
| Very hard once set | A strong joint in a thin layer | Vulnerable to impact |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;It cannot be used where water touches, in damp places, or on items left outdoors.

---
### 3-5. Fish Glue (Isinglass)
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This is glue obtained from the swim bladders of fish such as croaker and sturgeon. The swim bladder is almost pure collagen membrane, so it has little fat and impurity, melts at a low temperature, and is clear and strong.

- It can be applied in a very thin layer. When horn, wood, and sinew are layered together in the Korean composite bow (gakgung), this glue withstands the large shear force on each layer as the bow flexes.
- It is made the same way as other hide glues, only at a lower temperature. Dry the swim bladders and store them, then soak them in water and melt them slowly at around 60 °C.
- Its only, and greatest, limit is that the material is hard to obtain.

## 4. Other Adhesives and Selection Criteria
### 4-1. Casein Glue
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Casein in milk clumps and sinks as a lump in an acidic environment. It is the same phenomenon as milk curdling when it spoils, and it also occurs when you add vinegar or sour fruit juice.

1. Strain the curdled lumps through a cloth, rinse them several times with water to remove the sourness, and squeeze them tight to make casein curd.
2. Mix in slaked lime. The calcium bridges the protein chains to form a net that is almost insoluble in water.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Unlike animal glue, it does not dissolve again once set, so it is quite water-resistant.

| Material | Ratio | Note |
|:---|---:|:---|
| Casein curd | 5 | Squeezed to remove moisture |
| Slaked lime | 1 | Made by pouring water on quicklime |
| Water | As needed | About the consistency of a thick paste |

- The mixed glue begins to set within a few hours, so make only what you will use that day. Curd keeps for a long time dried and powdered.
- Limits: it is not fully waterproof. If it stays wet for a long time, mold grows. The alkaline content leaves black stains on woods such as oak.

---
### 4-2. Starch Paste
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;When starch from rice, wheat, potato, or acorn is stirred into water and heated, the starch granules absorb water, swell, and burst, releasing their chains. These chains tangle and become sticky, which is starch paste; as it dries it works into the fibers of paper and cloth and sets.

- Advantages: it does not stiffen or discolor paper and cloth when applied, and it is easily removed with water.
- Disadvantages: it cannot be used for joints that bear large loads, such as wood and bone. It dissolves instantly in water and is food for mold and insects. A little alum or salt slows mold somewhat.

---
### 4-3. Resins, Rubber, Lacquer, and Bitumen
| Material | How to obtain it | Properties and cautions |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Natural rubber | White latex of rubber trees, milkweed, and dandelion | Highly elastic and water-resistant, but low bond strength |
| Lacquer | Sap of the lacquer tree | Sets in humidity and warmth and is extremely waterproof. The sap before it sets causes severe skin inflammation |
| Bitumen | Natural asphalt, outcrops where oil seeps out | Excellent for waterproofing, but flows when hot and cracks when cold |
| Beeswax | Honeycomb | Weak bond strength. Used as a waterproofing agent and plasticizer |
| Tree sap | Fir, spruce, cherry | Used the same way as pine resin. The setting speed differs |

> **Caution:** Lacquer performs best among natural materials but is also the most dangerous to handle. Contact with the sap before it sets causes a severe rash and blisters a few days later, and, depending on the person's constitution, also affects the respiratory system. When collecting and applying it, keep bare skin covered, and handle it in a ventilated place so that you are not exposed to the fumes given off while it sets.

---
### 4-4. Comparing Properties and Choosing by Use
| Adhesive | Water resistance | Heat resistance | Strength | Reversibility | Storability |
|:---|:---|:---|:---|:---|:---|
| Pine resin glue | Medium | Low | Medium | High | Very good |
| Birch bark tar | High | Low | Medium | High | Very good |
| Animal hide glue | Low | Low | High | High | Good when dried |
| Fish glue | Low | Low | Very high | High | Good when dried |
| Casein glue | High | Medium | High | None | Good as powder |
| Starch paste | Very low | Medium | Low | High | Poor |
| Lacquer | Very high | High | High | None | Good when sealed |
| Bitumen | Very high | Very low | Low | High | Very good |

| Use | Recommended adhesive | Reason |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Fixing arrowheads and fletching | Pine resin glue + sinew wrapping | Can be repaired on the spot outdoors with just a fire |
| Indoor woodworking | Animal hide glue | Strong, and can be removed and repaired later |
| Laminated structures such as bows | Fish glue | Keeps layers thin while withstanding shear |
| Waterproofing boats, barrels, roofs | Bitumen, birch bark tar | Repels water and does not crack when the material moves |
| Paper and cloth | Starch paste | Does not stiffen or discolor the material |
| Repairing pottery and bowls | Lacquer, pine resin glue | Fills gaps as it sets |
| Joints that get wet | Casein glue, lacquer | Do not dissolve again once set |

### 4-5. Combining Gluing and Lashing
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Natural adhesives are not as strong as modern synthetic ones, so traditional methods do not leave all the force to the adhesive. The force is borne by the wrapped fibers, and the adhesive holds the fibers so they do not come loose and keeps water from seeping in. The basis of natural-material joinery is to layer gluing and lashing.

**Why lashing is effective**

1. Sinew and plant fibers are very strong in tension and take over the tensile load of the joint.
2. Wet sinew shrinks greatly as it dries, so the wrapped part tightens by itself. It is as if a clamp that presses while the glue hardens were built into the joint.
3. The wrapped fiber covers the edges of the joint and removes the place where peeling, the weakest load, would begin.

**Procedure**

1. Apply a thin layer of adhesive.
2. Wrap the sinew or fiber tightly. Do not wrap in only one direction; cross-wrapping lets it withstand twisting as well.
3. Apply more adhesive over the top to cover the fiber.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Mechanical joints such as tenons, wedges, and nails are used together on the same logic. The aim is to build a structure that does not collapse at once even if the adhesive lets go.
