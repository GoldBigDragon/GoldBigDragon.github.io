## 1. Adhesion
### 1-1. Operating Principles
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The first principle is mechanical interlocking. When an adhesive is liquid, it flows into microscopic crevices and pores on the surface of a material; upon solidifying in place, the hardened adhesive acts as countless tiny hooks clinging to the surface. For porous materials such as wood, leather, bone, paper, and fired ceramics, this force accounts for most of the adhesion.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The second principle is intermolecular attraction. When adhesive molecules and surface molecules are brought extremely close together, attractive forces arise between them. Although strong, this force diminishes rapidly as distance increases, making it effective only when two molecules are bonded at the nanometer scale. On smooth surfaces like glass or metal, this second force is virtually everything.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Both principles lead to the same practical conclusion: the adhesive must make physical contact with the surface.

---
### 1-2. No Bonding Without Wetting
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;If an applied adhesive beads up into droplets on a surface, the actual contact area is only a fraction of the apparent area. Conversely, if it spreads out thin, nearly the entire surface touches the adhesive. This difference is called wetting, and what spoils wetting is usually grease.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Dust interferes in a different way. Dust particles get trapped between the adhesive and the surface, becoming a weak layer themselves. Because the adhesive merely sticks to the dust and the dust to the surface, failure ultimately occurs within the dust layer. Old wood surfaces behave similarly. Cut surfaces exposed for a long time become oxidized and clogged; simply scraping or shaving them immediately before bonding to reveal fresh wood dramatically improves adhesive strength.

> **Scraping a surface fresh and roughening it often boosts bonding strength far more than switching adhesive types.**

---
### 1-3. Modes of Adhesive Failure
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Examining a detached joint reveals what went wrong.

| Failure Mode | Appearance at the Joint | Cause and Remedy |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Adhesive Failure | The adhesive remains entirely on one side only | The surface was dirty or wetting was poor. Remedy the surface preparation |
| Cohesive Failure | The adhesive splits down the middle, leaving half on each side | The adhesive itself was weak or the layer was too thick. Adjust the formulation and layer thickness |
| Substrate Failure | Wood or bone was torn out along the joint | The bond succeeded. No further improvement is needed |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The goal is always substrate failure. Once the bond becomes stronger than the base material, there is no further reason to change adhesives.

---
### 1-4. Thinner Adhesive Layers Are Stronger
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;It might seem that applying adhesive thickly makes it stronger, but the opposite is true. A thick layer shrinks as it hardens, leaving internal stress, and thicker layers carry a higher probability of defects like air bubbles and cracks. Above all, when load is applied, a thick layer splits within itself.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Consequently, attempts to fill gaps using only adhesive fail almost without exception. Gaps should be filled with splints or wedges of the same material, assigning the adhesive merely the role of bridging the thin space between them. The same logic applies to pressing two surfaces together. Clamping squeezes out excess adhesive to keep the layer thin while holding the surfaces together so they do not separate while curing.

---
### 1-5. Force Direction Determines Strength
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Even when bonding the same surface area with the same adhesive, how long it holds varies by more than tenfold depending on the direction of the load.

| Load Type | Mode of Applied Force | Resistance of the Bonded Joint |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Shear | Slides sideways along the bonded plane | Strongest |
| Compression | Pushes directly against the bonded plane | Strong |
| Tension | Pulls perpendicularly away from the bonded plane | Moderate |
| Cleavage | Wedges one side open | Weak |
| Peel | Peels away gradually starting from one edge | Weakest |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Peel stress is exceptionally weak because force is concentrated not across the entire bonded area, but along a single thin line at the peeling front. Bonding a large surface area does no good here. Therefore, when designing joints, overlap the two materials instead of placing them end-to-end, and wrap or clamp the edges to prevent lifting, thereby eliminating the starting point for peeling. This is the primary reason binding always accompanies adhesion when using natural materials.
