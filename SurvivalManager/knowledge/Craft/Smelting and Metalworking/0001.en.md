## Introduction
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Once you use up everything left in the storehouse, the next problem begins. Blades become dull, and repeatedly sharpening them eventually wears them away completely. Nails rust, saw teeth break off, and cauldrons develop holes. In a world where replacement parts no longer arrive, if you cannot produce new metal, technological capabilities quietly regress while you try to conserve what remains. Though human survival is possible with only soil and wood, the very tools used to work soil and wood are made of metal.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;However, most metals do not exist in the ground in metallic form. Aside from gold, native copper, and meteoric iron, almost all metals used by humans are buried as mineral ores bonded with oxygen or sulfur. Smelting is ultimately the process of breaking these chemical bonds to remove the oxygen, and hot carbon is what performs this task. Thus, the history of smelting is a history of searching for ways to maintain higher temperatures for longer periods. What determines the temperature is not merely the type of fuel, but how rapidly oxygen is forced in. The person operating the bellows determines the limits of the furnace.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This document is not intended for memorizing blacksmithing work procedures. Instead, it aims to clarify why iron is extracted from swamp beds, why burning raw coal makes iron brittle, why iron produced in a bloomery emerges as a solid sponge-like mixture rather than a liquid, and why bronze with 12% tin is better suited for blades than bronze with 20% tin. Understanding the underlying principles enables sound judgment even when the available ore and fuel differ from those described in textbooks.
