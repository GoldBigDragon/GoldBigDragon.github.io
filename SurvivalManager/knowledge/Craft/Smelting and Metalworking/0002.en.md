## 1. Where Iron Is Found
### 1-1. Metals Buried as Minerals
- Gold hardly reacts with oxygen, so it can be picked up as grains from a stream bed. Copper occurs in some regions as native copper, and meteoric iron is already an alloy of iron and nickel. Apart from these three, metals are combined with oxygen or sulfur.
- How strongly a metal holds oxygen differs from metal to metal. Copper oxide lets go of its oxygen relatively easily, but iron oxide holds on much harder, requiring a higher temperature and dependable reducing conditions.
- The Bronze Age came before the Iron Age because of the temperature required, not because of reserves. Iron is the fourth most abundant element in the Earth's crust, hundreds of times more plentiful than copper.

---
### 1-2. Identifying Iron Ore
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Identify ore by weight, magnetism, and streak. The streak is the color of the powder left when a mineral is rubbed on a piece of rough pottery or the back of a broken tile, and unlike the apparent color it hardly ever changes.

| Ore | Composition | Iron content | How to recognize it |
|:---|:---|---:|:---|
| Magnetite | Fe₃O₄ | Up to 72% | Black and heavy, strongly attracted to a magnet. Streak black |
| Hematite | Fe₂O₃ | Up to 70% | Varies from reddish brown to silvery gray, but the streak is always red |
| Limonite | FeO(OH)·nH₂O | 50-60% | Yellowish brown lumps. Streak yellow-brown. Holds water, so the content is low |
| Siderite | FeCO₃ | 48% | Gray-brown. Fizzes weakly when vinegar is dropped on it |
| Bog iron | A kind of limonite | 30-55% | Orange lumps from the bottom of a wetland. Soft and full of holes |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The iron contents in the table are for pure minerals; actual lumps are lower because sand and soil are mixed in. Limonite and bog iron hold bound water, so roasting them red-hot before smelting to drive off the water and make them brittle makes them easier to handle.

### 1-3. Bog Iron
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In a disaster, the most realistic source of iron is not a mine but a wetland. You do not need to dig deep, a single shovel is enough, and it forms again in the place you took it from.

- Formation: in oxygen-free groundwater, iron is dissolved as ferrous (2+) ions. When this water meets air at the edge of a wetland or at a spring, the iron is oxidized to ferric (3+) iron and drops out as an iron hydroxide precipitate. Iron-oxidizing bacteria (Gallionella, Leptothrix) live on the energy from this oxidation and greatly speed up the reaction. The orange mud on a wetland bottom is iron that microbes have collected over decades.
- Renewal: according to Scandinavian records, a bog that had been dug out was left for 20-30 years and then dug again. Unlike a vein, it does not run out.

| Clue | What it looks like | Caution |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Iridescent film | An oily sheen on the surface of standing water | If it breaks apart like glass shards when stirred with a stick, it is iron; if it merges back together, it is oil |
| Red sediment | Rust-colored deposit where spring water flows out | If it feels slimy to the touch, it is a colony of iron bacteria |
| Orange mud | A deep orange layer at the bottom of a wetland | It dries into hard lumps, and these are the ore |
| Pebble-shaped lumps | Holey brown stones from pea size to fist size | Clearly heavier than stones of the same size |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Limitation: bog iron has a relatively high phosphorus content. Phosphorus causes cold shortness (breaking easily when cold). Iron made from bog iron stretches well when hammered but easily breaks as an axe edge in winter.

### 1-4. Iron Sand
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;When granite and andesite weather, grains of magnetite are left behind. Heavy and slow to wear down, they are sorted by water and waves in rivers and on beaches and gather in layers of black sand.

- Collecting: put a magnet in a thin cloth or plastic bag and sweep it over the sand. Pulling only the magnet out over a container, bag and all, makes the iron sand that was stuck to it fall off all at once. If you touch the magnet to the sand bare-handed, it is hard to remove the powder that sticks. Repeating it three or four times yields a concentrate with a high proportion of magnetite.
- Advantages: it is already finely crushed so no grinding is needed, and it has few impurities.
- Disadvantages: the grains are so fine that they easily block the gaps through which gas passes in the furnace, and being light they are blown away by the blast. This is why Japanese tatara ironmaking added iron sand a little at a time over a long period.
