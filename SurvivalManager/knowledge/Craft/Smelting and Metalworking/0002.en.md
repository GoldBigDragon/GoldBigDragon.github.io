## 1. Where Iron Is Found
### 1-1. Metals Are Buried as Minerals
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The reason humans processed gold first was not because gold was rare, but because gold exists as pure gold. Since gold barely reacts with oxygen, it can be picked up as grains from stream beds. Copper also occurs naturally as native copper in some regions, and meteoric iron fallen from the sky is already an alloy of iron and nickel. However, these three are exceptions; almost all other metals are bound to oxygen or sulfur.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The strength with which a metal holds onto oxygen varies from metal to metal. Copper oxide releases oxygen relatively easily, but iron oxide holds onto it much more tightly, requiring higher temperatures and stronger reducing conditions. The reason the Bronze Age preceded the Iron Age is not because copper is more abundant than iron. Iron is the fourth most abundant element in the Earth's crust, hundreds of times more plentiful than copper. What determined the historical sequence was not reserves, but temperature.

### 1-2. How to Identify Types of Iron Ore
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The most practical methods for identifying ores are weight, magnetism, and streak color. Streak color refers to the color of the powder left behind when a mineral is rubbed against a piece of unglazed porcelain or the back of a broken tile; unlike superficial appearance, it almost never changes.

| Ore | Composition | Iron Content | How to Identify |
|:---|:---|---:|:---|
| Magnetite | Fe₃O₄ | Up to 72% | Black, heavy, and strongly attracted to magnets. Streak is black. |
| Hematite | Fe₂O₃ | Up to 70% | Ranges from reddish-brown to silver-gray, but streak is always red. |
| Limonite | FeO(OH)·nH₂O | 50–60% | Yellowish-brown mass with a yellowish-brown streak. Contains bound water, resulting in lower iron content. |
| Siderite | FeCO₃ | 48% | Grayish-brown, effervesces weakly when vinegar is dropped on it. |
| Bog Iron | A type of limonite | 30–55% | Orange lumps found at the bottom of wetlands; soft and porous. |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The iron contents in the table represent pure mineral values; actual dug-up lumps have lower percentages due to mixed sand and soil. Since limonite and bog iron contain bound water, roasting them red-hot before smelting to drive off water and make them brittle makes processing significantly easier.

### 1-3. Bog Iron Is Created by Bacteria
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In a crisis, the most realistic source of iron is not a mine, but a wetland. Bog iron does not require digging deep into the ground, needs nothing more than a shovel, and most importantly, it regenerates.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The mechanism of formation lies in the two-faced nature created by the presence or absence of oxygen. In oxygen-deprived groundwater, iron dissolves in the form of ferrous ions (Fe²⁺). When this water encounters air at wetland edges or springs, the ferrous iron oxidizes into ferric iron (Fe³⁺). Because ferric iron is virtually insoluble in water, it precipitates out as iron hydroxide. While this reaction occurs spontaneously, iron-oxidizing bacteria such as Gallionella or Leptothrix ochracea feed on the small amount of energy released during this oxidation process, greatly accelerating the reaction. In other words, the orange mud accumulating at the bottom of a swamp is iron collected by microorganisms over decades.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Consequently, bog iron regenerates in the same spot after being harvested. Old Scandinavian records state that after harvesting a bog, it was left for twenty to thirty years before being dug up again. Unlike mineral veins, the fact that it is a non-depletable resource carries great significance in survival situations.

| Clue | Visual Appearance | Cautions |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Rainbow oil film | Oil-like iridescent film shimmering on standing water surface | When stirred with a stick, if it breaks into glass-like fragments, it is iron. Oil merges back together. |
| Red sediment | Rust-colored sediment where spring water flows out | Slippery to the touch; this indicates an iron bacteria colony. |
| Orange mud | Deep orange layer at the bottom of the wetland | Hardens into tough lumps when dry; this is the ore. |
| Pebble-like lumps | Porous brown stones ranging from pea to fist size | Noticeably heavier than rocks of similar size. |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;However, bog iron contains a relatively high amount of phosphorus. Phosphorus causes cold shortness, making iron brittle when cold. Therefore, iron made from bog iron may stretch well when hammered hot, but is prone to snapping when used as an axe blade in winter. This is the fundamental limitation of bog iron smelting.

### 1-4. Collecting Iron Sand with Magnets
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;When granite or andesite weathers, magnetite grains contained within them remain. Because magnetite is heavy and resistant to wear, it gets sorted and concentrated in specific spots by water and waves along rivers and beaches. This forms layers of black sand.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The collection method is simple. Place a magnet inside a thin cloth or plastic bag, sweep it over the sand, and then remove only the magnet from the bag over a container; the adhering iron sand will drop off all at once. Applying a magnet to sand with bare hands makes removing the attached powder difficult. Repeating this process three to four times yields a concentrate with a significantly high proportion of magnetite.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Iron sand has the advantage of already being finely crushed, requiring no separate pulverization, and having low impurity levels. However, because the grains are extremely fine, they easily clog the passages for gas flow inside the furnace and can be blown away by the blast air. This is why Japanese Tatara ironmaking used a method of feeding iron sand gradually in small batches over a long period.
