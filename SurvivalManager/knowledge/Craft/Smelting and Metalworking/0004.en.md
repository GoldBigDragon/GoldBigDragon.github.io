## 3. Temperature and Airflow Determine Furnace Limits
### 3-1. Melting Temperatures by Metal
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Which metals you can work with is determined by the temperatures your furnace can generate. The melting points below are based on pure metals; most alloys melt at lower temperatures than pure metals.

| Metal | Melting Point | Boiling Point | Remarks |
|:---|---:|---:|:---|
| Tin | 232°C | 2,602°C | Melts even in campfire ashes |
| Lead | 327°C | 1,749°C | Emits toxic vapor from around 500°C |
| Zinc | 420°C | 907°C | Boiling point is lower than the melting point of copper |
| Aluminum | 660°C | 2,470°C | Melts in charcoal fire, but slumps as its skin softens due to oxide film |
| Silver | 962°C | 2,162°C | Achievable with air-blown charcoal fire |
| Gold | 1,064°C | 2,856°C | Achievable with air-blown charcoal fire |
| Copper | 1,085°C | 2,562°C | Requires forced air draft |
| Cast Iron | 1,150–1,300°C | - | Carbon content over 2.1%, far lower than pure iron |
| Pure Iron | 1,538°C | 2,862°C | Difficult to reach with primitive furnaces |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There are two key observations to draw from the table. First, cast iron melts at temperatures nearly 400°C lower than pure iron. As carbon content lowers the melting point, if iron absorbs too much carbon inside the furnace, it liquefies and runs out. This is the operating principle of a blast furnace to extract liquid pig iron, yet simultaneously represents a failure in a bloomery. Second, zinc's boiling point of 907°C is lower than copper's melting point of 1,085°C, which marks the starting point of hazards that must be remembered when melting brass.

### 3-2. Actual Temperatures Produced by Fire
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The theoretical maximum temperature a fuel can generate differs completely from the temperature a furnace actually delivers.

| Heat Source | Approximate Temperature |
|:---|---:|
| Wood campfire | 600–900°C |
| Naturally ventilated charcoal fire | 800–1,000°C |
| Bellows-blown charcoal fire | 1,100–1,300°C |
| In front of the tuyere in a bloomery furnace | 1,200–1,400°C |
| Forced-draft crucible furnace | 1,200–1,400°C |
| Modern blast furnace | 1,500–2,000°C |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Learning to gauge temperatures by color allows assessment without a thermometer. However, observations must be made in dark surroundings for accuracy.

| Color | Approximate Temperature |
|:---|---:|
| Dark red | 550–700°C |
| Bright red | 800–900°C |
| Orange | 950–1,050°C |
| Yellow | 1,100–1,250°C |
| Near-white yellow | Above 1,300°C |

### 3-3. Air, Not Fuel, Raises the Temperature
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Stacking twice as much charcoal does not raise the temperature. Temperature is determined not by how much heat is stored, but by the difference between how fast heat is generated per unit time and how fast it escapes. Because heat is released only when carbon meets oxygen, the rate of heat generation is directly tied to the supply rate of oxygen.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;From this, three furnace design principles emerge. First is forcing air into the furnace. Second is reducing paths through which heat escapes, which is achieved using thick clay walls and narrow hearth openings. Third is concentrating the zone where combustion occurs in one spot. A widely spread fire, no matter how fiercely it burns, cannot heat a single point sufficiently.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;However, blowing in air indiscriminately is not the solution. Nitrogen accounts for 78% of air and does not participate in the reaction; it merely carries away heat as it flows through the furnace and exits the chimney. Excess air beyond what is needed cools the furnace rather than heating it, and simultaneously creates an oxidizing atmosphere inside, re-oxidizing the iron that was painstakingly reduced. Good air supply is not simply strong blowing, but uninterrupted, steady airflow.

---
### 3-4. Natural Draft Furnaces Work on the Chimney Effect
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Numerous examples of smelting without bellows remain widely across various regions of Africa. The governing principle is the stack effect. Because hot gases inside the furnace are lighter than ambient air, they rise upward, pulling cold air through bottom openings to fill the void. This force is proportional to the temperature difference inside and outside as well as the height of the furnace; thus, stacking the furnace higher strengthens the draft.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;For this reason, natural draft smelting furnaces are built tall, reaching 2 to 3 meters, with multiple small openings around the base. Rather than making a single large hole, creating several small holes accelerates incoming air velocity so it reaches deep into the center of the furnace. This also explains why many furnace remnants are found on ridges or valley entrances where winds blow consistently.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The limitation of natural draft is speed. Achieving the same quantity of iron requires significantly more time and charcoal, but eliminates the need for manual continuous pumping of bellows for hours. It is an advantageous option under labor-scarce conditions.

---
### 3-5. Air Supply Must Be Uninterrupted
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The simplest bellows design consists of a leather bag opened, closed, and squeezed by hand to force air out. While simple to construct, airflow stops whenever the bag is refilled. The moment airflow stops, the temperature directly in front of the tuyere drops and a brief oxidizing atmosphere forms inside the furnace. As these short interruptions accumulate, yield drops noticeably.

| Type | Operating Mechanism | Characteristics |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Single leather bellows | Squeezes a bag to generate air | Simple to build, but airflow is interrupted |
| Double bellows | Alternately squeezes two bags for continuous draft | Virtually no interruption. Standard in Africa and ancient Egypt |
| Box bellows | Uses piston and valves to push air on both forward and backward strokes | Enables one person to provide continuous airflow. Widely used in East Asia |
| Foot bellows | Operated by stepping to compress a large bag | Keeps hands free and allows long-term operation |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The tuyere should extend slightly beyond the furnace wall into the interior, but if it protrudes into the heart of the flame, it burns away. This is why clay tubes are used, or the tip of a metal tube is coated with clay. The angle is generally pointed downward so that airflow passes through the hottest zone near the furnace floor.
