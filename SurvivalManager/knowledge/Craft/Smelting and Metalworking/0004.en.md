## 3. Temperature and Blast Set the Limits of the Furnace
### 3-1. Melting Temperatures of Metals
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The metals you can work are determined by the temperature your furnace can produce. Melting points are for pure metals, and most alloys melt at lower temperatures than the pure metals.

| Metal | Melting point | Boiling point | Notes |
|:---|---:|---:|:---|
| Tin | 232 °C | 2,602 °C | Melts even in campfire ashes |
| Lead | 327 °C | 1,749 °C | Gives off vapor from about 500 °C |
| Zinc | 420 °C | 907 °C | Boiling point is lower than the melting point of copper |
| Aluminum | 660 °C | 2,470 °C | Melts in a charcoal fire, but because of the oxide film the outside softens and slumps |
| Silver | 962 °C | 2,162 °C | Possible in a charcoal fire with blast |
| Gold | 1,064 °C | 2,856 °C | Possible in a charcoal fire with blast |
| Copper | 1,085 °C | 2,562 °C | Forced blast needed |
| Cast iron | 1,150-1,300 °C | - | Carbon 2.1% or more. Far lower than pure iron |
| Pure iron | 1,538 °C | 2,862 °C | Hard to reach with a primitive furnace |

- Cast iron melts at a temperature nearly 400 °C lower than pure iron. Mixing in carbon lowers the melting point, so when iron absorbs a lot of carbon in the furnace it becomes liquid and runs down. This is the principle by which a blast furnace taps molten iron, and in a bloomery it is regarded as a failure.
- The boiling point of zinc (907 °C) is lower than the melting point of copper (1,085 °C). This is the starting point of the danger in melting brass.

### 3-2. Fire Temperatures
| Heat source | Approximate temperature |
|:---|---:|
| Firewood campfire | 600-900 °C |
| Naturally drafted charcoal fire | 800-1,000 °C |
| Charcoal fire blown with bellows | 1,100-1,300 °C |
| In front of the tuyere of a bloomery furnace | 1,200-1,400 °C |
| Forced-draft crucible furnace | 1,200-1,400 °C |
| Modern blast furnace | 1,500-2,000 °C |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Reading temperature by color lets you judge without a thermometer. It is accurate only when viewed in the dark.

| Color | Approximate temperature |
|:---|---:|
| Dark red | 550-700 °C |
| Bright red | 800-900 °C |
| Orange | 950-1,050 °C |
| Yellow | 1,100-1,250 °C |
| Yellow close to white | 1,300 °C or more |

### 3-3. Blast
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Piling up twice as much charcoal does not raise the temperature. Temperature is set by the difference between the rate at which heat is produced and the rate at which it leaks away, and because heat is produced when carbon meets oxygen, the rate of heat generation is tied to the rate of oxygen supply.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There are three principles of furnace design.

1. Force air in.
2. Reduce the places heat can escape (thick clay walls, a narrow opening).
3. Concentrate the place where combustion occurs in one spot. A fire spread over a wide area cannot heat a single point, however fiercely it burns.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Do not simply increase the air. 78% of air is nitrogen, which takes no part in the reaction and leaves through the chimney carrying only heat. Excess air cools the furnace and makes the inside oxidizing, re-oxidizing the iron that was so hard to reduce. Good blast is not strong blast but steady blast that never breaks off.

---
### 3-4. Natural-Draft Furnaces
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There are cases in several parts of Africa of smelting without bellows. The principle is the chimney effect. The hot gas in the furnace is lighter than the outside air and rises, and cold air entering through holes at the bottom fills its place. Draft is proportional to the temperature difference between inside and outside and to the height of the furnace, so the taller the furnace is built, the stronger it gets.

- Build the furnace tall, 2-3 m, and make several small holes at the bottom. The air speed is higher than with one large hole, so air can be pushed to the center of the furnace.
- Build it on a ridge or at the mouth of a valley where the wind blows steadily.
- Limitation: it takes much more time and charcoal to get the same amount of iron. In return, there is no need to work bellows for hours, which is an advantage when labor is short.

---
### 3-5. Bellows
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;When the blast stops, the temperature in front of the tuyere drops and an oxidizing atmosphere briefly forms in the furnace. When these short interruptions add up, the yield drops noticeably, so bellows must not break off.

| Type | How it works | Characteristics |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Single leather bellows | A bag is pressed to produce wind | Easy to make, but the wind breaks off |
| Double bellows | Two bags pressed alternately to blow in turn | Almost no interruption. Standard in Africa and ancient Egypt |
| Box bellows | A piston and valves blow on both the push and the pull | One person can blow continuously. Widely used in East Asia |
| Foot bellows | A large bag pressed by stepping on it | Hands are free and it can last a long time |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The tuyere should pass through the furnace wall and go in a little way, but if it goes into the middle of the flame it burns up. Use a pipe shaped from clay, or wrap the end of a metal pipe in clay. Angle it downward so that the wind passes through the hottest zone near the furnace floor.
