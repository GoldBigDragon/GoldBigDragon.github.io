## 2. Chemistry of Fuel and Reduction
### 2-1. Why Charcoal Is Required for Smelting
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The primary reason charcoal became the fuel for smelting is its purity. Because trees accumulate almost no sulfur as they grow, the sulfur remaining in charcoal is often well under 0.1%. In contrast, coal contains 0.5% to 3% sulfur—and sometimes even more—depending on its origin.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Why sulfur is problematic is that sulfur entering iron bonds with it to form iron sulfide, which then intercalates into the boundaries between crystal grains as a thin film. The melting point of iron sulfide is far lower than that of iron, meaning it softens or melts at forging temperatures. As a result, the moment red-hot iron is hammered, the grain boundaries split open, causing the iron to tear like paper. This phenomenon is hot shortness; because it crumbles only when hot and appears normal once cooled, pinpointing the cause is even more difficult.

| Impurity | Source Path | Symptoms Offered |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Sulfur | Coal, ore mixed with pyrite | Hot shortness. Splits when hammered in a red-hot state. |
| Phosphorus | Bog iron, certain limonites | Cold shortness. Snaps under impact when cooled. |
| Silicon | Ore gangue, furnace wall clay | Forms slag. A moderate amount is necessary, but excess reduces yield. |
| Ash | Inorganic matter in fuel itself | Accumulates at the bottom of the furnace, blocking ventilation. |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The second advantage of charcoal is its physical strength. Inside the furnace, the fuel must support the weight of the ore while maintaining channels for gas to pass through. Crumbled fuel clogs these passages and smothers the air blast. This is why charcoal made from dense hardwoods like oak or birch is far better suited for smelting than pine charcoal.

### 2-2. Coke
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Coke is the carbon residue remaining after coal is placed in an airtight chamber without oxygen and heated to around 1,000°C to drive off volatile matter and tar. The underlying principle is identical to making charcoal by dry distillation of wood; the raw material is simply coal instead of wood.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;During this process, a portion of the sulfur escapes as gas, and above all, as the coal melts and re-solidifies, it fuses into hard, porous lumps. Because charcoal is weak, stacking it high causes the bottom layers to crush under their own weight; consequently, charcoal-fueled furnaces could not be expanded beyond a certain size. Coke resists crushing under weight, allowing furnaces to be built dozens of meters tall, which served as the starting point for mass steel production.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;However, securing a stable supply of coal during a crisis is difficult. Unless operating near open-pit coal seams or abandoned mines, charcoal remains virtually the only choice, and it is entirely sufficient.

### 2-3. Carbon Monoxide
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;While it is often stated that carbon steals oxygen from iron oxide, what actually does the work inside the furnace is gaseous carbon monoxide rather than solid carbon. This is because the contact area between solids is tiny, whereas gas can penetrate into the microscopic pores of the ore grains.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The reaction unfolds in three stages. First, directly in front of the tuyere, carbon reacts with oxygen to become carbon dioxide, generating immense heat. As this hot carbon dioxide passes through the layer of red-hot charcoal above, it reacts with carbon again, converting into two molecules of carbon monoxide. This endothermic reaction becomes active generally above 700°C. Then, this carbon monoxide reacts with the ore to strip away the oxygen.

| Stage | Reaction | Heat Direction |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Combustion | C + O₂ → CO₂ | Strongly exothermic |
| Regeneration | CO₂ + C → 2CO | Endothermic, active above 700°C |
| Reduction 1 | 3Fe₂O₃ + CO → 2Fe₃O₄ + CO₂ | Weakly exothermic |
| Reduction 2 | Fe₃O₄ + CO → 3FeO + CO₂ | Weakly endothermic |
| Reduction 3 | FeO + CO → Fe + CO₂ | Weakly exothermic |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Two practical conclusions emerge from this sequence. First, a sufficiently thick layer of charcoal must be stacked above the tuyere to allow enough time for carbon dioxide to revert into carbon monoxide. If ore is placed too low, instead of being reduced, it gets re-oxidized by carbon dioxide. Second, the ore must be crushed finely into pea to acorn sizes. Because gas must enter into the grains, large lumps leave the interior unreduced while only the surface is processed.

### 2-4. Iron Does Not Melt in a Bloomery
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The melting point of pure iron is 1,538°C, but the interior of a bloomery furnace powered by charcoal and bellows reaches between 1,200°C and 1,400°C at best. Therefore, the reduced iron cannot become a liquid and remains in place as tiny metallic grains. These grains adhere and aggregate to form a porous, sponge-like mass called a bloom.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Slag plays a critical role here. Silica mixed in the ore combines with unreduced iron oxide to form iron silicate, which has a melting point of roughly 1,170°C—substantially lower than iron. Thus, only the slag melts and flows down inside the furnace; as it flows, it coats the surface of the iron grains to prevent re-oxidation and creates space for the grains to fuse together.

> **If the furnace temperature fails to reach the melting point of slag, the iron grains remain scattered and lost; if the temperature is excessively high and iron absorbs too much carbon, it melts into weak, brittle pig iron. The art of bloomery smelting is maintaining this narrow temperature window for hours.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Nearly half of the bloom extracted from the furnace is still slag. It must therefore be repeatedly hammered while red-hot to squeeze out the slag like liquid. With every strike, slag sparks out and the mass becomes increasingly dense. In this process, only about 15–30% of the initial ore weight remains as usable iron. This is why forging is a refining process long before it is a shaping operation.
