## 2. The Chemistry of Fuel and Reduction
### 2-1. Why Smelting Needs Charcoal
- Cleanliness: the sulfur in charcoal is commonly under 0.1%. Depending on where it was mined, coal contains 0.5-3% sulfur or more.
- Strength: in the furnace, the fuel has to bear the weight of the ore and keep the gas passages open. Crumbled fuel blocks the passages and kills the blast. Charcoal from hard woods such as oak and birch is better suited to smelting than pine charcoal.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Sulfur that gets into iron becomes iron sulfide, which lodges like a thin film on the grain boundaries. Iron sulfide has a melting point far lower than iron and softens or melts at forging temperature. When red-hot iron is hammered, the grain boundaries split and the iron tears like a sheet of paper (hot shortness). Once cool it looks perfectly sound, so the cause is hard to find.

| Impurity | How it gets in | Symptom |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Sulfur | Coal, ore mixed with pyrite | Hot shortness. Cracks when hammered hot |
| Phosphorus | Bog iron, some limonite | Cold shortness. Breaks under impact when cold |
| Silicon | Gangue in the ore, soil from the furnace wall | Forms slag. Some is needed, and too much lowers the yield |
| Ash | Mineral matter in the fuel | Piles up on the furnace floor and blocks airflow |

### 2-2. Coke
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Coke is a lump of carbon made by heating coal in a sealed chamber without air to around 1,000 °C, driving off the volatiles and tar. The principle is the same as destructive distillation of wood into charcoal. Part of the sulfur leaves as gas, and as the coal melts and sets again it becomes a hard, porous lump.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Charcoal piled high gets crushed at the bottom under its own weight, which limited furnace size. Coke holds up under pressure, so furnaces could be raised to tens of meters, which became the starting point of mass ironmaking. In a disaster, coal is hard to secure reliably. Unless you are near an open coal seam or an abandoned mine, charcoal is practically the only option, and charcoal is enough.

### 2-3. Carbon Monoxide Reduction
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;What strips the oxygen from iron oxide in the furnace is not solid carbon but the gas carbon monoxide (CO). A gas can penetrate into the tiny pores of the ore grains.

1. Right in front of the tuyere, carbon meets oxygen to become carbon dioxide, giving off a great deal of heat.
2. This hot carbon dioxide passes through the glowing charcoal layer above, meets carbon, and turns into two molecules of carbon monoxide. This reaction absorbs heat and is generally active above 700 °C.
3. The carbon monoxide meets the ore and strips off its oxygen.

| Stage | Reaction | Direction of heat |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Combustion | C + O₂ → CO₂ | Strongly exothermic |
| Regeneration | CO₂ + C → 2CO | Endothermic, proceeds above 700 °C |
| Reduction 1 | 3Fe₂O₃ + CO → 2Fe₃O₄ + CO₂ | Weakly exothermic |
| Reduction 2 | Fe₃O₄ + CO → 3FeO + CO₂ | Weakly endothermic |
| Reduction 3 | FeO + CO → Fe + CO₂ | Weakly exothermic |

- Pile the charcoal layer above the tuyere thick enough. The carbon dioxide needs time to turn back into carbon monoxide. If ore is put in too low, instead of being reduced it is oxidized again by carbon dioxide.
- Crush the ore finely, from bean size to acorn size. If the lumps are large, gas cannot get inside, so only the outside is reduced and the inside is left as it was.

### 2-4. Bloomery Ironmaking
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The melting point of pure iron is 1,538 °C, and the inside of a bloomery furnace built with charcoal and bellows reaches 1,200-1,400 °C at best. The reduced iron cannot become liquid and remains as small grains that stick and clump together into a sponge-like lump full of holes. This is the bloom.

- Slag: silica in the ore combines with unreduced iron oxide to become iron silicate. Its melting point, about 1,170 °C, is well below that of iron, so in the furnace only the slag melts and runs down. As it runs down, it coats the surface of the iron grains to prevent re-oxidation and creates places for the grains to join.
- Temperature range: if the temperature does not reach the slag's melting point, the process ends with the iron grains scattered. If it is so high that the iron absorbs a lot of carbon, it melts and runs down as soft, brittle pig iron. Bloomery smelting is the skill of holding this narrow temperature range for several hours.
- Refining: nearly half of the bloom taken from the furnace is slag. Squeeze the slag out by hammering repeatedly while red-hot. Only 15-30% of the weight of the ore first put in remains as usable iron. Forging is refining work before it is shaping.
