## 4. Alloy Ratios and Heat Treatment
### 4-1. The Principle of Hardening Through Mixing
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Pure metals are generally soft. Metal deforms when layers of atoms within its crystal lattice slide past one another, a movement facilitated by the motion of lattice defects called dislocations. When atoms of different sizes are mixed into the structure, the lattice becomes locally distorted, and these distorted sites block the movement of dislocations. Consequently, alloys become harder than their constituent pure metals.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;At the same time, the melting point generally decreases. This occurs because mixing different atoms makes it difficult to form a regular crystal lattice. At specific ratios, a eutectic point appears where the mixture melts at a temperature far lower than any of its constituent metals. This is the fundamental reason solder can exist.

---
### 4-2. Bronze
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Bronze is an alloy of copper and tin; as tin content increases, it becomes harder but simultaneously more brittle. Understanding this trade-off clarifies why ancient craftspeople used different ratios for different items.

| Tin Ratio | Properties | Suitable Applications |
|---:|:---|:---|
| 5–8% | Soft and tough; can be hammered thin | Vessels, sheets, ornaments |
| 10–12% | Hard while withstanding impacts | Knives, axes, arrowheads, tools |
| 15–20% | Extremely hard and sharpens well; shatters under impact | Mirrors, gongs, bearings |
| 20–25% | Brittle like glass, but produces a clear sound | Bells, percussion instruments |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;When tin was difficult to obtain, lead was sometimes mixed in. While lead improves the flow of molten metal to easily fill complex molds, it reduces strength and leaves toxic residue. Lead should not be added to bronze used for tableware or cooking utensils.

### 4-3. Brass and Low-Melting-Point Alloys
| Alloy | Composition | Properties and Cautions |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Brass | Copper + 5–40% Zinc | Highest ductility around 30% zinc. Exceeding 37% increases hardness but makes cold working difficult |
| Solder | 60 Tin + 40 Lead | Begins melting around 183°C. Lead-free tin-silver solder has a higher melting point |
| Pewter | 85–99 Tin + Copper, Antimony | Melts around 230°C. Lead contained in historical pewter is unsuitable for tableware |
| Gunmetal | 88 Copper + 10 Tin + 2 Zinc | Highly resistant to seawater and friction; used for valves and bearings |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;A critical caution when making brass directly is that zinc boils and evaporates before copper even melts. Traditionally, rather than adding metallic zinc, zinc ore, charcoal, and copper were heated together so that zinc vapor was immediately absorbed directly into the copper. Dropping zinc pieces into molten metal causes a significant portion to vaporize into white smoke, which is the primary cause of metal fume fever (toxicity).

### 4-4. Steel
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Iron, steel, and cast iron are not different metals, but the same metal containing different amounts of carbon. A difference in carbon content of less than 1% completely alters its properties.

| Carbon Content | Name | Properties | Applications |
|:---|:---|:---|:---|
| Under 0.05% | Wrought Iron | Extremely tough and soft; does not harden when quenched | Nails, chains, wire, hinges |
| 0.05–0.25% | Mild Steel | Tough and easy to forge and weld | Structural material, shovels, tool handles |
| 0.3–0.6% | Medium Carbon Steel | Hardens significantly when quenched | Springs, hammers, axes |
| 0.6–1.0% | High Carbon Steel | Sharpens well and retains its edge long | Knives, chisels, plane blades |
| 1.0–2.1% | Tool Steel | Extremely hard but vulnerable to impacts | Files, cold chisels, drill bits |
| Over 2.1% | Cast Iron | Easy to melt and suitable for casting, but shatters when hammered | Cauldrons, stoves, cast parts |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The easiest way to identify these distinctions when recycling scrap metal is the spark test. When ground against a grindstone or rough rock, sparks that extend long and straight indicate lower carbon content, while sparks that burst like stars into multiple branches indicate higher carbon content. Automotive leaf springs, files, bearing balls, and old hand tools are prime sources of high carbon steel.

---
### 4-5. Heat Treatment
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Steel with sufficient carbon content changes its properties dramatically through heating and cooling alone. Heating to approximately 750–850°C alters the crystal structure of iron, allowing carbon to dissolve uniformly within the lattice.

| Treatment | Method | Result |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Quenching | Rapid cooling in water or oil from a red-hot state | Extremely hard, but shatters like glass |
| Tempering | Reheating quenched steel to 150–350°C | Yields a bit of hardness to regain toughness |
| Annealing | Cooling extremely slowly inside ashes or sand after heating | Becomes softest and easiest to machine |
| Normalizing | Cooling in still air after heating | Homogenizes structure to balanced intermediate properties |
| Carburizing | Prolonged heating in charcoal powder at 900–950°C | Carbon enters only the surface, making the exterior hard while keeping the core tough |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Quenching provides hardness due to the formation of martensite. Rapid cooling leaves no time for carbon dissolved in the lattice to escape, creating a distorted crystal forcefully trapping carbon. Because this distortion almost completely blocks the movement of dislocations, the metal hardens; however, substantial internal stress remains, causing it to shatter under small impacts. In order of cooling speed (fastest to slowest): saltwater, plain water, oil, and air—with faster rates carrying a higher risk of cracking. Immersing a thin blade in saltwater frequently cracks it on the spot.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Therefore, quenched items must undergo tempering. Tempering temperature can be read by the color of the surface oxide film; polishing the quenched blade clean and slowly heating it causes colors to pass in sequence.

| Oxide Film Color | Approximate Temperature | Suitable Items |
|:---|---:|:---|
| Light straw | 220°C | Razor blades, wood carving chisels |
| Dark straw | 240°C | Knives, plane blades |
| Brown | 260°C | Chisels, scissors |
| Purple | 280°C | Axes, woodworking tools |
| Deep blue | 300–310°C | Springs, saws |

> **When a thermometer is unavailable, the most reliable way to determine the austenitizing temperature is with a magnet. Because iron loses its magnetism at approximately 770°C, the moment a magnet no longer sticks to heated iron signals that the quenching temperature has almost been reached.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Carburizing is a method that allows making edged tools even from carbon-free wrought iron. Packing the item in a container filled with charcoal and bone powder and maintaining it around 900°C for a long time causes carbon to slowly diffuse from the surface inward. Because diffusion depth is proportional to the square root of time, obtaining double the depth requires four times the time. Even maintaining 900°C for eight hours yields a depth of only around 1 mm. Consequently, carburized items leave almost no margin for grinding away material.
