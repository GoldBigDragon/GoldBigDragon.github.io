## 4. Alloy Ratios and Heat Treatment
### 4-1. The Principle of Alloys
- Hardness: pure metals are generally soft. A metal bends because layers of atoms inside the crystal slide, and the sliding occurs as dislocations, which are lattice defects, move. When atoms of different sizes are mixed in, the lattice is locally distorted and blocks the movement of dislocations, so an alloy is harder than the original metal.
- Melting point: it generally goes down. At certain ratios a eutectic point appears, melting at a temperature far lower than any of the constituent metals. This is why solder can exist.

---
### 4-2. Bronze
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;An alloy of copper mixed with tin. The more tin, the harder it becomes and at the same time the more brittle.

| Tin ratio | Properties | Suitable uses |
|---:|:---|:---|
| 5-8% | Soft and tough. Can be hammered flat | Vessels, sheets, ornaments |
| 10-12% | Hard while withstanding impact | Knives, axes, arrowheads, tools |
| 15-20% | Very hard and takes a good edge. Shatters on impact | Mirrors, gongs, bearings |
| 20-25% | Brittle as glass but with a clear sound | Bells, percussion instruments |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;When there is not enough tin, lead is sometimes mixed in. Lead improves the flow of the molten metal so it fills complicated molds more easily, but lowers strength and leaves toxicity. Do not put lead in bronze for tableware and cooking utensils.

---
### 4-3. Brass and Low-Melting Alloys
| Alloy | Composition | Properties and cautions |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Brass | Copper + 5-40% zinc | Stretches best around 30% zinc. Above 37% it gets harder but is difficult to work cold |
| Solder | Tin 60 + lead 40 | Begins to melt at about 183 °C. Lead-free tin-silver solder has a higher melting point |
| Pewter | Tin 85-99 + copper, antimony | Melts at around 230 °C. The lead in old pewter makes it unsuitable for tableware |
| Gunmetal | Copper 88 + tin 10 + zinc 2 | Resistant to seawater and friction, used for valves and bearings |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;When making brass, the zinc boils off and flies away before the copper melts. Traditionally, zinc was not added directly; zinc ore, charcoal, and copper were heated together so that the zinc vapor was absorbed straight into the copper. If you throw pieces of zinc into molten metal, a good part of it turns into white smoke and disappears, and this smoke is the cause of poisoning.

### 4-4. Steel
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Iron, steel, and cast iron are not different metals but the same metal differing only in carbon content. A difference of less than 1% carbon changes the properties completely.

| Carbon content | Name | Properties | Uses |
|:---|:---|:---|:---|
| Under 0.05% | Wrought iron | Very tough and soft. Does not harden when quenched | Nails, chains, wire, hinges |
| 0.05-0.25% | Mild steel | Tough, easy to forge and weld | Structural members, shovels, tool handles |
| 0.3-0.6% | Medium-carbon steel | Becomes quite hard when quenched | Springs, hammers, axes |
| 0.6-1.0% | High-carbon steel | Forges well and the edge lasts | Knives, chisels, plane blades |
| 1.0-2.1% | Tool steel | Extremely hard but weak against impact | Files, punches, drills |
| 2.1% or more | Cast iron | Melts easily so suited to casting, but breaks when hammered | Pots, braziers, cast parts |

- Spark test: judge the carbon content of scrap by the sparks thrown off when it is ground on a whetstone or a rough stone. If the sparks are long and shoot out straight, the carbon is low; if the ends burst into several branches like stars, the carbon is high.
- Sources of high-carbon steel: automobile leaf springs, files, ball bearings, old hand tools

---
### 4-5. Heat Treatment
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Steel with enough carbon changes greatly in properties through heating and cooling alone. Heating it to roughly 750-850 °C changes the crystal structure of the iron, and carbon dissolves evenly into the lattice.

| Treatment | Method | Result |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Quenching | Rapid cooling in water or oil from the heated state | Very hard but shatters like glass |
| Tempering | Reheating the quenched piece to 150-350 °C | Gives up a little hardness and recovers toughness |
| Annealing | After heating, cooling very slowly in ashes or sand | Becomes the softest and easiest to work |
| Normalizing | After heating, cooling in air | The structure is evened out, giving intermediate properties |
| Carburizing | Heating for a long time at 900-950 °C in charcoal powder | Carbon enters only the surface, so the outside is hard and the inside tough |

- Why quenching makes steel hard: with rapid cooling, the carbon in the lattice has no time to escape, and a distorted crystal (martensite) forms with the carbon trapped inside. The distortion almost completely blocks the movement of dislocations, making the steel hard, but the internal stress is large, so it breaks even from a small impact.
- Cooling speed: the faster, the harder. In order of salt water, plain water, oil, and air, and the risk of cracking increases in the same order. It is not uncommon for a thin blade dipped in salt water to crack on the spot.
- Always temper quenched items. If you rub the quenched blade clean to a shine and heat it slowly, the colors of the surface oxide film change in order and tell you the temperature.

| Oxide film color | Approximate temperature | Suitable items |
|:---|---:|:---|
| Pale straw | 220 °C | Razor blades, carving tools |
| Dark straw | 240 °C | Knives, plane blades |
| Brown | 260 °C | Chisels, scissors |
| Purple | 280 °C | Axes, woodworking tools |
| Deep blue | 300-310 °C | Springs, saws |

- When you have no thermometer, judge the quenching temperature (austenitizing temperature) with a magnet. Iron loses its magnetism at about 770 °C, so the moment a magnet no longer sticks to the heated iron is a signal that it has nearly reached quenching temperature.
- Carburizing makes it possible to make blades even from wrought iron with no carbon. If you bury the piece in a container packed with charcoal powder and bone meal and hold it at around 900 °C for a long time, carbon diffuses from the surface inward. The diffusion depth is proportional to the square root of time, so twice the depth takes four times the time. Even holding 900 °C for 8 hours gives a depth of only about 1 mm, so a carburized piece has almost no margin to be ground down.
