## 5. Shaping, Refractories, and Metal Toxicity
### 5-1. Forging and Casting
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There are two main ways to give shape to metal: forging, which stretches metal by hammering, and casting, which melts and pours metal into molds to solidify. The choice between them is determined not by preference, but by the melting point of the metal and the type of forces exerted on the finished object.

| Criteria | Forging | Casting |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Required Temperature | Can be well below the melting point | Must be fully melted |
| Microstructure | Grain structure elongates, forming fibrous flow lines | Grains are coarse with no directional alignment |
| Strength and Toughness | High | Low and vulnerable to impact |
| Shape Complexity | Advantageous for simple shapes | Capable of complex and hollow geometries |
| Repeat Production | Requires identical labor every single time | Fast once molds are available |
| Suitable Items | Knives, axes, nails, hinges, agricultural tools | Cauldrons, bells, handles, fittings, ornaments |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This is why iron tools are almost without exception forged in situations where reaching temperatures high enough to melt iron is difficult. Conversely, copper-based alloys melt around 1,100°C, making casting a natural choice.

### 5-2. Molds Must Leave a Path for Gas to Escape
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Sand molds are made by mixing silica sand with clay and adding a small amount of water to bind them. The standard for proper mixing is that the mixture holds its shape when squeezed firmly in hand, and when split in half, the cut face remains clean without collapsing. Excessive clay creates a tight bond that prevents gases from escaping, while excessive water generates large volumes of steam upon contact with molten metal.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;When molten metal is poured in, air and moisture inside the mold expand instantly. If this gas has no escape route, it pushes against the molten metal, leaving pockmarks on the surface, or in severe cases, spews the metal back out through the pouring sprue. Therefore, vent holes are poked throughout the mold with a fine rod, and dedicated air vents are created alongside the pouring sprue. Furthermore, because metal shrinks in volume as it solidifies, a riser holding excess molten metal must be provided above the mold to feed shrinkage and prevent internal voids.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Investment casting (lost-wax casting) is the oldest technique for obtaining complex shapes. The desired form is sculpted in wax, coated with several layers of fine clay, allowed to dry, and then encased thickly with coarse clay and sand. Once completely dry, it is inverted and heated so the wax melts and drains out, leaving an exact hollow cavity behind. Subsequently, the mold is heated red-hot to thoroughly burn off remaining wax and moisture before pouring molten metal. Skipping this firing process after merely draining the wax will cause residual wax to release gas and ruin the casting.

### 5-3. Crucibles Break from Thermal Shock Rather Than Heat
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Forming a crucible from clay alone almost certainly leads to cracking. Clay changes volume during heating and cooling; when a temperature gradient exists between the inner and outer walls, differing expansion rates cause thermal stresses that form cracks. Formulating refractory materials is the practice of creating room to absorb this volumetric change.

| Material | Reason for Addition |
|:---|:---|
| Refractory Clay | Serves as the structural framework. White or gray clay low in iron content withstands high temperatures well |
| Sand or Grog | Grains that have already been fired and no longer shrink absorb expansion differences |
| Straw, Sawdust, or Dry Dung | Prevents cracking during drying and leaves insulating pores as it burns off during firing |
| Graphite | Distributes heat evenly to reduce thermal shock while maintaining a reducing atmosphere inside the furnace |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;A commonly used mix consists of one part refractory clay to one to one-and-a-half parts sand or grog; if graphite is available, clay and graphite are mixed in roughly equal volumes. Finely crushed charcoal powder can also partially substitute for graphite.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Formed crucibles must be dried thoroughly in the shade over days to weeks and then fired by slowly raising the temperature from a low level. Heating a crucible containing residual moisture causes internal steam that cannot escape to burst the walls. This is not merely a matter of ruined castings, but a hazard causing personal injury.

### 5-4. Metal Vapor Is Already Too Late When Visible
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Metals with low boiling points emit vapor while melting. This vapor oxidizes immediately in air into extremely fine particles that bypass filtration in the nose and throat, reaching deep into the lungs.

| Metal | Point Where Hazard Begins | Symptoms Offered |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Zinc | Boiled at 907°C | Metal fume fever. Chills, fever, and muscle aches develop after 4–12 hours, usually resolving within 24–48 hours |
| Lead | Emits vapor starting from around 500°C | Accumulative neurotoxin causing abdominal pain and cognitive decline; damage is irreversible |
| Cadmium | Boiled at 767°C | Causes pulmonary edema even in tiny quantities and can be fatal |
| Mercury | Boiled at 357°C | Directly damages the nervous system |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;While metal fume fever is often taken lightly because one recovers after a day or so of illness, cadmium vapor released under similar conditions yields entirely different consequences. Cadmium is present in vintage plated hardware, certain solders, and old bolts, and is emitted alongside zinc. When white smoke rises, one must step away from the area; standing upwind to keep smoke away from the face is the absolute minimum precaution.

> **Galvanized sheet metal, soldered components, and painted scrap metal must never be melted without verification. In a crisis, the easiest metal to obtain is often the most dangerous.**

### 5-5. Water, Eyes, and Clothing
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The most common cause of injury in casting is not the molten metal itself, but water. As 1 cc of water turns into steam, its volume expands roughly 1,700 times. When molten metal contacts a damp mold or ladle containing residual moisture, the trapped water under the metal vaporizes instantly, scattering molten metal in all directions. This is not splashing; it is an explosion.

> **All molds, crucibles, tongs, and ladles must be completely dry and heated red-hot before use to drive off residual moisture. Pouring molten metal over rain-soaked ground, wet sand, or concrete surfaces is equally hazardous for the same reason.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Eyes are damaged by light rather than heat. Red-hot metal emits strong infrared radiation, and molten metal heated to near-white adds blue light to it. Even without welding arcs, repeatedly staring into the furnace for hours damages the lens and accelerates cataract formation. If dark glass or welding goggles are unavailable, cultivating a habit of avoiding direct, prolonged gazes into the furnace is necessary.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Clothing must be made of natural fibers. Synthetic fibers melt and adhere to the skin upon contact with spattered molten metal instead of burning off, significantly deepening burns. Heavy cotton, wool, or leather aprons are fundamental, and sleeves and pant legs must overlap the outside of gloves and footwear. If spattered molten metal enters inside gloves or boot collars, it continues to burn through until removed.
