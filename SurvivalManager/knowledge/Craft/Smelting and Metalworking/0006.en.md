## 5. Forming, Refractories, and Metal Toxicity
### 5-1. Forging and Casting
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The ways to give metal shape are forging, which hammers it out, and casting, which melts it and pours it to set. Which to choose is decided by the metal's melting point and the kind of force the object will bear.

| Criterion | Forging | Casting |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Temperature needed | Can be well below the melting point | Must be melted |
| Structure | Grains are elongated into a fibrous structure | Coarse grains with no directionality |
| Strength and toughness | High | Low and weak against impact |
| Shape | Favors simple forms | Complex and hollow forms are also possible |
| Repeat production | The same labor every time | Fast once you have a mold |
| Suitable objects | Knives, axes, nails, hinges, farm tools | Pots, bells, handles, parts, ornaments |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Because it is hard to reach a temperature that melts iron, iron tools are almost without exception forged. Copper-based metals melt at around 1,100 °C, so casting is natural for them.

### 5-2. Molds
- Sand mold: mix silica sand with clay and pack it with a little water. It is suitable if the lump holds when squeezed tightly in the hand and the cut face does not collapse when it is split in half. More clay packs it firmly but the gas cannot escape, and more water produces a lot of steam on meeting the molten metal.
- Venting: when molten metal enters, the air and moisture in the mold expand in an instant. With no way out, they push the molten metal back and leave pits like pockmarks on the surface, and in bad cases spit molten metal back out of the pouring hole. Poke vent holes here and there with a thin skewer, and make a path for air to come out separate from the sprue where the molten metal enters.
- Riser: metal shrinks in volume as it solidifies. Make a riser, which holds extra molten metal, at the top of the mold to make up for the shrinkage so that the inside is not left hollow.

**Lost-Wax Casting**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This is the oldest way to obtain complex shapes.

1. Model the shape you want to make in wax.
2. Apply several coats of fine clay and let it dry.
3. Cover the outside thickly with coarse clay and sand.
4. When completely dry, stand it upside down and heat it to melt out the wax. An empty space of the same shape is left.
5. Heat the mold red-hot to burn off the remaining wax and moisture completely.
6. Pour in the molten metal.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;If you only drain the wax and skip the firing, the remaining wax gives off gas and ruins the casting.

### 5-3. Crucibles
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;A crucible shaped from clay alone almost always cracks. Clay changes in volume with heating and cooling, and when the inside and outside are at different temperatures, the degree of expansion differs, and the difference creates cracks. Mixing a refractory is making room to absorb this volume change.

| Material | Why it is added |
|:---|:---|
| Fireclay | The skeleton. White or gray clay low in iron withstands high temperatures well |
| Sand or grog | Grains that have already been fired once and no longer shrink absorb the difference in expansion |
| Straw, sawdust, dried dung | Prevent cracking during drying, and burn away during firing leaving insulating pores |
| Graphite | Spreads heat evenly to reduce thermal shock and keeps the furnace reducing |

- Mix: fireclay 1 : sand or grog 1-1.5 (by volume). If you have graphite, mix clay and graphite in similar volumes. Finely crushed charcoal powder can substitute for part of the graphite.
- Drying and firing: dry completely in the shade over a few days to a few weeks, then fire it, starting at a low temperature and raising it slowly. If a crucible with moisture left in it is heated suddenly, the steam inside cannot escape and the wall bursts, leading to an accident that injures people.

### 5-4. Metal Vapors
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Metals with low boiling points give off vapor while they melt. The vapor oxidizes immediately in the air into a fine powder, which is not filtered out by the nose and throat and goes deep into the lungs. By the time you can see it, it is already too late.

| Metal | Where danger begins | Symptoms |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Zinc | Boils at 907 °C | Metal fume fever. Chills, fever, and muscle pain after 4-12 hours. Usually recovers in 24-48 hours |
| Lead | Gives off vapor from about 500 °C | A neurotoxin that accumulates in the body. Leaves abdominal pain and cognitive decline, and does not recover |
| Cadmium | Boils at 767 °C | Causes pulmonary edema in very small amounts and can be fatal |
| Mercury | Boils at 357 °C | Directly damages the nervous system |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;It is easy to take metal fume fever lightly because you are ill for about a day and then recover, but the cadmium vapor that comes from the same spot produces a completely different result. Cadmium is found in old plated parts, some solders, and old bolts, and comes off together with zinc. If white smoke rises, leave the spot. At the very least, stand with your back to the wind so that the smoke does not come toward your face.

> **Caution:** Do not melt zinc-galvanized sheet iron, soldered parts, or painted scrap without checking. In a disaster, the metal easiest to get is the most dangerous metal.

### 5-5. Water, Eyes, Clothing
- Water: the most common cause of injury in casting is not the molten metal but water. When 1 cc of water becomes steam, its volume increases about 1,700 times. When molten metal touches a wet mold or a ladle with moisture left on it, the water trapped under the molten metal vaporizes in an instant and throws the molten metal in all directions. It is not a splash but an explosion.
- Eyes: red-hot metal gives off strong infrared radiation, and molten metal heated almost to white adds blue light. Looking into it repeatedly for hours damages the lens and brings forward cataracts, even without a welding arc. If you cannot get dark glass or welding glass, at the very least do not stare at the furnace head-on for long.
- Clothing: wear natural fibers. When splashed with molten metal, synthetic fibers melt instead of burning and stick to the skin, making the burn much deeper. Thick cotton or wool and a leather apron are the basics, and sleeves and trouser hems should be worn outside the gloves and shoes. If a splash of molten metal gets inside a glove or the top of a shoe, it keeps burning until you throw it off.

> **Caution:** Every mold, crucible, tong, and ladle must be completely dry, and before use heat them red-hot to drive off any remaining moisture. Pouring molten metal on wet ground after rain, wet sand, or a concrete floor is dangerous for the same reason.
