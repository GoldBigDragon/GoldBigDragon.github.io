## Introduction
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Wood is everywhere. However, there are things wood alone cannot do. It cannot melt iron, bake pottery, or maintain warmth through the night in a narrow indoor space. When burning raw wood, the flames are long and brilliant, but most of the heat within those flames is spent warming smoke and water vapor, escaping through the chimney. One of the earliest things humans learned as they built civilization was how to burn wood once more to convert it into a fuel superior to wood. That is charcoal.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Making charcoal is not an affair of pure profit. More than half of the energy contained in raw timber flies away as smoke and heat during the carbonization process. By weight, charcoal obtained from 100 kg of raw timber is barely 20 kg at best. Nevertheless, the reason humans have endured this loss for thousands of years is that charcoal yields three things raw wood cannot provide: temperatures exceeding 1,000°C with simple forced draft, clean combustion virtually free of smoke and moisture, and shelf life that remains unchanged for years as long as it stays dry. **Charcoal maximizes temperature, cleanliness, and shelf life at the cost of discarding total energy.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This document is not intended for memorizing the sequential steps of charcoal burning, but for understanding why wood begins to burn spontaneously at 280°C, why charcoal made from the same wood ignites easily when baked at 400°C but ignites with difficulty when baked at 1,000°C, and why one can discern what is happening inside the kiln simply by observing the color of the smoke. Once you understand the underlying principles, you can make sound judgments even if the wood at hand and local terrain differ from textbook descriptions. Premised on a situation where electricity, gas, and logistics are entirely cut off, this guide focuses on methods that function with nothing more than a single shovel and soil.
