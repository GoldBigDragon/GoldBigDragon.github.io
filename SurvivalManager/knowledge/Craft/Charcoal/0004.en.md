## 3. Principles of the Kiln and Air Control
### 3-1. Heat and Kilns
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Carbonization requires heat, and depending on where that heat is derived, all kilns in the world are broadly divided into two categories.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The first is the partial combustion type (direct firing). A portion of the wood loaded into the kiln is deliberately burned, and that heat is used to carbonize the remainder. Pit kilns, earth kilns, and traditional brick kilns all belong to this category. They offer the advantages of requiring no separate fuel and having a simple structure; however, because a certain percentage of the loaded wood must be sacrificed as fuel, the yield is lower, and because combustion and carbonization occur simultaneously in the same space, air control is tricky.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The second is the indirect heating type, namely the retort. Wood is placed inside a sealed container, and that container is heated from the outside. Because the wood never comes into contact with air, there is no loss from burning away, resulting in high yields, and the degree of carbonization can be directly controlled via temperature. In return, it requires metals or refractory materials to fashion the container, as well as separate fuel to heat the vessel.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The clever feature of a retort is that volatile gases escaping from the wood can be redirected back into the fire to be burned. Doing so significantly reduces smoke, and past the mid-stage of carbonization, heating is sustained by those gases alone, requiring virtually no external fuel. With just two oil drums, this principle can be implemented directly.

### 3-2. Comparison by Kiln Type
| Type | Key Construction Method | Approx. Yield | Time Required | Requirements |
|:---|:---|---:|:---|:---|
| Pit Kiln | Dig a pit, fill with wood, ignite, and cover with soil | 12–20% | 1–3 days | Only a shovel needed |
| Earth Kiln (Mound Kiln) | Stack wood above ground, seal with grass and soil | 15–25% | 3 days–2 weeks | Shovel, grass, clay soil |
| Drum Kiln | Fill an oil drum with wood, kindle fire underneath, control via air vents | 20–30% | 6–10 hours | 200L oil drum |
| Brick Kiln | Build a semi-permanent kiln with firebricks, installing vents and a chimney | 25–35% | 2–7 days | Bricks, clay, skill |
| Retort | Heat a sealed container externally and reuse exhaust gas for combustion | 30–40% | 4–8 hours | Two metal drums |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;A pit kiln is the choice when a single shovel is the only tool available. Soil serves simultaneously as the covering lid and as insulation, requiring no extra materials; however, soil trickles in between the wood to mix with the charcoal, and in areas with a high water table, water rises from the bottom to cause failure. An earth kiln can be built larger than a pit and allows slightly easier air control, but as the covering soil dries, it frequently cracks, allowing air to enter and turn charcoal into ash—requiring continuous patching of cracks throughout carbonization.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;A drum kiln is a practical compromise in a crisis situation. Reliable sealing keeps the failure rate low, results are produced within a single day, and the drum itself can be used repeatedly. Both methods are used: standing the drum upside down with several air holes punched near the bottom side, or laying the drum horizontally with a chimney fitted at one end.

### 3-3. Controlling Air Volume
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;If the entire endeavor of charcoal making were reduced to a single sentence, it is deciding how much air to let in. Too much oxygen burns even the carbonized charcoal away into ash; zero oxygen halts carbonization because the temperature cannot rise. The narrow band between these two failures is the working zone of charcoal making.

| Air State | What Occurs Inside Kiln | Result |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Excessive | Carbon undergoes complete combustion with oxygen into carbon dioxide | White ash; no charcoal remains |
| Optimal | Partial combustion generates heat to pyrolyze the rest | Charcoal |
| Deficient | Temperature drops below 280°C and decomposition halts | Charred outside, wood inside |
| Completely Sealed (Heating) | Pyrolysis proceeds solely via external heat | High-quality charcoal (retort method) |
| Completely Sealed (Cooling) | Combustion halts and charcoal is preserved | Carbonization completion and safety |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Actual manipulation relies on two openings. The bottom air vent determines the incoming oxygen volume, while the top chimney determines the outgoing smoke volume. Narrowing the chimney raises internal pressure inside the kiln, reducing air inflow and lowering temperature; narrowing the air vent suppresses combustion. During early carbonization, both vents are opened wide to drive out water; past the mid-stage, as the exothermic reaction becomes self-sustaining, they are gradually narrowed.

> **The moment carbonization is judged complete, all vents must be completely sealed with soil and wet ash, and allowed to cool for at least one full day before opening. Opening a kiln containing hot charcoal prematurely causes the entire mass to ignite at once due to sudden air inflow.**

### 3-4. Smoke Color and Kiln Temperature
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;When a thermometer cannot be inserted into the kiln, the sole measurement method is the smoke emerging from the chimney. Because emitted substances differ at each stage, smoke color and odor change accordingly.

| Smoke Appearance | Substance Actually Emitted | Stage Inside Kiln |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Thick white smoke, water droplets condensing at chimney tip | Water vapor | Drying stage; still around 100°C |
| Thin white smoke with sour odor | Acetic acid, methanol, water vapor | Torrefaction; 170–280°C |
| Thick yellow or brown smoke, acrid oily smell | Tar and heavy volatiles | Active carbonization; 280–400°C |
| Smoke thins and turns bluish | Carbon monoxide and light gases | Carbonization nearly complete |
| Smoke barely visible, only heat haze | Carbon dioxide and air | Carbonization complete; sealing point |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;When blue smoke appears, one must prepare to seal the kiln. Allowing the process to continue past this stage burns away already formed charcoal, reducing yield. When judgment is difficult, hold a dry wooden stick near the chimney exit; if tar is still escaping, oily residue condenses on the stick, whereas if carbonization is complete, no residue condenses.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;However, the stages of blue smoke and clear exhaust are also when invisible carbon monoxide is emitted at its highest concentration. Placing one's face directly in front of the chimney to check smoke is extremely dangerous; one must stand on the side with the wind blowing from behind to observe.

### 3-5. Reasons for Carbonization Failure
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Failures in charcoal making mostly trace back to a few set causes.

| Failure Mode | Direct Cause | Root Cause |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Almost no charcoal, only ash remains | Continuous air inflow | Cracks in cover soil, sealing failure, chimney over-opened |
| Charred outside, wood inside | Core temp. fails to reach 280°C | Thick timber, high moisture, insufficient carbonization time |
| Temp. fails to rise | Heat consumed boiling water | Using green wood, wet ground, rained upon |
| Chimney clogged and smoke backdrafts | Tar hardens and blocks flue | Heavy use of softwoods, low chimney temp. |
| Reignites upon opening after cooling | Air contacts unburned carbon | Imperfect sealing, insufficient cooling time |
| Charcoal crumbles into fine pieces | Overheating weakens structure | Localized overheating, uneven ventilation |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The root causes repeating in this table are two: water in the wood, and gaps in the kiln. Adequately drying the wood and securely sealing the kiln eliminate the vast majority of failures.
