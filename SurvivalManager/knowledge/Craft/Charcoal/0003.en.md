## 2. Temperature Determines the Properties of Charcoal
### 2-1. Black Charcoal and White Charcoal
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Traditional charcoal making in Korea and Japan divides charcoal into black charcoal (geomtan) and white charcoal (baektan). The difference is not the color but the temperature at which carbonization ends and the way it is cooled.

- Black charcoal: carbonization finishes at 400-600 °C, then the kiln mouth and chimney are sealed completely to cut off air and the charcoal cools slowly inside the kiln over several days.
- White charcoal: the temperature is raised to around 1,000 °C, then the glowing red charcoal is raked out of the kiln, covered with ash and soil powder, and cooled all at once.

| Category | Black charcoal | White charcoal |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Final carbonization temperature | 400-600 °C | 900-1,200 °C |
| Cooling method | Kiln sealed and cooled slowly | Taken out of the kiln and quenched with ash and soil |
| Fixed carbon | Roughly 75-85% | Roughly 90-95% |
| Ignition | Easy | Very difficult |
| Heat duration | Burns briefly and fast | Burns long and steadily |
| Hardness | Crumbles by hand | Hard, and rings with a metallic sound when struck |
| Appearance | Black and glossy | Gray-white ash on the surface |
| Suitable uses | Cooking, heating, everyday meals | Smithing, pottery, long-duration cooking |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In a disaster, learn black charcoal first. White charcoal is dangerous at every stage (the kiln structure, temperature control, pulling out the red-hot charcoal), takes skill, and has a low yield. Unless you are setting up a forge, black charcoal is enough.

---
### 2-2. Why White Charcoal Is Hard to Light
- Black charcoal: about 15% volatiles remain. These volatiles escape as gas at a low temperature and make a flame, and that flame heats the charcoal surface, so even a sheet of paper can pass the fire on to it.
- White charcoal: with no volatiles, the carbon has to react directly with oxygen, and for this reaction to sustain itself the surface temperature must be quite high. A carbon skeleton that has been through 1,000 °C is dense, with fewer pores and a small reactive surface area, and its high thermal conductivity spreads heat applied at one point through the whole lump. A match flame cannot raise even one spot on the surface to ignition temperature.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Light white charcoal by putting it on black charcoal or a wood fire that is already burning so that heat keeps being supplied, or by blowing hard to heat one spot on the surface in a concentrated way. Once lit, white charcoal does not go out easily.

---
### 2-3. Why White Charcoal Is Quenched
1. Stopping combustion: charcoal at 1,000 °C left in the air burns until it becomes ash. A powder of ash mixed with soil cuts off the air and removes heat quickly, ending the reaction. Do not pour water on it, because of the risk of steam explosion and because the charcoal absorbs water and becomes unusable.
2. Hardening the structure: the carbon skeleton quenched from high temperature shrinks and becomes dense, which gives white charcoal its characteristic hardness and metallic ring when struck. Slowly cooled charcoal does not become this hard even when fired at the same temperature.
3. White ash on the surface: the origin of the name white charcoal, it slows the rate at which charcoal absorbs moisture in storage and reduces the surface crumbling that leaves soot on your hands.

---
### 2-4. Wood Species
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The properties of charcoal are half decided by the carbonization temperature and half by the wood. The biggest variable is density. Dense wood has more carbon in the same volume, so charcoal of the same size burns longer.

| Species group | Typical species | Properties as charcoal |
|:---|:---|:---|
| High-density hardwoods | Oaks, chestnut, ash, Schmidt's birch (Betula schmidtii) | Hard, long-burning, few sparks. Suited to white charcoal and smithing |
| Medium-density hardwoods | Birch, maple, cherry | Middling, widely used for cooking |
| Low-density hardwoods | Willow, poplar, paulownia | Light, fast-burning, and fragile. For kindling and gunpowder |
| Conifers | Pine, Korean pine, fir | Rich in resin, so a lot of tar comes off and sparks fly. Usable in a hurry |
| Bamboo | Moso bamboo and similar | Low density but a dense pore structure that excels at adsorption and deodorizing |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Conifers do not make bad charcoal; the burning process is tricky. Because of the resin, a large amount of tar comes off during carbonization and clogs the chimney and outlet, blocking the draft. The finished charcoal also has large pores and residual resin, so it throws sparks and should not be used in a small room or near clothing. If you cannot afford to choose wood, you can make charcoal from conifers too.

### 2-5. Judging Charcoal Quality
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Evaluate finished charcoal by observation, with no tools.

| Observation | Good charcoal | Problem charcoal | Cause |
|:---|:---|:---|:---|
| Cross-section | Evenly black and glossy | Brown in the middle, or wood grain remains | Carbonization temperature too low, wood too thick |
| Sound | A clear ring when struck | A dull sound | Incomplete carbonization, moisture absorption |
| Weight | Very light for its size | Heavy | Holding water or uncarbonized |
| When broken | Breaks cleanly | Crushes and deforms | Insufficient carbonization |
| When lit | Almost no smoke | Yellow smoke and odor | Residual volatiles |
| What comes off on hands | A little soot | Stickiness | Residual tar |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The most common failure is charcoal on the outside and wood on the inside. Heat is conducted from the outside in, so thick logs take a long time to carbonize to the center. Split wood thicker than 10 cm in diameter before putting it in, and keep the thickness even within one kiln.
