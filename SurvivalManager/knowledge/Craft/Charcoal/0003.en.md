## 2. Temperature Determines the Properties of Charcoal
### 2-1. Black Charcoal and White Charcoal
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Traditional charcoal making in Korea and Japan broadly divides charcoal into two categories. Although the names derive from color, the actual difference lies not in color, but in the temperature at which carbonization ends and the cooling method used.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Black charcoal finishes carbonization between 400–600°C, after which the kiln inlet and chimney are completely sealed to cut off air, allowing it to cool slowly inside the kiln over several days. White charcoal has its temperature raised to around 1,000°C, after which the glowing red charcoal is raked out of the kiln with long rakes and extinguished instantly by covering it with a powder mixture of ash and soil.

| Category | Black Charcoal | White Charcoal |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Final Carbonization Temp. | 400–600°C | 900–1,200°C |
| Cooling Method | Sealing the kiln for slow cooling | Extracted from kiln for quenching with ash and soil |
| Fixed Carbon | Approx. 75–85% | Approx. 90–95% |
| Ignition | Easy | Very difficult |
| Heat Duration | Burns fast and short | Burns long and steady |
| Hardness | Crumbles by hand | Hard, emits a metallic ring when struck |
| Appearance | Black and lustrous | Surface coated with greyish-white ash |
| Suitable Uses | Cooking, heating, everyday meals | Blacksmithing, ceramics, long-duration cooking |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In a crisis situation, the first type to master is black charcoal. White charcoal involves hazards and demands high skill in kiln structure, temperature management, and the task of extracting glowing charcoal; its yield is also lower than that of black charcoal. Unless setting up a blacksmith forge, black charcoal is more than sufficient.

### 2-2. Why White Charcoal Is Hard to Ignite
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Ignition means the fuel's temperature rises enough to sustain combustion on its own. Black charcoal retains around 15% volatile matter, and these volatiles escape as gases at relatively low temperatures to produce flames. Those flames further heat the charcoal surface, allowing even a single sheet of paper to ignite it.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;White charcoal lacks those volatiles. Carbon must directly react with oxygen, and for this reaction to become self-sustaining, the surface temperature must be exceptionally high. Furthermore, the carbon skeleton that underwent 1,000°C heat is densely rearranged, reducing pores and reaction surface area; its high thermal conductivity quickly disperses heat applied at a single point across the entire mass. A heat source the size of a match flame cannot raise even a single point on the surface to ignition temperature.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Therefore, when lighting white charcoal, it must be placed atop already burning black charcoal or firewood to continuously supply heat, or one spot on the surface must be intensely heated using strong forced draft. For the same reason, once white charcoal is lit, it does not easily go out.

---
### 2-3. Three Reasons for Quenching White Charcoal
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Raking glowing red charcoal out of the kiln and covering it with ash is dangerous and laborious. Nevertheless, there are clear reasons for doing so.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;First is halting combustion. If charcoal at 1,000°C is left exposed to open air, it continues reacting with oxygen until it turns entirely to ash. The mixture of ash and soil cuts off air while rapidly absorbing heat, terminating the reaction. Water is not doused due to the hazard of steam explosions and because the charcoal would absorb moisture, rendering it useless.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Second is tissue hardening. The carbon skeleton, rapidly cooled from high temperatures, contracts and becomes dense, resulting in white charcoal's characteristic hardness and metallic ringing sound when struck. Charcoal cooled slowly does not become this hard even if baked at the same temperature.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Third is the surface layer of white ash, which is both the origin of its name and a beneficial side effect. This ash layer slightly slows the rate at which the charcoal absorbs moisture during storage and reduces crumbling that leaves soot on hands.

### 2-4. What Wood Species Determine
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Half of a charcoal's properties are determined by carbonization temperature, while the remaining half is determined by the raw timber. The variable with the greatest impact is wood density. Dense wood contains more carbon per unit volume, so a single piece of charcoal of equal size burns longer.

| Species Group | Representative Species | Characteristics as Charcoal |
|:---|:---|:---|
| High-Density Hardwoods | Oak species, Chestnut, Ash, Birch (Betula schmidtii) | Hard, burns long with minimal sparks. Ideal for white charcoal and blacksmithing |
| Medium-Density Hardwoods | Paper Birch, Maple, Cherry | Well-balanced, widely used for cooking |
| Low-Density Hardwoods | Willow, Poplar, Paulownia | Light, burns fast, crumbles easily. Used for tinder or gunpowder raw material |
| Softwoods (Conifers) | Pine, Korean Pine, Fir | High resin content produces excess tar and sparking. Usable in emergencies |
| Bamboo | Madake, Moso Bamboo | Low density, but dense pore structure makes it superior for adsorption and deodorization |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;While it is often advised to avoid softwoods, it is not that softwood charcoal is inherently useless, but rather that the process of burning softwoods is tricky. High resin content releases large amounts of tar during carbonization, which coagulates in chimneys and exhaust vents to block ventilation. Finished softwood charcoal also has large pores and residual resins that cause sparks when burning, making it unsuitable for use in confined indoor spaces or near clothing. If there is no luxury of choosing timber, charcoal can still be made from softwoods.

### 2-5. How to Determine Quality Charcoal
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There are observable criteria for evaluating finished charcoal without specialized tools.

| Observation | Good Charcoal | Problematic Charcoal | Cause |
|:---|:---|:---|:---|
| Cross-section | Uniformly black and lustrous | Brown center or visible wood grain | Insufficient carbonization temp., wood too thick |
| Sound | Emits a clear ring when tapped | Emits a dull thud | Incomplete carbonization, moisture absorption |
| Weight | Extremely light for its size | Heavy | Absorbed moisture or uncarbonized core |
| When Broken | Snaps cleanly | Crushes or deforms under pressure | Insufficient carbonization |
| When Lit | Virtually no smoke | Yellow smoke and odor produced | Residual volatiles remaining |
| Hand Residue | Leaves slight soot | Feels sticky | Residual tar remaining |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The most common failure is when the outside becomes charcoal while the core remains wood. Because heat transfers from the outside inward, thick logs take far longer to carbonize to the center. Splitting logs exceeding 10 cm in diameter and keeping log thickness as uniform as possible inside a single kiln is the most reliable way to reduce failures.
