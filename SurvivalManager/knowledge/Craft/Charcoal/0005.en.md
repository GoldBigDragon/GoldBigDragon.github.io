## 4. Carbon Monoxide, Byproducts, and Non-Fuel Uses
### 4-1. How Charcoal Kills
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Charcoal is the most useful fuel in a crisis situation, while simultaneously being the fuel that has quietly claimed the most lives. Accidents where an entire family dies while burning charcoal indoors after a typhoon or power outage recur in the exact same pattern all across the world.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The culprit is carbon monoxide. When carbon meets sufficient oxygen, it becomes carbon dioxide, but when oxygen is deficient, it becomes carbon monoxide with only one oxygen atom attached. Because a charcoal fire burns on its surface and rapidly consumes surrounding oxygen, carbon monoxide inevitably accumulates in enclosed spaces. Smaller rooms do not increase the absolute danger, but rather cause lethal concentrations to be reached much faster.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;What makes carbon monoxide uniquely dangerous is its affinity to hemoglobin in the blood, binding over 200 times more strongly than oxygen. Because captured hemoglobin cannot transport oxygen, no matter how deeply one breathes air into the lungs, oxygen never reaches body tissues. Worse yet, there is no warning signal whatsoever during this process. There is no feeling of suffocation, no coughing, and no smell. What comes first is a headache and lethargy; if one mistakes this for fatigue and lies down, consciousness is lost right then and there.

| Air Concentration | Exposure Time | Exhibited Symptoms |
|---:|:---|:---|
| 50 ppm | 8 hours | No distinct symptoms in healthy adults |
| 200 ppm | 2–3 hours | Mild headache, fatigue, nausea |
| 400 ppm | 1–2 hours | Severe headache. Life-threatening after 3 hours |
| 800 ppm | 45 minutes | Dizziness, vomiting, convulsions. Unconsciousness within 2 hours |
| 1,600 ppm | 20 minutes | Headache and dizziness. Death possible within 1 hour |
| 6,400 ppm | 1–2 minutes | Convulsions and respiratory arrest. Death within 15 minutes |

> **Even when no flames are visible and no smoke is produced, a charcoal fire continues to generate carbon monoxide. In fact, smoldering red without flames is the state that releases the highest amount of carbon monoxide. Burning charcoal inside tents, vehicles, rooms with closed windows, or basements cannot be justified for any reason, and opening a window slightly will not resolve the danger.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The word ventilation creates misunderstandings. Safe ventilation refers to a state where an active airflow actually passes through—meaning two opposing openings are created to let the wind blow through. Leaving a single window open by the width of a hand fails to create airflow. Furthermore, because carbon monoxide has almost the same density as air, it neither settles at the bottom nor floats to the top, but spreads uniformly throughout the room; lowering one's body provides zero protection.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;If carbon monoxide poisoning is suspected, the first priority is moving the person to open outdoor air. Detaching carbon monoxide from hemoglobin takes several hours even in fresh air, and everyone who was in the same space must evacuate together, even if exhibiting no symptoms. Rescuers entering the room becoming secondary victims is the classic pattern of these tragedies.

### 4-2. Pyroligneous Acid and Tar
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Smoke escaping during carbonization turns into liquid when cooled. Collecting this liquid and letting it settle for a few days separates it into three layers. A light oily layer floats at the top, a brown aqueous solution sits in the middle, and black, sticky tar settles at the bottom. The middle layer is pyroligneous acid (wood vinegar).

| Byproduct | Primary Components | Uses | Cautions |
|:---|:---|:---|:---|
| Raw Pyroligneous Acid | Water, acetic acid, methanol, phenols | Never use directly immediately after collection | Contains mixed carcinogenic substances of concern |
| Refined Pyroligneous Acid | Acetic acid-centered aqueous solution | Soil improvement, pest repellent, odor elimination | Must settle for 6+ months to separate top oil and bottom tar |
| Wood Tar | Phenols, creosote | Wood preservation, rope waterproofing, sealing boat joints, adhesive raw material | Strongly irritates skin and is flammable |
| Pitch | Boiled and hardened tar | Waterproof sealing, binder base for arrowhead adhesives | Prone to catching fire during heating |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In a crisis situation, the value of these byproducts lies in preservation and waterproofing. Wooden stakes coated with wood tar rot at a drastically slower rate when buried in soil, and saturating ropes or cloth with it repels water. Pitch, created by further reducing tar, serves as a base material for adhesives.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;However, consuming pyroligneous acid or applying it to open wounds is strongly discouraged. Unrefined pyroligneous acid contains polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons and other potential carcinogens, and there is no reliable method to separate them at home.

### 4-3. Uses of Charcoal Beyond Burning
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The value of charcoal does not end as a fuel. Because the cell structure of wood remains intact while contents are driven off during carbonization, dense microscopic channels formed by original xylem vessels and cell walls remain inside the charcoal. This structure grants charcoal an immense surface area.

| Purpose | Operating Principle | Limitations |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Odor Removal | Odor molecules physically adhere to pore surfaces | Effect ceases once pores are saturated. Partially restored by sun drying |
| Dehumidification | Pores absorb water vapor and release it when ambient air turns dry | Capacity is small; insufficient for spacious areas |
| Improving Water Taste and Odor | Adsorbs dissolved organic matter and chlorine | Virtually ineffective against bacteria and viruses |
| Soil Improvement | Pores hold water and nutrients while providing micro-organism habitats | Adding directly steals nutrients initially. Mix with compost to age first |
| Gunpowder Ingredient | Supplies carbon to potassium nitrate, the oxygen source during combustion | Low-density wood carbonized at low temperatures is suitable |
| Writing and Marking | Carbon particles remain on surfaces | Easily washed away by water |
| Wound Dressing Auxiliary | Reduces exudate through adsorption | No antiseptic effect. Harmful if used on infected wounds |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;A clear distinction must be made between ordinary charcoal and activated carbon. Activated carbon is charcoal re-processed under 800–1,000°C steam or chemical treatment to artificially expand pores, achieving surface areas ranging from hundreds to 1,500 m² per gram. The surface area of ordinary charcoal is merely a small fraction of that number. Most published figures regarding water purification and deodorization are based on activated carbon and cannot be applied directly to self-burned charcoal.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Another critical fact is that adsorption is not decomposition. Charcoal merely captures molecules without eliminating them. Once pores are full, adsorption ceases, and charcoal used in water for long periods becomes a breeding ground for bacteria feeding on trapped organic matter. Charcoal layers used for water purification must be replaced periodically.

### 4-4. How to Store Charcoal
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Charcoal neither rots nor decays, but it is vulnerable to two things: moisture and physical impact.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Due to its porous structure, charcoal continuously absorbs moisture from the air. Charcoal left exposed during rainy seasons can see water account for over 10% of its total weight, and attempting to ignite it in this state results in poor ignition, excessive smoke, and diminished heating power. Wet charcoal need not be discarded; drying it for a few days restores its original performance. Storing it elevated off the ground inside sealed containers or thick sacks is the standard rule.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Vulnerability to impact is a matter of handling. Bumping against each other during transport crumbles charcoal, and powdered charcoal has reduced utility as fuel due to lack of airflow. However, powder should not be thrown away, as it can be used directly as an adhesive filler, soil amendment, or gunpowder ingredient.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Finally, freshly burned charcoal may retain internal heat even when appearing completely cool on the outside. In fact, a significant portion of fires in charcoal making occur when charcoal believed to be cool is bagged and brought into a shed, only to ignite hours later. Charcoal extracted from a kiln must be spread out on an outdoor, non-combustible floor for at least a full day and verified completely cold to the touch before being placed into storage.
