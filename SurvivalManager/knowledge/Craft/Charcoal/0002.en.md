## 1. The Principle of Carbonization and the Gains and Losses of Charcoal
### 1-1. Green Wood and Moisture
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The dry-basis moisture content (relative to oven-dry weight) of freshly cut wood is 40-100% and can be higher depending on species and season. It falls to 12-18% only after the wood has been stacked and dried for a year in a place with airflow, reaching the air-dry state.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Water does not burn and still takes heat away twice. First, the sensible heat of warming it from room temperature to 100 °C; second, the latent heat of evaporation as it turns to steam at 100 °C. Turning 1 kg of water into steam from room temperature takes about 2.6 MJ, and this heat escapes up the chimney.

| Fuel state | Moisture content (wet basis) | Effective heating value |
|:---|---:|---:|
| Freshly cut green wood | 45-55% | About 6-9 MJ/kg |
| Semi-dry firewood | 25-35% | About 11-13 MJ/kg |
| Air-dry firewood | 12-18% | About 15-16 MJ/kg |
| Oven-dry wood | 0% | About 19-20 MJ/kg |
| Charcoal | 3-7% | About 29-33 MJ/kg |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The same wood differs in heating value by more than double just by drying. Dry the wood before you make charcoal. If you burn wet wood, all the heat in the kiln goes into boiling the water and never reaches the carbonization temperature.

---
### 1-2. Gains and Losses of Carbonization
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Carbonization is the process of heating wood with almost no air to drive off water and volatile components, leaving only the carbon skeleton. The methane, hydrogen, carbon monoxide, methanol, acetic acid, and tar that escape were themselves fuel components. In an earth kiln the yield by weight is 15-25%, and only about half the heat of the wood remains in the charcoal.

| What you lose | What you gain |
|:---|:---|
| 75-85% of the wood's weight | Firepower that exceeds 1,000 °C with just blowing air |
| About half the wood's heat | Combustion with almost no smoke or steam |
| From a day to several weeks of time | Light weight for its volume, easy to carry |
| More fragile than wood | Keeps for years unchanged if moisture is avoided |
| Labor and watching during the burn | Chemically stable, with no insects or rot |

- Worth it: heating iron, firing pottery, cooking where smoke cannot be made, carrying fuel over a long distance
- A waste: warming up or boiling water over a big campfire in an open field

---
### 1-3. Why Charcoal Burns So Hot
- Firewood: the long yellow flame is volatile gas from the wood meeting air away from the surface and burning. The hottest points are scattered through the flame, and the heat escapes on the rising air current.
- Charcoal: the volatiles are already gone, so there is almost no flame. Oxygen touches the charcoal surface directly and the carbon oxidizes right there, so the place where heat is made and the place that needs to be hot coincide, and the glowing lumps give off radiant heat.
- Blowing: more oxygen reaches the surface and the reaction rate rises in step. This is why working a bellows visibly raises the temperature of a charcoal fire.
- Cleanliness: charcoal makes almost no steam. Steam and carbon dioxide re-oxidize hot iron, and impurities such as sulfur and phosphorus from wood make iron brittle. The reason charcoal is an irreplaceable fuel in metalworking is this cleanliness rather than its firepower.

---
### 1-4. Temperature Stages
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;As temperature rises, wood breaks down component by component, in order. To judge the state inside a kiln you need to know this order.

| Temperature range | What happens | Direction of heat |
|:---|:---|:---|
| 100 °C | Free water between the cells evaporates. The temperature hardly rises until the moisture content nears zero | Endothermic |
| 100-170 °C | Water bound to the cell walls and low-boiling components leave | Endothermic |
| 170-280 °C | Hemicellulose breaks down first, giving off acetic acid and methanol. The torrefaction stage, when the wood turns brown | Endothermic |
| Around 280 °C | The turning point at which decomposition starts to give off heat by itself. The reaction continues without external heating | Switches to exothermic |
| 280-400 °C | Cellulose and lignin break down in earnest, releasing large amounts of tar and wood vinegar | Strongly exothermic |
| 400-600 °C | Residual volatiles leave and the fixed carbon fraction rises | Weakly exothermic |
| Above 600 °C | The carbon skeleton rearranges densely, and hardness and electrical conductivity rise | External heating needed |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;280 °C is the turning point. Beyond it the wood keeps decomposing on its own heat, so you do not have to keep feeding the fire. This is why a traditional kiln is fired only at the start and then closed. If the inside of the kiln does not cross this line, the wood only scorches and is yellow inside when split. It is the most common form of carbonization failure.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Once the exothermic stage is reached, control is hard. If you let in a lot of air, the reaction runs away and you get ash, not charcoal. The skill of carbonization is keeping the exothermic reaction alive only as much as needed.

### 1-5. Carbonization Temperature and Charcoal Composition
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The figures below are laboratory measurements. In a real kiln, part of the material burns completely, so the yield is lower.

| Carbonization temperature | Fixed carbon | Volatiles | Yield vs. oven-dry wood |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 300 °C | 68% | 31% | 42% |
| 500 °C | 86% | 13% | 33% |
| 700 °C | 92% | 7% | 30% |

- The higher the temperature, the better the quality of the charcoal and the smaller the amount.
- Fixed carbon in fuel charcoal is roughly 75% or more, which requires going up to around 500 °C.
- Charcoal stopped at 300 °C may look high-yielding, but a third of it is still volatiles, so it smokes and does not burn long.
- The yield of an earth kiln is 15-25% by oven-dry wood and around 10% by the weight of green wood. When calculating the amount of wood, check which basis the weight refers to.
