## 1. What Charcoal Gives and What It Takes Away
### 1-1. Green Wood
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;A substantial portion of freshly felled wood's weight consists of water. Expressed as dry-basis moisture content based on oven-dry wood weight, green wood ranges from 40–100%, rising even higher depending on tree species and season. Only after being stacked in a well-ventilated area to dry for a year to reach an air-dry state does the moisture content drop to the 12–18% range.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The way this water ruins wood as a fuel is not simple dilution. Water steals heat twice without burning itself. The first is sensible heat used to warm it from ambient temperature to 100°C, and the second is latent heat of vaporization used to convert it into water vapor at 100°C. Converting just 1 kg of water from ambient temperature into steam requires roughly 2.6 MJ, and this heat escapes through the chimney without ever returning.

| Fuel State | Moisture Content (Wet Basis) | Approximate Effective Calorific Value |
|:---|---:|---:|
| Freshly Felled Green Wood | 45–55% | Approx. 6–9 MJ/kg |
| Semi-Dried Firewood | 25–35% | Approx. 11–13 MJ/kg |
| Air-Dried Firewood | 12–18% | Approx. 15–16 MJ/kg |
| Oven-Dry Wood | 0% | Approx. 19–20 MJ/kg |
| Charcoal | 3–7% | Approx. 29–33 MJ/kg |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;What should be read from the table is not merely a single number for charcoal, but the fact that the calorific value of the same wood doubles simply by drying. In a crisis situation, what must be done before burning charcoal is drying the wood; attempting to burn charcoal with wet wood causes all heat inside the kiln to be consumed boiling water, failing to reach carbonization temperature.

---
### 1-2. Charcoal
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Carbonization is the process of heating wood in an environment nearly devoid of air to drive off water and volatile components, leaving only a carbon skeleton. What is driven off at this time is not useless residue, but components that were fuels in their own right. Methane, hydrogen, carbon monoxide, methanol, acetic acid, and tar escape alongside smoke.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;As a result, yield by weight stays around 15–25% in an earthen kiln. Converted to energy, only about half of the heat value originally held by the raw timber remains within the charcoal. This is the true cost of making charcoal.

| What Is Lost | What Is Gained |
|:---|:---|
| 75–85% of raw wood weight | Heating power exceeding 1,000°C with simple forced draft |
| Roughly half of raw wood heat value | Combustion virtually free of smoke and water vapor |
| Time ranging from a day to several weeks | Easy transportation due to light weight relative to volume |
| Greater brittleness compared to raw wood | Shelf life unchanged for years if kept away from moisture |
| Labor and monitoring during burning | Chemical stability with zero insects and zero decay |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Where this trade-off is profitable and where it is a loss are clearly divided. If warming oneself or boiling water with a large bonfire in a spacious outdoor setting, burning charcoal is a clear waste. Conversely, if forging iron, firing pottery, cooking where smoke cannot be produced, or carrying fuel over long distances on one's back, nothing short of charcoal will suffice.

### 1-3. How Charcoal Reaches High Temperatures
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The long yellow flame seen when firewood burns is not the wood itself burning, but volatile gases escaping from the wood meeting air and burning a short distance away from the wood surface. Thus, the hottest point of a firewood fire is not on the wood surface, but scattered somewhere within the flames, and that heat escapes upward with updrafts.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Because volatile matter has already escaped from charcoal, it produces virtually no flames. Instead, oxygen directly touches the surface of the charcoal, oxidising carbon right on the spot. Because the reaction occurs on the surface, the place where heat is generated coincides with the place that needs to be hot, and the lump of charcoal itself glows red to emit radiant heat. Forced air increases the oxygen reaching the surface, directly accelerating reaction speed—which is why charcoal fire temperatures rise noticeably with just a few pumps of a bellows.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Another crucial point is that charcoal produces almost no water vapor when burning. Water vapor and carbon dioxide re-oxidize heated iron at high temperatures, while impurities such as sulfur and phosphorus released by wood embrittle iron. The reason charcoal became an irreplaceable fuel in metallurgy lies in this cleanliness even more than its thermal power.

### 1-4. Temperature Stages of Wood Becoming Charcoal
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Wood does not suddenly transform into charcoal at a specific temperature; rather, it decomposes component by component in sequence as temperature rises. To judge what is occurring inside the kiln, one must understand this sequence.

| Temperature Range | What Occurs | Heat Direction |
|:---|:---|:---|
| 100°C | Free water between cells evaporates. Temperature barely rises until moisture content approaches zero | Endothermic |
| 100–170°C | Moisture bound to cell walls and low-boiling components escape | Endothermic |
| 170–280°C | Hemicellulose decomposes first, releasing acetic acid and methanol. Torrefaction zone where color turns brown | Endothermic |
| Around 280°C | Turning point where decomposition begins generating its own heat. Reaction continues without external heating | Transitions to Exothermic |
| 280–400°C | Cellulose and lignin decompose in earnest, releasing large amounts of tar and pyroligneous liquor | Strongly Exothermic |
| 400–600°C | Remaining volatile matter escapes and fixed carbon ratio increases | Weakly Exothermic |
| Above 600°C | Carbon skeleton rearranges densely, increasing hardness and electrical conductivity | Requires External Heating |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The figure 280°C is the most critical. Once past this temperature, wood continues to decompose using heat it generates itself, so external stoking is no longer required. This property is why traditional kilns are stoked only at the beginning and have their inlets sealed thereafter. Conversely, if the temperature inside the kiln fails to cross this line, the wood remains merely charred black on the outside while remaining yellow inside when split. This is the most common form of carbonization failure.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Furthermore, once entering the exothermic range, control becomes difficult. Introducing excessive air at this stage causes the reaction to run wild, turning the wood into ash rather than charcoal. The art of carbonization is ultimately the skill of maintaining this exothermic reaction only as much as necessary.

### 1-5. Carbonization Temperature and Charcoal Composition
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Even with the same wood, entirely different substances emerge depending on the temperature reached. Below are values measured under laboratory conditions; in an actual kiln, yield is lower because a portion is completely combusted.

| Carbonization Temp. | Fixed Carbon | Volatiles | Yield Relative to Oven-Dry Wood |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 300°C | 68% | 31% | 42% |
| 500°C | 86% | 13% | 33% |
| 700°C | 92% | 7% | 30% |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;What the table demonstrates is a trade-off: as temperature rises, charcoal quality improves, but quantity decreases. Usable fuel charcoal requires fixed carbon of roughly 75% or higher, which necessitates raising the temperature to around 500°C. Charcoal stopped at 300°C may appear to offer high yield, but with a third of its composition remaining as volatiles, it produces smoke when burned and does not sustain fire long.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In an actual earthen kiln, part of the wood inside is burned to carbonize the remainder with that heat, placing dry-basis yield roughly between 15–25%. Based on green wood weight, it drops to around 10%. When calculating raw timber requirements, one must always verify which weight basis is being referenced.
