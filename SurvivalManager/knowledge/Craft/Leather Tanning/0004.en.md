## 3. Tanning Methods
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The properties of the finished leather vary depending on what is introduced into the prepared hide. There are three main methods feasible using materials available in nature, each differing in the substance packed between fibers and the nature of the bond that substance forms with collagen.

---
### 3-1. Brain Tanning
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;More than half the weight of an animal's brain consists of lipids, a significant portion of which are phospholipids, including lecithin. A phospholipid molecule has an amphiphilic structure with one water-loving (hydrophilic) end and one oil-loving (lipophilic) end; when dissolved in water, it acts as an emulsifier that holds oil in microscopic droplets.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The reason this property is utilized in tanning is that water acts as a carrier. Pure oil cannot penetrate wet hide. However, an emulsion made by mixing brain matter into water penetrates deep into every nook between fibers alongside water; as the hide dries and water evaporates, only phospholipids and fats remain on fiber surfaces, forming a thin film. This film prevents fibers from directly contacting one another, averting adhesion.

| Item | Details |
| --- | --- |
| Solution Temperature | 40–50°C. If too hot, collagen is damaged; if cold, fat hardens and fails to penetrate |
| Number of Repetitions | Soaking, wringing, and massaging at least 2–3 times. A single pass will not reach the center |
| Nature of Bonding | Physical coating rather than chemical bonding, so it can be washed out |
| Essential Subsequent Steps | Staking and smoking. Skipping either causes the leather to harden again |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;A widely known rule of thumb states that every animal possesses enough brain matter to tan its own hide. In practice, because larger animals have both larger hides and larger brains, this generally holds true, but it is not a strictly precise proportion. Some species, like humans and pigs, have large brains relative to body weight, while others, like cattle, have hides so thick that a single brain is insufficient.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;When brain matter is unavailable, alternative materials can be substituted. The key is supplying phospholipids or emulsifiable fats.

| Alternative Material | Active Component | Remarks |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Egg Yolk | Lecithin and fat | Composition is closest to brain matter, making it an excellent substitute |
| Liver | Phospholipids and fat | Ground and mixed into water for use |
| Bone Marrow | Primarily fat | Emulsifying power is weak, so use alongside soap or egg yolk |
| Animal Fat + Soap | Fat and surfactant | Soap takes on the role of the emulsifier |
| Vegetable Oil + Egg | Fat and lecithin | Avoid oils prone to quick rancidity |

---
### 3-2. Vegetable Tannin Tanning
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Tannins are polyphenolic compounds contained in tree bark, leaves, and fruits. Because a single tannin molecule carries multiple hydroxyl groups, it forms hydrogen bonds simultaneously at multiple sites along peptide bonding regions on collagen surfaces. When a single molecule bridges two adjacent collagen chains, it acts as a cross-link, and when countless cross-links form, the collagen structure itself becomes permanently fixed.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;If brain tanning is a physical treatment coating fiber surfaces with a film of oil, tannin tanning chemically bonds with collagen to alter the fundamental properties of the protein itself. Thus, leather tanned with tannins does not wash out its tanning agents even when soaked in water, and it becomes extremely difficult for bacteria to decompose the collagen. It is also because of these bonds that the shrinkage temperature rises to 75–85°C.

| Material | Tannin Content | Characteristics |
| --- | :---: | --- |
| Oak Bark | 8–16% | Most widely used, yielding a beautifully smooth light brown color |
| Chestnut Bark and Wood | 10–13% | Similar to oak, with high extraction yield |
| Sumac and Anacardiaceae Leaves | 25–30% | High content yielding bright colors, but avoid if sensitive to urushi/poison ivy |
| Pine and Spruce Bark | 5–18% | Easy to obtain, but yields a reddish hue mixed with resin |
| Alder Bark | Highly variable | Easily extracted from young trees |
| Willow Bark | Moderate | Readily gathered along waterways |
| Acorn Cups (Acorn Caps) | 25–35% | Can be gathered in large quantities in autumn |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Tannin solutions must strictly start weak and gradually increase in concentration. If immersed in a dense solution from the beginning, tannins instantly bond with collagen on the surface, tightening that outer layer so that tannins can no longer penetrate into the interior. This results in a state where only the surface is tanned while the core remains raw hide; upon drying, the center layer hardens and splits. Conversely, starting weak allows tannins to penetrate deeply and slowly, after which raising the concentration completes the cross-linking.

> **Rushing vegetable tanning leaves the surface tanned and the interior raw. The pace at which concentration is raised is the quality itself.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The time required is proportional to hide thickness. Thin rabbit or goat skins take a few weeks, deer hides take one to two months, and thick cowhides take six months to a year. Because tannins in the solution are nearly exhausted within a single day when the hide is first introduced, refresh the solution frequently at early stages and lengthen intervals later. Progress is judged by slicing the hide to check whether the cross-sectional color has penetrated uniformly to the center.

---
### 3-3. Alum Tanning
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Alum is a double salt of aluminum and potassium that releases aluminum ions when dissolved in water. These ions form coordination bonds with the carboxyl groups of collagen, fixing the fibers. Finishing in just a few days and producing white, soft results, it was traditionally used for gloves and thin leathers.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;However, there is a decisive limitation. The bond between aluminum and collagen is reversible, so when re-exposed to water, aluminum ions detach and wash away. Getting rained on once undoes the tanning effect, and the leather hardens again upon drying. Thus, strictly speaking, it is sometimes classified into a separate category rather than true tanning (tawing). Thoroughly stuffing alum-tanned leather with oil to block water penetration can partially compensate for this flaw.

| Method | Required Duration | Materials | Finished Leather Properties | Water Resistance |
| --- | :---: | --- | --- | :---: |
| Brain Tanning | A few days | Brains, egg yolks, liver, oils | Extremely soft, supple, and stretchy | Secured upon smoking |
| Vegetable Tannin | A few weeks to one year | Tree bark, acorn cups | Hard, shape-retaining, and thick | Good |
| Alum | A few days | Alum | White, soft, and thin | Extremely poor |
| Smoking Only | One to two days | Smoke | Meaningful only as a finishing touch for other methods | Contributes auxiliary resistance |
| Untanned (Rawhide) | Drying time only | None | Extremely hard and stiff | Softens upon wetting |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;These three methods serve different purposes. Brain tanning is best suited for parts touching the skin or folded frequently, such as clothing and shoe linings. Tannin tanning is right for components that must retain shape, like shoe soles, belts, and sheaths. Alum is used restrictively only for thin leathers that will not contact water. In practice, multiple techniques are often combined—such as tanning a hide with tannins and then stuffing it with oil—to obtain both the stability of tannins and the flexibility of oils.
