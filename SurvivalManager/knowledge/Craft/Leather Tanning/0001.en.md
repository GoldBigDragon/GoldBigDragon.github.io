## Introduction
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In a situation where logistics are cut off, one of the first items to run short is durable cloth. Clothing, shoes, cords, pouches, coverings for roofs, and water containers all fall into this category. Synthetic fibers cannot be manufactured and textiles require a long supply chain of cultivation, spinning, and weaving; however, raw hide comes directly from a single hunted animal. The problem is that if left as it is, skinned hide rots within a few days, or if dried out, hardens like a wooden board and becomes unusable.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Tanning is the process of preventing both of these outcomes simultaneously. Leather hardens because collagen fibers inside the dermis stick together as they lose water, and tanning is the work of filling the spaces between these fibers to prevent them from adhering to one another. Whether using brain matter, plant tannins from tree bark, or oils, every method achieves this single objective using different materials. Once you grasp this perspective, why each process follows its specific sequence and why that specific amount of time is required all naturally follow.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Starting from the principles of how raw hide rots and hardens, this document summarizes why preparatory processes dictate the final results, what chemistry drives brain tanning, vegetable tannin tanning, and alum tanning respectively—what each delivers and what each sacrifices—and why the final two steps, staking and smoking, cannot be skipped. Tanning is a craft where the causes of failure are clear, and almost all of those causes stem from skipping steps without understanding the underlying principles.
