## 2. From Skinning to Neutralization: Why Preparation Processes Determine the Outcome
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Most people who fail at tanning do not fail at the tanning stage itself. They fail because they glossed over somewhere in the preceding preparation processes. The purpose of preparation is to leave only the collagen fibers of the dermis while opening passages between them for tanning agents to penetrate.

---
### 2-1. Skinning
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;A hide begins to rot the moment it is removed from the body. Once separated from the organism, blood circulation ceases and tissue dies; bacteria transferred during slaughter already inhabit the surface; and the dermis remains warm and moist. Under summer temperatures, a few hours is the limit, and even on cool days, a single day is the absolute maximum.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The crucial rule in skinning is to minimize knife cuts. The more a knife is used, the more the dermis gets nicked, and those nicks become holes or thin spots in the finished leather. There is a relatively easy cleavage plane between the subcutaneous tissue and the dermis, allowing a significant portion to be worked off using fists or a blunt stick without a knife. This technique leaves virtually no nicks, making it particularly advantageous for large animals.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;When immediate processing is impossible, you must buy time by creating conditions under which bacteria cannot grow. Two primary methods exist, both sharing the same underlying principle: depriving the hide of water.

| Method | Principle | Conditions and Limitations |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Salting | Salt draws moisture out of tissue via osmotic pressure, denying bacteria usable water | Rub salt thoroughly on the flesh side equal to 30–50% of hide weight. Insufficient salt causes rotting from the center outward |
| Drying | Evaporates water to lower water activity | Spread taut in the shade with good ventilation to dry. Direct sunlight hardens only the surface, causing the interior to rot |
| Freezing | Lowers temperature to halt bacteria and enzymatic activity | Feasible only in winter; repeated freezing and thawing damages collagen fibers |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Dried hide must later be revived by soaking in water, a step called rehydration (soaking). The longer a hide has been dried, the more days it takes for water to penetrate; if the water becomes warm during this time, putrefaction begins in the vat. Water must be changed at least once a day during rehydration.

---
### 2-2. Unhairing
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Hair cannot be removed simply by pulling. Hair roots are embedded deep within the dermis, so pulling tears the dermis along with them. Thus, unhairing is not a matter of mechanical force, but a matter of chemistry: dissolving the proteins that hold the hair roots in place.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Keratin, which forms hair and the epidermis, is rich in an amino acid called cystine, and disulfide bonds formed between cystines bind keratin tightly together. Strong alkalis break these disulfide bonds. Under alkaline conditions, hydroxide ions attack the bonds, cleaving disulfide bonds into thiol groups and collapsing the keratin structure, causing hair to slip out of follicles on its own.

| Item | Details |
| --- | --- |
| Target pH | pH 11–13. Hair will not slip if lower than this range |
| Duration | 3 to 10 days depending on temperature. Cold weather requires longer |
| How to Judge Completion | Complete when hair slips off in handfuls upon rubbing by hand |
| Excessive Exposure | Alkali hydrolyzes collagen itself, making the hide slippery and prone to tearing when pulled |
| Side Effect | Fiber bundles swell and separate, opening passages for tanning agents to penetrate |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This side effect is not secondary, but actually the second primary objective. As the dermis swells in alkali, gaps between fiber bundles widen, and non-collagenous substances binding the fibers together dissolve away. A hide that has not undergone this process leaves its core raw and unprocessed no matter how long it sits in tanning solutions.

> **Unhairing serves two distinct purposes: releasing hair roots and opening passages between fibers—and the second purpose dictates final quality even more strongly.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;A non-alkaline alternative is natural fermentation (sweating). Immersing the hide in lukewarm water for a few days allows bacteria to decompose the epidermis first, releasing hair. While advantageous in requiring no added chemicals, judging when to stop is extremely difficult; a slight delay causes bacteria to break down the dermis itself, ruining the hide. The odor is also unbearable. If alkali is available, it is far better to use it.

---
### 2-3. Fleshing
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Fleshing is the process of scraping away fat, subcutaneous tissue, and thin membranes clinging to the flesh side of the hide. It is the most physically demanding and most frequently rushed step in preparation, as well as the most common root cause of tanning failure.

| What Happens If Left Behind | Reason |
| --- | --- |
| Tanning agents cannot penetrate | Fat repels water, preventing water-soluble tanning agents from passing through |
| Those specific spots harden stiff | Fibers untouched by tanning agents fuse together as they dry |
| Turns rancid over time | Remaining fat oxidizes, emitting foul odors and discoloring the leather |
| Mold grows | Subcutaneous tissue is rich in nutrients, serving as food for mold |
| Drying becomes uneven | Areas with differing thicknesses dry at different rates, causing warping |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Fleshing is performed by draping the hide over a rounded log or beam, securing it with body weight, and pushing downward with a dull-edged tool. Because a sharp blade cuts the dermis, a blunt bone or dull metal tool is far more suitable than a sharp knife. A rounded beam is used to narrow the contact area and concentrate scraping pressure; on a flat board, force disperses broadly, failing to strip away tissue.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In addition to the flesh side, the epidermal layer on the hair side must also be removed. Following unhairing, a thin, slick membrane remains on the surface, which blocks water and prevents agent penetration. If this membrane remains through tanning, that side stays shiny and stiff.

---
### 2-4. Neutralization
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;A hide that has completed unhairing is thoroughly saturated with alkali. In this state, no tanning method will function properly. Tannins oxidize in alkaline environments, yielding color without bonding, while brain-tanning emulsions fail to form stable films under alkaline conditions. Residual alkali also continues to hydrolyze collagen over time.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Deliming and neutralization proceed in two steps. First, wash the hide through multiple water changes to remove the vast majority of alkali, and then neutralize the remainder using a mild acid. Weak acids such as vinegar, sour fruit juice, or fermented grain water are sufficient; using strong acids will cause the acid itself to hydrolyze collagen.

| Judgment | Method |
| --- | --- |
| Checking for Residual Alkali | If it feels slick like soap to the touch, alkali remains |
| Checking Swelling Reduction | Swollen, taut hide turns limp and thins in thickness |
| Washing Frequency | Change water once a day in cold weather, 2–3 times a day in warm weather |
| Signal of Completion | Hide drapes limply in water and yields clear water when squeezed by hand |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Once neutralization is complete, squeeze out a substantial amount of water. If spaces between fibers are completely saturated with water, there is no room for tanning agents to enter. Wring or press the hide over a bar until it stops dripping before proceeding to the tanning stage.
