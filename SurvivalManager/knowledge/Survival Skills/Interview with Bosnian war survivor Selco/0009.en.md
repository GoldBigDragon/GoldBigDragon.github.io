## 8-1. Movies and Reality
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Disaster situations were nothing like survival movies. It was horrible. We did what we had to do to survive. No one won. We just survived and had many bad dreams.   

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There was no electricity, no running water, and the sewage system didn’t work for months. In broken homes, corpses were left unattended, and waste overflowed. It was even hard to smell anything good. There were no heroic movie moments; it was a dirty, foul-smelling reality.   

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Most of the injuries were from gunshots, and treatment was nearly impossible due to the lack of specialists and equipment. Even if the injured found a doctor, the survival rate was only about 30%. Again, it wasn’t like the movies, and most people eventually died.   

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The first thing that comes to my mind from that time is not gunfights or safety issues, but the constant cold and foul-smelling environment. I am now 185 cm tall and weigh 95 kg, but back then I only weighed about 65 kg. By the end of the war, I had lost 15 teeth and suffered from a skin disease for three years.  

---

## 8-2. Humanitarian Aid
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The United States dropped not only MREs (Meals Ready to Eat) but also various other supplies. A large box containing 300-500 MREs would drop from a parachute, and at a certain altitude, the box would break apart, with MREs falling like rain. The second type consisted of large pallets with parachutes, most of which contained flour. There was a lot of flour. What I remember most are small metal cans with cookies or biscuits inside.   

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;I never took anything other than food. I heard stories about boxes containing other supplies, but I never saw them myself. Those stories were mostly rumors. Anyway, they didn’t drop at any specific time.  

---

## 8-3. Public Safety
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In most situations, people attacked me because they thought they were stronger. They acted that way because they didn’t really know what I had.   

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;I didn’t have pets at that time. I don’t remember seeing many pets back then either. I’m not sure if anyone ate their pets, but they probably did.   

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Accidental fires were rare, but intentional fires were common.

## 8-4. Lantern Oil
**Questioner:** What were the characteristics of the lantern oil made by your neighbors?   

**Selco:** The lantern oil made by the neighbors was yellow, sometimes brown. The lamp was primitive; it was filled with oil in a bottle, and a 10 cm long shoelace was passed through the bottle cap and stuck into it. The shoelace stuck out 2-3 cm above the cap, and the rest was submerged in oil.   

**Selco:** The smell was awful, and it produced a lot of black smoke, but it still provided light. You could also use it for cooking, but it was always too valuable to use often.   

**Questioner:** Given the color, my guess is that it was turpentine from pine trees. If turpentine was mixed with diesel, it might have been to mask the smell of the turpentine. That way, no one would know what oil was being used. And if there was a shortage, it could have been mixed to stretch it.   

**Questioner:** To make turpentine, you basically need pine trees. You make a hole in the tree bark about 1-2 cm deep, the size of half a little finger, and insert an inclined pipe to collect the sap. This sap is distilled at 100ºC-160ºC to make turpentine. Turpentine can be harvested year-round, but the best time to get it is usually late winter to early spring. It is used as a disinfectant and, when mixed with wax, as a polish. It has many uses.   

**Selco:** Thank you very much. I used that many times, but I didn’t know what it was.
