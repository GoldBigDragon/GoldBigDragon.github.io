## 4. Using By-products and Preservation
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In a disaster, the distinction between lean meat and by-products does not hold. Lean meat is less than half of the calories from one animal, and the fat and vitamins that become scarcest are mostly found somewhere other than the lean meat. What you decide is not which parts to throw away but in what order to use them.

---
### 4-1. Fat and Rabbit Starvation
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Fat is about 9 kcal per gram, more than twice as dense as the 4 kcal of protein and carbohydrate. It contains almost no water, so it is hard for bacteria to grow, and its keeping quality is also the best. In a state of hunger, fat is worth more than lean meat.

| Location of fat | Properties | Main use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Fat around the kidneys | High melting point, hard, high proportion of saturated fat | Lasts the longest in storage when rendered and solidified |
| Back and subcutaneous fat | Thick and plentiful | Rendered oil, a protective layer wrapping lean meat |
| Omentum of the abdominal cavity | A thin net-like membrane | Wrapping lean meat for roasting, or slowing drying-out during drying |
| Intermuscular fat | Dispersed among muscle fibers | Melts during cooking and gives flavor, hard to recover separately |
| Bone marrow | Very high fat content | Crack the bone and eat it right away, or boil and skim off the oil |
| Brain and around the eyes | Rich in lipids and phospholipids | Besides food, material for tanning hides |

- Rendering fat: Heating slowly over low heat bursts the membranes of fat cells so oil flows out, and the connective tissue shrinks and remains as residue. Heat until the remaining moisture evaporates. If moisture remains, mold and bacteria grow during storage. If the fire is strong, the oil burns and rancidity begins, so heat for a long time at a low temperature.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The problem that appears when fat is lacking is rabbit starvation. It refers to the metabolic disturbance that occurs when one continues to eat only the lean meat of small wild animals with almost no body fat. The cause is the processing capacity of the liver and kidneys. To use protein for energy, nitrogen must be removed from amino acids, and the nitrogen turns to urea via ammonia and leaves in the urine. The processing rate of the urea cycle is a little over 2 g per kg of body weight per day, with a limit of 200-300 g a day for an adult. Beyond that, ammonia and amino acids build up in the blood, and dehydration also follows as large amounts of water are used to excrete the urea.

| Course | Symptoms |
| :---: | --- |
| 3-4 days | Severe fatigue, nausea, hunger that no amount of eating satisfies |
| 7-10 days | Diarrhea begins and dehydration compounds it |
| 2-3 weeks | Weakness progresses rapidly and may lead to death |

- Safe range: Keep protein below 35% of total calorie intake, preferably below 25%. In recorded cases, symptoms appeared when it exceeded 45%.
- Get 30-50% of daily calorie intake from fat. If you have only lean meat, don't eat more to fill the calories; eat something that provides fat or carbohydrate together with it, such as roots and nuts.

> **Caution:** When you have only lean meat and no fat, eating more is not the answer. Until you can get fat or carbohydrates, it is actually safer to eat less meat.

### 4-2. Nutrition of Organ Meats and Excess Vitamin A
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Organ meats concentrate nutrients that lean meat cannot provide. The liver is a storehouse of vitamin A, iron, vitamin B12, folate, and copper. Most of the nutrients whose deficiency appears within weeks on grain and meat alone are found here.

| Part | Main nutrition | Key points of handling |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Liver | Vitamin A, iron, vitamin B12, folate, copper | Remove it without rupturing the gallbladder. If there are white spots or nodules, discard it all |
| Heart | Composition close to muscle, iron and coenzyme Q10 | Remove the valves and clotted blood and use it like ordinary muscle |
| Kidney | Iron and B vitamins | Smells strongly of urine, so split in half and soak in water before cooking |
| Lungs and spleen | Protein and iron | The tissue is loose and spoils quickly, so consume first |
| Brain | Lipids and phospholipids | Has food value, but avoid it in species with a risk of prion disease |
| Bone marrow | Fat and calories | The densest calorie source obtainable right away by cracking a bone |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The liver has a risk in the opposite direction. Vitamin A is fat-soluble, so it is not easily excreted and accumulates in the liver, and the livers of some animals contain vitamin A at levels a person cannot handle. There are several records of Arctic expeditions who ate the liver of polar bears, seals, and sled dogs and suffered severe headaches, vomiting, drowsiness, and, a few days later, skin peeling. Eating just 500 g of polar bear liver reaches an acute poisoning dose. The higher up the food chain an animal is, the more vitamin A is concentrated at each step.

> **Caution:** Do not eat the liver of large carnivorous mammals and marine mammals. The liver of herbivorous and omnivorous animals such as deer, rabbits, cattle, and pigs (excluding wild boar) is safe in sensible amounts. Even for other livers, do not exceed about 100 g a day, and do not eat it every day.

### 4-3. Bones, Blood, Intestines, and Hides
- Bones: Not edible in themselves, but they contain marrow, gelatin, and minerals. Cut and boiled for a long time, the collagen of joints and cartilage melts into gelatin and gives the broth body, making a good base for boiling tough cuts and roots together. Crushing them beforehand increases the surface area so extraction is faster and the marrow is recovered as well.
- Blood: Rich in protein and iron, but it spoils fastest. Stir it immediately on collecting to prevent clotting, or conversely let it set and then cook it. If not stirred, it separates into clots and clear liquid and is hard to handle. Adding salt slows coagulation. Because it is the medium in which bacteria grow best, always heat it thoroughly.
- Intestines: After stripping out the contents, turn them inside out, scrape off the inner mucosa, and rinse several times, and only a thin, tough membrane remains. This membrane becomes the casing for sausages and blood sausage, and when dried is also used for bowstrings and sutures. The stomachs of ruminants are especially thick and become a separate foodstuff.

| By-product | Value as a tool |
| --- | --- |
| Sinew | Dried and pounded, it splits into fibers and, wetted, sticks together again, making the strongest natural cord |
| Bone | Awls, needles, fishhooks, arrowheads, scrapers |
| Horn and hooves | Soften when boiled and can be shaped, then harden when cool. Boiled long, they become glue |
| Hide | Raw hide hardens like stone when dry and becomes cord and containers; tanned, it becomes clothing |
| Bladder and stomach | When dried, become pouches to hold water and oil |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;If a hide is not processed right after skinning, decay begins within hours. The principle is covered in the Leather Tanning document.

### 4-4. Disposing of Remains and Preservation
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Where to put the unusable remains is both a hygiene problem and a safety problem. If visceral contents and bone fragments are discarded near the workplace, maggots settle in within three days, and predators that catch the smell come all the way to the human dwelling. Bears and wild boar detect the smell of decay from several kilometers away.

| Disposal method | Principle | Limits |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Bury deep | Soil blocks the spread of smell | Must dig at least 60 cm; if shallow, it is dug up |
| Burn | Breaks down organic matter and removes the smell | Takes a lot of fuel and the smoke gives away your position |
| Keep away from water | Prevents contamination of drinking water sources | Must be at least 50 m from the water supply |
| Move away from the dwelling | Keeps predators away from people | Move it to the downwind side |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;When dismemberment is finished, a race against time begins. The meat a person can eat in a day is limited, and a single medium-sized animal is more than several days' worth. The last stage of dismemberment is always preservation, and the preservation plan is made before dismemberment starts.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;All preservation methods converge on a single principle: depriving bacteria of the water they can use, or creating an environment they cannot endure.

- Drying: Evaporate water to lower water activity.
- Salting: Draw water out of bacteria by osmotic pressure.
- Smoking: The phenols and aldehydes in smoke disinfect the surface and help drying.
- Sealing in fat: Pour melted fat over the meat and let it harden, physically blocking access by oxygen and bacteria.
