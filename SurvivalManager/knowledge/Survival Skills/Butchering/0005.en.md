## 4. Butchering With No Waste
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Ordinary butchery treats lean meat as the center and calls everything else a byproduct, but that distinction does not hold in a disaster situation. Lean meat makes up less than half of the calories a single animal yields, and fat and vitamins — the nutrients people run short of first — are mostly found outside the lean meat. The real question is not which parts to throw away, but in what order to use them.

---
### 4-1. The Value of Fat and Rabbit Starvation
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Fat provides about 9kcal per 1g, more than double the density of protein or carbohydrate at 4kcal. For the same weight carried, fat delivers an overwhelming amount of energy, and because it holds almost no water, bacteria have a hard time growing in it, which also makes it the best-keeping part. This is why, in a starvation situation, fat is worth more than lean meat.

| Location of Fat | Properties | Main Use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Fat around the kidneys | High melting point, firm, high proportion of saturated fat | Keeps longest once rendered and set |
| Back and subcutaneous fat | Thick and abundant | Rendered fat, and a protective layer wrapped around lean meat |
| Caul fat in the abdominal cavity | A thin, net-like membrane | Wraps lean meat for roasting, or slows drying-out during preservation |
| Intermuscular fat | Dispersed between muscle fibers | Melts during cooking to build flavor; hard to recover separately |
| Bone marrow | Very high fat content | Crack the bone and eat it directly, or boil it and skim off the fat |
| Brain and around the eyes | Rich in lipids and phospholipids | Edible, and also used as a material for tanning hide |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The principle behind rendering fat is simple. Heated slowly over low heat, the membranes of the fat cells rupture and the oil flows out, while the connective tissue shrinks down into solid cracklings. You must keep heating until all remaining moisture has evaporated, because any moisture left behind lets mold and bacteria grow right there in storage. If the heat is too high, the fat burns and rancidity sets in, so the rule is low heat for a long time.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The problem that appears when fat is lacking is called rabbit starvation. The name comes from rabbits, but it refers broadly to the metabolic disorder that results from eating nothing but the lean meat of small wild animals with almost no body fat. The cause lies in the processing capacity of the liver and kidneys. To use protein for energy, nitrogen must be stripped from the amino acids, and the stripped nitrogen passes through ammonia to become urea, which leaves the body in urine. The urea cycle has an upper limit on how fast it can work — roughly 2g per 1kg of body weight per day, or about 200~300g a day for an adult. Beyond that, ammonia and amino acids build up in the blood, and because flushing out urea requires large amounts of water, dehydration sets in alongside it.

| Time Elapsed | Symptoms |
| :---: | --- |
| 3-4 days | Severe fatigue, nausea, hunger that no amount of eating satisfies |
| 7-10 days | Diarrhea begins, compounded by dehydration |
| 2-3 weeks | Rapid decline that can progress to death |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The safe range is to keep protein's share of total calorie intake below 35%, and preferably below 25%. In recorded cases, symptoms appeared once this share exceeded 45%. In practical terms, 30-50% of daily calorie intake should come from fat; if all you have is lean meat, the answer is not to eat more of it to meet your calorie needs, but to eat it together with something that supplies fat or carbohydrate, such as roots or nuts.

> **If all you have is lean meat and no fat, eating more is not the answer. Until you can get fat or carbohydrate, it is actually safer to eat less meat.**

---
### 4-2. The Nutrition in Organs and Vitamin A Toxicity
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Organs concentrate the nutrients you can get in the wild that lean meat cannot supply. The liver especially is a storehouse of vitamin A, iron, vitamin B12, folate, and copper. Most of the nutrients that become deficient within a few weeks on a diet of only grain and lean meat are found here.

| Organ | Main Nutrients | Key Handling Points |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Liver | Vitamin A, iron, vitamin B12, folate, copper | Remove it without rupturing the gallbladder, and discard it entirely if it has white spots or nodules |
| Heart | Composition close to muscle, with iron and coenzyme Q10 | Remove the valves and any clotted blood, then use it like ordinary muscle meat |
| Kidneys | Iron and B vitamins | Have a strong urine smell, so split in half and soak in water before cooking |
| Lungs and spleen | Protein and iron | Loose-textured and quick to spoil, so use them first |
| Brain | Lipids and phospholipids | Has food value, but avoid it in species with a risk of prion disease |
| Bone marrow | Fat and calories | The most calorie-dense source you can get simply by cracking a bone |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The liver also carries a risk running in the opposite direction. Vitamin A is fat-soluble, so it is not easily excreted from the body and instead accumulates in the liver. Some animals' livers, though, contain far more vitamin A than a person can handle. There are multiple recorded cases of polar expedition members who ate the liver of polar bears, seals, or sled dogs and suffered severe headache, vomiting, drowsiness, and skin peeling days later. Eating just 500g of polar bear liver is enough to reach an acute toxic dose.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This buildup happens because these animals sit near the top of the food chain, where vitamin A becomes concentrated at every step. By contrast, the liver of herbivorous or omnivorous animals such as deer, rabbit, cattle, and pigs (excluding wild boar) is safe as long as you eat a sensible amount. The rule is simple: do not eat the liver of large carnivorous mammals or marine mammals, and for any other liver, do not exceed about 100g a day, and do not eat it every day.

### 4-3. Bones, Blood, Intestines, and Hide
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Bone itself is not edible, but it holds marrow, gelatin, and minerals. If you cut the bones and simmer them for a long time, the collagen in the joints and cartilage dissolves into gelatin, giving the broth body, and this broth makes the best base for simmering tough cuts together with roots. Breaking the bones beforehand increases the surface area, speeding up extraction and recovering the marrow along with it.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Blood is rich in protein and iron, but it is also the part that spoils fastest. As soon as it is collected, you must either stir it to keep it from clotting or let it set and then cook it; if left unstirred, it separates into clots and clear liquid and becomes hard to handle. Adding salt delays clotting. Because blood is the medium bacteria grow best in, though, it must always be cooked all the way through.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;After the intestine's contents are stripped out, if you turn it inside out, scrape off the inner mucosa, and rinse it several times, only a thin, tough membrane is left. This membrane becomes casing for sausage and sundae (Korean blood sausage), and when dried it can also serve as bowstring or suture thread. In ruminants the stomach is especially thick and becomes a separate food ingredient of its own.

| Byproduct | Value as a Tool Material |
| --- | --- |
| Sinew | Dried and pounded, it splits into fibers; wetted again, it becomes the strongest natural cordage |
| Bone | Awls, needles, fishhooks, arrowheads, scrapers |
| Horn and hooves | Boiling softens them so they can be shaped, and they harden again on cooling. Boiled for a long time, they become glue |
| Hide | Rawhide becomes hard as stone when dried, making cordage and containers; tanned, it becomes clothing |
| Bladder and stomach | Dried, they become pouches for carrying water or fat |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;If the hide is not treated right after it is removed, decay begins within a few hours. The details are covered in the survival-skills document on tanning hide.

### 4-4. Disposing of Remains and Moving On to Preservation
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Where you put unusable remains is both a hygiene issue and a safety issue. If intestinal contents and bone fragments are dumped near the work site, fly maggots take hold within three days, and predators that smell it can follow the scent all the way to where people are staying. Bears and wild boar can detect the smell of decay from several kilometers away.

| Disposal Method | Principle | Limitation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Burying deep | Soil blocks the smell from spreading | Must dig at least 60cm; if too shallow, animals will dig it up |
| Burning | Breaks down organic matter and eliminates the smell | Uses a lot of fuel, and the smoke reveals your location |
| Keeping away from water | Prevents contaminating drinking-water sources | Must stay at least 50m from any water source |
| Moving it away from where you stay | Keeps predators away from people | Move it downwind |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Once butchering is finished, the race against time begins. The amount of meat a person can eat in a day is limited, and a single medium-sized animal hands you more than several days' worth all at once. This is why the final step of butchering is always preservation, and the plan for preservation needs to be set before butchering even begins.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;All preservation methods converge on a single principle: take away the water bacteria can use, or create an environment bacteria cannot survive in. Drying evaporates water to lower water activity; salting uses osmotic pressure to pull water out of bacteria; smoking sterilizes the surface with the phenols and aldehydes in smoke while also helping it dry. Sealing with fat works by pouring melted fat over the meat and letting it harden, physically blocking oxygen and bacteria from reaching it. The specific conditions and combinations for each method continue in the food-preservation document.
