## 3. Rigor Mortis, Aging, and the Properties of Meat by Cut
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The widespread idea that meat from a freshly killed animal tastes best is not actually true. The muscle of an animal just after death is soft; after a few hours it stiffens like a block of wood; and only after several more days does it become tender again. If you don't know these three stages and cook the meat while it is still in the stiff phase, you get tough meat no matter what method you use.

---
### 3-1. The Chemistry Behind Rigor Mortis
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;For a muscle to contract, two proteins called actin and myosin must pull against each other and latch together; to release the contraction, the two proteins must be pulled apart again. Pulling them apart, however, takes energy. The substance that supplies that energy in muscle is ATP, and ATP is used to relax the muscle, not to contract it.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;When an animal dies, its oxygen supply is cut off, and the muscle briefly keeps making ATP by converting its remaining glycogen into lactic acid. But the glycogen runs out within a few hours, and the ATP disappears along with it. The moment ATP runs out, actin and myosin can no longer separate and stay locked together permanently. This is rigor mortis: the state in which the entire muscle is fixed in maximum contraction.

| Animal | Onset of Rigor | Peak of Rigor | Minimum Time to Resolve Rigor |
| --- | :---: | :---: | :---: |
| Cattle | 6~12 hr | 12~24 hr | 2~3 days; full tenderization takes 14 days at 3°C |
| Pigs | 15 min~3 hr | 4~8 hr | About 60 hours |
| Sheep and goats | 4~8 hr | 12~18 hr | One to two days |
| Deer and water deer | 4~10 hr | 10~20 hr | Two to four days |
| Birds such as chicken and pheasant | 30 min~1 hr | 2~4 hr | About 36 hours |
| Rabbit | 1~3 hr | 4~8 hr | A little over a day |
| Fish | Minutes to a few hours | A few hours | Eaten right away without aging, or preserved immediately |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This gives one practical rule. Cooking the meat right after the catch, or cooking it after rigor has fully passed, both work fine — but cooking it in between produces the toughest result. Since it takes large animals several days for rigor to pass, the realistic choices are to eat before rigor sets in or to wait it out. The traditional practice of eating a hunted animal's liver and heart the same day while hanging the carcass for several days comes from exactly this constraint.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Cooling the meat sharply before rigor has ended makes the situation worse. If pre-rigor muscle is dropped rapidly below 15-16°C, cold shortening occurs, in which the muscle fibers contract far more strongly than normal, leaving a toughness that does not recover even after rigor has ended. This is why, for an animal caught in winter, hanging it to cool slowly in a ventilated, shaded spot is better than laying it straight down in the snow.

---
### 3-2. How Aging Makes Meat Tender
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Rigor does not release on its own because the actin-myosin bond comes apart again. The bond stays exactly as it is; instead, enzymes already present in the muscle gradually cut apart the structure of the muscle fibers. Aging, then, is not a process of the muscle relaxing, but a process of it breaking down.

| Enzyme | Activation Conditions | What It Breaks Down |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Calpain | Activated by calcium ions; most active near pH 6 | Structural proteins that link the myofibrils together, such as desmin, titin, and nebulin |
| Cathepsin | Released from lysosomes; active in acidic conditions | Myofibrillar proteins in general and some connective tissue |
| Calpastatin | Inhibits calpain | The more of this an individual animal has, the slower the aging and the tougher the meat |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;During rigor mortis, the pH inside the muscle drops to around 5.5, and as the cell membranes break down, calcium ions flood into the cytoplasm. This calcium wakes up calpain, which cuts the scaffold proteins that hold the myofibrils together. The muscle fiber is still contracted, but because the structure holding it in place has loosened, the tissue collapses much more easily when you chew it. Cathepsin acts more slowly but far more broadly, producing the flavor changes seen in the later stages of aging.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The temperature and length of aging are a balance point between two competing processes. Higher temperature speeds up enzyme action, but it speeds up bacterial growth even more. Enzymes keep working, if slowly, even at low temperatures, while most spoilage bacteria effectively stop at low temperatures, so aging for a long time at a low temperature is overwhelmingly the better choice.

| Temperature Range | Enzyme Action | Spoilage Risk | Verdict |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0-4°C | Slow but steady | Very low | Best suited for aging |
| 5-10°C | Moderate | Low but not negligible | Workable for up to a few days |
| 10-15°C | Fast | High | Hard to go beyond a day |
| Above 15°C | Very fast | Very high | This is spoilage, not aging |

> **Aging without refrigeration turns into spoilage the moment the air temperature passes 10°C. Outside late autumn through early spring, aging is not an option.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;So in the warmer seasons, the right choice is to give up on aging and move straight to preservation. Tough meat can still be eaten if boiled for a long time, but spoiled meat has no remedy. Aging is an option only when you can afford it; in survival priorities, preservation always comes first.

---
### 3-3. Why the Properties of Meat Differ by Cut
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Nearly all of the difference in texture between cuts is explained by how often, and how forcefully, that muscle was used while the animal was alive. A heavily used muscle has to withstand tension, so the connective tissue wrapping its fibers — collagen — develops thick. Collagen is an extremely tough protein with a triple-helix structure that chewing alone cannot break.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Collagen has one more property, though. If it is heated for a long time in the presence of water, the triple helix unwinds and turns into gelatin, and gelatin dissolves in water to give a soft, viscous body. This conversion starts around 60-70°C, but to actually proceed at a meaningful rate it needs several hours above 80°C. This conversion is why a tough cut becomes tender when boiled for a long time.

| Cut | Role in the Living Animal | Connective Tissue | Suitable Cooking |
| --- | --- | :---: | --- |
| Loin and tenderloin | Mostly for posture, rarely used | Little | Short dry-heat cooking |
| Neck and shoulder | Support the head and move the forelegs | A lot | Long moist-heat cooking |
| Foreleg and shank | Bearing weight and walking | Very much | Very long simmering |
| Ribs | Continuously engaged in breathing | Medium | Moist-heat cooking followed by roasting |
| Hind leg | Large muscle providing propulsion | Medium to a lot | Moist-heat cooking, or drying and salting |
| Tail and around the neck bones | Constant, fine, small movements | A lot | Best for making broth |
| Diaphragm | Moves without stopping for the animal's entire life | Medium, with thick muscle fibers | Grill briefly and slice against the grain |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The direction of slicing also governs texture. Muscle fibers are very tough lengthwise but break apart easily sideways. Slicing at a right angle to the grain shortens the length of fiber your teeth have to work through, so the same piece of meat feels far more tender. Slicing tough cuts thin, always against the grain, is the most effective tenderizing method available without any tools.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Age is also a factor. The collagen of a young animal has fewer cross-links and converts to gelatin easily, while the collagen of an older animal has denser cross-linking and does not break down well even when boiled for the same length of time. If the animal you caught is older, it is better to plan from the start around broth and moist-heat cooking.
