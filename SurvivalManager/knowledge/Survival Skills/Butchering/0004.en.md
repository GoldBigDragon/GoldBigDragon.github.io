## 3. Rigor Mortis, Aging, and the Properties of Meat by Cut
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;It is not true that the meat of a freshly killed animal tastes best. Immediately after death the muscle is soft, after a few hours it stiffens like a block of wood, and it takes several more days to become tender. If you cook it while it is stiff, you get tough meat whatever method you use.

---
### 3-1. Rigor Mortis
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;For muscle to contract, actin and myosin pull on each other, and to relax, the two proteins must be pulled apart. Pulling them apart takes energy (ATP). When an animal dies, the oxygen supply is cut off, and the muscle briefly makes ATP by converting its remaining glycogen to lactic acid. Glycogen runs out in a few hours and the ATP disappears as well. Without ATP, actin and myosin remain bound and cannot separate, and are fixed in place. This is rigor mortis, and the whole muscle stiffens in its maximally contracted state.

| Animal | Onset of rigor | Peak of rigor | Minimum time needed to resolve rigor |
| --- | :---: | :---: | :---: |
| Cattle | 6-12 hours | 12-24 hours | 2-3 days; sufficient tenderizing takes 14 days at 3 °C |
| Pigs | 15 minutes-3 hours | 4-8 hours | About 60 hours |
| Sheep and goats | 4-8 hours | 12-18 hours | One to two days |
| Deer and water deer | 4-10 hours | 10-20 hours | Two to four days |
| Birds such as chicken and pheasant | 30 minutes-1 hour | 2-4 hours | About 36 hours |
| Rabbits | 1-3 hours | 4-8 hours | A little over a day |
| Fish | A few minutes-a few hours | A few hours | Eat right away without aging, or preserve immediately |

- Cooking right after the kill, or after rigor has completely passed, is fine; cooking in between gives the toughest meat. For large animals, rigor takes days to pass, so the only realistic choices are to eat before rigor or to wait. The traditional practice of eating the liver and heart that same day and hanging the carcass for several days comes from this.
- Chilling the meat rapidly before rigor ends makes it worse. If muscle before rigor is rapidly dropped below 15-16 °C, cold shortening occurs, in which muscle fibers contract far more strongly than normal, and toughness remains even after rigor ends. Rather than laying an animal killed in winter on a snowfield, it is better to hang it in ventilated shade and let it cool slowly.

---
### 3-2. The Principle of Aging
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Rigor resolves by itself not because the bonds between actin and myosin come apart. The bonds remain, and enzymes within the muscle gradually cut the structure of the muscle fibers. Aging is a process in which muscle is broken down, not one in which it relaxes.

| Enzyme | Activating conditions | What it breaks down |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Calpain | Activated by calcium ions. Most active around pH 6 | Structural proteins such as desmin, titin, and nebulin that hold myofibrils together |
| Cathepsin | Released from lysosomes. Active in acid | Myofibrillar proteins in general and some connective tissue |
| Calpastatin | Inhibits calpain | The more of it an individual has, the slower the aging and the tougher the meat |

- During rigor mortis the muscle pH falls to around 5.5, the cell membranes collapse, and calcium ions pour into the cytoplasm. Calpain is awakened by this calcium and cuts the scaffold proteins that support the myofibrils. The muscle fibers are still contracted, but the structures that held them have loosened, so the tissue collapses far more easily when chewed. Cathepsin acts more slowly but more broadly, and produces the flavor changes of late aging.
- A higher temperature speeds enzyme action, but bacterial growth speeds up even more. Enzymes keep working, though slowly, even in the cold, and most spoilage bacteria virtually stop in the cold, so keeping the meat for a long time at a low temperature is overwhelmingly advantageous.

| Temperature range | Enzyme action | Spoilage risk | Judgment |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0-4 °C | Slow but steady | Very low | Best suited to aging |
| 5-10 °C | Moderate | Low but not negligible | Possible for up to a few days |
| 10-15 °C | Fast | High | Hard to keep past a day |
| 15 °C or more | Very fast | Very high | Not aging but spoilage |

> **Caution:** Aging without refrigeration turns from aging into spoilage the moment the temperature exceeds 10 °C. Unless it is between late autumn and early spring, aging is not an option.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In warm seasons, give up on aging and move straight to preservation. Tough meat can be eaten by boiling it for a long time, but spoiled meat has no remedy. In the priorities of survival, preservation comes before aging.

---
### 3-3. Properties of Meat by Cut
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Differences in texture between cuts are explained almost entirely by how often and how forcefully that muscle was used in life. Muscles that are used heavily must withstand tension, so the connective tissue (collagen) that wraps the muscle fibers develops thickly. Collagen is an extremely tough protein with a triple-helix structure and cannot be broken by chewing.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;When collagen is heated for a long time in the presence of water, the triple helix unwinds and becomes gelatin, and gelatin dissolves in water to give a soft viscosity. This conversion starts around 60-70 °C, but to proceed at a meaningful rate it needs several hours at 80 °C or above. This is why boiling tough cuts for a long time makes them tender.

| Cut | Role in life | Connective tissue | Suitable cooking |
| --- | --- | :---: | --- |
| Loin and tenderloin | Posture maintenance, hardly used | Little | Short dry-heat cooking |
| Neck and shoulder | Supports the head and moves the forelegs | Much | Long moist-heat cooking |
| Foreleg and shank | Bears body weight and walking | Very much | Boiling for a very long time |
| Rib area | Continues respiratory movement | Moderate | Moist-heat cooking, then grilling |
| Hind leg | Large muscles that produce propulsion | Moderate to much | Moist-heat cooking, or drying and salting |
| Tail and around the neck bones | Constant tiny movements | Much | Best for making broth |
| Diaphragm | Moves without rest all its life | Moderate, thick muscle fibers | Grill briefly and slice against the grain |

- Slicing direction: Muscle fibers are very tough lengthwise but break easily sideways. Cutting at right angles to the grain shortens the length of fiber you bear when chewing and makes the meat far more tender. The tougher the cut, the thinner you slice it, against the grain. It is the most effective means of tenderizing that needs no tools.
- Age: The collagen of a young animal has few cross-links and easily becomes gelatin. The collagen of an old animal has dense cross-links and does not unwind easily even if boiled for the same time. Plan for older animals from the start on the premise of broth and moist-heat cooking.
