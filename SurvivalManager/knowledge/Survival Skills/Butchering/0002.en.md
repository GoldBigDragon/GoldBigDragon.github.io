## 1. Slaughtering Is a Hygiene Task
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;If you think of slaughtering as simply the technique of cutting up meat, the order and the reasons behind each step stay invisible. Slaughtering is, at its core, a task of preventing contamination, and cutting is only the means to that end. The muscle tissue of a living animal is protected by the immune system and is effectively sterile. Meat spoils not because something inside it wakes up, but because bacteria that were outside have gotten in.

---
### 1-1. Three Routes of Contamination
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There are, in practice, only three routes by which bacteria reach meat. Once you know these three, you can see that every hygiene rule derives from a single underlying principle.

| Route | What Gets In | How to Block It |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Digestive-tract contents | Hundreds of millions of gut bacteria per gram — E. coli, Salmonella, Campylobacter, and others | Tie off the esophagus and rectum, avoid rupturing the intestines, and remove the viscera as a whole |
| Hair and hide | Dirt, feces, soil bacteria, ticks, mold spores | Peel the hide outward so the hair never touches the meat surface |
| Human hands, knife, and work surface | Mostly carried over from the first two sources | Always wash and switch knives after touching a contaminated area |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Of the three routes, the digestive tract is the most dangerous. 1g of rumen contents in a ruminant animal holds more than 10 billion bacteria, and the large-intestine contents are not far behind. If even a single drop of this content touches the meat surface, that spot instantly becomes a contamination source on the order of millions of bacteria; the bacteria cannot burrow from the surface into the interior, but as the meat is cut apart, the knife carries them through the whole piece.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;For this reason, the rule is to cut away a contaminated spot, not to wash it off. Washing with water does not rinse the bacteria away — it spreads them across the whole surface, and bacteria multiply far more readily on a wet surface than on a dry one. This is why slaughtering work uses water sparingly and favors dry cloths.

> **Contaminated meat is cut away, not washed. Water does not remove contamination — it spreads it.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The knife is the most efficient carrier of contamination. If you cut into the flesh with the same knife you used to skin the hide, everything on the hide's surface transfers straight into the muscle. If you have two or more tools, keep one for the hide and one for the meat; if you have only one, wipe it clean every time you switch steps. Industrial facilities disinfect knives by dipping them in water at 82°C, a temperature chosen because it instantly kills the vegetative cells on the surface. If you can boil water, dipping the knife briefly in boiling water gives the same effect.

### 1-2. How Bleeding Affects Keeping Quality
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Blood is drained not to make the meat's color brighter, but to extend how long it keeps. Blood is an almost perfect growth medium for bacteria: it has protein, iron, glucose, and a near-neutral pH all at once, so wherever blood remains between a vessel and the surrounding tissue, that spot becomes a starting point for bacterial growth.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Bleeding has a second reason behind it. Once muscle loses its oxygen supply, it switches to anaerobic metabolism and converts glycogen into lactic acid, which drops the muscle's pH from around 7.2 down to 5.4-5.7. This acidification is the meat's own defense mechanism, suppressing the growth of spoilage bacteria. But if the animal is chased for a long time or becomes extremely excited before death, its glycogen is used up in advance, and the acidification after death cannot go far enough. The result is dark, firm, dry meat with a pH that stays at 6.0 or above, and this meat spoils far faster than normal meat.

| Condition | Final pH | Result |
| --- | :---: | --- |
| Normally bled meat | 5.4~5.7 | Normal color and texture, keeps well |
| Long-term stress before death | 6.0 and above | Dark and firm, with fast bacterial growth that makes it hard to keep |
| Sudden stress combined with high temperature | Plunges to 5.4 or below | Pale and soft, with heavy loss of meat juices |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Bleeding is only efficient while the heart is still beating. Once it stops, blood can only be drained by gravity, and the recovery rate drops sharply. This is why the head is positioned downward so gravity can help, and the major vessels are opened all at once so the flow is not interrupted. If you cut through the windpipe and esophagus along with the vessels of the neck, stomach contents can flow back and contaminate the wound surface, so it is more hygienic to sever only the vessels precisely.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In practice, only about half of the total blood volume actually drains out; the rest stays in the organs and muscles. Because complete bleeding is impossible, it should be understood as work that reduces the contamination source, not one that eliminates it. The blood that is collected is itself a food rich in protein and iron, and its use is covered in the final chapter.

### 1-3. Second-Best Options When Water Is Scarce
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;When clean water is not available, the priorities of hygiene change. Washing with dirty water is worse than not washing at all. Stream water carries Leptospira, Giardia, and various intestinal bacteria, so using it only adds a new source of contamination to the meat.

| Item | Best Option | Second-Best Option |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hand washing | Soap and running water | Rub with ash, then wipe with a dry cloth |
| Knife disinfection | Dip in boiling water | Pass the blade quickly through a flame |
| Work surface | A washed and dried board | Freshly debarked wood, large leaves, or a clean stone |
| Meat surface | Keep dry without washing | Cut away only the contaminated part |
| A cut on your hand | Step away from the work | Cover it completely with resin or a clean cloth |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Wood ash is the most practical way to clean your hands when there is no water. The potassium carbonate in ash is alkaline and breaks down grease to some extent, while its particles physically scrape off contamination. Its germ-killing power still falls short of soap, though, so it remains only a second-best option.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The single most important principle is to keep the meat surface dry. Bacteria cannot multiply on a surface with low water activity. Hanging the meat in a ventilated, shaded spot lets a thin dry crust form on the surface, and this crust itself becomes a protective layer for the meat inside. This is the cheapest preservation method available when supply chains have broken down.
