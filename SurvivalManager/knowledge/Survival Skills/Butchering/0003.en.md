## 2. Timing of Evisceration and the Order of Butchering
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In slaughtering, the step where timing matters most is evisceration. Other tasks can be delayed by a few hours and still be recovered, but meat from an animal whose organs were removed too late cannot be saved by any method. The reason becomes clear once you look at what happens inside the digestive tract the moment the animal dies.

---
### 2-1. What Happens in the Digestive Tract After Death
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;While the animal is alive, the intestinal wall is protected in two ways: the physical barrier formed by the mucosal cells, and the immune system. The moment death stops the blood flow, the intestinal mucosal cells lose their oxygen supply and begin self-digestion within minutes. The lysosomes inside the cells rupture, triggering autolysis that breaks down the cells' own proteins, and the intestinal wall grows thinner and thinner over time until it finally develops holes.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Meanwhile, the bacteria inside the intestine move in the exact opposite direction. Body temperature is still warm, the immune system is gone, and food is unlimited. Under the right conditions, E. coli doubles every 20 minutes. Doubling every 20 minutes means an 8-fold increase in one hour, a 512-fold increase in three hours, and a 260,000-fold increase in six hours. Once bacteria begin crossing the thinned intestinal wall, the entire abdominal cavity — and every muscle bordering it — becomes contaminated.

| Time Since Death | Changes in the Digestive Tract | Effect on the Meat |
| :---: | --- | --- |
| 0~30 min | Blood flow stops, mucosal cells begin self-digestion | None yet |
| 30 min~2 hr | Gut bacteria surge, gas begins to form | If abdominal temperature is high, odor begins |
| 2~6 hr | Intestinal-wall permeability increases, bacteria begin moving through | Muscle bordering the abdominal cavity becomes contaminated |
| 6~12 hr | Abdominal bloating, leakage of intestinal contents | Most cuts become close to unusable |
| 12 hr and beyond | Whole-body decomposition progresses | Not edible |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The times in this table change greatly with air temperature. Because bacterial growth rate depends strongly on temperature, in summer when the air temperature exceeds 30°C you should treat the table's times as cut at least in half, while in below-freezing winter you can allow several times longer. So even for the same animal, the delay you can tolerate depends entirely on the season.

> **Evisceration is the only step in butchering where time is the deciding factor, and on a hot day it comes before every other task.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Removing the organs also serves to let heat escape. The abdominal cavity is the thickest, best-insulated part of the body, so as long as the organs remain inside, the core stays close to body temperature for hours. Once the organs are out and the cavity is left open, air can circulate, body heat drops quickly, and bacterial growth slows along with it. This is also why industrial facilities target 28-36 hours for cattle, 12-16 hours for pigs, and 24-30 hours for sheep to bring the core temperature down to 6-7°C. Without refrigeration, the best cooling method is to prop the abdominal cavity open with a stick and hang the carcass in a well-ventilated, shaded spot.

---
### 2-2. Rupturing the Gallbladder, Bladder, or Intestines
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Almost every failure in evisceration comes down to rupturing one of three organs. Each leaves a different kind of damage in the meat.

| Ruptured Organ | Contents | Damage Left in the Meat |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Gallbladder | Bile acids and bilirubin | A strong bitterness dissolves into the fat and will not wash out |
| Bladder | Urea and ammonia | A urine smell soaks in and remains even after drying or smoking |
| Stomach and intestines | Digestive fluid and gut bacteria | Bacterial contamination — the most hazardous hygienically |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The gallbladder is a particular problem because of what bile contains. Bile acids are surfactant molecules that dissolve readily into fat, and the taste threshold for their bitterness is very low. Meat that bile has touched keeps its bitterness inside the fat layer even after washing with water, leaving no option but to cut it away. Because the gallbladder is attached to the liver, it easily ruptures while the liver is being removed, so it is safer to tie off the neck of the gallbladder first, or to leave a generous piece of liver attached to the gallbladder and cut the whole piece out together.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The bladder is full whenever the animal has not urinated just before slaughter. It sits inside the pelvis and bursts under pressure when the hind legs are spread apart or the pelvis is split. The rule is to tie off the urethra before removing it; if you have nothing to tie it with, simply pressing it empty by hand already reduces the risk greatly.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The rectum is cut free in a circle around the anus, tied off, and pushed back inside; the esophagus is tied where it joins the stomach. Industrial facilities also require tying off the esophagus-stomach junction and the stomach-duodenum junction if the digestive tract is to be used for food. Both ends must be tied so the entire digestive tract becomes a closed sack and its contents cannot leak out.

### 2-3. The Logic of Butchering Order by Animal Size
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The order of butchering is not a matter of custom; it results from the physical constraints of size. There are two criteria: whether the weight is something one person can handle alone, and how quickly heat escapes from inside the body.

| Category | Approximate Weight | Logic of the Order |
| --- | :---: | --- |
| Large livestock and large game | 50kg and up | Bleeding, evisceration, skinning, splitting in half, cutting into sections. It cannot be handled without hanging, so the work proceeds from top to bottom with the carcass suspended |
| Medium animals | 10~50kg | Bleeding, evisceration, skinning, cutting into sections. There is no need to split it in half, so it is divided whole around the spine |
| Small game animals | 0.5~10kg | Evisceration and skinning almost at the same time. The small body loses heat fast, so there is less time pressure, but the risk of contamination from hair or feathers is actually greater |
| Birds | 0.5~2kg | Pluck the feathers first, then remove the organs. If you cut open the belly before plucking, feathers get into the abdominal cavity |
| Fish | Any size | Remove the gills and organs immediately. Their digestive enzymes are especially strong, and if left untended the belly flesh turns soft within hours |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In large animals, evisceration comes before skinning because of heat and time. A large animal has a large volume relative to its surface area, so heat does not escape the abdominal cavity easily; if skinning, which can take hours, is done first, bacteria multiply inside the cavity all the while. Small animals, by contrast, lose heat quickly, so there is more flexibility in the order.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The reason birds are plucked before evisceration is the opposite logic. Feathers are very light and scatter easily, so they readily get into an open abdominal cavity, and once inside they are very troublesome to remove. Dipping the bird briefly in hot water to loosen the feather roots makes plucking much easier, but if the water is too hot, the skin cooks and tears.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Fish are the most urgent case because of the strength of their digestive enzymes and their body temperature. Fish digestive enzymes evolved to stay highly active even at low temperatures, and they continue breaking down the fish's own tissue after death. On top of this, rigor mortis in fish passes within minutes to a few hours, so removing the gills and organs immediately after the catch and keeping the fish in cold water or shade is, in practice, the only effective response.
