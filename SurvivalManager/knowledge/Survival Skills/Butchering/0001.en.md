## Introduction
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There is a wider gap than people expect between catching an animal and eating it. A single rabbit caught in a trap holds roughly 1,000kcal of potential energy, but whether that energy actually reaches the human body or spoils and is thrown away within a few hours depends almost entirely on how the slaughtering and butchering were done. Throwing away a whole animal's meat in a starvation situation is not a simple loss — it is a double loss, since it also wastes the calories already burned to obtain that meat.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The way to think about slaughtering comes down to one point. The muscle of a living animal is naturally almost sterile. Bacteria do not arise inside the meat on their own — they come in from outside. There are three main routes in: the contents of the digestive tract, the hair and hide on the body surface, and the human hands and knife. Nearly every procedure in this document <why bleeding comes first, why the internal organs must be removed quickly, why the hide must be peeled so the hair never touches the meat, why the knife is washed often> serves this single purpose: blocking these three routes.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This document covers how bleeding and evisceration affect keeping quality, the chemical mechanism by which rigor mortis and aging change the texture of meat, why the properties of the meat differ from cut to cut, and how to use every part beyond the lean meat without waste. The zoonotic diseases that come with handling wild animals, and the everyday hunting regulations, are covered in the Trapping and Hunting document, and the same constraints apply here as well. Meat obtained in the wild must always be cooked completely through to the core with no exception, and under normal circumstances it must be handled only within the limits set by your own country's hunting laws and livestock-product hygiene regulations.
