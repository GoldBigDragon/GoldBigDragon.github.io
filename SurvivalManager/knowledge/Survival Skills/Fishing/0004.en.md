## 3. Traps, Nets, Fish Weirs, and Longlines
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Every kind of gear covered here works while the person is away. The shared principle is to make the fish enter on its own, and once inside, make it unable to find the way back out. The key point is that these rely not on force to confine the fish, but on the fish's own behavioral habits.

---
### 3-1. The Principle Behind How a Trap Confines Fish
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The entrance of a trap is shaped like a funnel that narrows from outside to inside, and the funnel's tip hangs suspended right in the middle of the trap's interior. Following the scent of bait, a fish enters through the funnel's wide mouth. Up to this point, nothing is difficult, because the wall naturally guides it inward.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The reason it cannot get back out comes from a fish's habit, when trapped in a dead end, of circling along the wall. A fish does not search for the small opening floating in the middle of the space; it tries to find the exit by circling along the outer wall. But the funnel's exit sits in the middle of the space, not on the wall, and on top of that it points inward to a narrow point, so it never intersects the path of a fish circling along the wall. That is the entire trick behind a trap. If the funnel's tip is made too close to the wall, or the funnel is so short that it leads straight from the wall, the escape rate rises sharply.

| Design Element | Standard | Reason for It |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Funnel entrance diameter | Large enough for the target fish to pass through | Too large, and big fish get in and break the gear trying to get out |
| Funnel tip diameter | A little larger than the target species' body thickness | The narrower it is, the lower the escape rate, but the entry rate drops too |
| Funnel length | Roughly 1/3 of the trap's depth | Keeps the tip near the center of the space, separated from the path along the wall |
| Gap between ribs | Narrower than the target species | Lets small individuals escape, reducing loss of the resource |
| Trap orientation | Entrance facing downstream | Fish generally face upstream, so they catch the scent and swim in against the current |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Bamboo and willow branches are good materials because they don't soften much even after long soaking, split into ribs of an even thickness, and become flexible enough to bend once wet. Bending them dry snaps them, so they are soaked in water before weaving. The weaving method is to stand the rib strips upright and wind thin branches across them horizontally; alternating the winding direction by one gap at a time locks the ribs in place so the structure holds itself together.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The placement must be somewhere fish actually pass through. A trap has only weak drawing power, so unless you place it somewhere fish already travel, it stays empty no matter how much bait you put in. Prioritize the edge of a weed bed, next to gaps between rocks, a narrow throat in the water, and a ledge where the depth changes.

---
### 3-2. Mesh Size and Types of Nets
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;A net catches fish in one of two ways. One is by wedging the body in the mesh so it cannot pull free; the other is by enclosing the fish so there is nowhere left to flee. The first is the gillnet, and the second is the cast net and the seine net.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;A gillnet's selectivity is decided by the circumference of the fish's body. If the head passes through but the widest part of the body is bigger around than the mesh opening, the fish can go neither forward nor backward, and its gill cover catches on the netting and holds it fast. This gives a gillnet both an upper and a lower limit on the size it catches at the same time. A fish that is too small simply swims through, and a fish that is too large cannot get its head in at all and turns back. Thanks to this property, changing the mesh size alone lets you decide what size of fish you catch.

| Mesh Side Length | Typical Length of Fish Caught (approx.) | Notes |
| :---: | :---: | --- |
| 20mm | About 10cm | Small species such as pale chub and smelt |
| 35mm | 15-20cm | Young crucian carp, young barbel steed |
| 50mm | 25-30cm | Medium crucian carp, young carp |
| 75mm | 35-45cm | Medium-to-large individuals |
| 100mm and up | 50cm and up | Limited to large species |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;For a net to stand up like a wall in the water, you need floats on top and sinkers on the bottom. Floats can be anything with a density lower than water, such as blocks of wood, dried gourds, or bundles of hollow reed; sinkers can simply be holed stones or pebbles strung on a cord. The net stretches taut only when buoyancy and sinking force are matched; if either is lacking, the net lies at an angle or collapses onto the bottom and catches nothing.

| Type | Principle | Conditions Required |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Gillnet | Set standing across the water so fish catch themselves | Uses little material and needs no one present |
| Trammel net | A fine inner net sandwiched between two loose outer layers forms a pocket | Low size selectivity, so even small individuals are caught |
| Cast net | A disc-shaped net is thrown to cover the fish, and the weights on its edge draw it closed to trap them | Requires skill, and the bottom must be free of snags |
| Seine net | A net encircles a wide area from the shore and is hauled in | Requires several people, and the bottom must be flat |
| Dip net, push net | A small net is held in place and fish are driven into it | The easiest method in a shallow stream |

### 3-3. Eosal and Dolsal Weirs
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Eosal (a fixed weir built from stakes) and dolsal (its stone-built counterpart) are structures that turn the movement of water itself into fishing gear. On a tidal flat with a large difference between high and low tide, the incoming tide carries fish up into shallow water, and the outgoing tide carries them back out. If you build a low stone wall across the channel the water drains through, the water escapes through the gaps between the stones, but the fish are left behind.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The reason the classic shape of a dolsal is a horseshoe is to catch the outgoing water. With the open side facing the sea and the wall curved concave inward, fish being pushed out by the ebb tide travel along the wall and converge at the single point where the curvature meets. At that point sits a pool built thicker and higher than the rest of the wall, called an imtong (holding pool). Even after all the water has drained away, water remains in the imtong, and fish collect there still alive.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The height of the wall is not constant: it starts at under 30cm on the landward side of the intertidal zone and rises higher toward the sea. This is because, on the side where the water gets deeper, the wall must be higher for fish not to be able to swim over it. Streams use the same principle. If you pile stones in a V shape at the lower end of a riffle with the point aimed downstream, fish coming downstream collect at the narrow gap at the point, and you simply place a trap or a screen there.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The decisive advantage of eosal and dolsal is that they need no bait, work for decades once built, and let the tide — an inexhaustible energy source — drive the fish in instead of a person having to. The drawback is that the initial labor is very large, and if the location is chosen wrong, all of that labor is wasted. You should observe the tidal flow and terrain across several tide cycles before building.

### 3-4. Longlines and Overnight Setting
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;A longline is gear made by attaching short branch lines at regular intervals along one long main line, with a hook or a geojeon tied to the end of each branch line. Instead of one person managing one hook with one rod, this method puts dozens of hooks in the water at the same time, so the expected return per hour is far greater.

| Element | Consideration |
| --- | --- |
| Spacing between branch lines | At least twice the branch-line length, so they don't tangle |
| Branch-line length | Shorter means less tangling; longer lets the bait move more naturally |
| Anchoring the main line | Tie both ends to a stake or a heavy stone so it does not drift away |
| Setting depth | On the bottom for catfish and eels; suspended mid-water for free-swimming species |
| Time to retrieve | Just before sunrise gives the largest catch |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There are two reasons for setting a longline out at night. First, nocturnal species that hunt by smell, such as catfish, eels, and torrent catfish, are active at night, and since they do not rely on sight, whether the line is visible does not matter. Second, this turns the hours a person spends sleeping into hours the gear spends working, which is the most favorable arrangement for calorie balance.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;However, a fish that has been hooked all night is often already dead, and if the water is warm, it may already have begun to spoil by the time you retrieve it in the morning. In summer, it is safer to shorten the retrieval interval or to rely more on traps, which hold fish alive. Also, if you forget where you set a piece of gear and cannot retrieve it, that gear becomes a device that keeps killing fish that no one will ever eat.
