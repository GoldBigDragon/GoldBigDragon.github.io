## 3. Fish Traps, Nets, Weirs, and Longlines
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;These gears work even while people are away. The common principle is to make fish come in on their own and, once in, be unable to find the way out. They use the behavioral habits of fish, not the power to confine.

---
### 3-1. Fish Traps
- Structure: The entrance is a funnel that narrows from outside to inside, and the funnel's end hangs in the very middle of the trap's interior. Fish follow the smell of bait in through the wide entrance, and the walls naturally guide them inward.
- Principle of preventing escape: Fish trapped in a dead end have a habit of circling along the wall. The funnel's exit is not on the wall but in the middle of the space and points sharply inward, so it does not meet the path of circling along the wall. If the funnel end is near the wall, or the funnel is short and continues straight from the wall, the escape rate rises greatly.

| Design element | Standard | Reason |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Funnel entrance diameter | Enough for the target fish to pass | If too large, large fish enter and break the gear and leave |
| Funnel end diameter | Slightly larger than the body girth of the target species | Narrower lowers the escape rate but also lowers the entry rate |
| Funnel length | About a third of the trap depth | Puts the end at the center of the space, separating it from the wall path |
| Slat spacing | Narrower than the target species | Lets small individuals out, reducing resource loss |
| Trap orientation | Entrance toward the downstream side of the flow | Fish generally face upstream, smell the bait, and come in against the current |

- Materials: Bamboo and willow twigs do not soften easily even when soaked in water for a long time, split into slats of uniform thickness, and become flexible when wet. They break if bent dry, so soak them in water before weaving.
- Weaving: Stand the warp slats vertically and wind thin twigs around them horizontally. If you alternate the winding direction by one slat each time, the warp slats are fixed and the structure holds itself.
- Placement: Passages where fish travel. A fish trap has weak power to call fish, so unless it is placed where fish already travel, it stays empty even with bait. Give priority to the edges of plant zones, beside gaps in rocks, necks where the water narrows, and steps where depth changes.

---
### 3-2. Nets
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Nets catch in two ways. Gill nets, in which the body gets caught in the mesh and cannot pull free, and cast nets and seines, which confine fish and remove any place to flee.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The selectivity of a gill net is set by the girth of the fish's body. If the head passes through but the largest body girth is greater than the mesh perimeter, the fish can go neither forward nor back, and its gill cover catches on the netting thread and holds it. Fish that are too small just slip through, and fish that are too large cannot get their heads in and turn back, so there are both upper and lower limits on the size caught. Just by changing the mesh size you can decide the size to catch.

| Mesh side length | Approximate body length mainly caught | Notes |
| :---: | :---: | --- |
| 20 mm | About 10 cm | Small species such as pale chub and pond smelt |
| 35 mm | 15-20 cm | Young crucian carp and skin carp |
| 50 mm | 25-30 cm | Medium crucian carp and young carp |
| 75 mm | 35-45 cm | Medium to large individuals |
| 100 mm or more | 50 cm or more | Limited to large species |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;For a net to stand like a wall in the water, attach floats along the top and sinkers along the bottom. Floats are things less dense than water, such as wooden blocks, dried gourds, and bundles of hollow reeds; sinkers are stones with holes or pebbles strung on the line. Buoyancy and sinking force must be balanced for the net to spread taut, and if one is lacking, the net lies slanting or flat on the bottom and catches nothing.

| Type | Principle | Required conditions |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Gill net | Set across the watercourse so fish snag themselves | Needs little material and no one needs to be present |
| Trammel net | A fine inner net is placed between two layers of coarse outer net to form a pocket | Low size selectivity, so even small individuals are caught |
| Cast net | A circular net is thrown to cover, and weights at the edge draw together to confine | Needs skill and the bottom must be free of snags |
| Seine net | A net encircles a wide area from the shore and is hauled in | Needs several people and a flat bottom |
| Hand net, dip net | Hold a small net and drive fish into it | The easiest method in shallow streams |

### 3-3. Fish Weirs and Stone Weirs
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;These are structures that turn the movement of water itself into gear. On tidal flats with a large tidal range, fish come into the shallows with the flood tide and leave with the ebb. If you build a low stone wall in the outgoing channel, the water drains through the gaps in the stones and the fish remain.

- Horseshoe shape: A form for turning back the outgoing water. If the open side faces the sea and is curved concavely inward, fish pushed out by the ebb move along the wall and concentrate at the one point where the curvature converges. The pool built thicker and higher at that point than elsewhere is called an imtong (the holding pool). Even when all the water has drained, water remains in the imtong and the fish gather alive.
- Wall height: Start below 30 cm on the inner side of the intertidal zone and rise toward the sea. The deeper the water fills, the higher the wall must be so that fish cannot get over it.
- Rivers: Pile stones in a V below a riffle, with the apex pointing downstream. Fish coming downstream gather in the narrow gap at the apex, so place a trap or a screen there.
- Advantages: No bait is needed, once built it works for decades, and the tide, an infinite energy source, drives the fish for you.
- Disadvantages: The initial labor is very large, and if the position is wrong, all the labor is wasted. Build it after observing the tidal flow and terrain over several tidal cycles.

### 3-4. Longlines and Setting at Night
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;A longline is gear in which short branch lines are attached to a long main line at regular intervals, with a hook or gorge hook on the end of each branch line. Dozens of hooks are put in the water at the same time, so the expected recovery per hour is much larger.

| Element | Considerations |
| --- | --- |
| Branch line spacing | At least twice the branch line length so they do not tangle |
| Branch line length | Short means less tangling, long lets the bait move naturally |
| Anchoring the main line | Tie both ends to stakes or heavy stones so it does not drift |
| Setting depth | On the bottom for catfish and eels, suspended mid-water for swimming species |
| Recovery time | The catch is largest just before sunrise |

- Reason one for leaving them in at night: Nocturnal species that find food by smell, such as catfish, eels, and bullhead catfish, are active at night, and since they do not depend on sight, whether the line is visible is not an issue.
- Reason two: It turns the hours people sleep into hours the gear works, which is the most advantageous for the calorie balance.
- Fish that hung on all night are often already dead, and if the water temperature is high, decay may have begun by the time you recover them in the morning. In summer, shorten the recovery interval or increase the traps that keep fish alive.
- If you cannot recover gear you set because you don't remember where it was, that gear becomes a device that keeps killing fish nobody will eat.
