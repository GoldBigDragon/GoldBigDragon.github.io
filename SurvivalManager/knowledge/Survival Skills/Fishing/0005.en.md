## 4. Bait, Handling After the Catch, and Food Safety
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Catching is half of fishing, and the other half is keeping what you caught in a state where it can be eaten safely. Fish spoil much faster than land animals.

---
### 4-1. Bait
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Bait gives fish two signals: smell and movement. Smell dissolves in water and diffuses to reach a wide range but carries weak directional information, and movement is visible only over a narrow range but induces immediate attack. Which signal works depends on the species and the situation.

| Bait | Signal that works | Suitable situation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Earthworm | Both smell and movement | The basic bait that works on almost all freshwater fish |
| Pupae, insect larvae | Strong smell | Turbid water, night, for traps |
| Grasshoppers, crickets | Movement on the surface | Species targeting the surface layer in summer |
| Fish entrails, gills | Very strong smell | Suited to traps and longlines. Also draws crabs and shrimp |
| Finely chopped fish flesh | Smell and sight | Carnivorous species in general |
| Clam, freshwater snail meat | Smell | Effective when the water is clear |
| Grain dough, boiled grain | Smell | Omnivorous species such as carp and crucian carp |
| Shiny pieces, feathers | Sight and movement | Only when the water is clear, there is light, and the fish are active |

- Artificial bait needs all three conditions. The fish must be active looking for food, the water must be clear enough for sight to work, and a person must be able to give the bait movement. If any one is missing, it barely works.
- With passive gear, a person cannot give movement, so strong-smelling bait is advantageous.
- Do not discard the entrails of fish you catch; recycle them as bait for the next trap.

---
### 4-2. Why Fish Spoil Quickly
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The muscle of a freshly caught fish is, in principle, sterile. Bacteria are on the skin, gills, and digestive tract, and not inside the flesh.

| Part | Bacterial density (approx.) | Meaning for handling |
| --- | :---: | --- |
| Flesh (muscle) | Nearly sterile | Contamination comes in from outside |
| Skin mucus | 10²-10⁷ cfu/cm² | Washing off the mucus greatly lowers density |
| Gills | 10³-10⁹ cfu/g | Blood is concentrated there, so bacterial growth is the fastest |
| Digestive tract | 10³-10⁹ cfu/g | Digestive enzymes are there too and break down the tissue |

- Why the viscera must be removed quickly: When a fish dies, digestive enzymes begin to digest not food but the fish itself, that is, the wall of the abdominal cavity. Once the digestive tract is breached, the bacteria inside go straight into the formerly sterile flesh, and decay proceeds simultaneously from the inside. That fish caught with a full stomach soften especially fast is also because of the abundant digestive enzymes.
- Why bleeding improves keeping quality: Blood is a nutrient-rich medium for bacteria, and the iron in hemoglobin is a catalyst that promotes fat oxidation. A fish that thrashed when dying accumulates lactic acid in the muscle, so pH falls further and the tissue softens. If you cut behind the gills as soon as it's caught and drain the blood while the heart is beating, the blood remaining in the flesh decreases, and both oxidation and bacterial growth slow. Once the heart stops, blood does not drain well, so it's a race against time.
- Draining the blood and removing the gills and viscera immediately upon catching is the greatest measure that can protect fish without refrigeration. If you don't, no other preservation treatment can reverse the internal decay that has already begun.
- Rigor mortis (FAO data): Cod stressed at 0 °C begin rigor in 2-8 hours and it resolves in 20-65 hours. Unstressed individuals begin after 14-15 hours and it resolves in 72-96 hours. The later rigor starts and the longer it lasts, the longer the flesh stays firm and the harder it is for bacteria to penetrate. This is why killing fish quickly without tormenting them for long benefits preservation.

### 4-3. Parasites of Freshwater Fish
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Eating freshwater fish raw is one of the few absolute taboos to avoid even in a disaster. The liver fluke uses freshwater shellfish as its first intermediate host and cyprinid freshwater fish as its second, and people are infected when they eat undercooked freshwater fish. According to the Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency, after infection there is an incubation period of about 3-4 weeks, and most cases are asymptomatic, but long-term infection is a risk factor for bile duct cancer. In a 2012 survey, the infection rate in Korea was 1.9% of the whole population.

| Parasite | Route of infection | Characteristics |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Liver fluke | Eating cyprinid freshwater fish raw | Persists for years without symptoms and raises the risk of bile duct cancer |
| Gnathostoma | Eating freshwater fish, amphibians, or reptiles raw | Larvae migrate under the skin and through organs and may invade the eyes and central nervous system |
| Yokogawa fluke | Eating ayu or dace raw | Diarrhea and abdominal pain |
| Broad tapeworm | Eating salmonid fish raw | Vitamin B12 deficiency anemia |
| Anisakis (whale worm) | Eating marine fish raw | Burrows into the stomach wall and causes acute abdominal pain |

- All are killed by sufficient heating. If the core of the flesh is 63 °C or higher it is safe, and in practice the criterion is that the flesh has turned white and opaque and separates easily from the bone.
- Salting, or soaking in vinegar or alcohol, cannot be relied on. Freezing is effective but hard to arrange in a disaster.
- Indirect infection via cooking tools is also possible. Pour boiling water over knives and cutting boards that handled raw fish.

### 4-4. Poisonous Fish
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Poisonous fish divide into those dangerous to eat and those dangerous to touch, and the responses are completely different.

| Subject | Nature of the poison | What to know |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Pufferfish | Tetrodotoxin. Concentrated in the ovaries, liver, viscera, and skin | Not destroyed even by heating at 120 °C for one hour, and the poison is highest in spring and summer, the spawning season |
| Devil stinger, redfin velvetfish, striped eel catfish | Protein venom in the spines of the dorsal and pectoral fins | Extreme pain if stabbed. The spines of dead individuals are dangerous too |
| Some moray eels, large tropical fish | Ciguatera toxin | Accumulates through the tropical coral reef food chain and is not destroyed by heating |
| Freshwater stingrays | Venom of the tail spine | The physical wound is large and the infection risk high |
| Blood of conger eels and moray eels | Ichthyotoxin | Destroyed by heating, so it's enough to avoid eating it raw |

> **Caution:** Tetrodotoxin cannot be removed by heating, has no antidote, and paralyzes muscles so that the respiratory muscles stop. According to Food Safety Korea, its toxicity is about 10 times that of potassium cyanide or more, progressing from vomiting and headache to speech disorder and muscle paralysis and, in severe cases, death within hours. To eat pufferfish safely you must identify the species exactly and remove all toxic parts, and even in ordinary times only qualified people do this. Attempting to eat pufferfish in a disaster is a choice to take on a risk greater than hunger.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Most venom from spines is protein and weak to heat. Soaking the wound in hot water as hot as you can bear reduces the pain. It only relieves pain and does not remove the risk of infection.

---
### 4-5. Principles for Unfamiliar Fish
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;When you are not sure of identification, there are morphological tendencies to consult. They are tendencies, not rules, and there are many exceptions.

| What to observe | Features that call for caution |
| --- | --- |
| Body shape | A body that inflates like a ball, or a belly that is unusually swollen |
| Skin | No scales, and thick leathery skin |
| Fins | Fins with prominent sharp, hard spines |
| Teeth | Teeth fused like a parrot's beak |
| Gill openings | Reduced to very small holes |
| Condition | Individuals found floating dead, individuals that smell |

- The very idea that you can judge the presence of poison from form is dangerous. Ordinary-looking species can have poison, and fearsome-looking species can be safe.
- The rule: eat only species whose name you know and are sure are safe, and if you are not sure, don't eat. Hunger can be endured for days, but neurotoxins take a life within hours. Use an individual you're not sure of as bait for the next trap instead of eating it.
- Be especially careful with the viscera. Most fish toxins accumulate in the liver, ovaries, and digestive tract and are far lower in the muscle. For a fish whose species you are not sure of, discard all the viscera, heat only the flesh thoroughly, and wash the hands and knife that touched the viscera.
