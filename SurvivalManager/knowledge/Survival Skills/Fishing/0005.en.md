## 4. Bait, Handling the Catch, and Food Safety
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Catching the fish is only half of fishing; the other half is keeping what you caught in a state safe to eat. Fish spoil far faster than land animals, and once you understand why, it becomes clear why certain steps of handling must be done in a hurry.

---
### 4-1. Conditions Under Which Bait Works
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Bait sends a fish two kinds of signal: smell and movement. Smell dissolves and spreads through the water, so it reaches a wide range but carries weak directional information; movement is visible only at close range but triggers an immediate strike. Which signal works depends on the species and the situation.

| Bait | Signal It Sends | Suitable Situation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Earthworm | Both smell and movement | A basic bait that works on almost every freshwater fish |
| Pupae, insect larvae | Strong smell | Suited to murky water, nighttime, and trap use |
| Grasshoppers, crickets | Movement on the surface | Effective for species targeting the surface layer in summer |
| Fish guts, gills | Very strong smell | Suited to traps and longlines, and also draws crabs and shrimp |
| Finely chopped fish flesh | Smell and sight | Works broadly on carnivorous species |
| Clam or freshwater snail flesh | Smell | Effective when the water is clear |
| Grain dough, boiled grain | Smell | Works on omnivorous species such as carp and crucian carp |
| Shiny fragments, feathers | Sight and movement | Works only when the water is clear, light is present, and fish are active |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The conditions under which artificial lures work are narrow. The fish must be actively searching for food, the water must be clear enough for sight to function, and a person must be able to give the lure movement. If even one of these three conditions is missing, an artificial lure barely works at all. With passive gear, a person cannot impart movement, so strong-smelling bait is fundamentally the better choice. Rather than throwing away the guts of a caught fish, the most efficient approach is to cycle them back in as bait for the next trap.

---
### 4-2. Why Fish Spoil Quickly
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The muscle of a freshly caught fish is, in principle, sterile. Bacteria live on the skin, in the gills, and in the digestive tract, but have not entered the flesh. According to FAO data, the bacterial count on the skin runs from 10^2 to 10^7 per 1cm², while inside the gills and intestine it reaches 10^3 to 10^9 per 1g. In other words, bacteria are concentrated in three places.

| Location | Bacterial Density (approx.) | Significance for Handling |
| --- | :---: | --- |
| Flesh (muscle) | Nearly sterile | Contamination comes in from outside |
| Skin mucus | 10^2-10^7 cfu/cm² | Washing off the mucus greatly lowers the density |
| Gills | 10^3-10^9 cfu/g | Blood pools here, so bacterial growth is fastest |
| Digestive tract | 10^3-10^9 cfu/g | Digestive enzymes are present too, breaking down tissue |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This is where the reason for removing the guts quickly comes from. Once a fish dies, its digestive enzymes stop digesting food and start digesting the fish itself — the wall of the abdominal cavity. Once the digestive tract is breached, the bacteria inside it move straight into the flesh that had been sterile, and from that point on, spoilage proceeds simultaneously from the inside as well as the surface. A fish caught with a full stomach turns soft unusually fast for the same reason: it is carrying a larger quantity of digestive enzymes.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The reason bleeding a fish improves its keeping quality is a little different. Blood is a nutrient-rich medium for bacteria, and the iron in hemoglobin acts as a catalyst that speeds up fat oxidation. Also, a fish that thrashed while dying builds up lactic acid in its muscle, which drops the pH further and softens the tissue. If you cut behind the gills right after catching it and bleed the fish while the heart is still beating, the amount of blood left in the flesh is reduced, slowing both oxidation and bacterial growth. Once the heart stops, blood no longer drains well, so this step is a race against time.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In summary, bleeding the fish and removing the gills and guts immediately after catching it are the single biggest steps you can take to protect it without refrigeration. Skip these two steps, and no other preservation treatment can reverse the internal spoilage that has already begun.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Rigor mortis is also worth knowing about. According to FAO data, cod that were stressed at 0°C begin rigor within 2-8 hours and resolve it at 20-65 hours, while unstressed individuals begin at 14-15 hours and resolve at 72-96 hours. The later rigor sets in and the longer it lasts, the longer the flesh stays firm, and the harder it is for bacteria to invade. This is why killing a fish quickly, without prolonged struggle, works in favor of keeping it.

### 4-3. Parasites in Freshwater Fish
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Eating freshwater fish raw is one of the few absolute taboos to avoid even in a disaster situation. The liver fluke uses freshwater snails as its first intermediate host and cyprinid (carp-family) freshwater fish as its second intermediate host, and infects a person who eats undercooked freshwater fish. According to KDCA data, it has an incubation period of roughly 3-4 weeks after infection and is mostly asymptomatic, but long-term infection is a risk factor for bile-duct cancer. A 2012 survey found the infection rate in South Korea to be 1.9% of the total population.

| Parasite | Route of Infection | Characteristics |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Liver fluke | Eating raw cyprinid freshwater fish | Persists asymptomatically for years and raises the risk of bile-duct cancer |
| Gnathostoma | Eating raw freshwater fish, amphibians, reptiles | Larvae migrate under the skin and through organs, and can invade the eyes and central nervous system |
| Metagonimus (Yokogawa's fluke) | Eating raw sweetfish, dace | Causes diarrhea and abdominal pain |
| Broad tapeworm | Eating raw salmonid fish | Causes vitamin B12 deficiency anemia |
| Anisakis | Eating raw marine fish | Burrows into the stomach wall, causing acute abdominal pain |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;What these parasites have in common is that thorough heating kills all of them. It is safe once the center of the flesh reaches 63°C or above; in practice, the standard is that the flesh has turned white and opaque and separates easily from the bone. Salting, soaking in vinegar, or soaking in alcohol cannot be relied on. Freezing works, but it is a condition hard to secure in a disaster situation. Indirect infection through cooking tools and cutting boards is also possible, so a knife and cutting board that have handled raw fish must be treated by pouring boiling water over them.

### 4-4. Fish That Carry Poison
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Poisonous fish fall into two groups: those dangerous to eat, and those dangerous to touch. The two dangers call for completely different responses.

| Species | Nature of the Toxin | What to Know |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Pufferfish | Tetrodotoxin, concentrated in the ovaries, liver, other organs, and skin | Not destroyed even by heating at 120°C for 1 hour; toxin is highest during the spring and summer spawning season |
| Devil stinger, spiny devilfish, striped eel catfish | Protein toxin in the spines of the dorsal and pectoral fins | Being stung causes severe pain, and the spines remain dangerous even on a dead individual |
| Some moray eels, large tropical fish | Ciguatera toxin | Accumulates through the tropical coral-reef food chain and is not destroyed by heating |
| Freshwater stingray (a wound like a viper bite) | Venom in the tail spine | The physical wound is large, and infection risk is high |
| Blood of conger eels, moray eels | Ichthyotoxin | Destroyed by heating, so it is enough to avoid eating it raw |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Tetrodotoxin is especially dangerous because it cannot be removed by heating, has no antidote, and paralyzes muscle to the point of stopping the muscles used for breathing. According to Food Safety Korea data, its toxicity is roughly 10 times or more that of potassium cyanide, and symptoms progress from vomiting and headache to speech impairment and muscle paralysis, reaching death within a few hours in severe cases. Eating pufferfish safely requires identifying the species precisely and removing every part that carries the toxin, and even in ordinary times this is work only a qualified person does. Trying to eat pufferfish in a disaster situation is a choice that risks something greater than hunger.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Venom delivered by spines is mostly protein-based and breaks down with heat. This is why soaking the wound in water as hot as you can tolerate reduces the pain after being stung. This only relieves pain, though — it does not remove the wound's risk of infection.

### 4-5. The Principle for Handling an Unknown Fish
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;When you are not sure of an identification, there are morphological tendencies you can refer to. These are tendencies, though, not rules, and there are many exceptions.

| What to Observe | Feature That Calls for Caution |
| --- | --- |
| Body shape | Body that puffs up like a ball, or an unusually swollen belly |
| Skin | No scales, thick leathery skin |
| Fins | Fins with prominent sharp, hard spines |
| Teeth | Teeth fused together like a parrot's beak |
| Gill openings | Gill openings reduced to very small holes |
| Condition | An individual found already dead and floating, or one that smells off |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;But the very idea that you can judge whether a fish is poisonous from its shape is itself dangerous. In reality, ordinary-looking species can be poisonous, and fearsome-looking species can be safe. The principle to hold to in a disaster situation is simple: eat only species you can name and are certain are safe, and if you are not certain, do not eat it. Starvation can be endured for days, but a nerve toxin can take a life within hours. For a fish you are not certain about, it is more rational to use it as bait for the next trap instead of eating it.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The internal organs need particular caution. Most fish toxins accumulate in the liver, ovaries, and digestive tract, and are present in far smaller amounts in the muscle. For a fish you cannot identify with certainty, you must discard all of the organs without exception and thoroughly heat only the flesh, and you must also wash the hands and knife that touched those organs.
