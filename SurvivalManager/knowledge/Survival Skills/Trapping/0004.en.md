## 3. Why Traps Fail, Operation, and Safety
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Nearly all the first traps you set are empty. This is not because the structure is wrong but because animals avoid the spot or never come there in the first place. The causes of failure can be sorted into a few, and most can be avoided once you know them.

---
### 3-1. Causes of Failure
| Cause | How it works | Response |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Human scent | Mammals learn human scent as a predator signal | Rub hands with soil and ash and keep setting time short |
| Smell and shine of metal | The oxidation smell of iron and reflected light are signals not found in nature | Singe the wire in a fire or coat it with mud |
| Smell of fresh wood | The sap smell of freshly cut green wood is very strong | Use dry wood or cover the cut face with soil |
| Set off the passage | A spot that is easy to see is a spot animals do not pass | Set only at bottlenecks with clear sign |
| Poor trigger sensitivity | If too stiff it does not work, and if too sensitive it collapses in the wind | Adjust it to release when pushed lightly with a finger |
| Disturbed surroundings | Animals are wary of spots where grass is broken and soil dug up | Cover trampled spots with leaves and stand broken grass back up |
| Loop sagging | If a snare is laid down, the animal just steps over it | Hold the loop up with a thin twig or grass stem |
| Insufficient falling weight | Only injures and loses the animal | Increase it to at least 5 times body weight |

- Human scent is the most often blamed, but in fact it is the second factor. The first is always location. Even if scent remains, a bottleneck that animals must pass catches within days, and if the location is wrong the trap stays empty forever even when the scent is completely gone. The time spent reading sign changes the catch far more than the time spent erasing scent.
- Trigger sensitivity is hard to quantify. The standard for a deadfall trigger is that it collapses when the bait stick is pushed lightly sideways with a finger but withstands wind and small vibrations. If you cut the contacts deep the structure is stable but does not release when the animal touches it, and if you trim them shallow it is sensitive but collapses by itself. The balance point differs with material and weight, so set it up several times and test it by hand.

---
### 3-2. Setting Density and Checking Interval
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Place traps along a patrol route. If scattered, the calories spent checking exceed the catch, so decide on one loop-shaped route and place them along it. The route length does not exceed the distance you can cover in a round trip once a day.

| Item | Standard | Reason |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Number of traps | At least 10, preferably 20 or more | The probability of a catch rises quickly with number |
| Trap spacing | At every bottleneck in the passage, generally 20-50 m | If too dense, one animal wrecks several traps |
| Patrol route | A loop that can be covered in a round trip each day | The cost of checking must not exceed the catch |
| Checking interval | At least once a day, in the morning | Recovers animals caught at night the soonest |
| Judgment to move | Move if there has been no sign for three days or more | A spot with no sign stays empty however long you set |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The reason for checking at least once a day is not only efficiency.

- Ethics: An animal caught in a snare or a poor deadfall may not die instantly and is in pain until the person comes. The person who sets traps bears the responsibility to minimize that pain.
- Decay: An animal that has been dead overnight in summer has begun to decay from the viscera by morning, and if left until midday it cannot be eaten.
- Theft: A caught animal is the easiest food for other predators too, so if you are late by even half a day, nothing is left.

---
### 3-3. Safety
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Traps cannot tell people from animals. A deadfall with a stone heavier than five times body weight, a large snare that chokes the neck, and a pit with sharpened stakes all work just the same on people. When starving and tired you cannot even remember exactly where the traps you set are, so always record the setting locations or leave conspicuous markers.

| Risk | Content |
| --- | --- |
| Caught in your own trap | Large traps cause fractures and lacerations in people |
| Someone else caught | People and children staying with you may pass by |
| Forgetting the location | If you rely on memory alone, you forget within days |
| Leaving without recovering | Traps keep killing animals even after the person is gone |
| Approaching an injured animal | A cornered animal is far more aggressive than usual |
| Infection | Bite and scratch wounds are hard to treat in a disaster |

- A marker must be something animals are not wary of but that catches the human eye. Piling stones on a tree a little way off the passage or bending a branch in a particular direction is enough. If you also leave a map drawn on paper, other people can recover the traps too.
- When leaving the place, take down all the traps. Traps left unrecovered keep killing animals nobody will eat and are dangerous to people who pass later. For snares, undo the loop; for deadfalls, lower the falling object; for pits, fill them or at least remove the cover so they are visible.

> **Caution:** Approaching an injured animal is the most dangerous moment in running traps. An animal that cannot escape will certainly resist, and not only water deer and wild boar but also raccoon dogs and badgers can inflict serious wounds. Even a small animal's bite carries a risk of rabies. Before approaching, check its reaction with a long pole, and if it is alive, keep your distance and dispatch it immediately with a long tool. Do not try to grab it by hand.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;An animal that crouches without raising its head and responds slowly, a nocturnal animal wandering in the daytime, and an animal that does not run even when it sees people are highly likely to be sick. Do not catch it; back away.
