## 3. Why Traps Fail, How to Run a Trapline, and Safety
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Nearly every trap set by a first-time trapper comes up empty. It is not because the structure is wrong, but because the animal avoids that spot, or never came near it in the first place. The causes of failure come down to a handful of things, and most of them are avoidable once you know them.

---
### 3-1. Causes of Failure
| Cause | How it works | Countermeasure |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Human scent | Mammals learn to treat human scent as a predator signal | Rub your hands with soil and ash, and keep setup time short |
| The smell and glint of metal | The smell of oxidizing iron and its reflected light are signals nature does not produce | Scorch the wire over a fire or coat it with mud |
| The smell of fresh-cut wood | The sap smell of a freshly cut green branch is very strong | Use dry wood, or cover the cut face with soil |
| Setting off the travel route | A spot that is easy for a person to see is a spot animals do not pass through | Set traps only at bottlenecks with clear sign |
| Poorly tuned trigger sensitivity | Too stiff and it never fires; too sensitive and it collapses in the wind | Tune it to release with a light push of a finger |
| A disturbed surroundings | Animals grow wary of a spot where grass is broken and soil is dug up | Cover trodden ground with dead leaves and stand broken grass back up |
| A collapsed loop | A snare lying flat just gets stepped over | Prop the loop upright with a thin twig or blade of grass |
| Insufficient falling weight | Only injures the animal, which then escapes | Increase it to at least 5 times body weight |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Human scent is the factor most often blamed, but it is actually only the second most important one. The first is always location. Even with scent still lingering, a bottleneck the animal has to pass through will produce a catch within days; but with the wrong location, the trap stays empty forever even if the scent vanishes completely. Spending time reading sign changes your yield far more than spending time trying to erase your scent.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Trigger sensitivity is the part that resists being quantified. The standard for a deadfall trigger is that it should collapse when you nudge the bait stick sideways with a finger, yet hold steady against wind or small vibrations. Cut the joints deep and the structure stays stable but will not release even when an animal disturbs it; cut them shallow and it becomes sensitive but collapses on its own. This balance point depends on the material and the weight involved, so you have to actually set it up several times and test it by hand.

---
### 3-2. Setup Density and Check Cycle
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Traps are placed along a single checking route. Scatter them and the calories spent checking on them exceed what they return, so the rule is to lay out one loop-shaped route and place traps along it. The length of that route must never exceed a distance you can walk out and back in one day.

| Item | Standard | Reason |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Number of traps | At least 10, ideally 20 or more | The probability of a catch rises quickly with the number of traps |
| Spacing between traps | At every bottleneck on the route, typically 20-50m | Too close together, and a single animal can wreck several traps at once |
| Checking route | A loop you can walk out and back in a day | The cost of checking must never exceed the yield |
| Check frequency | At least once a day, in the morning | Recovers an animal caught overnight as quickly as possible |
| Deciding when to relocate | Move it if there is no sign at all for 3 days or more | A spot with no sign stays empty no matter how long you leave a trap there |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The reason to check at least once a day is not efficiency. The first reason is ethics. An animal caught in a snare or a poorly built deadfall sometimes does not die instantly, and it stays in pain until the person arrives. Setting a trap means also taking on the responsibility to minimize that suffering. The second reason is spoilage. An animal that died overnight in summer already has decomposition starting in its gut by morning, and by midday it is no longer fit to eat. The third reason is theft. A caught animal is also the easiest possible meal for other predators, so being even half a day late can mean nothing is left at all.

---
### 3-3. Safety
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;A trap cannot tell a person from an animal. A deadfall that drops a rock more than five times a person's body weight, a large snare that tightens around the neck, a pit lined with sharpened stakes — all of these work exactly the same way on a person. Especially when hungry and exhausted, you can lose track of exactly where you set your own traps, so every location must be recorded or marked with a clearly visible sign.

| Danger | Details |
| --- | --- |
| Getting caught in your own trap | A large trap can cause fractures and lacerations in a person |
| Someone else getting caught | Others in your group, including children, may pass through the area |
| Forgetting the location | Relying on memory alone, you will forget it within a few days |
| Leaving without retrieving it | A trap keeps killing animals even after the person is gone |
| Approaching an injured animal | A cornered animal is far more aggressive than usual |
| Infection | A bite or scratch wound is hard to treat in a disaster situation |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;A marker needs to be visible to a person without putting the animal on guard. Stacking stones against a tree a short distance from the route, or bending a branch to point in a particular direction, is enough. Keeping a hand-drawn map on paper alongside it lets someone else retrieve the traps too.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;When you leave the area, every trap must be taken apart without exception. An unretrieved trap becomes a device that keeps killing animals no one will ever eat, and it is also a hazard to anyone who passes through later. Undo the loop on a snare, lower the falling weight of a deadfall, and either fill in a pit or at least remove its cover so it is visible.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Approaching an injured animal is the most dangerous moment in running a trapline. An animal that cannot flee will always fight back, and raccoon dogs and badgers, not to mention water deer and wild boar, can inflict serious injuries on a person. Even a small animal carries a risk of rabies if it bites. Before approaching, first check its response with a long stick, and if it is still alive, finish it immediately with a long tool while keeping your distance. Never attempt to grab it with your bare hands, under any circumstances. In particular, an animal that cannot lift its head and is hunched over with slow reactions, a nocturnal animal that is out in daylight, or an animal that does not flee at the sight of a person is more likely to be diseased — do not catch it, and back away instead.
