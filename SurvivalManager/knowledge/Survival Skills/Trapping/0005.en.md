## 4. Hunting Tools and Diseases from Wild Animals
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There are also situations traps alone cannot handle. In open ground with no travel routes, at waterside spots where birds gather, or in an environment with no material to build traps from, you need tools that throw or shoot. Every hunting tool ultimately comes down to the same question: how do you store the energy a person's muscles produce, and how small an area do you concentrate it onto.

---
### 4-1. Hunting Tools by How They Deliver Energy
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The human arm is short and slow. The speed of a stone thrown by hand is set by the length of the arm and how fast it rotates, and it cannot go any higher than that. Every projectile weapon uses one of two methods to get past this limit: making the lever longer, or storing energy in advance and releasing it all at once.

| Tool | How it increases energy | Characteristics |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Throwing stick | Rotational inertia and a wide striking surface | Easiest of all to make; effective against ground birds and small animals |
| Sling | Extends the radius of rotation by the length of the cord | Powerful, but takes a very long time to become skilled with |
| Throwing spear | Uses the whole arm as a lever | Short range, and easy to retrieve |
| Spear-thrower (atlatl) | Artificially extends the length of the arm | Raises the spear's speed substantially for the same effort |
| Bow | Stores the force applied while drawing the string in the bow stave | Releases the stored energy all at once |
| Trap | Does not use human effort at all | Not comparable to the tools above |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The spear-thrower increases power by extending the effective length of the arm. A short stick with a hook that catches the butt of the spear extends out from the wrist, and the tangential speed at the moment the spear is released grows in proportion to that radius of rotation. If a person with a 70cm arm uses a 50cm spear-thrower, the effective radius becomes 1.7 times longer, and at the same angular velocity, the spear's speed rises by that same factor. Since kinetic energy is proportional to the square of speed, the energy becomes roughly 3 times greater.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The bow works on a different principle. The work a person does while drawing the string is stored as elastic energy in the bow stave, and the instant the string is released, that energy transfers to the arrow. The amount of work stored is close to force multiplied by distance — the area under the curve of draw force times draw length. So for the same draw weight, a bow with a longer draw length stores more energy, and for the same draw length, a bow with a heavier draw weight is stronger. This is why bows are made large. A short bow, drawn the same distance, bends its stave too sharply and breaks, so the draw length cannot be increased.

| Element | Principle | Cause of failure |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Bow stave material | Must withstand both compression and tension | Dry, dead wood splits on the tension side |
| Bow stave thickness | Tapering thinner toward both ends | A uniform thickness bends only in the middle and breaks there |
| Draw length | The longer, the more energy stored | Exceeding half the bow's length is dangerous |
| Bowstring | Must not stretch | A string that stretches absorbs the energy itself |
| Straightness of the arrow shaft | A bent shaft throws off the direction | Straighten a bent branch with heat before using it |
| Arrow weight | Heavier gives more penetration, lighter gives more speed | Too light, and it can break the bow |
| Fletching | Stabilizes flight through spin and drag | Without fletching, the arrow tumbles sideways in flight |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Bow material should be highly elastic and straight-grained, such as ash, mulberry, oak, or bamboo. The best form keeps the outer layer of the wood as the back of the bow. The outer fibers of a tree resist tension well and the inner fibers resist compression well, so this simply matches the bow's force distribution to the structure the tree already grew with. Reverse this orientation, and the bow will almost certainly break.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;But the bow is one of the hardest tools to make in a disaster. Drying and shaping the stave takes several weeks, you also need a string, arrows, and fletching, and hitting anything reliably takes a long time to practice. It is not something a person who is starving right now should take on first. The realistic order is to build a basic supply of food with traps first, and start on the bow as a long-term investment only once you have some margin to spare.

### 4-2. Diseases That Pass from Wild Animals to People
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Handling wild animals carries a risk that is far more common and far quieter than a gunshot wound or a bite: zoonotic disease. These diseases matter not simply because they are dangerous, but because their route of infection directly dictates how the animal must be cooked and handled.

| Disease | Main carrier | Route of infection | Constraint on handling |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Rabies | Raccoon dog, badger, bat, canids | Bites; saliva through a wound | Do not catch or touch an animal that is behaving strangely |
| Tularemia | Hares, rodents, ticks | Skin wounds, inhalation, undercooked meat | Never skin it with bare hands, and cook it completely |
| Brucellosis | Wild boar, deer | Contact with blood and body fluids; eating raw | Keep body fluids from splashing into your eyes or mouth while butchering |
| Leptospirosis | Rats and other rodents, contaminated water | Contact with urine through a wound or mucous membrane | Do not go into water if you have an open wound |
| Toxoplasmosis | Felids, meat | Cysts in undercooked meat | Destroyed by thorough cooking |
| Hantavirus | Striped field mouse (Apodemus agrarius) and other rodents | Inhaling dried droppings | Never sweep out a burrow or nest while it is dry |
| Trichinosis | Wild boar, bear | Meat containing larvae in the muscle | Must be cooked all the way through to the center |
| Avian influenza | Wild birds | Contact with droppings, body fluids | Do not touch a bird found already dead |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Three rules repeat throughout this list. First, body fluids and blood enter through skin wounds and mucous membranes, so if you have a wound on your hand, either do not butcher the animal or cover the wound with something first. Second, most pathogens are killed by sufficient heat, so wild meat must be cooked completely without exception, and cooking that only browns the surface has no effect at all on larvae inside the muscle. Third, since inhaling dry droppings is itself a route of infection, never sweep out or dust off an animal's burrow or nest while it is dry — wet it down to settle the dust first, and only then clear it out.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Do not catch an animal that is behaving differently than usual. A nocturnal animal out and about in daylight, one that does not flee at the sight of a person, or one that staggers or drools is easy to catch precisely because it is more likely to be sick. Even among animals already caught in a trap, discard any that are unusually thin with patchy fur, that have white spots on the liver or spleen, or that have pus and a foul smell in the abdominal cavity. What you stand to lose from an animal like this is far greater than the calories you would gain from eating it.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;These rules boil down to a single sentence: cook wild meat all the way through to the center without exception, and discard any animal with strange behavior or diseased-looking organs no matter how hungry you are.

### 4-3. Law and Ethics
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;What this document sets out is not knowledge to be put into practice as-is under ordinary conditions. In South Korea, only a person who has obtained a hunting license and received hunting approval at a designated hunting ground under South Korea's Wildlife Protection and Management Act may take the designated species during the designated season. Hunting licenses are split into Class 1, for firearms, and Class 2, for other tools, and obtaining either requires passing an exam, taking training, and a physical examination. In particular, setting snares, traps, and pit traps is prohibited in principle, since they catch whatever species happens by, including endangered ones, and unlicensed hunting is punishable by up to 2 years in prison or a fine of up to 20 million won. Most other countries have regulations to the same effect. This document sets out knowledge that assumes a survival situation in which public institutions are no longer functioning; under ordinary conditions, you must check your own country's hunting laws and wildlife protection laws and act only within them.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Even in a survival situation, a few principles still hold: catch only what you need, never waste what you catch, and take up every trap before you leave. Clear an area of all its animals, and it becomes hard to get through the next season there, and the longer a disaster lasts, the more that loss comes back around to the person who caused it. How to use a caught animal down to nothing continues in the slaughtering and butchering document.
