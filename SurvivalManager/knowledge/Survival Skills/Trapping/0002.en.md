## 1. Comparing Trapping and Hunting, Reading Sign
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Half of setting traps is the skill of making them, and the other half is the judgment of where to set them. Even a well-made trap stays empty forever if placed in the wrong spot, and even a crude trap catches if it is where animals must pass. Knowledge of reading sign comes before the skill of making traps.

---
### 1-1. Trapping and Hunting by Calorie Balance
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The values below vary greatly with the situation, but they are enough to show the difference in order of magnitude.

| Item | Hunting by pursuit | Running ten traps |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Daily calories burned | 2,000-3,000 kcal | 300-600 kcal (setting and checking) |
| Initial input | Making tools and building skill | Half a day to a day to make the traps |
| Operating time | Only while the person is awake and active | 24 hours a day |
| Risk of injury | High | Low |
| Loss on failure | The entire calories spent | Only time is lost, the traps remain |
| Stability of the return | Very unstable | More stable the more you set |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The real value of traps lies in the repetition of independent trials. When one trap's daily success rate is 5%, the probability of getting at least one animal in a day is about 40% for 10 traps, about 64% for 20, and about 79% for 30 (1 - 0.95 to the power n). Increasing the number raises the probability of a catch faster than raising the quality of the traps. Traps produce success rate not by performance but by number. In the time it takes to make one perfect trap, it is better to set five crude ones.

---
### 1-2. The Principle of Finding Passages
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Wild animals also try to save energy, so they use a route repeatedly once they have taken it. Repeated passage presses down the vegetation to make a path, and that path induces the next passage. Finding these passages is the starting point of setting traps.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;An animal's daily movement mostly takes place on a triangle linking its bed, water, and feeding ground. Passages are also concentrated between these three points, and the paths down to water are shared by many individuals and many species, so the sign is clearest there.

| Sign | Information read from it |
| --- | --- |
| Tracks | Species, size, direction of travel, number of animals. Best preserved on mud, sand, and snow |
| Droppings | Species and type of food. Moist means recent, dry and cracked means old |
| Feeding sign | Gnawed bark, dug-up soil, and the height of torn grass give an idea of the size of the species |
| Pressed grass and passages | Evidence of repeated passage. The narrower and clearer, the higher the frequency of use |
| Hair and rub marks | Hair is left on branches or barbed wire where a passage narrows |
| Beds | Spots where the grass is pressed into a hollow. There is always an entrance and an escape route nearby |
| Claw marks, antler marks | Marks left on tree trunks that indicate the presence of large individuals |

- The place to set a trap is, among passages, a bottleneck. A bottleneck is a place where the routes an animal can choose are reduced to one. Examples are a spot where an animal must pass under a fallen tree, a gap between rocks, a point where a fence or thicket breaks, a shallow ford where it crosses a stream, and a point where two passages merge. At a bottleneck the uncertainty about where the animal passes is small, and the success rate is several times higher than when you set in the middle of a passage.
- If there is no bottleneck, make one. If you build a fence by sticking branches in at an angle on both sides of a passage, the animal feels it is easier to go around than to jump over, and passes through the open gap (a funnel fence). The fence need not be high enough that the animal cannot jump it. It lets a few traps cover a wide passage.

### 1-3. Traps by Animal Size
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;A trap must suit the size of the target animal. A trap that is too small only injures the animal and loses it, and a trap that is too large does not work because a small animal cannot trip the trigger.

| Target | Approximate weight | Suitable trap | Cautions |
| --- | :---: | --- | --- |
| Voles, moles | 0.03-0.2 kg | Small deadfall, bottle pit trap | Small return, but the highest success rate |
| Chipmunks, squirrels | 0.2-0.4 kg | Snare ladder set on a tree trunk | Uses their habit of climbing trees |
| Rabbits and hares | 1-2.5 kg | Neck snare, spring snare, medium deadfall | Clear passages make it the easiest target to handle |
| Pheasants, ducks, and other birds | 0.8-1.5 kg | Bird net, bait trap, foot snare | Must block the direction they fly up |
| Raccoon dogs, badgers | 5-10 kg | Large snare, box trap | They are strong, so the lashing breaks first |
| Water deer | 15-25 kg | Large neck snare | A size that can injure people too |
| Wild boar | 50-150 kg | Pit, large snare | Just approaching is extremely dangerous |

- The realistic targets in a disaster are small animals. A rabbit is about 1,000 kcal and a chipmunk a little over 200 kcal, but they are numerous, their passages are clear, and they do not break traps.
- If, because large animals give more calories, you pour resources only into large traps, you often cannot hold out until success. Set many small traps to create daily supply, and add large traps when you have the means.
- Eating only small animals causes protein-excess disorders from lack of body fat. See the rabbit starvation section of the Butchering document.
