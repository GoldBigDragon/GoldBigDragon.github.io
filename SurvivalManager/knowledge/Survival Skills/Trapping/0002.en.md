## 1. Why Trapping Comes Before Hunting, and How to Read Sign
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Half of setting a trap is the skill of building it, and the other half is the judgment of deciding where to put it. Set a well-built trap in the wrong spot and it stays empty forever; even a crude trap catches something if it sits where an animal has to pass. So the knowledge of reading sign comes before the skill of building traps.

---
### 1-1. Trapping vs. Hunting, by Calorie Balance
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Comparing hunting and trapping in numbers makes clear why trapping comes first. The figures below vary greatly by situation, but they are enough to show the difference in order of magnitude.

| Item | Pursuit hunting | Running ten traps |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Calories spent per day | 2,000~3,000kcal | 300~600kcal (setting and checking) |
| Initial investment | Making the tool and gaining skill with it | Half a day to a day to build the traps |
| Time spent working | Only while the person is awake and active | 24 hours a day |
| Risk of injury | High | Low |
| Loss on failure | The full amount of calories spent | Only time is lost; the trap remains |
| Reliability of return | Very unstable | Grows more stable the more you set |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The real value of a trap lies in repeating independent trials. If a single trap has a 5% daily success rate, then setting ten of them gives a probability of catching at least one animal that day equal to 1 minus 0.95 raised to the 10th power — about 40%. Twenty traps bring that to about 64%, thirty to about 79%. This means that increasing the number of traps raises your odds of a catch faster than improving the quality of any single trap, which is why people who trap for a living prefer many simple traps over a few elaborate ones.

> **Traps build their success rate through numbers, not performance. It is always better to set five crude traps in the time it would take to make one perfect one.**

---
### 1-2. The Principle of Finding Travel Routes
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Wild animals do not move around at random. Like people, animals try to conserve energy, so they reuse a route once they have traveled it. Repeated passage presses down the vegetation into a path, and that path in turn guides the next animal along it. Finding these formed travel routes is where setting a trap begins.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;An animal's daily movement usually takes place on a triangle connecting three points: its bedding site, water, and its feeding ground. So travel routes concentrate between these three points, and the trails leading down to water in particular are shared by many individuals and many species, which makes their sign the clearest of all.

| Sign | What it tells you |
| --- | --- |
| Tracks | Species, size, direction of travel, and how many animals. They show up best in mud, sand, and snow |
| Droppings | Species and diet. Moist droppings are recent; dry, cracked ones are old |
| Feeding sign | Gnawed bark, dug-up soil, and the height at which grass has been cropped indicate the size of the species |
| Flattened grass and trails | Evidence of repeated travel; the narrower and clearer it is, the more heavily it is used |
| Hair and rub marks | Hair catches on branches or wire fencing at points where the route narrows |
| Bedding site | A hollow where grass has been pressed down. An entry route and an escape route are always nearby |
| Claw marks, antler rubs | Marks left on tree trunks that signal the presence of a large animal |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The place to set a trap is a bottleneck within a travel route — a spot where the animal's choice of path narrows down to just one option. This includes a spot where the animal has to pass under a fallen tree, a gap between rocks, a break in a hedge or thicket, a shallow ford across a stream, or a point where two routes merge. At a bottleneck, the uncertainty about exactly where the animal will pass becomes small, so the very same trap succeeds several times more often there than if it were placed in the middle of an open trail.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;If a bottleneck does not occur naturally, you can build one. Plant branches at an angle along both sides of a route to make a fence, and the animal will tend to go through the open gap rather than bother climbing over. The fence does not need to be tall enough that the animal cannot get over it — it only needs to make going around feel easier than climbing over. This principle is called a drift fence, and it lets a small number of traps cover a wide travel route.

### 1-3. Animal Size and Matching the Trap
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;A trap has to match the size of its target animal. A trap that is too small only injures the animal and lets it escape; a trap that is too large never triggers, because a small animal cannot reach or move the trigger.

| Target | Approximate weight | Suitable trap | Caution |
| --- | :---: | --- | --- |
| Field mouse, mole | 0.03~0.2kg | Small deadfall, bottle trap | Yields little per catch, but has the highest success rate |
| Chipmunk, squirrel | 0.2~0.4kg | A snare ladder set up a tree trunk | Takes advantage of their tree-climbing habit |
| Hares | 1~2.5kg | Neck snare, spring snare, medium deadfall | Their trails are distinct, making them the easiest target to work with |
| Birds such as pheasant, duck | 0.8~1.5kg | Bird net, bait trap, foot snare | Must block the direction the bird would take off in |
| Raccoon dog, badger | 5~10kg | Large snare, box trap | Strong enough that the anchor point tends to break first |
| Water deer | 15~25kg | Large neck snare | Large enough to injure a person too |
| Wild boar | 50~150kg | Pit trap, large snare | Approaching it at all is very dangerous |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In a disaster, small animals are the realistic target. A single hare gives about 1,000kcal and a single chipmunk just over 200kcal, but they are abundant, their trails are distinct, and they cannot break a trap. Put all your resources into large traps because a single large animal offers more calories, and you often cannot hold out through the period it takes to succeed. The safer order is to set many small traps to build a daily supply, and add large traps only once you have some margin to spare.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There is, however, a limit to living on small animals alone. Small wild animals like hares and chipmunks carry very little body fat, and eating nothing but these continuously causes problems from excess protein. This issue is covered in detail in the slaughtering and butchering document.
