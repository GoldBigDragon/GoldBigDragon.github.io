## Introduction
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Hunting is the most commonly overrated activity for procuring food in a disaster. Picking up a spear or a bow and heading into the mountains feels like it should bring back meat, but what actually happens is usually the opposite. Wild animals notice a person's approach far sooner than the reverse, a person's speed on foot is slower than any game animal's, and the odds of an unskilled person landing an effective hit on a wild animal are extremely low. A single failed day of hunting burns more than 2,000kcal and returns nothing at all, and that loss comes back out of the next day's judgment and physical strength.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Trapping fundamentally changes this structure. Building a single trap costs only tens of kcal, and once it is set, it keeps working for 24 hours straight no matter what the person is doing. Set ten of them, and you are effectively hunting 240 hours' worth in a single day; even if each individual trap has only a 5% success rate, the probability that at least one of ten succeeds reaches 40%. Above all, because a trap means the person never confronts the animal directly, the risk of injury is close to zero. An injury sustained while already starving is dangerous in itself, so on the strength of not being exposed to that risk alone, trapping comes before hunting.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This document sets out the physical principles behind how traps and hunting tools work, why most traps end up empty, and how the diseases that pass from wild animals to people shape the way they must be cooked and handled. Under ordinary conditions in South Korea, hunting is governed by South Korea's Wildlife Protection and Management Act. A hunter must obtain a hunting license and receive hunting approval at a designated hunting ground, and the species and seasons that may be taken are set by law. Setting snares, traps, and pit traps is prohibited in principle, and unlicensed hunting is punishable by up to 2 years in prison or a fine of up to 20 million won. Most other countries have regulations to the same effect. This document sets out knowledge that assumes a survival situation in which public institutions are no longer functioning; under ordinary conditions, you must check your own country's hunting laws and wildlife protection laws and act only within them.
