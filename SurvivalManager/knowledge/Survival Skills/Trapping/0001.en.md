## Introduction
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In a disaster, hunting is often overestimated. Wild animals notice a person's approach first, a person's travel speed is slower than that of any game animal, and the probability that an unskilled person lands an effective hit is extremely low. A failed day of hunting burns more than 2,000 kcal and returns nothing, and that loss drains again from the next day's judgment and strength.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Traps change this structure. Making one trap costs a few dozen kcal, and once set it works 24 hours a day whatever the person does. Even if each trap has a 5% success rate, setting ten gives a 40% chance of at least one success a day. Because the person does not face the animal directly, there is almost no risk of injury. An injury while starving is itself deadly, so for this reason alone traps come before hunting.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This document covers the physical principles of traps and hunting tools, why most traps are empty, and how diseases passed from wild animals to people determine cooking and handling.

> **Caution:** In ordinary times in South Korea, hunting is governed by the Act on the Protection and Management of Wildlife. You must obtain a hunting license and receive hunting approval at a designated hunting ground, and the kinds of animals you may take and the seasons are set by law. Setting snares, traps, and pits is in principle prohibited, and unlicensed hunting is punishable by up to two years in prison or a fine of up to 20 million won. Most other countries have regulations of the same intent. This document assumes a survival situation in which public structures do not function; in ordinary times, check the hunting laws and wildlife protection laws of your country and act only within them.
