## 5. Electric Shock, Fire, and Hazards in Disaster Situations
### 5-1. It Is Current That Causes Injury
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;While it is commonly said that high voltage is dangerous, what actually damages the human body is the magnitude of the current passing through it, the duration of flow, and the pathway it takes. Voltage is merely one of the conditions that generates that current; even at the exact same voltage, changes in body resistance drastically alter the flowing current.

| Body Condition | Approximate Human Body Resistance |
|:---|---:|
| Dry hands, hand-to-hand pathway | 10,000–100,000 Ω |
| Sweaty hands | 1,000–5,000 Ω |
| Wet hands | 500–1,500 Ω |
| Submerged in water | 200–500 Ω |
| Skin with open wounds/abrasions | A few hundred Ω |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The stratum corneum (outer layer of skin) accounts for most of the body's electrical resistance. While this layer is a fairly good insulator when dry, it becomes a conductive pathway when wet with water, and salt in sweat adds electrolytes to lower resistance even further.

> **Touching the same 220V source can result in a current of barely 20 mA with dry hands, but over 200 mA with wet hands. Water and sweat increase danger not because voltage rises, but because body resistance drops to a fraction of its dry value.**

---
### 5-2. Differences Created by Current Magnitude
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Below are the approximate physiological effects when commercial frequency AC flows from hand to foot. Actual effects vary depending on individual differences and exposure duration.

| Current | Physiological Effect on the Body |
|---:|:---|
| 0.5–1 mA | Barely perception threshold |
| 1–5 mA | Stinging sensation; may cause startle and loss of balance |
| 5–10 mA | Painful sensation and muscle contraction |
| 10–20 mA | "Let-go" threshold exceeded; muscle freezing prevents releasing grip |
| 20–50 mA | Respiratory muscles paralyze, causing inability to breathe |
| 50 mA or higher | Risk of ventricular fibrillation increases significantly |
| Several Amperes or higher | Cardiac arrest and deep tissue burns occur |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The most critical range in this table is between 10–20 mA. Because flexor muscles that curl fingers inward are stronger than extensor muscles, current flow causes hands to involuntarily clench, gripping the wire tighter. Inability to let go prolongs contact time; as time lengthens, skin burns away, lowering resistance and increasing current further. This is why duration is even more decisive than current magnitude in electrocution accidents.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Pathway is equally crucial. Current flowing from hand to hand, or from hand to the opposite foot, passes directly through the heart. This is why the safety habit of keeping one hand in a pocket or behind the back when working with electricity is recommended.

---
### 5-3. Why AC Is More Dangerous Than DC at Lower Currents
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Alternating current (AC) changes direction over 100 times per second, repeatedly stimulating muscles and nerves. This repetitive stimulation causes muscle freezing ("let-go" failure) at much lower current levels and increases the probability of striking the vulnerable window of the cardiac cycle. Consequently, the minimum current required to trigger ventricular fibrillation in DC is several times higher than in AC.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;However, this does not mean direct current (DC) is safe. DC inflicts severe shocks at the moments of contact and break, and its non-extinguishing arc characteristics make it even more dangerous regarding thermal burns and fires. Large battery banks, despite low voltage, produce massive short-circuit currents capable of instantly melting metal tools. This is why rings, watches, and necklaces must be removed when working near battery banks.

---
### 5-4. What to Do Upon Discovering an Electrocution Victim
> **Power must be disconnected before touching a person experiencing electric shock. A person in contact with electricity is a live conductor, and grabbing them instantly pulls the rescuer into the exact same circuit.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;If there is no way to cut off power, push the victim away using a non-conductive object such as a dry wooden pole, dry rope, or thickly folded dry cloth. Wet objects or metallic tools will transmit current directly to the rescuer.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In cases where a downed high-voltage power line touches the ground, approaching the area itself is hazardous. As current radiates through the ground, step potential differences are created on the surface; if two feet are separated, the voltage difference between feet alone causes severe shock. If movement is unavoidable, move using tiny shuffle steps with both feet glued together; the absolute rule, however, is not to approach at all.

### 5-5. Electrical Fires
| Cause | How Fire Ignites |
|:---|:---|
| Overload | Wire continuously heats up past allowable current, degrading insulation until ignition |
| Short Circuit | Two live conductors touch directly, causing massive instantaneous current and arcing sparks |
| Bad Contact Arc | Loose connections create repetitive micro-arcing, reaching localized temps of thousands of degrees |
| Tracking | Dust and moisture settle on insulating surfaces, forming conductive carbonized paths over time |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Among the four, tracking is the hardest to detect. It progresses over months without drawing enough current to trip circuit breakers, slowly broadening a carbonized black track until it suddenly culminates in a short circuit. Behind outlets, inside dust-filled power strips, and between the two prongs of plugs left inserted for long periods are classic occurrence points. Periodically unplugging devices, wiping away dust, and inspecting for scorched marks is the sole prevention.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Water must never be used on a fire if the power source remains energized. The stream of water acts as a conductor, routing electric current back to the firefighter. Disconnecting power comes first; once power is isolated, water can be applied. However, large battery banks retain internal energy even if circuit breakers are opened, remaining dangerous.

### 5-6. Hazards Specific to Disaster Situations
| Hazard | Why It Occurs |
|:---|:---|
| Temporary Extension Cables | Laid across floors, subjected to stepping, pinching, and moisture, accumulating insulation damage |
| Daisy-Chained Outlets | Excessive load concentrates on a single circuit, exceeding wire ampacity |
| Coiled Cable Reels | Self-generated heat cannot dissipate, causing overheating even below rated current |
| Submerged Panels | Even after drying, corrosion and debris left on contacts invite tracking fires |
| Wet Flooring | Human body resistance drops sharply and grounding pathways form |
| Generator Backfeeding | Electrocutes utility restoration workers and neighbors on the grid |

> **Backfeeding electricity into a building by plugging a generator output directly into a wall receptacle must never be done under any circumstances. Power from the generator travels back through building wiring into the utility transformer, stepping up to thousands of volts in reverse and electrocuting line workers or neighbors who assumed the grid was dead.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The correct procedure is plugging appliances directly into the generator, or connecting through a transfer switch that physically isolates building wiring from the utility grid. This transfer switch must be structurally designed so that generator power and grid power can never be connected simultaneously.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Hazards of the generator engine itself must not be forgotten. Internal combustion generators emit carbon monoxide—a colorless, odorless gas that knocks victims unconscious without warning. Running a generator near open windows, inside partially open garages, in basements, or near tents is physically no different from running it indoors.

### 5-7. Measurement and Testing
| Verification Item | Measurement Method | Cautions |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Presence of Voltage | Measure across points in Voltage Mode | Ensure fingers do not touch metallic probe tips |
| Polarity | Measure in DC Voltage Mode | A negative sign indicates reversed polarity |
| Continuity | Measure in Resistance or Continuity Mode | Must be performed strictly with power disconnected |
| Current | Break circuit and measure in Series (Current Mode) | Placing probes across a circuit in current mode burns the meter |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The most fundamental rule is never to assume power is disconnected, but to verify through measurement. Because the measuring instrument itself can fail, follow a strict three-step procedure: test the meter on a known live circuit to verify function, measure the target circuit, and re-test the meter on the known live circuit to verify it remains functional. Skipping this procedure leads to false safety assumptions when a broken meter continuously reads 0 volts.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Another common error is selecting the wrong measurement mode. Measuring a DC circuit in AC mode yields values close to zero, creating a dangerous illusion that no electricity is present. Always verify what is being measured and whether the meter setting matches that parameter before probing.
