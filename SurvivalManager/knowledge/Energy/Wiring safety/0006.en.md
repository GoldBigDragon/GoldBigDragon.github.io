## 5. Electric Shock, Fire, and Hazards in a Disaster
### 5-1. Current and Body Resistance
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;What injures the body is the magnitude of the current that passed through it, how long it flowed, and the path it took. Voltage is only one of the conditions that produces that current, and even at the same voltage, the current that flows varies greatly if body resistance changes.

| Condition of the body | Approximate body resistance |
|:---|---:|
| Dry hands, hand to hand | 10,000-100,000Ω |
| Sweaty hands | 1,000-5,000Ω |
| Wet hands | 500-1,500Ω |
| Immersed in water | 200-500Ω |
| Wound with the skin stripped | A few hundred Ω |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Most of the body's resistance is provided by the keratin layer of the skin. Dry, it is an excellent insulator, but wet with water it becomes a pathway for electricity, and the salt in sweat adds an electrolyte. Touching the same 220V, a little over 20mA may flow if the hands are dry, and over 200mA if they are wet. Water and sweat raise the danger not because the voltage goes up but because the body's resistance falls to a fraction of a few tens.

---
### 5-2. Effects by Current
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;These are rough effects when commercial-frequency AC flows from hand to foot. They vary with the individual and the contact time.

| Current | Effect on the body |
|---:|:---|
| 0.5-1mA | Barely perceptible |
| 1-5mA | Tingling; you may be startled and lose balance |
| 5-10mA | Pain, muscle contraction |
| 10-20mA | Inability to let go on your own |
| 20-50mA | Respiratory muscles lock and you cannot breathe |
| 50mA or more | Increasing risk of ventricular fibrillation |
| Several A or more | Cardiac arrest and deep burns |

- Inability to let go (10-20mA) is the range that deserves the most attention. The muscles that close the fingers are stronger than those that open them, so when current flows the hand clenches by itself and grips the wire harder. Contact time grows, and as the skin burns the resistance falls further and the current grows. This is why, in electric shock, time is more decisive than the magnitude of the current.
- Current flowing from hand to hand, or from hand to the opposite foot, crosses the heart. This is why the habit of keeping one hand in a pocket or behind your back is recommended when handling electricity.

---
### 5-3. The Dangers of AC and DC
- AC: it changes direction more than 100 times a second and repeatedly stimulates muscles and nerves. Inability to let go occurs at a much lower current, and the chance of striking the short window in the heartbeat cycle that is most vulnerable to stimulation is also high. The minimum current that causes ventricular fibrillation is several times higher for DC than for AC.
- DC: this does not mean it is safe. It gives a strong jolt at the moment of contact and at the moment of release, and since the arc does not go out, it is more dangerous in terms of burns and fire. A large battery bank, even at low voltage, delivers a huge current in a short circuit that melts metal tools in an instant. This is why you take off rings, watches, and necklaces when working around batteries.

---
### 5-4. When You Find a Shock Victim
> **Caution:** Cut the power first before laying a hand on a person who has been shocked. A person in contact is himself a conductor carrying electricity, and the moment you grab him, the rescuer also enters the same circuit.

- If there is no way to cut the power, push the person away with something that does not conduct electricity, such as a dry wooden stick, dry rope, or thickly folded dry cloth. Wet objects and metal transmit the current directly.
- If a downed high-voltage line is touching the ground, approaching is itself dangerous. The current spreads through the ground, creating a potential difference on the surface, so if your feet are apart, you can be shocked just by the voltage difference between your feet. The rule is not to approach. If you must move, keep your feet together and shuffle with small steps.

### 5-5. Electrical Fires
| Cause | How the fire starts |
|:---|:---|
| Overload | The wire keeps heating beyond its allowable current, and the sheath deteriorates and ignites |
| Short circuit | Two conductors touch directly, a large current flows instantly, and sparks fly |
| Poor-contact arcing | Tiny sparks repeat at a loose connection, locally reaching thousands of degrees |
| Tracking | Tiny currents flow over the surface of insulation coated with dust and moisture, and a carbonized path grows |

- Tracking is the hardest to notice. It progresses over months without a current large enough for the breaker to respond, and the black carbonized line widens until at some moment it leads to a short circuit. Typical places are behind outlets, inside dusty power strips, and between the two prongs of a plug left in for a long time. Unplugging periodically, wiping off the dust, and looking for scorch marks is the only prevention.
- Do not use water if the power is live. The stream of water becomes a conductor and current returns to the person putting out the fire. Cut the power first, and after it is cut you may use water. A large-capacity battery still holds energy even with the breaker down, so it remains dangerous.

### 5-6. Hazards Peculiar to Disasters
| Hazard | Why it arises |
|:---|:---|
| Makeshift wiring | Laid on the floor, trampled, pressed, and wetted, with sheath damage accumulating |
| Octopus connections | Loads crowd onto one circuit and exceed the wire's allowable current |
| Reels used coiled | Heat cannot escape, so it overheats even below the rating |
| Flooded distribution panel | Even after the water dries, corrosion and foreign matter left on the contacts invite tracking |
| Wet floor | Body resistance falls sharply and a ground path is created |
| Generator backfeed | Shocks restoration workers and neighbors |

> **Caution:** Do not feed electricity into a building by plugging a generator backward into an indoor outlet. When the generator's electricity flows through the house wiring into the pole transformer, the transformer works in reverse and steps up to thousands of volts, shocking restoration workers and neighbors who are working believing the power is cut.

- The correct way is to connect devices directly to the generator, or to go through a transfer switch that physically separates the building wiring from the grid. This switch must be of a structure that cannot connect the generator and the external grid at the same time.
- An internal-combustion generator emits carbon monoxide. It has no color or smell, so people collapse without noticing. Running it near a window, in a half-open garage, in a basement space, or near a tent is the same as running it indoors.

### 5-7. Measurement
| Item to check | How to measure | Points of caution |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Presence of voltage | Measure across both ends in voltage mode | Keep fingers from touching the metal part of the probe |
| Polarity | Measure in DC voltage mode | If the sign is negative, the polarity is reversed |
| Continuity | Measure in resistance or continuity mode | Only with the power cut |
| Current | Break the circuit and insert in series to measure | If put across the circuit while in voltage mode, the meter burns out |

- Do not trust the fact that the power was cut; confirm it by measurement.
- Meters can fail too. Go through three steps: check that the meter works on a live circuit, measure the target circuit, and check again on a live circuit that the meter is still working. If you skip it, you will wrongly judge it safe when a broken meter keeps pointing to 0V.
- Putting it in the wrong mode is also common. Measuring a DC circuit in AC mode gives a value near 0, making it easy to think there is no electricity. Before measuring, check that what you are about to measure matches what the meter is now set to measure.
