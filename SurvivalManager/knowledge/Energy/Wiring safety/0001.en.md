## Introduction
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The sequence in which people get injured after a power outage follows a roughly predictable pattern. At first, no one is using electricity, so there are no accidents. Then, generators or batteries start appearing one by one, temporary extension cords are run in haste, multiple appliances are plugged into a single outlet, and wires stretching across yards are left exposed even in the rain—accumulating danger. Temporary wiring set up by humans after an outage causes far more fires and electrocutions than the blackout itself.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This document does not deal with generating electricity or designing electrical systems. It focuses on how to safely transmit, interrupt, and protect the electricity that has been generated. Because the fundamental relationships among voltage, current, and resistance, as well as the configuration and design of solar systems, have already been outlined in the Solar Panel Installation document, weight is placed here on criteria for selecting wire gauge, what fuses actually protect, the operating principles of grounding and residual current circuit breakers (RCCBs/GFCIs), and what occurs when electric current flows through the human body.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Among the concepts covered here, two facts in particular must be held firm. One is that fuses and circuit breakers are devices designed to protect the wiring, not the appliances. The other is that the true danger of electric shock is determined by the current flowing through the body, not the voltage. Understanding these two principles allows one to self-explain why raising a fuse rating leads to a fire, and why touching the exact same wire kills only when hands are wet. A person who grasps the underlying principles can make sound judgments even in unfamiliar situations.
