## 2. Wire Gauge and Voltage Drop
### 2-1. Problems Caused by Thin Wires
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;First is voltage drop. Because the voltage consumed by the wire lowers the voltage reaching the appliance, light bulbs grow dim, motors lose power, and batteries fail to charge. This problem is particularly severe in battery charging circuits; even if the charger output is 14V, if 1V is lost in the wire, only 13V reaches the battery, halting the charging process altogether.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Second is heat generation. The product of the voltage lost in the wire and the flowing current represents the power converted directly into heat. If this heat exceeds the temperature limit the insulation can withstand, the insulation breaks down. This is the pathway that leads directly to electrical fires.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Both problems share the exact same root causes: the wire is too thin, the run is too long, or the current is too high. Fixing any one of these factors improves both symptoms.

### 2-2. Allowable Current by Cross-Sectional Area
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Below is the approximate allowable current (ampacity) for copper wires. Actual values vary significantly depending on insulation material, ambient temperature, whether multiple conductors are bundled together, and whether they are enclosed in conduit or exposed; thus, these values should serve as a starting reference rather than an absolute rule.

| Cross-Sectional Area | Conductor Diameter | Approximate Allowable Current |
|---:|---:|---:|
| 0.75 ㎟ | Approx. 1.0mm | 7A |
| 1.5 ㎟ | Approx. 1.4mm | 15A |
| 2.5 ㎟ | Approx. 1.8mm | 20A |
| 4 ㎟ | Approx. 2.3mm | 27A |
| 6 ㎟ | Approx. 2.8mm | 35A |
| 10 ㎟ | Approx. 3.6mm | 48A |
| 16 ㎟ | Approx. 4.5mm | 65A |
| 25 ㎟ | Approx. 5.6mm | 85A |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There are several situations where these baseline values must be derated. Placing multiple conductors inside a single conduit or bundle causes mutual heating, requiring a 30–50% reduction in rated capacity. The same applies to wiring buried inside thermal insulation or installed in high ambient temperatures. This is also why extension cord reels are dangerous when used while coiled. Wires tightly wound on a reel cannot dissipate self-generated heat, causing them to overheat at currents far lower than when fully unreeled.

### 2-3. Voltage Drop Calculation
> **Voltage Drop (V) = 2 × One-Way Distance (m) × Current (A) × 0.0178 ÷ Cross-Sectional Area (㎟)**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Multiplying by 2 accounts for current traveling through both the supply and return lines, and 0.0178 represents the resistivity of copper. For aluminum, use 0.0286.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Calculating a scenario in a 12V system where 10A is sent over a distance of 10m: using a 2.5㎟ wire results in a drop of approximately 1.42V. This corresponds to 11.9% of 12V, making the system completely unusable. Under identical conditions, upgrading to a 10㎟ wire reduces the drop to 0.36V, or 3%.

| Application | Recommended Maximum Voltage Drop |
|:---|---:|
| Battery Charging Circuits | 2% or less |
| Lighting | 3% or less |
| General Loads | 5% or less |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The reason voltage drop is so fatal in low-voltage systems is proportional ratio. A 1V drop in a 220V system represents merely 0.45% and has no noticeable effect, but a 1V drop in a 12V system equals 8.3%, preventing proper equipment operation. In low-voltage systems, the absolute rule is "short and thick." If wiring must cover long distances, raising system voltage or moving batteries and generators closer to the load is a far better solution.

### 2-4. Aluminum Wiring and Connections
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Aluminum is lightweight, readily available, and conducts electricity better than copper on a per-weight basis. Nevertheless, the reason it is tricky to handle lies not in the wire itself, but in its connection points.

| Problem | What Occurs |
|:---|:---|
| Oxide Film | An insulating oxide film forms on the surface immediately upon cutting |
| Creep | Under continuous pressure, the metal slowly deforms and flows, loosening the joint |
| Thermal Expansion Difference | Repeated heating and cooling cycles loosen screw connections |
| Dissimilar Metal Contact | Direct contact with copper causes galvanic corrosion in the presence of moisture |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;All four of these factors work together to increase contact resistance. Increased contact resistance generates localized heat at the joint, and that heat loosens the connection further. This is why nearly all fires originating from aluminum wiring start at connection points rather than along the wire runs.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;If joining copper and aluminum is unavoidable, never place them in direct contact; use dedicated bimetallic connectors or apply anti-oxidation grease to contact surfaces. Furthermore, aluminum connections must be retightened periodically—unlike copper connections, which are finished after a single tightening.
