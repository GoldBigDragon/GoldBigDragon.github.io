## 2. Wire Thickness and Voltage Drop
### 2-1. Problems When the Wire Is Thin
- Voltage drop: the voltage reaching the device falls by the voltage the wire consumed. Bulbs dim, motors cannot deliver power, and batteries do not charge. It is especially serious in charging circuits. Even if the charger output is 14V, if the wire consumes 1V, only 13V reaches the battery and charging does not proceed.
- Heating: the product of the voltage the wire consumed and the current flowing is the power that becomes heat in the wire. When this heat exceeds the sheath's limit, the insulation breaks down and leads to fire.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The two problems have the same cause: the wire is thin or long, or the current is large; fixing either way improves both.

---
### 2-2. Allowable Current by Cross-Sectional Area
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;These are rough allowable currents for copper wire. They vary greatly with the insulation material, ambient temperature, bundling, and whether the wire is in a conduit or exposed, so use them as a starting point, not an absolute standard.

| Cross-sectional area | Conductor diameter | Rough allowable current |
|---:|---:|---:|
| 0.75 mm² | about 1.0mm | 7A |
| 1.5 mm² | about 1.4mm | 15A |
| 2.5 mm² | about 1.8mm | 20A |
| 4 mm² | about 2.3mm | 27A |
| 6 mm² | about 2.8mm | 35A |
| 10 mm² | about 3.6mm | 48A |
| 16 mm² | about 4.5mm | 65A |
| 25 mm² | about 5.6mm | 85A |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There are cases where these values must not be used as is.

- Putting several wires in one conduit or one bundle makes them heat each other, so derate by 30-50%.
- Also derate wiring in places with high ambient temperature or buried in insulation.
- A cord reel used while coiled cannot get rid of the heat it produces, so it overheats at a much lower current than the same wire uncoiled.

---
### 2-3. Calculating Voltage Drop
> **Voltage drop (V) = 2 × one-way length (m) × current (A) × 0.0178 ÷ cross-sectional area (mm²)**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The factor of 2 is because the current passes through both the outgoing and the returning wire. 0.0178 is the resistivity of copper; for aluminum use 0.0286.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;When sending 10A to a point 10m away in a 12V system, a 2.5 mm² wire drops about 1.42V (11.9% of 12V), which makes it unusable. Under the same conditions, switching to 10 mm² brings it down to 0.36V (3%).

| Use | Recommended voltage drop |
|:---|---:|
| Battery charging circuit | 2% or less |
| Lighting | 3% or less |
| General loads | 5% or less |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The lower the voltage, the more fatal a voltage drop is as a ratio. At 220V a 1V drop is 0.45%, but at 12V a 1V drop is 8.3% and the system does not work properly. Wire low-voltage systems short and thick. If the wiring must run long, raise the voltage, or move the battery and generator closer to the load.

---
### 2-4. Aluminum Wire and Connections
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Aluminum is light, easy to get, and conducts electricity better than copper for the same weight. The difficulty in handling it is not the wire but the connections.

| Problem | What happens |
|:---|:---|
| Oxide film | An insulating oxide film forms on the surface immediately after cutting |
| Creep | If left pressed for a long time, the metal slowly flows and the connection loosens |
| Difference in thermal expansion | Repeated warming and cooling loosens screw connections |
| Dissimilar-metal contact | Direct contact with copper causes galvanic corrosion in moisture |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;All four increase contact resistance, increased contact resistance produces heat at that spot, and heat loosens it again. Almost every aluminum wiring fire starts not in the middle of the wire but at a connection point.

- If you must join copper and aluminum, do not butt them directly; use a dedicated connection part. At the very least, apply grease that prevents oxidation to the contact surfaces.
- Retighten aluminum connections periodically. This is unlike a copper connection, which is done once it is tightened.
