## 3. Fuses, Circuit Breakers, and Grounding
### 3-1. Fuses Protect Wires, Not Appliances
> **Fuses and circuit breakers are not devices designed to protect downstream appliances, but to prevent currents that exceed what the connected wiring can safely handle.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This single sentence clears up nearly every misconception regarding fuses. If an appliance fails, the appliance itself is already ruined; however, if a wire overheats, a fire begins invisibly inside walls or under floorboards. The object requiring protection is the hidden part.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Therefore, the criterion for determining fuse rating is not how much current the appliance consumes, but how much current the wire can withstand. The sequence is always the same: first, select the wire gauge based on the required current; second, choose a fuse with a rating lower than the wire's allowable ampacity; third, operate appliances within that fuse capacity.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;If you repeatedly increase the fuse rating because it keeps blowing, the exact opposite occurs. Even if a current exceeding the wire's limit flows, the fuse remains intact while the wire heats up first. Inserting copper wire, nails, or aluminum foil in place of a fuse completely eliminates protection from that circuit—a classic cause of electrical fires in disaster situations.

---
### 3-2. Installation Location
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;A fuse must be installed at the very beginning of the wire it protects—as close to the power source as possible. The segment ahead of the fuse receives no protection whatsoever, so the longer this segment is, the greater the risk.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In particular, the primary wire coming directly from a battery must have a fuse. When shorted across terminals, lead-acid batteries can instantly release hundreds to thousands of amperes, a current capable of vaporizing wires in a flash of flame. As a rule, place the fuse within 20 cm of the battery terminal; if multiple batteries are connected in parallel, install a separate fuse for each battery.

| Location | Protected Object |
|:---|:---|
| Immediately adjacent to battery terminal | All wires leaving the battery |
| Both sides of charge controller | Solar generation system and battery system |
| Each branch of distribution panel | Wires connected to that specific branch |
| Immediately ahead of appliance | Appliance itself (optional) |

### 3-3. Fast-Acting vs. Time-Delay Fuses
| Type | Operating Characteristic | Applications |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Fast-Acting | Blows instantly upon slightly exceeding rating | Electronic circuits, semiconductor devices |
| Time-Delay (Slow-Blow) | Withstands brief high currents, blows if sustained | Motors, compressors, transformers |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This distinction is necessary because of inrush current. The moment a motor turns on, it draws 3 to 7 times its rated current for a fraction of a second. This brief spike is not long enough to make the wire dangerous and does not need to be interrupted, but a fast-acting fuse cannot differentiate this and blows every time the device powers on.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;A common trap arises here. Because it blows upon startup, people raise the fuse rating; while this allows startup, the fuse can no longer protect against sustained overloads. The correct solution is not raising the rating, but switching to a time-delay fuse.

---
### 3-4. What a Blown Fuse Signifies
| Blowing Pattern | Meaning |
|:---|:---|
| Blows only at the moment of turning on | Inrush current. Switch to a time-delay fuse |
| Blows after extended operation | Overload. Reduce load or increase wire gauge and fuse size together |
| Blows instantly upon insertion/powering on | Short circuit. Do not reinsert; locate and fix the short |
| Blows irregularly | Damaged insulation or loose contact. Inspect entire wiring system |
| Blows only during summer | High ambient temperature reduced the wire's allowable ampacity |

> **A blown fuse is a warning, not a mere failure. If it blows repeatedly in the same location, a definitive cause exists; raising the rating does not fix the cause, but merely turns off the alarm.**

### 3-5. Grounding
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;If wire insulation strips off inside an appliance and contacts the metal casing, the entire enclosure becomes energized. If a person touches the enclosure in this state, current flows through the human body into the earth—the person becomes part of the electrical circuit.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Grounding is the act of preparing for this situation in advance by establishing an extremely low-resistance path between the metal enclosure and the earth. Because electric current concentrates along the path of lowest resistance, a properly connected ground wire routes leaked current through the ground wire rather than the human body. Simultaneously, the large current flowing through that path trips the fuse or circuit breaker to interrupt the circuit. Grounding protects humans while revealing internal faults.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The hazard of ungrounded systems lies in the fact that nothing appears wrong. Even if electricity leaks to the casing, it looks normal on the outside, and a path for current to flow is created only at the moment a person touches it. Ground wires are easily omitted in temporary setups simply because appliances run normally without them.

---
### 3-6. Residual Current Circuit Breakers (RCCB / GFCI)
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The operating principle of a Residual Current Circuit Breaker (RCCB/GFCI) is simple. It continuously compares the current flowing into a circuit with the current returning from it; if the values match, operation is normal, but if they differ, it detects a leak elsewhere and breaks the circuit. Units for human protection typically detect a current difference as small as 30 mA within 30 milliseconds to trip.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;A crucial advantage is that RCCBs operate even without grounding, because current leaking to the earth through a human body still registers as a discrepancy between incoming and outgoing current. This makes RCCBs invaluable in temporary wiring where establishing reliable grounding is difficult.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;However, they are not all-powerful. If a person grips both the live and neutral wires simultaneously with both hands, the incoming current equals the returning current, rendering the RCCB blind to the event. Furthermore, overloads and short circuits are not current leakages, requiring separate overcurrent protection. Finally, because circuit breakers can fail, the test button must be pressed once a month to verify mechanical tripping. An RCCB that does not trip when tested is equivalent to having no protection at all.
