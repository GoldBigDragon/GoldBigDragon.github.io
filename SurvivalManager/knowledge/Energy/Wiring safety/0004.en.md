## 3. Fuses, Breakers, and Grounding
### 3-1. What a Fuse Protects
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;A fuse or breaker is not a device that guards the equipment behind it, but one that prevents a current the connected wire cannot handle from flowing. A device gets ruined when it fails anyway, but if a wire overheats, fire starts inside a wall or floor.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The fuse rating is decided not by how much the device uses but by how much the wire can withstand.

1. Decide the wire thickness to suit the current needed.
2. Choose a fuse with a value lower than that wire's allowable current.
3. Use the device within that fuse capacity.

> **Caution:** Do not raise the rating just because a fuse keeps blowing. Even when a current beyond the wire's limit flows, the fuse stays intact and the wire heats up first. Putting copper wire, a nail, or aluminum foil in place of a fuse completely removes the protection of that circuit and is a classic cause of electrical fires in a disaster.

---
### 3-2. Installation Location
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Fit a fuse as close as possible to the starting point of the wire to be protected, that is, the power-source side. The section ahead of the fuse is unprotected, so the longer that section, the more dangerous.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The first wire leaving a battery must always have a fuse. When the terminals of a lead-acid battery are short-circuited, it instantly pours out hundreds to thousands of amps, and this current vaporizes the wire with sparks. The rule is to put the fuse within 20cm of the battery terminal, and if several batteries are tied in parallel, fit one separately at each battery.

| Location | What it protects |
|:---|:---|
| Right next to the battery terminal | All wires leaving the battery |
| Both sides of the charge controller | The generation system and the battery system |
| Each branch of the distribution panel | The wires connected to that branch |
| Right in front of a device | The device itself (optional) |

---
### 3-3. Fast-Acting and Time-Delay Types
| Type | Operating characteristic | Where used |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Fast-acting | Blows immediately if the rating is exceeded even slightly | Electronic circuits, semiconductor devices |
| Time-delay | Withstands a short large current and blows if it lasts long | Motors, compressors, transformers |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The reason the distinction is needed is the starting current. At switch-on, a motor draws 3-7 times its rated current for a fraction of a second. This brief current is not long enough to endanger the wire and need not be interrupted, but a fast-acting fuse cannot tell the difference and blows every time you switch on. Raising the rating allows starting but also fails to stop a sustained overload. Do not raise the rating; switch to a time-delay type.

---
### 3-4. Patterns of Blowing
| Pattern of blowing | Meaning |
|:---|:---|
| Blows only at switch-on | Starting current. Replace with a time-delay type |
| Blows after being used a while | Overload. Reduce the load, or increase the wire and fuse together |
| Blows immediately on insertion | Short circuit. Do not reinsert; find the cause |
| Blows irregularly | Damaged sheath or poor contact. Inspect all the wiring |
| Blows only in summer | The ambient temperature is high and the allowable current has dropped |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;A blown fuse is not a failure but a warning. If it keeps blowing at the same spot, there is always a cause. Raising the rating does not fix the cause; it turns off the alarm.

---
### 3-5. Grounding
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;If the wire sheath is stripped inside a device and touches the metal enclosure, the whole enclosure becomes live. If a person touches the enclosure then, current flows through the body to the ground. The person becomes part of the circuit.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Grounding is building in advance a very-low-resistance path between the enclosure and the earth. Current crowds toward the lower resistance, so if the ground wire is properly connected, the leaked current goes to the ground wire instead of the person. A large current flows then, and the fuse or breaker operates and cuts the circuit. Grounding protects people and reveals faults.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The danger without grounding is that nothing happens. Even when electricity is leaking to the enclosure, it looks normal from outside, and only at the moment a person touches it does a path for current appear. The reason the ground wire is easily omitted in a temporary system is that the device works fine without grounding.

---
### 3-6. Ground-Fault Breakers
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;It compares the current entering the circuit with the current returning, judges it normal if the two are equal and leaking somewhere if different, and cuts the circuit. Those for personal protection generally detect a difference of 30mA within 30ms and cut off.

- It works even without grounding. Current leaking through a person's body to the earth also shows up as the difference between the amount that went in and the amount that came out. Its value is especially great in temporary wiring where grounding is hard to provide.
- Limitation: if a person grabs two wires at once with both hands, the current that went in comes back out as is, so it detects nothing.
- Overload and short circuits are not ground faults, so separate overcurrent protection must be there too.
- Breakers also fail. Press the test button once a month to confirm that it actually trips. A ground-fault breaker that does not trip when pressed is the same as not having one.
