## 1. Looking at Electricity Through the Lens of Wiring
### 1-1. Wires Are Resistors Too
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The relationships among voltage, current, and resistance, as well as the definitions of power and electrical energy, are summarized in the Solar Panel Installation document. When handling wiring, you only need to add one more fact: the wire itself is a component that possesses resistance.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Because wires have resistance, when current flows, the wire consumes a portion of the voltage. The voltage reaching the appliance drops accordingly. At the same time, the wire consumes power proportional to that share, and all consumed power converts into heat. Voltage drop and heat generation are not two separate issues, but two perspectives of the exact same phenomenon.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;What is crucial here is that the heat generated from a wire is proportional to the square of the current. If the current doubles, heat generation quadruples; if the current is halved, heat generation drops to a quarter. This squared relationship governs nearly every judgment in wiring design.

---
### 1-2. Raising Voltage Simplifies Wiring
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;When transmitting the same amount of power, raising the voltage decreases the current proportionally. Since heat generation is proportional to the square of the current, doubling the voltage reduces the heat generated in the same wire to a quarter.

| System Voltage | Current When Transmitting 500W | Relative Loss Comparison in Same Wire |
|---:|---:|---:|
| 12V | 41.7A | 100 |
| 24V | 20.8A | 25 |
| 48V | 10.4A | 6.3 |
| 220V | 2.3A | 0.3 |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This is why transmission lines send electricity at tens of thousands of volts, and the same principle applies directly to small systems. A 12V system has the advantage of readily available parts and compatibility with automotive accessories, but it requires thick wires and cannot run long wiring distances. If the wiring distance exceeds a few meters, raising the voltage to 24V or 48V is advantageous in terms of both wire cost and energy loss.

---
### 1-3. Where Direct Current and Alternating Current Diverge
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Because the definitions of direct current (DC) and alternating current (AC) are covered in another document, only the points where they diverge regarding wiring and safety are summarized here.

| Item | Direct Current (DC) | Alternating Current (AC) |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Battery Storage | Directly possible | Requires conversion |
| Voltage Transformation | Requires dedicated circuitry | Simple with a transformer |
| Long-Distance Transmission | Disadvantageous | Advantageous |
| When Breaking a Switch | Arcs do not extinguish easily | Arcs extinguish naturally |
| Corrosion | Galvanic corrosion progresses at one pole | Relatively less severe |
| Electric Shock Hazard | Relatively less dangerous at same current | More dangerous at lower current |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Among these, the fourth line is what leads to real-world accidents. Because AC passes through zero current more than a hundred times per second, the arc created when switch contacts separate extinguishes naturally at that instant. In DC, current does not cross zero, so the arc continues through the air even after contacts separate.

> **If AC switches and circuit breakers are used in DC circuits, the arc will persist even after contacts break, melting the contacts together and failing to interrupt the circuit. Circuits handling DC, such as batteries and solar panels, must strictly use components marked with a DC rating.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In disaster situations, focusing on DC is generally more reasonable. Batteries and solar panels are both DC, and small devices like LEDs, radios, and chargers operate internally on DC. Eliminating the path of converting to AC and then back to DC reduces both energy loss and required components.

---
### 1-4. Resistance Is Determined by Length, Thickness, and Material
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The resistance of a wire is proportional to its length and inversely proportional to its cross-sectional area. Pulling a wire twice as long doubles its resistance; doubling the cross-sectional area halves the resistance. Material characteristics multiply this value.

| Conductor | Relative Resistance | Remarks |
|:---|---:|:---|
| Silver | 0.95 | Lowest, but impractical |
| Copper | 1.00 | Standard reference |
| Aluminum | Approx. 1.6 | Light and cheap, but must be thicker for same performance |
| Iron | Approx. 6 | Unsuitable for use as wiring |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Additionally, as temperature rises, the resistance of metals increases alongside it. This is one reason why overloaded wires are dangerous. When high current heats up a wire, its resistance increases; increased resistance generates even more heat at the same current, and that heat further increases resistance. When this feedback loop crosses a critical threshold, the insulation begins to melt, and melted insulation leads directly to short circuits.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;When selecting wires scavenged from buildings or junked cars in disaster situations, this point must be kept in mind. Wires that have already experienced overheating, causing the insulation to harden stiffly or discolor, have compromised insulation even if they appear intact on the surface. If the insulation cracks or crumbles when bent, it is safer not to use them.
