## 4. Connections and Insulation
### 4-1. Where Electrical Accidents Occur
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Electrical accidents rarely originate in the middle of a continuous wire run. They almost always begin at points where two wires are joined, clamped into terminal screws, or plugged into receptacles. This is because connection points possess three distinct vulnerabilities simultaneously: the actual physical contact area is smaller than the wire cross-section, the metal is exposed to air and undergoes oxidation, and connections loosen over time due to mechanical vibration and thermal expansion/contraction.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;All three of these factors act together to increase contact resistance, and increased contact resistance generates localized heat at that narrow junction. Heat accelerates oxidation and deforms the metal, loosening the connection further and causing contact resistance to rise even higher. Once initiated, this self-worsening feedback loop represents the true physical nature of fires caused by bad electrical contacts.

---
### 4-2. Comparison of Connection Methods
| Method | Mechanical Strength | Electrical Reliability | Characteristics |
|:---|:---|:---|:---|
| Simple Twist and Tape | Low | Low | Loosens with vibration and heat; for temporary use only |
| Twist-On Wire Connector (Wire Nut) | Medium | Medium | Quick and reusable/reversible |
| Crimp Terminal | High | High | Requires dedicated crimping tool; incomplete if squeezed with pliers |
| Soldering | Medium | High | Solder-wicked section hardens and breaks under vibration |
| Screw Terminal Clamping | Medium | Medium | Requires periodic retightening |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The most reliable method is crimping followed by covering with heat-shrink tubing. Proper crimping plastically deforms the metals together, creating an airless contact surface that is as structurally sound as the wire itself. However, if using incorrect tools, the connection may only appear pressed on the surface; always pull crimped terminals firmly by hand to verify they cannot be pulled apart.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;While soldering is electrically excellent, it must never be relied upon for mechanical strength. At the boundary where solder wicks into stranded wire and stops, the wire suddenly becomes stiff; under repeated vibration, it will snap precisely at that boundary. Avoid soldering in vibrating environments, or strain-relieve and anchor the soldered joint separately so vibration is not transmitted.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Joining wires of vastly different gauges must also be avoided. The current capacity of a circuit is governed by its thinnest segment. No matter how long or thick the rest of the wire run is, inserting a single hand-span of thin wire creates a severe heating hotspot at that exact spot.

### 4-3. Insulation and Moisture
| Method | Durability | Waterproofing | Remarks |
|:---|:---|:---|:---|
| Electrical Tape | Low | Low | Stretches with heat and peels away over time |
| Heat-Shrink Tubing | High | Medium | Suitable for basic indoor connections |
| Adhesive-Lined Heat-Shrink Tubing | High | High | Suitable for outdoor and damp locations |
| Waterproof Connector | High | High | Best option if available |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Water is particularly troublesome in electrical wiring because its effects do not stop at touching the outer connection. Water is drawn into the tiny gaps between individual copper strands via capillary action, traveling tens of centimeters underneath the outer insulation jacket. Corrosion then progresses inside a wire that looks pristine on the surface, causing failures far from the original point of entry.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Therefore, in outdoor wiring, connections must never be positioned at the lowest point of a wire run. Create a "drip loop" by dropping the wire down before bringing it up into an enclosure or appliance. Water running down the cable will then drip off at the lowest point of the loop rather than entering the connection.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Sunlight also destroys insulation. Standard insulation jackets harden and crack under continuous ultraviolet (UV) exposure, crumbling in a matter of months depending on the material. Temporary outdoor wiring should be routed through shade or enclosed in conduit; if that is impossible, bend the wire regularly to inspect for surface cracking.

### 4-4. Wiring Differences in Series vs. Parallel
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;How voltage and capacity change in series and parallel connections is covered in the Solar Panel Installation document. Here, key differences are highlighted strictly from the perspective of wiring and circuit protection.

| Connection | Wiring Impact |
|:---|:---|
| Series | Voltages add up, raising both electric shock hazards and insulation requirements |
| Series | The single weakest battery/cell limits the current of the entire chain |
| Parallel | Available short-circuit current adds up, requiring fuse recalculations |
| Parallel | If branch wire lengths differ, current will not distribute evenly |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Wiring four 12V batteries in series results in 48V, a voltage level capable of delivering a dangerous shock when touched with wet hands. Accidents frequently happen when handling a 48V system with the careless habits formed around 12V systems.

> **Never mix and connect batteries of differing capacity, chemistry/type, manufacturing age, or state of charge. In series, the weaker cell over-discharges and dies first; in parallel, the higher-voltage battery dumps massive current into the lower-voltage one, ruining both.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;When connecting batteries in parallel, pulling main cables from one end creates lower path resistance for nearby batteries and higher resistance for distant ones, causing nearby batteries to be overworked and age prematurely. Main cables must be connected from the positive terminal at one end of the bank and the negative terminal at the opposite end, equalizing total path lengths for all batteries in the bank.
