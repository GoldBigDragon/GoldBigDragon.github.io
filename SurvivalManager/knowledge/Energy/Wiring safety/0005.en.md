## 4. Connections and Insulation
### 4-1. Where Accidents Begin
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Electrical accidents begin not in the middle of a wire but where two wires are joined, where one is tightened onto a terminal, or where one is plugged into an outlet. A connection has three weak points.

- The contact area through which electricity actually passes is smaller than the wire's cross-section.
- It is exposed to air and oxidizes.
- It loosens with vibration and temperature change.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;All three increase contact resistance, and when contact resistance increases, heat is produced at that narrow spot. Heat speeds oxidation and deforms the metal so it loosens further, and contact resistance grows again. This self-worsening feedback, once started, is the reality of a poor-contact fire.

---
### 4-2. Comparing Connection Methods
| Method | Mechanical strength | Electrical reliability | Characteristics |
|:---|:---|:---|:---|
| Twisting and taping | Low | Low | Comes loose with vibration and heat. Use only temporarily |
| Tightening with a connector cap | Medium | Medium | Fast and can be undone again |
| Crimp terminal | High | High | Needs a dedicated tool. Pressing with pliers is incomplete |
| Soldering | Medium | High | The solder-wicked end becomes stiff and breaks under vibration |
| Tightening with a screw | Medium | Medium | Must be retightened periodically |

- The most reliable method is to crimp and then cover with heat-shrink tubing. Crimping plastically deforms the metal to make a gapless contact surface between the two conductors, and done properly it is as strong as the wire itself. If the tool does not fit, it ends up pressed only in appearance, so pull hard on the crimped terminal by hand to check that it does not come off.
- Soldering is excellent electrically, but do not rely on it for mechanical strength. At the boundary between the solder-wicked section and the rest, the wire suddenly becomes stiff, and under repeated vibration it breaks at that point. Do not use soldering where things move, or secure the soldered part separately so that vibration does not reach it.
- Do not join wires of very different thickness. The current a circuit can carry is set by the thinnest section, so if a span of thin wire is inserted in the middle of thick wire, that span becomes the heating point.

### 4-3. Insulation and Moisture
| Method | Durability | Waterproofing | Notes |
|:---|:---|:---|:---|
| Insulating tape | Low | Low | Stretches in heat and peels off over time |
| Heat-shrink tubing | High | Medium | Suitable for basic indoor connections |
| Heat-shrink tubing with built-in adhesive | High | High | Suitable outdoors and in damp places |
| Waterproof connector | High | High | Best if you can get one |

- Water does not stop at touching the connection. It is drawn by capillary action along the gaps between the strands of a stranded conductor and travels tens of centimeters along the inside of the sheath. Corrosion proceeds inside a wire that looks perfectly fine, and the problem appears far from the spot.
- Arrange outdoor wiring so that connections are not at the lowest point of the water's path. Just before the wire enters a device, make a drip loop by letting the wire hang down once and then come back up, so that water running down the wire drips off at the lowest point of the loop.
- Sunlight also ruins insulation. Ordinary sheathing hardens and cracks under continuous ultraviolet light, and depending on the type crumbles within months. Route temporary wiring that crosses outdoors into shade or put it in a conduit. If that is not possible, bend it periodically to check for cracks.

---
### 4-4. Series and Parallel Wiring
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The changes in voltage and capacity with series and parallel connection are in the Solar panel installation document. The differences from the viewpoint of wiring and protection are as follows.

| Connection | What changes in wiring |
|:---|:---|
| Series | Voltage adds, so shock hazard and insulation requirements rise together |
| Series | The weakest one limits the whole current |
| Parallel | The current that can flow in a short circuit adds up, so fuses must be recalculated |
| Parallel | If the wire length of each branch differs, the current does not divide evenly |

- Four 12V batteries tied in series make 48V, a voltage dangerous enough if touched with wet hands. This is the point where accidents happen when people work by the habit of letting their guard down because it is "12V."
- If parallel wiring is drawn from one end, the near battery has low resistance and the far battery high, so only the near one is used intensively and ages first. Draw from the positive of the battery at one end and the negative of the battery at the opposite end, so that the path length to each battery is equal.

> **Caution:** Do not mix batteries that differ in capacity, type, manufacturing date, or state of charge. In series, the weak cell is overdischarged first and dies, and in parallel, the higher-voltage side pours a large current into the lower side and both are ruined.
