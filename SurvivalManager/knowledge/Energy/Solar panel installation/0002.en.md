## 1. Electrical Basics
---
### 1-1. Electricity and Conductors
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;All matter is made of atoms, and inside atoms are electrons carrying negative (−) charge. Electrons flowing in one direction is electricity (current). Materials fall into two groups according to how easily electrons flow.

| Category | Description | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Conductor | Electrons flow easily | Copper, aluminum, iron, salty water, the human body |
| Non-conductor (insulator) | Electrons hardly flow | Rubber, plastic, dry wood, glass, dry air |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The copper inside a wire is a conductor and the outer sheath is a non-conductor. Thanks to the sheath, it is safe to hold a wire in your hand, but where the sheath is stripped or wet, that spot is dangerous.

---
### 1-2. Voltage, Current, Resistance
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;It is easy to understand with the analogy of a water tank and pipe. The higher the tank (voltage ↑), the harder the water comes out (current ↑), and the narrower the pipe (resistance ↑), the less water flows (current ↓).

| Concept | Analogy | Symbol | Unit | Reference |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Voltage | The height of the tank (water pressure). The force that pushes electricity | V | Volt (V) | AA battery 1.5V, car or solar battery 12V, household outlet 220V |
| Current | The amount of water flowing in the pipe. The amount of electricity actually flowing | I | Ampere (A) | 1A = 1,000mA (milliamperes) |
| Resistance | How narrow the pipe is. How much it blocks flow | R | Ohm (Ω) | Greater with thinner and longer wire, smaller with thicker and shorter wire |

---
### 1-3. Ohm's Law
> **V = I × R** (voltage = current × resistance)

| Value to find | Formula | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Voltage | V = I × R | To pass 2A through a 4Ω resistance, 2 × 4 = 8V |
| Current | I = V ÷ R | Connecting a 6Ω bulb to a 12V battery gives 12 ÷ 6 = 2A |
| Resistance | R = V ÷ I | If applying 24V makes 3A flow, 24 ÷ 3 = 8Ω |

---
### 1-4. Power (W) and Energy (Wh)
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Power and energy express how much electricity is used, and they are the basis for deciding the size of a solar system.

- Power (W): the rate at which electricity is being used right at this moment. 1kW = 1,000W.

> **P = V × I** (power = voltage × current)
> Example) An electric blanket using 5A from a 220V outlet: 220 × 5 = 1,100W (1.1kW)

- Energy (Wh): the amount obtained by adding up how long it was used at that rate. It is the basis for electricity bills and battery capacity. 1kWh = 1,000Wh.

> **Wh = W × h** (energy = power × time)
> Example) A 10W LED light on for 5 hours: 10 × 5 = 50Wh

#### Calculating Daily Energy Consumption
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Multiply each device's power consumption by its daily hours of use and add them all up.

| Device | Power consumption | Daily use | Daily energy consumption |
|---|---|---|---|
| LED light | 10W | 5 hours | 50Wh |
| Phone charging | 10W | 2 hours | 20Wh |
| Laptop charging | 50W | 3 hours | 150Wh |
| Fan | 40W | 6 hours | 240Wh |
| Small refrigerator | Intermittent | 24 hours | about 500-800Wh |
| Total (example) | | | about 1,000-1,300Wh |

- Refrigerator: the compressor cycles on and off, so multiplying the rated 100W × 24 hours = 2,400Wh overestimates. In practice it is often 500-800Wh a day. Dividing the product's annual energy consumption (kWh) label by 365 gives the daily value.
- Inverter loss: in converting DC to AC, about 10-15% is lost as heat (efficiency 85-90%). It also consumes standby power just by being switched on without a device in use. Designing without this would drain the battery quickly, so multiply the total of device energy consumption by 1.2 to leave a margin.
- This daily energy consumption is the starting point for deciding panel and battery capacity in Chapter 4.

### 1-5. Direct Current (DC) and Alternating Current (AC)
| Category | How it flows | Where used |
|---|---|---|
| Direct current (DC) | The + and − poles are fixed and current flows in one direction only | Batteries, solar panels, USB, cars—storing electricity and powering electronics |
| Alternating current (AC) | The direction of current reverses dozens of times a second | Household outlets (220V), power plants, transmission lines—things that must be sent far and need transformers |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Solar panels and batteries are DC. Most household appliances (TVs, refrigerators, etc.) run on AC, so an inverter converts DC to AC for them. DC devices such as USB and LED can be used directly without an inverter.

---
### 1-6. Series and Parallel Connection
- Series: connect components in a line, opposite poles together (A's + pole to B's − pole). Voltage adds and current (capacity) stays the same. Example) Two 12V batteries in series give 24V, and the capacity (Ah) is the same as with one.
- Parallel: join same poles together (+ with +, − with −) side by side. Voltage stays the same and current (capacity) adds. Example) Two 12V 100Ah in parallel give 12V 200Ah.

> **Caution:** When connecting batteries in parallel, the voltage (remaining charge) of the two batteries must be exactly the same. If the voltages differ, a large current flows instantly and can cause a fire or explosion. Connect only batteries of the same maker, capacity, and purchase date.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In solar systems, if voltage is short, connect in series; if current (capacity) is short, connect in parallel.

- Panels in series: connecting two 12V panels in series can be used for a 24V system.
- Panels in parallel: current (power) increases and more electricity is produced.
- Batteries are built by the same principle into the voltage and capacity you want, such as 12V or 24V.
