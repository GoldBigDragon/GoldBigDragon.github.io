## 1. Basics of Electricity
---
### 1-1. What is Electricity?
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;All matter is composed of tiny atoms, and inside these atoms are electrons that carry a negative (−) electrical charge. The flow of these electrons en masse in a single direction is precisely what constitutes electricity (electric current).

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Matter can be classified into two categories based on how easily electrons flow through it:

| Classification | Description | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Conductors | Electrons flow easily | Copper, aluminum, iron, saline water, the human body |
| Insulators | Electrons barely flow | Rubber, plastic, dry wood, glass, dry air |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In the case of electrical wires, the inner copper is a conductor, while the outer sheathing that wraps around it is an insulator. Thanks to this insulation, it is safe to hold wires with your bare hands; however, if the sheathing is stripped or becomes wet, that area becomes hazardous.

### 1-2. Analogizing Electricity to Water
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Electricity can feel difficult to comprehend because it is invisible. However, you can understand it much more easily by analogizing it to a water tank and pipes.

```
　　┌────────────────┐
　　│　　　　　　　　│
　　│～～～～～～～～│　← Water Tank Height = Voltage (V)
　　│　　　　　　　　│　　(Higher height = stronger water flow)
　　└─────┐　　┌─────┘
　　　　　│　　│
　　　　　│　　│
　　　　　│　　│　　　　← Pipe = Wire
　　　　　│　　│
　　　　　│　　│
　　　　　└┐　┌┘
　　　　　 │　│　 　　　← Narrowed Section = Resistance (R)
　　　　　 │　│　　 　　　(Narrower section = less water flow)
　　　　　 │　│
　　　　　  ↓↓
 　Amount of Flowing Water = Current (I)
```

- The higher the water tank (Voltage ↑), the stronger the water flows (Current ↑).
- The narrower the pipe (Resistance ↑), the less water flows (Current ↓).

| Concept | Analogy | Unit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Voltage (V) | Water tank height (Water pressure) | Volt (V) |
| Current (I) | Amount of water flowing through the pipe | Ampere (A) |
| Resistance (R) | Narrowness of the pipe | Ohm (Ω) |

---
### 1-3. Voltage, Current, and Resistance
#### 1-3-1. Voltage (V, Volts): 'The Pushing Force'
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The pressure that pushes electricity forward. It corresponds to the height of the water tank.
- Symbol: V, Unit: Volt (V)
- Examples: 1.5V household batteries, 12V automotive/solar batteries, 220V residential wall outlets

#### 1-3-2. Current (I, Amperes): 'The Flowing Volume'
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The actual amount of electricity flowing through a circuit. It corresponds to the volume of water passing through a pipe.
- Symbol: I, Unit: Ampere (A)
- Smaller unit: Milliampere (mA), where 1A = 1,000mA

#### 1-3-3. Resistance (R, Ohms): 'The Degree of Obstruction'
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The degree to which the flow of electric current is opposed. It increases as the pipe becomes narrower.
- Symbol: R, Unit: Ohm (Ω)
- The thinner and longer a wire is, the higher its resistance (↑); the thicker and shorter it is, the lower its resistance (↓).

---
### 1-4. Ohm's Law
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Voltage, current, and resistance are interconnected by the following single equation:

> ## V = I × R
> (Voltage = Current × Resistance)

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;By rearranging this equation, you can solve for whichever variable you need to find:

| Value to Solve For | Formula |
| --- | --- |
| To find Voltage | V = I × R |
| To find Current | I = V ÷ R |
| To find Resistance | R = V ÷ I |

#### 1-4-1. The VIR Triangle
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In this triangle, covering the variable you want to find with your finger reveals the formula to use.  

```
        ┌─────────┐
        │    V    │     · Cover V →  I × R
        ├────┬────┤     · Cover I →  V ÷ R
        │  I │ R  │     · Cover R →  V ÷ I
        └────┴────┘
   (Top/Bottom represents division; Left/Right represents multiplication)
```

#### 1-4-2. Examples
Example 1) What is the current when a light bulb with a resistance of 6Ω is connected to a 12V battery?
→ I = V ÷ R = 12 ÷ 6 = 2A

Example 2) When 24V was applied to a device, a current of 3A flowed through it. What is the resistance of this device?
→ R = V ÷ I = 24 ÷ 3 = 8Ω

Example 3) How many volts are required to drive a current of 2A through a resistance of 4Ω?
→ V = I × R = 2 × 4 = 8V

### 1-5. Power (W) and Energy (Wh)
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;While voltage, current, and resistance define the "properties of electricity," power and energy indicate "how much electricity is being consumed." These values serve as crucial baselines for sizing a solar power system.

#### 1-5-1. Power (W, Watts): 'The Real-Time Intensity'
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Power represents the rate at which electricity is being consumed at any given moment.

> ## P = V × I
> (Power = Voltage × Current)  

- Unit: Watt (W); larger unit: Kilowatt (kW), where 1kW = 1,000W.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;(Example) An electric blanket drawing 5A from a 220V outlet: P = 220 × 5 = 1,100W (= 1.1kW)

#### 1-5-2. Energy Consumption (Wh, kWh): 'Duration of Use = Accumulated Amount'
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Energy consumption is the cumulative power used over a specific duration. If power (W) is analogous to "how wide a faucet is turned open," energy consumption (Wh) corresponds to "the total volume of water collected." It serves as the metric for electric bills and battery capacities.

> ## Energy (Wh) = Power (W) × Time (h)

- Unit: Watt-hour (Wh); larger unit: Kilowatt-hour (kWh), where 1kWh = 1,000Wh.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;(Example) Operating a 10W LED bulb for 5 hours: 10W × 5h = 50Wh

#### 1-5-3. Calculating Daily Energy Consumption
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Identify the electronic appliances used in your household and multiply their power consumption by their daily usage hours to determine the total daily energy consumption.

| Appliance | Power Rating | Daily Usage | Daily Energy Consumption |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| LED Bulb | 10W | 5 hours | 50Wh |
| Phone Charging | 10W | 2 hours | 20Wh |
| Laptop Charging | 50W | 3 hours | 150Wh |
| Electric Fan | 40W | 6 hours | 240Wh |
| Small Refrigerator | (See explanation below) | 24 hours | Approx. 500–800Wh |
| Total (Example) | | | Approx. 1,000–1,300Wh |

- Precautions for Refrigerator Calculations: Even though a refrigerator remains plugged in constantly, its compressor cycles on and off (intermittent operation). Therefore, a simple multiplication like "100W rating × 24 hours = 2,400Wh" will drastically overestimate consumption. In reality, actual usage often stays around 500–800Wh per day. You can obtain a realistic daily estimate by dividing the appliance's stated annual energy consumption (kWh) by 365.  
- Inverter Conversion Losses: An inverter is a device that converts DC power into AC power. When operating an inverter, approximately 10–15% of the power is dissipated as heat during the DC-to-AC conversion process (resulting in an efficiency of roughly 85–90%). Furthermore, an inverter consumes a certain baseline "idle/standby power" simply by being turned on, even when no appliances are actively drawing electricity. Failing to account for these factors means your battery bank will drain much faster than anticipated in a disaster scenario, causing the system to fail prematurely. Therefore, you must multiply the total consumption of your household appliances by a factor of 1.2 to build in an appropriate safety margin.  

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The calculated "daily energy consumption" figure serves as the essential starting point for determining the required solar panel array and battery bank capacities in [4. System Design].

### 1-6. Direct Current (DC) vs. Alternating Current (AC), and Series vs. Parallel Connections
#### 1-6-1. Direct Current (DC) and Alternating Current (AC)
| Classification | Flow Mechanism | Applications |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Direct Current (DC) | The positive (+) and negative (−) terminals are fixed, causing the electric current to flow in a single, continuous direction. | Used for energy storage or within electronic devices, such as batteries, solar panels, USB ports, and automotive electronics. |
| Alternating Current (AC) | The direction of the electric current alternates back and forth dozens of times per second. | Used in residential wall outlets (220V), power plants, and transmission lines where electricity must be transmitted over long distances and voltage transformation is required. |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Both solar panels and batteries operate entirely on DC. However, most household appliances (such as TVs and refrigerators) require AC to run. Consequently, an "inverter" is implemented to bridge this gap by converting DC into AC. (Conversely, native DC devices like USB chargers or LED lights can be powered directly from the system without an inverter).

### 1-6-2. Series Connections: 'Voltages Aggregate'
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;A configuration where components are connected end-to-end in a single line by joining opposite terminals (e.g., connecting the positive terminal of Component A to the negative terminal of Component B). In a series circuit, the total voltage aggregates while the current (and overall capacity) remains unchanged.  

```
 [+]──[ 12V Battery 1 ]──[ 12V Battery 2 ]──[−]
               Combined Total = 24V
(The Ah capacity remains identical to a single battery)
```

### 1-6-3. Parallel Connections: 'Currents (Capacities) Aggregate'
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;A configuration where components are connected side-by-side by grouping identical terminals together (+ to +, and − to −). In a parallel circuit, the voltage remains constant while the current (and overall capacity) aggregates. However, when connecting batteries in parallel, it is an absolute requirement that both batteries possess completely identical voltage levels (states of charge). Wiring batteries with disparate voltages can instantly trigger an massive rush of current, resulting in fire risks or catastrophic battery explosions. It is highly recommended to only parallel batteries that share the identical manufacturer, capacity rating, and purchase date.  

```
        ┌──[ 12V Battery 1 ]──┐
   [+]──┤                     ├──[−]
        └──[ 12V Battery 2 ]──┘
Voltage = Remains 12V,  Capacity = Doubles
(e.g., Two 12V 100Ah batteries → 12V 200Ah)
```

### 1-6-4. Series and Parallel Connections in Solar Systems
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;A firm grasp of series and parallel connections is indispensable for configuring a solar power system to match your targeted voltage and capacity specifications.

- Wiring solar panels in series → Increases the voltage (e.g., Connecting two 12V panels in series allows them to be utilized in a 24V system).
- Wiring solar panels in parallel → Increases the current and overall wattage output (generating a greater volume of electricity).

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;With this knowledge, you can build your desired solar power system by connecting components in series to increase voltage, or in parallel to scale up current and capacity. Batteries follow the exact same principle to construct a storage bank tailored to 12V, 24V, or any required design.
