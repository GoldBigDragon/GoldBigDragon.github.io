## Introduction
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;When a power outage drags on, the first thing people think of is a generator. However, electricity itself is not light or heat, but merely a convenient form for transferring and precisely managing energy. Converting any form of energy into electricity and then converting it back into heat or light incurs losses at every single stage. Therefore, trying to boil water with electricity when firewood is available, or running a generator to turn on lights when an oil lamp is at hand, amounts to doing the easiest tasks by the most expensive means.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There are distinct domains where electricity is truly irreplaceable: telecommunications, receiving and recording information, medical equipment, and charging flashlights and two-way radios. These cannot function without electricity at all, and they share the common trait of having very low power consumption. The energy required to run a single LED bulb, a radio, and a mobile phone all day long is less than what an electric kettle consumes in 10 minutes. Therefore, a power generation plan in a disaster situation is not about securing a large generator, but about first paring down the list of essential electrical needs and selecting the minimal setup that fits that list.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This document is not intended for memorizing the steps to assemble a generator, but for understanding why hydropower is the most realistic continuous power source in disaster situations, why head (drop height) is a far easier variable to handle than flow rate, why wind power becomes useless when wind speeds drop even slightly, and why lead-acid batteries should only be discharged down to 50%.
