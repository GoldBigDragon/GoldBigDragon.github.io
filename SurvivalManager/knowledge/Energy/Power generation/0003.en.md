## 2. Common Principles of Generation and Generators
### 2-1. Relative Motion of Magnet and Coil
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Except for solar power, almost all power generation methods share the exact same final step: electromagnetic induction, where voltage is generated across a conductor when the conductor and a magnetic field move relative to each other. Whether hydropower, wind, or steam, what is done in the preceding stages ultimately boils down to creating this relative motion.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The magnitude of the generated voltage is proportional to how rapidly the magnetic field changes. Thus, there are only three ways to increase voltage: using a stronger magnet, winding more turns of coil, or spinning it faster. Among these three, the easiest to alter in the field is rotation speed; therefore, the design of power generation equipment mostly comes down to a matter of matching the RPM.

---
### 2-2. Turning a Motor in Reverse Makes a Generator
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Motors and generators are not inherently different machines. Supplying electricity to turn the shaft makes it a motor; turning the shaft to draw electricity makes it a generator. However, not all motors perform equally well as generators.

| Type | Can It Be Used as a Generator? | Characteristics and Conditions |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Permanent Magnet DC Motor | Works extremely well | Outputs DC directly proportional to RPM. Salvaged from power drills, treadmills, electric scooters |
| Brushless Motor | Works | Outputs 3-phase AC, requiring rectification. Salvaged from drones and e-bikes |
| Stepper Motor | Works for small scale | Outputs voltage even at very low RPM, but power output is small |
| Car Alternator | Works conditionally | Requires exciting current to the field coil first and needs 1,500+ RPM |
| Standard AC Induction Motor | Tricky | Requires capacitors for self-excitation and has strict RPM constraints |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In a disaster situation, permanent magnet devices hold an overwhelming advantage because they begin generating power on their own without an external power source. Car alternators are common, robust, and include built-in rectification circuits, making them seem attractive; however, because consuming electricity is required to excite the field coil, power consumption exceeds output if rotated too slowly. They become usable only when significantly stepped up in speed via belts and pulleys, a process during which efficiency losses accrue once more.

---
### 2-3. Rotation Speed Determines Voltage
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Most permanent magnet generators produce a voltage nearly directly proportional to their rotation speed. This simple relationship creates two major constraints.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;First, if the rotation is too slow, the generator voltage drops below the battery voltage, and no current flows at all. To charge a 12V lead-acid battery, roughly 13.5V or higher must be produced; until reaching the rotation speed that yields that voltage, nothing happens even if the generator spins. Second, if the rotation is too fast, overvoltage occurs, boiling the battery and scorching appliances.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Thus, power sources that spin very slowly, like water wheels, require step-up mechanisms. Rotation speed is increased via pulley diameter ratios or gear tooth ratios, but because losses multiply as stages increase, it is best to complete the step-up in a single stage if possible. Belts cause losses through slippage, but they also serve to protect the machinery by slipping when overloaded.

| Problem | Symptom | Countermeasure |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Insufficient RPM | Voltage is lower than battery, failing to charge | Increase step-up ratio or add more coil turns |
| Excessive RPM | Voltage spikes and battery off-gasses | Slow down rotation or limit via charge controller |
| Excessive Load | Generator drags heavily and RPM drops | Reduce load or enlarge water turbine |
| No Load | Rotation runs away uncontrollably | Keep a load connected at all times |

---
### 2-4. Blocking Diode
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;If a generator and a battery are connected directly, during periods when the generator stops—such as at night or during dry spells—the battery pushes current back into the generator. At that moment, the generator turns into a motor, consuming battery power and spinning uselessly. The absurd situation where a water wheel spins backwards against the flow actually occurs.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Preventing this requires a blocking diode. A single component that restricts current flow strictly from the generator toward the battery resolves the problem; without it, the battery drains empty in a matter of days. Since a voltage drop of around 0.5V occurs across the diode, the generator voltage must have sufficient margin to compensate.

---
### 2-5. Excess Power Needs a Dump Load
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Unlike solar power, hydropower and wind power do not stop on their own. When the battery reaches full charge and the charge controller disconnects charging, the generator suddenly loses its load, causing its rotation speed to spike instantaneously. In wind power, turbine blades break; in hydropower, overvoltage damages wiring and equipment.

> **Continuous power generation systems must have a load to dump excess power as heat. Installing a heating element or resistor immersed in a water tank allows the generator to reliably maintain a load even after the battery is fully charged.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This dump load disposes of electricity, but it is not entirely wasted. Using it to heat water yields hot water, and using it to dry grain aids in drying. Since it is electricity that would otherwise dissipate due to lack of storage, putting it to use even at low efficiency is far better.
