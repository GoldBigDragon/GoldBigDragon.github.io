# Candle
---
## 1. Principle
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The mechanism by which a candle illuminates is rooted in physical phenomena. The wick is composed of numerous microscopic fibers; once ignited, the fuel surrounding the wick liquefies and is drawn upward through the minute gaps of the wick via capillary action.  

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Upon reaching the tip of the wick, the liquid fuel transitions into a gaseous state due to the heat of the flame. This vaporized fuel then reacts with oxygen in the air to undergo combustion (forming a flame), emitting light and heat.  

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The thermal energy from the flame continuously melts the solid wax or liquid oil below, sustaining a cyclical process that draws fuel up into the wick through capillary action.

---
## 2. Materials
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The components required for manufacturing a candle are categorized into fuel, wicks, and containers. Historically, initial candle manufacturing utilized animal fats (tallow, lard) and beeswax as fuel sources. Modern candles employ a diverse range of materials, including paraffin, beeswax, and soy wax derived from soybean oil.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Cotton twine, paper, cloth, and even fibrous citrus stems can serve as wicks. However, care must be exercised when using printed paper (e.g., newspaper), as it produces significant soot and carries a risk of generating sparks during combustion.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Glass, tin cans, and ceramics are suitable candle containers as they prevent liquid oil or melted wax from leaking. Materials such as plastic and wood are vulnerable to thermal damage and should be avoided.

---
## 3. Precautions
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;When manufacturing and operating candles, safety guidelines must be strictly observed to minimize fire hazards and health risks.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Prior to utilizing a candle, eliminate any combustible materials from the surrounding area, and always extinguish the candle before leaving a room unattended.  

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Because the base of a candle container can reach high temperatures, do not place it directly on wooden or heat-sensitive surfaces; instead, utilize a slate or heat-resistant coaster.  

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Improvised emergency candles can generate soot and particulate matter, and pose risks of oxygen depletion; therefore, periodic ventilation is mandatory during indoor use.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Always trim the wick to a length of 0.6 cm before lighting a candle. An excessively long wick causes the flame to flare too high and flicker, which generates soot and promotes mushrooming (carbon accumulation) that can lead to overheating.  

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;For safety purposes, ensure that oil-based emergency candles do not exceed 4 hours of continuous combustion.

## 4. Manufacturing
### 4-1. Tuna Can Candle
| Step | Instructions |
| :---: | :--- |
| 1 | Prepare a cotton string and a tuna can packed in vegetable oil. |
| 2 | Cut the cotton string slightly longer than the height of the tuna can. |
| 3 | Pierce a small hole in the center of the can lid, insert the cotton wick through the hole, and allow it to saturate fully in the oil. |
| 4 | Ignite the tip of the wick to initiate combustion. |

### 4-2. Orange Peel Candle
| Step | Instructions |
| :---: | :--- |
| 1 | Cut an orange or grapefruit in half, then carefully extract the pulp, ensuring the central white pith remains intact and attached to the bottom of the peel. |
| 2 | Fill the peel container with vegetable oil to a level that leaves only the tip of the pith exposed to the air. |
| 3 | Apply a small amount of vegetable oil to the top of the pith to facilitate easy ignition. |
| 4 | Ignite the tip of the pith to initiate combustion. |

### 4-3. Butter Candle
| Step | Instructions |
| :---: | :--- |
| 1 | Cut an appropriately sized block from a stick of butter. |
| 2 | Drill a deep hole through the center using a thin screwdriver or an awl. |
| 3 | Insert the wick into the hole, and apply a small amount of butter to the top of the wick to facilitate easy ignition. |
| 4 | Ignite the tip of the wick to initiate combustion. |

### 4-4. Wax Candle
| Step | Instructions |
| :---: | :--- |
| 1 | Measure the required amount of wax to match the volume of the mold. |
| 2 | Melt the wax using a double boiler to a temperature of 70–80°C, ensuring the temperature does not exceed 90°C. |
| 3 | Remove the wax from the heat source once it reaches 70°C. |
| 4 | If incorporating fragrance oil, measure it at 6% of the wax weight by weight (g), rather than by volume (ml), and blend. |
| 5 | Stir gently for 2 minutes after adding the fragrance oil to ensure it physically disperses and dissolves uniformly within the wax. |
| 6 | Wait for the wax to cool to approximately 55–60°C (when it begins to turn slightly opaque) or to the pouring temperature recommended by the wax manufacturer. |
| 7 | Pour the wax slowly and steadily into the center of the mold to prevent surface shrinkage. |
| 8 | Secure the wick in the center while the wax solidifies, and allow it to cool for at least 24 hours in an environment free from temperature fluctuations. |
| 9 | Cure the candle for 3 to 14 days after the initial solidification process to enhance its performance and quality. |

## 5. Raw Material Extraction Methods
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;When commercial wax is unavailable during emergency scenarios, natural materials harvested from the environment can be refined and utilized as candle fuel.

### 5-1. Animal Wax Extraction
| Step | Instructions |
| :---: | :--- |
| 1 | Thoroughly wash the fatty tissues of beef (tallow) or pork (lard) to remove impurities. |
| 2 | Chop the fat as finely as possible to maximize the surface area exposed to heat. |
| 3 | Place a minimal amount of water along with the fat in a pot and render over low heat using a double boiler method. |
| 4 | Once the fat melts into a liquid, filter out the solid residues using a fine cloth or mesh. |
| 5 | Allow the strained liquid to cool, which solidifies it into a natural, firm animal wax. |

### 5-2. Comb Beeswax Extraction
| Step | Instructions |
| :---: | :--- |
| 1 | Place the honeycomb material remaining after honey harvesting into water and heat it. |
| 2 | Once the beeswax has melted completely, filter out the impurities through a cloth while the mixture is still hot. |
| 3 | Allow the filtered solution to cool undisturbed; because beeswax is lighter than water, it solidifies into a distinct layer on the surface. |
| 4 | Detach the solidified beeswax block, scrape away any remaining debris from the bottom layer, and repeat the refining process once more before use. |
