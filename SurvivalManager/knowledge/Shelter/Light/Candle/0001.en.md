# Candle
---
## 1. Principle
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;A candle gives light through capillary action and a continuous combustion cycle.

1. Once lit, the fuel around the wick liquefies and rises through the tiny gaps in the wick by capillary action.
2. The liquid fuel that reaches the tip of the wick is vaporized by the heat of the flame.
3. The vaporized fuel combines with oxygen in the air and burns, producing light and heat.
4. The heat of the flame keeps melting the solid wax or liquid oil below and draws it up the wick.

---
## 2. Materials
- Fuel: Early candles used animal fat (beef fat, pork fat, beeswax). Modern candles use paraffin, beeswax, soy wax extracted from soybean oil, and similar materials.
- Wick: Cotton thread, paper, cloth, or the fibers of citrus fruit can be used. Printed paper such as newspaper produces a lot of soot and can throw sparks while burning.
- Container: Glass, a can, or ceramic that does not leak oil or wax. Plastic and wood are vulnerable to heat and should not be used.

---
## 3. Precautions
- Before use, clear flammable objects from the surroundings, and put the candle out whenever you leave.
- The bottom of a candle gets hot, so do not place it directly on wood or other heat-sensitive surfaces. Use a slate or a heat-resistant stand.
- Homemade emergency candles give off soot and fine particles and can cause poor ventilation, so ventilate the room regularly when using them indoors.
- Trim the wick to 0.6 cm before burning. A long wick makes the flame tall and unsteady, and soot and mushroom-shaped carbon buildup can cause overheating.
- Burn an oil-based emergency candle for no more than 4 hours continuously.

## 4. Making Candles
### 4-1. Tuna Can Candle
1. Prepare a can of tuna packed in cooking oil and a cotton string.
2. Cut the cotton string slightly longer than the height of the can.
3. Poke a small hole in the center of the can lid, insert the cotton string as a wick, and soak it thoroughly in the oil.
4. Light the tip of the wick.

### 4-2. Orange Peel Candle
1. Cut an orange or grapefruit in half and carefully remove the flesh. Leave the white central pith (the wick) in place at the bottom of the peel without damaging it.
2. Fill the peel with cooking oil so that only the tip of the pith is exposed to air.
3. Rub a little cooking oil on top of the pith so that it lights easily.
4. Light the tip of the wick.

### 4-3. Butter Candle
1. Cut a suitable piece from a block of butter.
2. Use a thin screwdriver or an awl to drill a deep hole in the center.
3. Insert the wick into the hole and rub a little butter on top of the wick so that it lights easily.
4. Light the tip of the wick.

### 4-4. Wax Candle
1. Measure the wax to match the volume of the mold.
2. Melt the wax in a double boiler to 70-80 °C, without exceeding 90 °C.
3. Remove the wax from the heat once it reaches 70 °C.
4. If you use fragrance, measure it by weight (g), not volume (ml), at 6% of the wax weight, and mix it in.
5. After adding the fragrance, stir gently for 2 minutes so that it disperses and dissolves in the wax.
6. Let the wax cool to about 55-60 °C, when it turns slightly opaque, or to the pouring temperature recommended by the wax manufacturer.
7. Pour the wax slowly and steadily into the center of the mold to prevent surface shrinkage.
8. While the wax sets, hold the wick in the center and let it cool for at least 24 hours in a place with no temperature changes.
9. After the initial hardening, cure the candle for 3-14 days to improve its quality.

## 5. Extracting Raw Materials
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;When commercial wax is unavailable, refine natural materials from your surroundings and use them as candle fuel.

### 5-1. Animal Wax
1. Wash the fatty tissue of beef (tallow) or pork (lard) thoroughly to remove impurities.
2. Cut the fat as finely as possible to widen the surface exposed to heat.
3. Put a very small amount of water and the fat in a pot and heat it gently in a double boiler.
4. When the fat has melted into a liquid, strain out the residue through a fine cloth or mesh.
5. When the strained liquid cools, it becomes solid animal wax.

### 5-2. Beeswax from Honeycomb
1. Put the honeycomb (beeswax) left after harvesting honey into water and heat it to no more than 85 °C.
2. When the beeswax has melted completely, strain out the impurities through a cloth while it is still hot.
3. Let the strained liquid cool as it is. Because beeswax is lighter than water, it forms a layer on top as it hardens.
4. Lift off the hardened slab of beeswax, scrape the impurities from its underside, and refine it once more before use.
