## 1. Do Not Eat What You Do Not Know
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The most important knowledge in foraging is not which plants are edible but the habit of never putting anything in your mouth that you are not sure about. Hunger clouds judgment, and clouded judgment always leans the same way: toward persuading yourself that it is probably fine.

> **Eat only what you know for certain. A judgment that it is probably that plant means do not eat it.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This rule must be stricter than other survival rules because of the asymmetry between gains and losses. If you misjudge and do not eat, you lose one meal's worth of calories. If you misjudge and eat, you lose several days of working capacity, your liver or kidneys, or your life. A person can last for weeks on water alone, but a damaged liver does not recover. Hunger can be reversed after a few days; poisoning cannot.

### 1-1. Calories
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Looking at the calories of wild plants in numbers makes it clear what to look for. The values below vary with species and condition, but they are enough to show the differences in orders of magnitude.

| Forage | Calories per 100 g | Main components |
| --- | :---: | --- |
| Wild leafy greens (mugwort, shepherd's purse, ixeris, etc.) | 20-50 kcal | 85-90% water; the rest is mostly fiber |
| Wild shoots (Korean angelica tree shoots, daylily, etc.) | 30-60 kcal | Water and a small amount of protein |
| Wild fruits (hardy kiwi, Korean wild grape, wild raspberry) | 50-80 kcal | Sugar and water |
| Raw roots of yam and kudzu | 100-130 kcal | Starch and fiber |
| Chestnut | 190-220 kcal | Starch |
| Acorn (flour after leaching) | 340-390 kcal | Starch and a small amount of fat |
| Starch extracted from kudzu | 330-360 kcal | Almost pure starch |
| Nuts such as pine nuts, walnuts, and Manchurian walnuts | 600-700 kcal | More than half is fat |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Between leafy greens and nuts there is a difference of more than tenfold, and this difference decides whether foraging succeeds. Climbing up and down a mountain all day to pick greens costs 800-1,500 kcal. Even 2 kg of leafy greens returns only 400-1,000 kcal, and much of that is indigestible fiber, so the amount actually absorbed is smaller still. Under a tree where acorns or chestnuts have fallen, on the other hand, 5,000 kcal or more can be gathered in the same time.

> **Leafy greens are a supplement, not a staple. Calories come only from seeds, nuts, and roots.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This does not mean leafy greens are useless. On stored grain and meat alone, vitamin C, folate, potassium, and magnesium run short within a few weeks, and bleeding gums and slow wound healing appear. Leafy greens are decisive in preventing these deficiencies but have almost nothing to do with filling the stomach. If the two roles are not distinguished, a person keeps picking greens every day and still grows weaker.

### 1-2. Seasons
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Plants change where they store resources with the seasons. In spring they push what was stored in the roots up into new shoots; in summer they move what the leaves produce into fruits and seeds; in autumn they store what remains back into roots and seeds. The seasonality of foraging is a matter of following this movement.

| Season | What mainly appears | Calorie contribution | Strategy |
| --- | --- | :---: | --- |
| Early spring (March-April) | New shoots, young leaves, inner bark of trees | Low | Replenish the micronutrients the body has run down over winter |
| Late spring and early summer (May-June) | Leafy greens, young roots, first fruits | Low | Harvest and dry before the leaves toughen |
| Midsummer (July-August) | Fruits and seeds; leaves toughen | Medium | Fruit is mostly sugar and hard to store, so it must be dried |
| Autumn (September-November) | Nuts, acorns, roots, mushrooms, seeds | Highest | Most of the year's stored food is decided in this period |
| Winter (December-February) | Roots, inner bark, stored food | Very low | Almost nothing new is obtained; it is a season of consumption |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Autumn is overwhelmingly important because that is when plants gather their resources in the most concentrated form. Seeds and nuts are the storehouses a plant builds for the next generation, so they are low in water and rich in starch and fat. Low water also means they dry well and keep for a long time, which makes them the best food for storage as well. The results of a few weeks in autumn decide survival until the following spring.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In winter, the only resources left are roots and the inner bark of trees. Roots store starch through the winter, so they are actually higher in calories than in summer. The thin cambium between the wood and the outer bark of pine, birch, elm, and similar trees contains sugars and starch and can be chewed or dried and ground into flour. However, a tree stripped all the way around its trunk loses its channel for water and nutrients and dies, so bark must be stripped lengthwise from one side only.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Place must be considered together with the season. Even for the same species, the timing differs by more than two weeks between a dry south-facing slope and a damp north-facing valley. If you miss the timing in one place, you can meet the same plant again by climbing higher or moving to a north-facing slope. Using this time lag can effectively extend the harvest period of a single species by two to three times.
