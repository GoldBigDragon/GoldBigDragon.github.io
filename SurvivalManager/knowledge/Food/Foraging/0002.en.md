## 1. If You Don't Know It, Don't Eat It
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The single most important skill in foraging is not knowing which plants are edible, but the habit of never putting something in your mouth unless you are certain of it. Hunger clouds judgment, and clouded judgment always leans the same way: toward convincing yourself that it is probably fine.

> **Eat only what you know for certain. "It's probably this" means the same thing as "don't eat it."**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This rule has to be stricter than other survival rules because the gains and losses are not symmetric. Misjudge and skip a plant, and you lose one meal's worth of calories. Misjudge and eat it, and you may lose days of ability to function, your liver, your kidneys, or your life. A person can go many weeks on water alone, but a damaged liver does not heal. Starvation can be reversed days later; poisoning often cannot be reversed at all.

### 1-1. The Calories Foraging Actually Returns
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Looking at the calorie counts of wild plants as numbers makes clear what to look for. The figures below vary by species and condition, but they are enough to show the difference in order of magnitude.

| Foraged food | kcal per 100g | Main component |
| --- | :---: | --- |
| Wild leafy greens (mugwort, shepherd's purse, toothed lettuce, etc.) | 20~50kcal | 85~90% water, the rest mostly fiber |
| Wild-green shoots (aralia shoots, daylily shoots, etc.) | 30~60kcal | Water and a small amount of protein |
| Wild fruit (hardy kiwi, wild grape, wild raspberry) | 50~80kcal | Sugar and water |
| Raw roots of Chinese yam and kudzu | 100~130kcal | Starch and fiber |
| Chestnuts | 190~220kcal | Starch |
| Acorns (flour after leaching) | 340~390kcal | Starch and a small amount of fat |
| Starch strained from kudzu | 330~360kcal | Almost pure starch |
| Nuts such as pine nuts, walnuts, Manchurian walnuts | 600~700kcal | More than half fat |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There is more than a tenfold difference between leafy greens and nuts, and this difference decides whether foraging succeeds or fails. Spending a full day climbing up and down the mountain gathering greens costs 800-1,500kcal. Even gathering 2kg of leafy greens alone recovers only 400-1,000kcal, and because much of that is indigestible fiber, the amount actually absorbed is smaller still. Under a tree that has dropped its acorns or chestnuts, by contrast, you can gather more than 5,000kcal in the same amount of time.

> **Leafy greens are not food, they are a supplement. Calories come only from seeds, nuts, and roots.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;That does not mean leafy greens are useless. Living on stored grain and meat alone leads within a few weeks to a shortage of vitamin C, folate, potassium, and magnesium, with bleeding gums and slow wound healing. Leafy greens are decisive for preventing this kind of deficiency, but they have almost nothing to do with filling the stomach. Fail to keep these two roles separate, and you keep growing weaker even while gathering greens every day.

### 1-2. What Each Season Gives Up
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Plants shift where they store their resources with the seasons. In spring they push what was stored in the roots up into new shoots; in summer they move what the leaves have made into fruit and seed; in autumn they store what is left back into roots and seed again. The seasonality of foraging means tracking this movement.

| Season | What mainly appears | Calorie contribution | Strategy |
| --- | --- | :---: | --- |
| Early spring, March~April | New shoots, young leaves, inner tree bark | Low | Replenish the micronutrients the body lost over winter |
| Late spring and early summer, May~June | Leafy greens, young roots, first fruit | Low | Harvest and dry before the leaves toughen |
| Midsummer, July~August | Fruit and seed; leaves toughen | Medium | Fruit is high in sugar and hard to store, so it must be dried |
| Autumn, September~November | Nuts, acorns, roots, mushrooms, seeds | Highest | Most of a year's stored food is decided in this period |
| Winter, December~February | Roots, inner tree bark, stored food | Very low | Almost nothing new is gained; this is the season of consuming what was stored |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Autumn matters overwhelmingly more because this is when plants gather their resources into the most concentrated form. Seeds and nuts are the storehouse a plant builds for the next generation, so they are low in water and high in starch and fat. Being low in water also means they dry well and keep for a long time, which makes them the best suited of all for storage as food. What is gathered in a few autumn weeks decides survival all the way through to the following spring.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The only resource still left in winter is roots and the inner bark of trees. Roots store starch over winter, so their calorie content is actually higher than in summer. In pine (Pinus densiflora), birch (Betula platyphylla), elm (Ulmus davidiana var. japonica), and similar trees, the thin cambium layer between the wood and the outer bark contains sugar and starch and can be chewed or dried and ground into flour. Stripping bark all the way around a trunk, however, cuts off the tree's channel for water and nutrients and kills it, so only one strip should be cut lengthwise along a single side.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Place matters alongside season. Even the same species can appear more than two weeks apart between a dry, south-facing slope and a damp, north-facing valley. If the window is missed in one spot, climbing higher or moving to a north-facing slope can bring you to the same plant again. Using this time lag can effectively double or triple the harvest window for a single species.
