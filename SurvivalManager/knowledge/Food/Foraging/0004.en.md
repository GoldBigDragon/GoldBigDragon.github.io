## 3. Deadly Poisonous Plants and the Edible Plants That Resemble Them
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Poisoning accidents do not happen because someone eats a plant they cannot identify. Most happen because someone was certain they knew the plant and ate it, and it turned out to be something else. So more important than memorizing a list of poisonous plants is knowing, for every edible plant you intend to eat, which poisonous plant resembles it and exactly what trait separates the two — knowing them as a pair.

---
### 3-1. Pairs You Must Know Together
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Most spring poisoning accidents in South Korea come from the combinations below. Knowing only one side of any pair is dangerous — you must know both sides together.

| Edible plant | Poisonous look-alike | Main toxin | Decisive distinguishing trait |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Daylily (wonchuri, Hemerocallis spp.) | False hellebore (yeoro, Veratrum maackii) | Veratrum alkaloids | Daylily's base is smooth; false hellebore's base is wrapped in net-like fiber. False hellebore's leaves are deeply pleated and hairy |
| Daylily, mountain garlic (sanmaneul, Allium ochotense) | False hellebore (baksae, Veratrum oxysepalum) | Veratrum alkaloids | Baksae's leaves are densely alternate along the stem, distinctly pleated, and hairy |
| Mountain garlic (sanmaneul, myeong-i, Allium ochotense) | Lily of the valley (eunbangulkkot, Convallaria keiskei) | Convallatoxin and other cardiac glycosides | Cutting mountain garlic releases a strong garlic smell; lily of the valley has no smell at all |
| Water dropwort (minari, Oenanthe javanica) | Water hemlock (dokminari, Cicuta virosa) | Cicutoxin | Water hemlock has a thick rhizome; cut it lengthwise and you see hollow, partitioned chambers and yellow sap. Water dropwort's root is thin and fibrous like a beard |
| Deodeok (Codonopsis lanceolata), ladybells (jandae, Adenophora triphylla) | Pokeweed (jarigong, Phytolacca esculenta) | Phytolaccatoxin, saponins | Cutting deodeok releases a white latex and a distinctive smell. Pokeweed's root is thick, oozes red sap, and has no smell |
| Chamnamul (Pimpinella brachycarpa) | Water parsnip (gaebalnamul, Sium suave), wild angelica (gaegurittae, Angelica anomala) | Cicutoxin-type compounds, phototoxic substances | Chamnamul's leaf stalk is purple-tinged with a distinct scent. Water-parsnip types grow at the water's edge and smell different |
| Gomchwi (Ligularia fischeri) | Marsh marigold (donguinamul, Caltha palustris) | Protoanemonin | Gomchwi's leaves have soft, fine hairs. Marsh marigold has no hairs, and its leaves are thick and glossy |
| Korean angelica (chamdanggwi, Angelica gigas) | Gaedanggwi (jirigangwhal, Angelica purpuraefolia) | Furanocoumarins and others | Korean angelica's leaf underside is green and glossy; gaedanggwi's underside is pale and dull |
| Umbrella plant (usannamul, Syneilesis palmata) | Whorled paris (satgatnamul, Paris verticillata) | Paristyphnin and other saponins | Umbrella plant's leaf is deeply divided into 5-9 whorled lobes. Whorled paris has 6-8 undivided leaves in a whorl |
| Butterbur (meowi, Petasites japonicus) | Leopard plant (teolmeowi, Farfugium japonicum) | Pyrrolizidine alkaloids | Butterbur is pale green with soft hairs. Leopard plant is dark green and glossy, with dense brown hairs on the underside |
| Hosta (bibichu, Hosta longipes) | Lily of the valley (eunbangulkkot, Convallaria keiskei) | Cardiac glycosides | Hosta's leaf has several fine pleats and a paler color |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;What is worth noting in this table is that every distinguishing trait is a very small detail — fiber at the base, the color of the leaf underside, the presence of hairs, the presence of a smell. If two plants look similar from a distance, go closer and check; and if you still are not certain up close, there is no option other than leaving it alone.

### 3-2. What Poisonous Plants Do to the Body
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;To rank poisonous plants by danger, look at how fast symptoms appear and which organs are damaged. A poison that acts fast feels frightening, but at least it gives a warning; a poison that acts late progresses with no warning at all.

| Toxin family | Representative plants | Where it acts | When symptoms appear |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Cicutoxin | Water hemlock (Cicuta virosa) | Blocks inhibitory signaling in the central nervous system | 15 minutes to 1 hour; starts with whole-body convulsions |
| Veratrum alkaloids | False hellebore (Veratrum maackii, V. oxysepalum) | Bind sodium channels; heart and nerves | 30 minutes to 3 hours; vomiting, slow heart rate, low blood pressure |
| Cardiac glycosides | Lily of the valley (Convallaria keiskei), foxglove (Digitalis purpurea) | Inhibit the sodium pump in heart muscle | 30 minutes to several hours; arrhythmia |
| Tropane alkaloids | Japanese scopolia (michigwangipul, Scopolia japonica), thorn apple (huindokmalpul, Datura stramonium) | Block the parasympathetic nervous system | 30 minutes to 2 hours; hallucinations and dilated pupils |
| Protoanemonin | Marsh marigold (Caltha palustris), the buttercup family (Ranunculaceae) | Directly irritates mucous membranes and skin | Immediate; severe burning in the mouth and stomach |
| Pyrrolizidine alkaloids | Leopard plant (Farfugium japonicum), groundsel (gaessukgat, Senecio vulgaris) | Gradually block the liver's veins | Weeks to months; accumulates with repeated intake |
| Saponins | Pokeweed (Phytolacca esculenta), whorled paris (Paris verticillata) | Destroy cell membranes and red blood cells | 1 hour to several hours; vomiting and diarrhea |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Water hemlock is especially dangerous because of both the strength of its toxin and the speed at which it acts. Cicutoxin blocks the receptors in the brain responsible for inhibitory signaling, so nerve cells cannot stop firing; the result is continuous whole-body convulsions until the breathing muscles exhaust themselves and death follows. The toxin is most concentrated in the root, which makes digging it up and eating the root the most lethal way to encounter it.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Pyrrolizidine alkaloids pose the opposite kind of danger. Eat them once and nothing happens, with nothing odd about the taste either, but they slowly damage the walls of the liver's small veins and cause hepatic vein occlusion over months. By the time symptoms appear, the plant that caused it was eaten many months earlier, making the connection hard to draw. This is exactly why caution is needed before eating any plant regularly over a long period.

> **The fact that nothing happened right after eating it is not proof that the plant is safe.**

### 3-3. Places Where You Should Avoid Foraging Altogether
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Even correctly identifying a plant does not make it edible if the place it grew is bad. Plants draw up whatever is in the soil through their roots as is, so an edible plant grown in contaminated ground becomes a source of contamination itself.

| Places to avoid | Reason |
| --- | --- |
| Around roads and railways | Heavy metals from exhaust and de-icing salt build up in the soil and leaves |
| Farmland edges and orchards | Pesticide and herbicide residue may remain |
| Downslope of livestock barns and manure treatment facilities | E. coli and parasite eggs are present on the surface and in the water |
| Around factories and incineration facilities | Heavy metals and persistent organic matter accumulate in the soil |
| Old mines and waste landfills | Arsenic, cadmium, and lead remain in the soil |
| Roadsides frequented by people and dogs | High risk of fecal contamination and parasites |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Heavy metals do not go away with washing or cooking. Heat kills microbes and breaks down some toxins, but it has no effect at all on elemental lead and cadmium. In places like these, not foraging at all is the only response, no matter how certain the identification is.
