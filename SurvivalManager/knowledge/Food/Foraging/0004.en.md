## 3. Deadly Poisonous Plants and the Edible Plants They Resemble
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Poisoning accidents do not happen because people eat plants they cannot recognize. Most happen when someone ate a plant believing they knew it, and it was something else. So what matters more than memorizing a list of poisonous plants is knowing, for each edible plant you intend to eat, which poisonous plant resembles it and which trait separates the two, as a pair.

---
### 3-1. Combinations That Must Be Known as Pairs
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Most spring poisoning accidents in Korea come from the combinations below. Knowing only one side of a pair is dangerous; both must be known side by side.

| Edible plant | Poisonous look-alike | Main toxic compounds | Decisive distinguishing trait |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Daylily (wonchuri) | False hellebore (yeoro, Veratrum maackii) | Veratrum alkaloids | Daylily has a smooth base, while yeoro has a base wrapped in a net of fibers. Yeoro leaves are deeply pleated and very hairy |
| Daylily, mountain garlic (sanmaneul) | False hellebore (baksae, Veratrum oxysepalum) | Veratrum alkaloids | Baksae leaves are densely alternate along the stem, clearly pleated, and very hairy |
| Mountain garlic (sanmaneul, myeongi) | Lily of the valley | Cardiac glycosides such as convallatoxin | Mountain garlic smells strongly of garlic when cut; lily of the valley has no smell |
| Water dropwort (minari) | Water hemlock (dok-minari) | Cicutoxin | Water hemlock has a thick rhizome, and a lengthwise cut shows hollow chambers with partitions and yellow sap. Water dropwort roots are thin and whisker-like |
| Deodeok (Codonopsis), ladybell (janndae) | Pokeweed (jarigong) | Phytolaccatoxin, saponins | Deodeok gives white latex and a characteristic fragrance when cut. Pokeweed root is thick, gives red sap, and has no fragrance |
| Chamnamul (Korean wild parsley) | Gaebalnamul-type plants, gaegureutdae | Cicutoxin-group compounds, phototoxic compounds | Chamnamul has purplish leaf stalks and a distinct fragrance. Gaebalnamul-type plants grow at the waterside and have a different scent |
| Gomchwi (Fischer's ligularia) | Marsh marigold (dongui-namul) | Protoanemonin | Gomchwi has soft, fine hairs on the leaves. Marsh marigold has no hairs, and its leaves are thick and glossy |
| Chamdanggwi (Korean angelica) | Gaedanggwi (jirigangwhal) | Furanocoumarins, etc. | Chamdanggwi has a green, glossy leaf underside; gaedanggwi has a whitish, matte underside |
| Usannamul (umbrella plant) | Satgatnamul (whorled paris) | Saponins such as paristyphnin | Usannamul leaves are deeply divided and whorled in 5-9 lobes. Satgatnamul has 6-8 undivided leaves in a whorl |
| Butterbur (meowi) | Leopard plant (teolmeowi) | Pyrrolizidine alkaloids | Butterbur is light green with soft hairs. Teolmeowi is dark green and glossy, with dense brown hairs on the underside |
| Hosta (bibichu) | Lily of the valley | Cardiac glycosides | Hosta leaves have several fine pleats and a pale color |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;What stands out in this table is that every distinguishing trait is a very small feature: fibers at the base, the color of the leaf underside, the presence of hairs, the presence of a smell. If plants look alike from a distance, go closer to check, and if you are still not sure up close, there is no choice other than to leave them alone.

---
### 3-2. What Poisonous Plants Do to People
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;To rank poisonous plants by danger, look at how quickly symptoms appear and which organs are damaged. Fast-acting poisons feel frightening but at least give a warning; slow-acting poisons progress without one.

| Toxin group | Representative plants | Site of action | When symptoms appear |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Cicutoxin | Water hemlock | Blocks inhibitory signals in the central nervous system | 15 minutes to 1 hour; begins with whole-body convulsions |
| Veratrum alkaloids | Yeoro, baksae | Bind to sodium channels; heart and nerves | 30 minutes to 3 hours; vomiting, slow heart rate, low blood pressure |
| Cardiac glycosides | Lily of the valley, foxglove | Inhibit the sodium pump of heart muscle | 30 minutes to several hours; arrhythmia |
| Tropane alkaloids | Japanese scopolia (michigwangipul), datura | Block the parasympathetic nervous system | 30 minutes to 2 hours; hallucinations and dilated pupils |
| Protoanemonin | Marsh marigold, buttercup family | Directly irritates mucous membranes and skin | Immediately; severe burning in the mouth and stomach |
| Pyrrolizidine alkaloids | Teolmeowi, groundsel | Gradually block the veins of the liver | Several weeks to several months; accumulates with repeated intake |
| Saponins | Pokeweed, satgatnamul | Destroy cell membranes and red blood cells | 1 hour to several hours; vomiting and diarrhea |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Water hemlock is especially dangerous because of the strength and speed of its toxicity. Cicutoxin blocks the receptors that carry inhibitory signals in the brain, so nerve cells cannot stop firing; the convulsions continue throughout the body until the respiratory muscles tire and death follows. The poison is most concentrated in the root, so digging up and eating the whole root is the most lethal way.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Pyrrolizidine alkaloids are a danger of the opposite character. Nothing happens after a single meal and there is nothing wrong with the taste, but they damage the walls of the small veins in the liver bit by bit and cause hepatic veno-occlusive disease over several months. By the time symptoms appear, the plant was eaten many months earlier, which makes the connection hard to see. This is why caution is needed when trying to eat a plant regularly over a long period.

> **That nothing happened right after eating is no evidence that the plant is safe.**

### 3-3. Places Where Foraging Itself Should Be Avoided
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Even if you identify a plant correctly, it cannot be eaten if the place where it grew is bad. Plants draw up the components of the soil through their roots as they are, so an edible plant grown in contaminated ground becomes a source of contamination itself.

| Places to avoid | Reason |
| --- | --- |
| Around roads and railways | Heavy metals from exhaust and de-icing agents accumulate in the soil and on the leaves |
| Edges of farmland and orchards | Pesticide and herbicide residues may remain |
| Downhill from livestock barns and manure treatment sites | E. coli and parasite eggs are on the surface and in the water |
| Around factories and incinerators | Heavy metals and persistent organic compounds accumulate in the soil |
| Old mines and waste landfills | Arsenic, cadmium, and lead remain in the soil |
| Roadsides frequently used by people and dogs | High risk of fecal contamination and parasites |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Heavy metals do not disappear with washing or cooking. Heat kills microorganisms and breaks down some toxins, but it has no effect on the elements lead and cadmium. In such places, no matter how certain the identification is, not foraging is the only response.
