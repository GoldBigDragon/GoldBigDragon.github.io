## 2. Common Edible Wild Plants in South Korea and Their Identifying Traits
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The most common failure in identifying plants is relying on the impression left by a single photograph. Even within the same species, size and color vary greatly with the sunlight, water, and timing of where it grew, so appearance alone cannot tell species apart. What can be trusted are the traits that change little regardless of environment.

---
### 2-1. What to Look At
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The traits below vary little between individuals, which is what makes them a basis for identification. The rule is never to conclude from just one trait, but to confirm only when several line up at once.

| Trait | What it tells you |
| --- | --- |
| Vein pattern | Parallel veins mean a monocot; net (reticulate) veins mean a dicot. This is the first clue for narrowing down the family |
| Leaf arrangement | Alternate, opposite, or whorled arrangement is fixed for each species |
| Presence and type of hairs | Note whether hairs are present, whether they are soft or coarse, and even their color |
| Smell | Crush a leaf and smell it. The scent of garlic, water dropwort, or angelica is a decisive clue |
| Latex/sap | Whether a white or yellow sap oozes from a cut |
| Stem cross-section | Whether it is round or angular, hollow or solid |
| Base and roots | Whether it is wrapped in fiber, and what structure a cut cross-section shows |
| Habitat | Habitat conditions such as waterside, dry slope, shade, or forest edge |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Of these, smell is an especially useful trait. A plant's aromatic compounds are species-specific secondary metabolites, so they vary little between individuals and can be checked instantly with no tools at all. Even in pairs that look extremely similar, such as mountain garlic (sanmaneul, Allium ochotense) and lily of the valley (eunbangulkkot, Convallaria keiskei), smell alone settles the question. But even when checking smell, you should go no further than crushing a leaf between your fingers and smelling it — never put it in your mouth.

---
### 2-2. Common Edible Plants That Are Relatively Easy to Tell Apart
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Below are species that are common in South Korea's mountains and fields and whose identifying traits are relatively clear-cut. Even so, the information here alone must never be used to make a final identification in the field; you still need to confirm it with someone who knows the plant firsthand or check it against a field guide.

| Name | Timing and place | Identifying clues | Caution |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Mugwort (ssuk, Artemisia princeps) | March~May, sunny fields and embankments | The underside of the leaf is covered in white down and looks pale, with a strong characteristic scent | Many similar-looking Asteraceae exist, but the pale underside and scent set it apart |
| Shepherd's purse (naengi, Capsella bursa-pastoris) | March~April, field edges and roadsides | The basal leaves lie flat on the ground in a radiating rosette, and the root has a shepherd's-purse scent | Once it flowers, it toughens |
| Dallae wild chive (Allium monanthum) | March~May, field edges and forest margins | Slender cylindrical leaves, a round bulb, a strong garlic smell | No smell means it is not dallae |
| Toothed lettuce (sseumbagwi, Ixeris dentata) and crepidiastrum (godeulppaegi, Crepidiastrum sonchifolium) | March~May, fields and roadsides | Cutting them releases a white latex, and the taste is very bitter | The bitterness lessens if they are blanched and soaked |
| Dandelion (mindeulle, Taraxacum spp.) | March~June, sunny spots | Basal leaves with backward-pointing teeth, a hollow flower stalk, white latex | Both the leaves and the root can be used |
| Plantain (jilgyeongi, Plantago asiatica) | April~September, roadsides and open ground | Several thick, parallel veins run through the leaf; grows where it gets trodden on | Only the young leaves are tender |
| Daylily (wonchuri, Hemerocallis spp.) | April~May, mountain slopes | Long, parallel-veined leaves form two ranks like a fan at the base | Must always be blanched and soaked before eating |
| Aralia shoots (dureup, Aralia elata) | April~May, forest margins | New shoots at the tip of a thorny stem | Must always be blanched; leave the side shoots |
| Hardy kiwi (darae, Actinidia arguta) and wild grape (meoru, Vitis coignetiae) | August~October, hillside vines | Hardy kiwi has smooth green fruit; wild grape ripens into a black, grape-like cluster | Many vine fruits are toxic, so confirmation is needed |
| Acorn (dotori, Quercus spp.) | September~November, under oak trees | A capped nut from an oak species | Must always have its tannin leached out |
| Chestnut (bam, Castanea crenata) | September~October, under chestnut trees | Brown nut inside a spiny bur | Can be eaten right away with no processing |
| Kudzu (chik, Pueraria montana var. lobata) | Year-round, vines on sunny slopes | Three broad leaflets, a thick, long root | Only the starch strained from the root is used |
| Chinese yam (ma, Dioscorea polystachya) | October~November, hillside vines | Heart-shaped leaves grow opposite each other, with bulbils in the leaf axils | Can also be eaten raw |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;One rule keeps repeating in this table: the higher the calorie content, the more processing is needed. Acorns and kudzu must have their tannin removed, and aralia shoots and daylilies must have their own irritant compounds removed, and this is no coincidence. The plant parts that store the most energy also carry the chemical defenses that protect them. Processing methods are covered in the final chapter of this document.

### 2-3. What Trees and Waterside Plants Provide
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Looking only at herbs misses half of what foraging offers. Trees give up bark, sap, fruit, and leaves, and in winter and early spring especially, they become almost the only resource available.

| Source | Timing | What you get | Principle and caution |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Pine (sonamu, Pinus densiflora) inner bark | Year-round, softest in spring | Starch and sugar | Use only the white cambium layer under the outer bark. Stripping the bark all the way around the trunk kills the tree |
| Birch (jajaknamu, Betula platyphylla) and painted maple (gorosoe, Acer pictum) sap | February~April | Sugar and minerals | Flows only while root pressure is rising, and spoils within a few days |
| Oak (chamnamu, Quercus spp.) and chestnut (bamnamu, Castanea crenata) | September~November | Acorns and chestnuts | The largest source of calories, and it stores well too |
| Mulberry (ppongnamu, Morus alba) | June~July | Mulberries and young leaves | The leaves are eaten blanched, the fruit eaten as is |
| Multiflora rose (jjille, Rosa multiflora) and wild raspberry (santtalgi, Rubus crataegifolius) | May~July | Young shoots and fruit | Fruit of the rose family is generally on the safe side |
| Cattail (budeul, Typha orientalis) and common reed (galdae, Phragmites australis) | Year-round | Starch from the rhizome, young shoots | The leading source of starch at the water's edge, but watch for water pollution |
| Water dropwort (minari, Oenanthe javanica) | March~May, waterside | Leaves and stems | Must always be told apart from water hemlock (dokminari, Cicuta virosa) |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;When foraging at the water's edge, the water itself is more of a problem than the plants. Aquatic plants in streams below livestock facilities or residential areas can carry E. coli, parasite eggs, and heavy metals on their surface. If you cannot tell what lies upstream, waterside plants must always be cooked to lower the risk of infection from liver flukes and lung flukes.
