## 2. Common Edible Wild Plants in Korea and How to Identify Them
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The most common failure in recognizing plants is relying on the impression left by a single photograph. Even within one species, size and color vary greatly with the sunlight, water, and season where it grew, so overall appearance alone cannot tell plants apart. What can be trusted are the traits that change little with the environment.

---
### 2-1. What to Look At
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The traits below hardly vary from one individual to another, so they serve as the basis of identification. The rule is not to reach a conclusion from any one of them but to confirm an identification only when several match at the same time.

| Trait | What it tells you |
| --- | --- |
| Leaf venation | Parallel veins indicate a monocot and net veins a dicot. This is the first clue for narrowing down the family |
| Leaf arrangement | Alternate, opposite, and whorled arrangements are fixed for each species |
| Presence and type of hairs | Check whether hairs are present, whether they are soft or coarse, and even their color |
| Smell | Rub a leaf and smell it. The scent of garlic, water dropwort, or angelica is a decisive clue |
| Latex | Whether white or yellow sap comes out at the cut |
| Stem cross-section | Whether it is round or angular, solid or hollow |
| Base and root | Whether it is wrapped in fibers, and what structure the cut surface has |
| Growing place | Habitat conditions such as waterside, dry slope, shade, or forest edge |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Of these, smell is an especially useful trait. The aromatic compounds of a plant are secondary metabolites specific to the species, so individual variation is small and they can be checked immediately without tools. Even for pairs that look very much alike, such as mountain garlic and lily of the valley, smell alone settles the question. Even when checking smell, however, do not put the plant in your mouth; stop at rubbing the leaf between your fingers and smelling it.

---
### 2-2. Common Edible Plants That Are Relatively Easy to Tell Apart
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The following are species that are common in the mountains and fields of Korea and have relatively distinct identifying traits. However, the contents written here alone must not be used to make a final identification in the field; the plant must be confirmed by someone who knows it in person or checked against a field guide.

| Name | Season and place | Identifying clues | Caution |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Mugwort (ssuk) | March-May, sunny fields and embankments | The underside of the leaf is covered with white down and looks white, and it has a strong characteristic fragrance | Many similar Asteraceae exist; tell them apart by the white underside and the fragrance |
| Shepherd's purse (naengi) | March-April, field edges and roadsides | The root leaves lie flat on the ground in a radiating rosette, and the root gives off the characteristic scent of naengi | Becomes tough once it flowers |
| Wild chive (dallae) | March-May, field edges and forest edges | Thin cylindrical leaves, a round bulb, and a strong garlic smell | If it has no smell, it is not dallae |
| Ixeris and crepidiastrum (ssumbagwi and godeulppaegi) | March-May, fields and roadsides | White latex comes out when cut, and the taste is very bitter | The bitterness is reduced by blanching and soaking |
| Dandelion | March-June, sunny places | Root leaves with backward-pointing teeth, a hollow flower stalk, white latex | Both leaves and roots can be used |
| Plantain (jilgyeongi) | April-September, roadsides and vacant lots | Several thick parallel veins on the leaf; grows in places that get trampled | Only young leaves are tender |
| Daylily (wonchuri) | April-May, mountain slopes | Long leaves with parallel veins fan out in two rows from the base | Must be blanched and soaked |
| Korean angelica tree shoots (durup) | April-May, forest edges | New shoots at the tips of thorny stems | Must be blanched; leave the side buds |
| Hardy kiwi and Korean wild grape (darae and meoru) | August-October, vines at the foot of mountains | Darae has smooth green fruit; meoru ripens black in grape-like clusters | Many vine fruits are poisonous, so confirmation is needed |
| Acorn | September-November, under oak trees | A nut with a cupule, from oak species | Tannin must be leached out |
| Chestnut | September-October, under chestnut trees | A brown nut inside a spiny burr | Can be eaten right away without processing |
| Kudzu | Year-round, vines on sunny slopes | Three broad leaflets and a thick, long root | Only the starch of the root is filtered out and used |
| Yam (ma) | October-November, vines at the foot of mountains | Heart-shaped leaves in opposite arrangement, with bulbils in the leaf axils | Can also be eaten raw |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;One rule repeats in this table: the more calories something holds, the more processing it needs. Acorn and kudzu must be freed of tannin, and Korean angelica tree shoots and daylily must be freed of their own irritant compounds. This is no coincidence. The parts where a plant stores a great deal of energy also carry chemical defenses to protect it. Processing methods are covered in the last chapter of this document.

### 2-3. What Trees and the Waterside Provide
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Looking only at herbs means missing half of foraging. Trees give bark, sap, fruit, and leaves, and in winter and early spring they are almost the only resource.

| Source | Season | What you get | Principle and caution |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Inner bark of pine | Year-round; softest in spring | Starch and sugar | Use only the white cambium beneath the outer bark. Stripping all the way around the trunk kills the tree |
| Sap of birch and Korean maple (gorosoe) | February-April | Sugar and minerals | Flows only while root pressure is rising and spoils within a few days |
| Oak and chestnut | September-November | Acorns and chestnuts | The largest calorie source, and it also stores well |
| Mulberry | June-July | Mulberries and young leaves | Blanch the leaves before eating; eat the fruit as it is |
| Wild rose and wild raspberry | May-July | Young shoots and fruit | Fruits of the rose family are generally on the safe side |
| Cattail and reed | Year-round | Starch from the rhizome, young shoots | A typical starch source at the waterside, but watch for water pollution |
| Water dropwort (minari) | March-May, waterside | Leaves and stems | Must be distinguished from water hemlock |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;When foraging at the waterside, the water is the problem rather than the plant itself. Aquatic plants in streams below livestock facilities or settlements may carry E. coli, parasite eggs, and heavy metals on their surfaces. If you cannot know what lies upstream, waterside plants must always be cooked before eating to lower the risk of infection with liver flukes and lung flukes.
