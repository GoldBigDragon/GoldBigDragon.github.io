## Introduction
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;When supply chains break down, the first alternative most people think of is going into the mountains to gather wild greens. Two misconceptions overlap in this choice. The first is the expectation that wild plants will fill the stomach, and the second is the belief that only a few kinds of plants are dangerous and that they can be recognized by eye. Both misconceptions lead to the same result: the forager goes hungry or gets poisoned.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In reality, wild leafy greens are mostly water and fiber and contain almost no calories. Spending a whole day gathering 2 kg of greens often yields fewer calories than the gathering itself burned. What actually fills the stomach is nuts and acorns, starch from roots, and grain-like seeds, and these are available only in particular seasons and particular places. For foraging to become a food source, the forager must read the seasons and work on the assumption that what is gathered will be stored.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The risk of poisoning, by contrast, is underestimated. Many deadly poisonous plants and mushrooms give no warning signal. They do not taste bitter, they are not brightly colored, and nothing happens immediately after they are eaten. By the time symptoms appear, the liver and kidneys have often already been damaged. In ordinary times, gathering wild animals and plants is permitted only within the limits set by the laws of one's own country; this is summarized at the end of the document.
