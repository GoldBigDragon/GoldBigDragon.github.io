## Introduction
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;When supply lines break down, the first alternative most people think of is heading into the mountains to gather wild greens. Two misunderstandings sit behind this choice. The first is the expectation that wild plants will fill the stomach; the second is the belief that only a few kinds are dangerous and that danger can be told by eye. The two misunderstandings point in opposite directions but lead to the same outcome: the forager goes hungry, or gets poisoned.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In practice, most wild leafy greens are mostly water and fiber and carry almost no calories. Spending a full day gathering 2kg of greens often yields fewer calories than were spent gathering them. What actually fills the stomach is nuts and acorns, the starch in roots, and grain-like seeds, and these appear only in particular seasons and particular places. For foraging to become food, it has to follow the seasons and assume storage from the start.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The risk of poisoning, by contrast, is underestimated. Many of the deadliest poisonous plants and mushrooms give no warning sign. They are not bitter, not brightly colored, and nothing happens right after eating them. By the time symptoms appear, the liver and kidneys are often already damaged. So a single principle runs through this entire document, and every other piece of knowledge here exists only to serve it. Outside a disaster, gathering wild plants and animals is permitted only within the limits set by your own country's laws, which are covered at the end of this document.
