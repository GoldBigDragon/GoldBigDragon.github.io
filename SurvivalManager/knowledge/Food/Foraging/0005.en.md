## 4. Poisonous Mushrooms and Popular Misconceptions
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Of everything you might forage, mushrooms have the worst ratio of risk to reward. Their calorie content, 20~40kcal per 100g, is no different from leafy greens, yet the consequences of picking wrong are more lethal than with anything else you could gather. Mushroom poisoning keeps happening anyway because nearly every widely believed method for telling them apart is wrong.

---
### 4-1. Why Every Popular Belief Is Wrong
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;What follows are the most widespread methods in South Korea for telling poisonous mushrooms apart, and none of them is true. The Ministry of Food and Drug Safety, the Korea Forest Service, and the Rural Development Administration repeat the same warning every year precisely because these popular beliefs are the actual cause of real deaths.

| Popular belief | The fact |
| --- | --- |
| If it tears cleanly lengthwise, it's edible | The deadly poisonous destroying angel (Amanita virosa) and fool's mushroom (Amanita verna) also tear cleanly lengthwise. The direction a cap tears is just a physical property that comes from how the gills are arranged, and has nothing to do with toxicity |
| If insects have eaten it, it's safe | Insects and slugs have a different target enzyme for amatoxin than humans do, so they are unaffected. Deadly poisonous mushrooms show insect bite marks just the same |
| A brightly colored mushroom is poisonous | Most of the deadliest amatoxin-group mushrooms are plain white. Conversely, the vividly orange Caesar's mushroom (Amanita hemibapha) is edible |
| If a silver spoon turns black in it, it's poisonous | Silver discolors by reacting with sulfur compounds. It does not react with amatoxin at all, and ordinary foods such as garlic and egg discolor silver more readily |
| Boiling or salting removes the toxin | Amatoxin is extremely heat-stable; it does not break down when boiled and survives drying as well |
| Cooking it with eggplant or perilla oil neutralizes it | There is no basis for this at all |
| No ring on the stem means it's safe | A ring is only a clue for narrowing down the species, not an indicator of toxicity |

> **No general rule exists for telling safe mushrooms apart. There is no way to do it other than knowing each species individually.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There is a reason these popular beliefs refuse to die. Most mushrooms sold at the market genuinely have no toxin, so using a wrong rule passes without incident the overwhelming majority of the time. People take this run of success as proof the rule works, but in reality they have simply never encountered a deadly poisonous mushroom. The moment they do, all that past success means nothing at all.

---
### 4-2. Amatoxin - The Poison That Comes Late
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Most deaths from mushroom poisoning are caused by this one group alone, the amatoxins. In South Korea, the destroying angel (Amanita virosa), fool's mushroom (Amanita verna), and yellow destroying angel (gaenari-gwangdae-beoseot, Amanita subjunquillea) belong to this group; all three are common in oak forests and resemble edible mushrooms in appearance.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;What makes amatoxin especially dangerous is not the strength of the toxin but its mechanism of action. This toxin blocks RNA polymerase II, which cells need to read genetic information and build proteins. Cells do not die immediately; instead, unable to make new proteins, they lose function gradually. Liver cells and the tubule cells of the kidney, which have the highest protein turnover, break down first, and this delay is what makes the clinical course so unusual.

| Stage | Timing | Condition |
| --- | :---: | --- |
| Latent stage | 6~24 hours after ingestion | No symptoms at all. During this time, the toxin has already entered the liver |
| Gastrointestinal stage | After 6~24 hours | Violent vomiting and diarrhea, abdominal pain, and severe dehydration begin |
| Apparent recovery stage | 24~36 hours after ingestion | Symptoms subside and it looks as if the person has recovered. Stopping treatment at this stage is fatal |
| Liver failure stage | 3~5 days after ingestion | Liver cell necrosis progresses, leading to jaundice, bleeding, and multi-organ failure |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The existence of this apparent recovery stage is the most dangerous feature of this kind of poisoning. When the vomiting and diarrhea stop, people conclude they have recovered, and cases have even been reported in which medical staff mistook it for simple gastroenteritis and sent the patient home. But during this stage, liver cells are already being destroyed, and by the time symptoms return, a liver transplant is often the only option left.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The lethal dose is also extremely small. The lethal dose of amatoxin is on the order of 0.1mg per 1kg of body weight, and a single mushroom can contain up to 15mg. The lethal dose for a 60kg adult is 6mg, so it genuinely happens that several people who shared a single mushroom are all poisoned at once.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Growing alongside edible mushrooms also makes the problem worse. Even if only one or two mushrooms picked from a single cluster are a different species, the whole batch becomes dangerous. This is why mushrooms must be checked individually rather than by the clump, and the rule is never to mix them together in the same container.

---
### 4-3. Other Families of Mushroom Toxin
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Beyond amatoxin there are several other families of toxin, each with its own symptoms and timing. A toxin that acts fast is usually survivable, but when symptoms begin only after 6 hours have passed, it is almost always a dangerous family.

| Family | Representative mushrooms | Onset of symptoms | Main damage |
| --- | --- | :---: | --- |
| Amatoxin | Destroying angel (Amanita virosa), fool's mushroom (Amanita verna) | 6~24 hours | Liver and kidneys; high fatality rate |
| Orellanine | Some webcaps (Cortinarius spp.) | 2~20 days | Kidney failure; has the longest latency of all |
| Gyromitrin | False morel (Gyromitra esculenta) | 6~12 hours | Liver and nerves |
| Muscarine | Fibrecaps (Inocybe spp.), funnel caps (Clitocybe spp.) | 15 minutes~2 hours | Sweating, drooling, slow heart rate |
| Ibotenic acid and muscimol | Fly agaric (Amanita muscaria), panther cap (Amanita pantherina) | 30 minutes~2 hours | Confusion and hallucinations, convulsions |
| Gastrointestinal irritants | Sulphur tuft (Hypholoma fasciculare), jack-o'-lantern mushroom (Omphalotus guepiniformis), and many others | 30 minutes~3 hours | Vomiting and diarrhea; usually recoverable |
| Coprine | Common ink cap (Coprinopsis atramentaria) | When eaten together with alcohol | Flushing and heart palpitations |
| Trichothecenes | Poison fire coral (Trichoderma cornu-damae) | Within 30 minutes | Fatal even in small amounts; avoid even touching it |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;One practical rule follows from this. If symptoms appear within 6 hours of eating a mushroom, it is usually a survivable family; if the first symptoms appear only after 6 hours have passed, it is likely a family that targets the liver or kidneys. But this rule can only help with diagnosis, not with foraging decisions, because by then the mushroom has already been eaten.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In a survival situation, the most rational decision about mushrooms is to leave them alone. The calories mushrooms provide are on the level of leafy greens, while gathering acorns for the same amount of time yields tens of times more. What you stand to lose is nowhere near comparable to what you stand to gain, so unless you are dealing with the handful of species you know for certain, mushrooms are better left off the foraging list.
