## 5. Processing to Remove Toxins, the Last Resort, and Sustainable Foraging
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Many wild plants cannot be eaten as they are but become excellent food once processed. Acorns, kudzu, and bracken fern are the clearest examples, and all of them reached the table only because people spent thousands of years working out how to remove what made them inedible.

---
### 5-1. Anti-Nutrients and the Principle Behind Removing Them
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The reason plants build chemical defenses into their storage organs is simple. Wherever starch and fat are concentrated is the most attractive target for animals, and without something to defend it, no seed would survive. Processing means using the physical properties of these defensive compounds to strip them away.

| Compound | Where it is found | What it does | Principle of removal |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Tannin | Acorns, kudzu, unripe persimmon | Binds to protein, blocking digestion and interfering with iron absorption, and gives an astringent taste | Dissolves well in water, so it is leached out by repeatedly changing the water |
| Ptaquiloside | Bracken fern (gosari, Pteridium aquilinum) | Carcinogenic, and breaks down vitamin B1 | Unstable to heat and soluble in water, so it is blanched and then soaked in water |
| Oxalic acid | Sorrel (suyeong, Rumex acetosa), creeping woodsorrel (gwaengibap, Oxalis corniculata), lambsquarters (myeongaju, Chenopodium album), rhubarb (daehwang, Rheum undulatum) | Binds calcium to form stones, and in large amounts damages the kidneys | The blanching water must always be discarded |
| Cyanogenic glycosides | Apricot and Japanese apricot (maesil, Prunus mume) pits, ginkgo (eunhaeng, Ginkgo biloba), cassava, flaxseed | Form hydrogen cyanide inside the body | Soaked in water, then thoroughly heated or fermented |
| Lectins | Legumes, adzuki beans, unripe beans | Agglutinate red blood cells and cause severe vomiting and diarrhea | Soaked thoroughly, then boiled completely |
| Saponins | Balloon flower (doraji, Platycodon grandiflorus), some other roots and seeds | Destroy red blood cells and irritate the digestive tract | Leached in water and heated |
| Phytic acid | Grain bran, nuts, beans | Binds zinc, iron, and calcium, blocking their absorption | Soaked in water and sprouted, or fermented |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Removing tannin from acorns illustrates the principle well. Tannin is a water-soluble polyphenol, while starch does not dissolve in cold water. This difference in solubility is what makes separation possible. Peel and grind the acorns, soak them in water, and change the water several times a day; only the tannin washes out, and the starch remains. With cold water it takes several days, but the starch stays intact and the resulting flour is of good quality; using hot water cuts the time to a few hours, but the starch gelatinizes instead, making the flour clump and lowering the yield. The traditional method of soaking a sack of ground acorns in a flowing stream simply lets the current do the work of continually changing the water.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Bracken fern relies on a different principle. Ptaquiloside is heat-unstable and breaks down even better under alkaline conditions, so simply blanching it in boiling water destroys a considerable amount. Studies show that 5 minutes of heating reduces it by about 60%, and soaking it in cold water for several hours after blanching removes most of what remains. Adding a little ash or baking soda makes the water alkaline and speeds up the breakdown. The tradition of always blanching and soaking bracken fern before eating it is a precise solution arrived at through experience.

### 5-2. The Universal Edibility Test and Its Limits
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The Universal Edibility Test found in the US Army survival manual is a procedure for testing an unfamiliar plant. The principle is to break exposure down into very small increments and check the body's reaction at each stage before increasing it.

| Step | What to do | Wait time |
| :---: | --- | :---: |
| 1 | Fast, drinking only water | 8 hours |
| 2 | Separate the plant into leaves, stem, root, flower, and seed | |
| 3 | Smell it. An almond or peach-pit smell, or a strong foul odor, rules it out immediately | |
| 4 | Rub the sap on the tender skin of the inner arm | 8 hours |
| 5 | Cook it if possible | |
| 6 | Touch it lightly to the outside of the lips | 3 minutes |
| 7 | Place it on the tongue without chewing | 15 minutes |
| 8 | Chew it without swallowing | 15 minutes |
| 9 | Swallow a very small amount | 8 hours |
| 10 | Eat about a handful | 8 hours |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Done properly, this procedure takes more than 24 hours, during which nothing else can be eaten. For someone already hungry, that alone is a considerable cost. But more important is the fact that there are poisons this test cannot catch.

| Limitation | Reason |
| --- | --- |
| Powerless against delayed-action poisons | Amatoxin has a latency of 6~24 hours, so an 8-hour wait does not screen it out |
| Powerless against cumulative poisons | A poison that builds up over months, like pyrrolizidine alkaloids, will not show up in a single test |
| Dangerous with potent alkaloids | A substance that is lethal even in a small amount causes poisoning during the test itself |
| Can never be used on mushrooms | The main toxins in mushrooms are all delayed-action, and the person dies after the test has already passed them |
| Must be redone for each plant part | Plants whose leaves are safe but whose seeds are lethal are common |
| Results change depending on whether it is cooked | Some toxins become safe once cooked, others remain even after cooking, and the two are easy to mix up |

> **The Universal Edibility Test is not a way to confirm safety — it is a last resort that slightly reduces risk only when there is no other option at all. It is never used on mushrooms, under any circumstances.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;So the proper place for this test is as a supplementary tool, not a substitute for foraging knowledge. If you know even a few species, it is always better to eat only those, and the test has value only in a situation where you know nothing at all and have no other food source.

### 5-3. The Sustainability of Foraging
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;If a disaster does not end in a matter of days, foraging stops being a single harvest and becomes a relationship that spans many years. What makes the decisive difference then is which part of the plant you take.

| Harvesting method | What remains the following year | Verdict |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Only some leaves and new shoots | The individual plant remains intact | Repeated foraging is possible |
| Fruit and seed | The individual remains, but reproduction declines | Some must be left behind |
| The entire stem | Recovers if perennial, dies out if annual | Depends on the species |
| Roots and bulbs | The individual is gone | Do this in only part of the colony |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The practical standard is to take no more than a third from any one colony. When digging roots, lower this proportion further, and rather than taking the largest individuals, thin out crowded patches instead — the plants left behind actually grow better as a result. For aralia shoots (dureup), pick only the large terminal shoot at the tip of the stem and leave the side shoots, since the tree survives only that way; strip even the terminal shoot and the tree dies. For plants that reproduce by bulb, like dallae wild chive and mountain garlic, dig only the large ones and leave the small ones buried.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Leaving seed behind matters too. Strip all the fruit before it ripens, and that colony cannot produce a next generation. Annual plants in particular vanish from that spot the following year if they fail to set seed that year. Splitting foraging sites into several locations and rotating between them is effective for the same reason. Strip one spot clean every year, and within a few years it goes empty — and that loss eventually comes back around to the person who caused it.

### 5-4. Law and Ethics
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;What this document sets out is not knowledge to be put into practice as-is under ordinary conditions. In South Korea, gathering wild plants and animals is governed by South Korea's Wildlife Protection and Management Act. Species designated as endangered wildlife may not be gathered, kept, or traded under any circumstances, and among the poisonous plants mentioned in this document, water hemlock is one example of a toxic plant that is also a designated endangered species. In addition, gathering forest products is prohibited in national parks, provincial parks, and forest genetic resource protection areas under South Korea's Natural Parks Act and Forest Protection Act, and gathering forest products from private or state-owned forest land without permission can amount to theft under South Korea's Mountain Districts Management Act and Criminal Act. Most other countries have regulations to the same effect. This document sets out knowledge that assumes a survival situation in which public institutions are no longer functioning; under ordinary conditions, you must check your own country's wildlife protection and forestry laws and act only within them.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Even in a survival situation, a few principles still hold: eat only what you know for certain, dig only as much as you need, and leave enough behind for the next season. How to store and process what you have gathered continues in the food preservation document; how to secure the core of your calories continues in the agriculture document.
