## 5. Detoxifying Preparation, the Last Resort, and the Sustainability of Foraging
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Many wild plants cannot be eaten as they are but make excellent food once processed. Acorns, kudzu, and bracken are typical examples, and all of them reached the dinner table because people spent thousands of years finding ways to remove their toxins.

---
### 5-1. Antinutrients and the Principles of Removing Them
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The reason plants place chemical defenses in their storage organs is simple. The places where starch and fat are stored are the most attractive targets for animals, so without something to protect them no seeds would survive. Processing is the work of using the physical properties of these defensive substances to separate them out.

| Compound | Where it is found | What it does | Principle of removal |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Tannin | Acorns, kudzu, unripe persimmons | Binds proteins and blocks digestion, interferes with iron absorption, and gives astringency | Dissolves well in water, so leach it out by changing the water repeatedly |
| Ptaquiloside | Bracken | Carcinogenic and breaks down vitamin B1 | Weak to heat and soluble in water, so blanch and then soak |
| Oxalic acid | Sorrel, wood sorrel, goosefoot, rhubarb | Binds calcium to form stones, and damages the kidneys in large amounts | Always discard the blanching water |
| Cyanogenic glycosides | Seeds of apricot and Japanese apricot, ginkgo, cassava, flaxseed | Produce hydrogen cyanide in the body | Soak, heat thoroughly, or ferment |
| Lectins | Legumes, adzuki beans, unripe beans | Agglutinate red blood cells and cause severe vomiting and diarrhea | Soak well, then boil completely |
| Saponins | Balloon flower root, some roots and seeds | Destroy red blood cells and irritate the stomach | Leach in water and heat |
| Phytic acid | Grain bran, nuts, beans | Binds zinc, iron, and calcium and blocks their absorption | Soak, sprout, or ferment |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The removal of tannin from acorns shows the principle well. Tannin is a water-soluble polyphenol, and starch does not dissolve in cold water. This difference in solubility is the basis for separation. If acorns are shelled, ground, and soaked in water, and the water is changed several times a day, only the tannin leaves with the water and the starch remains. With cold water it takes several days, but the starch is preserved intact and the flour is of good quality; with hot water it shortens to a few hours, but the starch gelatinizes, the flour clumps, and the yield drops. The traditional method of leaving the sack in a flowing stream leaves the work of changing the water to the current.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Bracken uses a different principle. Ptaquiloside is unstable to heat and breaks down more readily in alkali, so blanching in boiling water alone destroys a considerable amount. According to research, heating for 5 minutes reduces it by about 60%, and soaking in cold water for several hours after blanching removes most of what remains. Adding a little ash or baking soda makes the water alkaline and speeds up the breakdown. The tradition of always blanching and soaking bracken before eating it is an accurate solution gained through experience.

### 5-2. The Universal Edibility Test and Its Limits
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The universal edibility test in the US military survival manual is a procedure for testing unknown plants. Its principle is to divide exposure into very small units and increase it while checking the reaction at each stage.

| Step | Content | Waiting time |
| :---: | --- | :---: |
| 1 | Fast, drinking only water | 8 hours |
| 2 | Separate the plant into parts: leaves, stems, roots, flowers, seeds | |
| 3 | Smell it. Immediately rule out the smell of almonds or peach pits, or a strong stench | |
| 4 | Rub the sap on the soft skin of the inner forearm | 8 hours |
| 5 | Cook it if possible | |
| 6 | Touch it lightly to the outside of the lips | 3 minutes |
| 7 | Place it on the tongue without chewing | 15 minutes |
| 8 | Chew but do not swallow | 15 minutes |
| 9 | Swallow a very small amount | 8 hours |
| 10 | Eat about a handful | 8 hours |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Done properly, this procedure takes more than 24 hours, during which nothing else can be eaten. For someone already starving, that is a considerable cost in itself. More important, however, is that there are poisons this test cannot detect.

| Limitation | Reason |
| --- | --- |
| Powerless against delayed toxins | Amatoxin has a latent period of 6-24 hours, so an 8-hour wait does not filter it out |
| Powerless against cumulative toxins | Toxins that build up over months, such as pyrrolizidine alkaloids, do not show up in a single test |
| Dangerous with strong alkaloids | With substances lethal in small amounts, poisoning already occurs during the test steps |
| Must never be used on mushrooms | The main mushroom toxins are all delayed, and people die after the test has passed them |
| Must be repeated for each part | Plants whose leaves are safe and whose seeds are lethal are common |
| Results differ depending on cooking | Some things become safe when cooked, while others remain toxic after cooking |

> **The universal edibility test is not a way of confirming safety but a last resort that reduces risk a little when there are no other options at all. It is never used on mushrooms under any circumstances.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Therefore the proper place of this test is not as a substitute for foraging knowledge but as an auxiliary measure. If you know even a few species of plants, it is always better to eat only those, and the test is meaningful only when you know nothing and have no other food source.

### 5-3. Sustainability of Foraging
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;If a disaster does not end in a few days, foraging becomes a relationship spanning many years rather than a single harvest. What makes the decisive difference then is which part of the plant you take.

| Method of harvest | What remains next year | Judgment |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Only some leaves and new shoots | The plant remains intact | Repeated harvest is possible |
| Fruits and seeds | The plant remains but reproduction decreases | Leave some behind |
| The whole stem | Recovers if perennial; dies out if annual | Depends on the species |
| Roots and bulbs | The plant is lost | Do so only from part of a colony |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;A practical standard is to take no more than one third from a colony. When digging roots, lower this ratio further, and thin out from densely growing spots rather than taking large individuals; the remaining plants then actually grow better. For the Korean angelica tree, take only the large shoot at the tip of the stem and leave the side shoots so that the tree survives; if every shoot is taken, down to the last, the tree dies. For plants that reproduce by bulbs, such as dallae and sanmaneul, dig only the large ones and bury the small ones.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Leaving seeds is also important. If all the fruit is picked before it ripens, the colony cannot produce the next generation. Annual plants in particular disappear from that spot the following year if they cannot leave seed in the current year. Dividing foraging sites among several places and using them in rotation is effective for the same reason. If one place is stripped every year, it will be empty within a few years, and in the end the loss returns to people.

### 5-4. Law and Ethics
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The contents summarized in this document are not knowledge that can be put into practice as it stands in ordinary times. In the Republic of Korea, the gathering of wild animals and plants is governed by the Act on the Protection and Management of Wildlife. Species designated as endangered wildlife are completely prohibited from being gathered, kept, or distributed, and among poisonous plants too there are species designated as endangered, such as the water hemlock mentioned in this document. In national parks, provincial parks, and forest genetic resource reserves, gathering forest products is prohibited under the Natural Parks Act and the Forest Protection Act, and gathering forest products without permission in private or national forests may constitute an offense under the Mountainous Districts Management Act and theft under the Criminal Act. Most other countries have regulations with the same purpose. This document is a compilation of knowledge premised on survival situations in which public rescue is not functioning; in ordinary times, you must check the wildlife protection laws and forestry regulations of your own country and act only within them.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Even in a survival situation, eat only what you know for certain, dig only as much as you need, and leave things so that they can grow in the next season. How to store and process what you gather is continued in the Food Preservation document, and how to secure the core of your calories in the agriculture documents.
