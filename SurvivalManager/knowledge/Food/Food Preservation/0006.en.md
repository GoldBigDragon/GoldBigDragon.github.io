## 5. Long-Term Preservation Recipes for Vegetables and Fruit
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Vegetables and fruit have high moisture content, which makes long-term preservation tricky, but the rate of spoilage can be slowed by using acid (vinegar), sugar, and salt, or by surface coating.

---
### 5-1. Compote
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Compote is a cooking method made by simmering fruit in sugar syrup, securing short-term keeping quality and flavor at the same time through sugar and acid.

| Item | Content |
| :---: | :--- |
| Principle | The relatively high sugar concentration raises osmotic pressure and delays microbial growth, and the acid in lemon juice lowers pH and slows spoilage. However, ordinary compote has high water activity and is not suitable for long-term room-temperature preservation. |
| Materials | 500 g of fruit, 100 ml of water, raw cane sugar (or sugar), lemon juice |
| Storage | Seal in a glass jar and refrigerate. |
| | It is premised on being consumed within a few weeks even in refrigeration. |

| Step | Method |
| :---: | :--- |
| 1 | Put the fruit in a pot, add water and raw cane sugar, and boil. |
| 2 | When it starts to boil, reduce to low heat and simmer until the fruit is soft. |
| 3 | Add lemon juice to add sourness. |
| 4 | Put it in a clean glass jar, seal it, and refrigerate. |

---
### 5-2. Wax Coating
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Coating foods with a hard surface such as eggs, citrus, and apples with wax reduces moisture loss and oxygen entry and extends shelf life.

| Item | Content |
| :---: | :--- |
| Principle | A thin protective film forms on the food surface, easing moisture evaporation and oxygen penetration and slowing the respiration rate to maintain freshness. |
| Materials | Edible wax (beeswax, paraffin, and so on) |
| Storage | Store in a cool place shielded from moisture and light. |
| Cautions | Eggs and apples have a natural protective film (bloom) formed right after harvest, so unwashed eggs need no additional coating. |
| | Washed eggs, whose protective film has been removed, can have their keeping quality supplemented with a wax coating. |

| Step | Method |
| :---: | :--- |
| 1 | Brush the soil and dust off vegetables and fruit with a dry cloth. (If wax is applied while moisture is present, enzymes rot the produce in no time, so washing is forbidden) |
| 2 | Slowly melt the edible wax with lukewarm water. |
| 3 | Apply the wax thinly and evenly to the outer skin of the vegetables and fruit. (Excessive coating can interfere with the fruit's respiration and cause internal rot) |
| 4 | When the wax has hardened completely, store in a cool place shielded from moisture and light. |

---
### 5-3. Blanching, Then Drying
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Going through blanching gives a longer shelf life than simply drying.

| Item | Content |
| :---: | :--- |
| Principle | Heat destroys the enzymes of spoilage and browning inside the vegetable, and then drying lowers water activity to 0.6 or below, creating an environment where microorganisms cannot grow. |
| Suitable foods | Most vegetables and fruit, such as carrots, broccoli, mushrooms, spinach, apples, and bananas |
| Storage | Put the dried vegetables in a Mylar bag with an oxygen absorber, seal it, and store in a cool place shielded from moisture and light. |

| Step | Method |
| :---: | :--- |
| 1 | Wash the vegetables, cut them to a uniform size, and blanch them briefly in boiling water for 1-3 minutes. |
| 2 | Immediately after blanching, soak in cold water to cool the residual heat and drain completely. |
| 3 | Dry completely in a dehydrator (60 °C) or well-ventilated shade until crisp. |

---
### 5-4. Salting
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;A method of preserving by raising the salt concentration to an extreme.

| Item | Content |
| :---: | :--- |
| Principle | Osmotic pressure draws all the moisture out of the food and dehydrates the cell membranes of microorganisms, killing them (salinity of 20% or more). |
| Suitable foods | Moisture-rich vegetables such as napa cabbage, radish, cucumber, eggplant, and chili pepper |
| Storage | Store the salting container in a cool place shielded from moisture and light. |

| Step | Method |
| :---: | :--- |
| 1 | Wash the vegetables and cut them to a uniform size. |
| 2 | Use a drying tray to completely remove surface moisture from the vegetables. |
| 3 | Spread at least 1 cm of salt on the bottom of the container and layer vegetables and salt at a ratio close to 1:1, or submerge them completely in a strong brine of 20% or more. |
| 4 | Cover the container and seal it tightly. |
| 5 | When eating, wash several times under running water, soak in water to remove the salt, and then cook. |

---
### 5-5. Lactic Acid Fermentation
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;A metabolic preservation method that uses only a small amount of salt (3%) to propagate beneficial lactic acid bacteria.

| Item | Content |
| :---: | :--- |
| Principle | Lactic acid bacteria eat the sugars in the vegetable and produce "lactic acid," turning the environment acidic (pH 4.6 or below). In an acidic environment, spoilage bacteria and food poisoning bacteria (including botulinum) cannot live. |
| Suitable foods | Cabbage (sauerkraut), cucumber (fermented pickles), radish, kimchi |
| Storage | Store the fermentation container in a cool place shielded from moisture and light. |

| Step | Sauerkraut recipe using cabbage |
| :---: | :--- |
| 1 | Shred cabbage and so on finely and toss it with salt equal to 3% of the total weight. |
| 2 | Press firmly until liquid seeps out of the vegetables, then weigh them down with a pressing stone so the vegetables are completely submerged in the liquid. (Blocking oxygen is the key) |
| 3 | Ferment for several days at room temperature (20 °C), releasing the gas, and then store in a cool place. |

| Step | Kimjang (kimchi-making) method |
| :---: | :--- |
| 1 | Whole napa cabbage for whole-head kimchi: Sprinkle coarse salt in layers on the stem parts and salt it for 8 hours in 12% brine at room temperature (20 °C). Afterward, check that the white stems bend softly without breaking when bent. |
| | Cabbage for cut kimchi: Cut the cabbage into bite-size pieces, sprinkle salt evenly, mix, and let it wilt for 2 hours. Afterward, check that a little water comes out of the cabbage. |
| | Radish for kkakdugi (cubed radish kimchi): Cut the radish into cubes, sprinkle salt and sugar together, and let it wilt for 1 hour. Afterward, check that the radish has softened slightly and radish juice has pooled at the bottom. |
| 2 | Rinse the salted vegetables cleanly twice in cold water to wash off the salt and impurities. |
| 3 | Leave the rinsed vegetables to drain in a colander for 1 hour so the water drains off naturally. (Pressing or wringing by hand destroys the cell walls, and the kimchi may not be crisp and may smell stale) |
| 4 | Boil glutinous rice flour or wheat flour in water to make a starch paste (pulguk). The paste becomes the initial food of the lactic acid bacteria, promoting fermentation, and helps the seasoning stick well to the vegetables. |
| 5 | Mix red pepper flakes, garlic, ginger, salted fermented seafood (or fish sauce), green onions, and so on into the paste to make the kimchi seasoning, then toss it with the salted vegetables. |
| 6 | Pack it firmly into the fermentation container to push out air, then cover it with outer cabbage leaves or press a hygienic plastic sheet tightly against it to block oxygen. |
| 7 | Leave it at room temperature (20 °C) for about a day, and as soon as gas begins to bubble, move it to the refrigerator (0-5 °C) immediately and age it at low temperature for several days to several weeks. (If the refrigerator cannot be used because of a power outage, bury it in the ground or in the coolest cellar to minimize room-temperature exposure) |
