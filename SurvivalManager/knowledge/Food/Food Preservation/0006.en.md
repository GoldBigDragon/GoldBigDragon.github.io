## 5. Long-Term Preservation Recipes for Fruits and Vegetables
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Although fruits and vegetables are challenging to preserve long-term due to their high moisture content, their spoilage can be delayed through the utilization of acid (vinegar), sugar, or salt, or by applying a surface coating.

---
### 5-1. Compote
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;A compote is a cooking method in which fruits are simmered in sugar syrup. This approach secures short-term preservation and flavor simultaneously by leveraging sugar and acid.

| Item | Content |
| :---: | :--- |
| Principle | A relatively high sugar concentration increases osmotic pressure to delay microbial growth, while the acid from lemon juice lowers the pH to slow down deterioration. However, typical compotes are unsuitable for long-term room-temperature storage due to their high water activity. |
| Materials | 500g fruit, 100ml water, raw sugar (or granulated sugar), lemon juice |
| Storage | Seal inside a glass jar and store under refrigeration. |
| | Consumption is expected within a few weeks, even in a refrigerated environment. |

| Step | Instructions |
| :---: | :--- |
| 1 | Place the fruit in a pot, add water and raw sugar, and bring to a boil. |
| 2 | Once it begins to boil, reduce the heat to low and simmer until the fruit softens. |
| 3 | Add lemon juice to enhance acidity. |
| 4 | Transfer into a clean glass jar, seal, and store under refrigeration. |

---
### 5-2. Wax Coating
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Applying wax to rigid-surfaced foods—such as eggs, citrus fruits, and apples—can extend their shelf life by mitigating moisture loss and oxygen influx.

| Item | Content |
| :---: | :--- |
| Principle | Forms a thin protective barrier on the food surface to reduce moisture evaporation and oxygen penetration, maintaining freshness by slowing down the respiration rate. |
| Materials | Food-grade wax (beeswax, paraffin, etc.) |
| Storage | Store in a cool, dark place protected from moisture and light. |
| Precautions | Eggs and apples develop a natural protective barrier (bloom) immediately after harvest; therefore, unwashed eggs do not require additional coating. |
| | Washed eggs, from which the natural protective barrier has been removed, can supplement their preservation capacity through a wax coating. |

| Step | Instructions |
| :---: | :--- |
| 1 | Remove dirt and dust from the fruits and vegetables using a dry cloth. (Washing is prohibited because applying wax while moisture is present will cause enzymatic action to rot the produce instantly). |
| 2 | Slowly melt the food-grade wax using lukewarm water. |
| 3 | Apply a thin, uniform layer of wax to the outer skin of the fruits and vegetables. (Excessive coating can obstruct the respiration of the fruit, leading to internal rot). |
| 4 | Once the wax solidifies completely, store the produce in a cool, dark place protected from moisture and light. |

---
### 5-3. Blanching Followed by Dehydration
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Incorporating a blanching step yields a longer shelf life compared to simple dehydration.

| Item | Content |
| :---: | :--- |
| Principle | Thermal treatment destroys the internal spoilage and browning enzymes of the vegetables, and subsequent dehydration lowers the water activity to 0.6 or below, creating an environment where microorganisms cannot proliferate. |
| Suitable Foods | Most fruits and vegetables, including carrots, broccoli, mushrooms, spinach, apples, and bananas. |
| Storage | Place the dehydrated vegetables in a Mylar bag along with an oxygen absorber, seal tightly, and store in a cool, dark place protected from moisture and light. |

| Step | Instructions |
| :---: | :--- |
| 1 | Wash the vegetables, cut them into uniform pieces, and blanch them briefly in boiling water for 1 to 3 minutes. |
| 2 | Submerge them in cold water immediately after blanching to dissipate residual heat, then drain the moisture completely. |
| 3 | Dehydrate them fully using a food dehydrator (60°C) or in a well-ventilated, shaded area until they become brittle. |

---
### 5-4. Salting
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This is a preservation method that maximizes salt concentration to an extreme degree.

| Item | Content |
| :---: | :--- |
| Principle | Extracts all internal moisture through osmosis and dehydrates the cell membranes of microorganisms, causing them to perish (at a salinity level of 20% or higher). |
| Suitable Foods | High-moisture vegetables such as napa cabbage, radishes, cucumbers, eggplants, and chili peppers. |
| Storage | Keep the salting container in a cool, dark place protected from moisture and light. |

| Step | Instructions |
| :---: | :--- |
| 1 | Wash the vegetables and slice them into uniform pieces. |
| 2 | Remove surface moisture from the vegetables completely using a colander. |
| 3 | Spread a layer of salt at least 1 cm thick at the bottom of the container, then layer the vegetables and salt alternately in an approximate 1:1 ratio, or fully submerge them in a concentrated brine of 20% or higher. |
| 4 | Cover the container with a lid and seal it securely. |
| 5 | For consumption, rinse multiple times under running water and soak in water to remove excess salt before cooking. |

---
### 5-5. Lactic Acid Fermentation
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This is a metabolic preservation method that uses a low amount of salt (3%) to proliferate beneficial lactic acid bacteria.

| Item | Content |
| :---: | :--- |
| Principle | Lactic acid bacteria consume the sugars in vegetables to produce 'lactic acid', shifting the environment to acidic conditions (pH 4.6 or lower). Spoilage bacteria and foodborne pathogens (including *Clostridium botulinum*) cannot survive in an acidic environment. |
| Suitable Foods | Cabbage (sauerkraut), cucumbers (fermented pickles), radishes, and various types of kimchi. |
| Storage | Keep the fermentation container in a cool, dark place protected from moisture and light. |

| Step | Sauerkraut Recipe Using Cabbage |
| :---: | :--- |
| 1 | Shred the cabbage finely and toss it with salt equivalent to 3% of the total weight. |
| 2 | Press down firmly until the juices are extracted from the vegetables, then weigh them down with a fermentation weight so that the vegetables are completely submerged in their own liquid. (Excluding oxygen is the key factor). |
| 3 | Ferment at room temperature (20°C) for several days while venting accumulated gas, then transfer to a cool location for storage. |

| Step | Kimchi-Making Method (Kimjang) |
| :---: | :--- |
| 1 | Whole napa cabbage for Poggi-kimchi: Sprinkle coarse salt between the leaves and cure the cabbage in a 12% brine for 8 hours at room temperature (20°C). Afterwards, verify that the thick white stem bends smoothly without snapping. |
| | Sliced napa cabbage for Mat-kimchi: Cut the cabbage into bite-sized pieces, sprinkle salt evenly, toss well, and let it wilt for 2 hours. Afterwards, verify that a moderate amount of water has accumulated from the cabbage. |
| | Radish for Kkakdugi: Dice the radish into cubes, sprinkle both salt and sugar, and let it wilt for 1 hour. Afterwards, verify that the radish has softened slightly and radish juice has pooled at the bottom. |
| 2 | Rinse the cured vegetables thoroughly twice in cold water to remove excess salt and impurities. |
| 3 | Place the rinsed vegetables in a colander for 1 hour to let the moisture drain naturally. (Pressing or twisting by hand destroys the cell walls, which can cause the kimchi to lose its crispness and develop an off-odor). |
| 4 | Boil glutinous rice flour or wheat flour in water to prepare a starch paste (porridge). The starch paste serves as the initial nutrient source for lactic acid bacteria to accelerate fermentation, and it helps the seasoning adhere to the vegetables. |
| 5 | Mix red pepper powder (Gochugaru), minced garlic, minced ginger, and fermented fish sauce into the starch paste to create the kimchi seasoning paste, then toss it thoroughly with the cured vegetables. |
| 6 | Pack the mixture firmly into a fermentation container to expel air, then cover the top with outer cabbage leaves or press a sanitary plastic wrap tightly against the surface to block oxygen. |
| 7 | Leave the container at room temperature (20°C) for about a day; once bubbles begin to form from gas generation, transfer it immediately to a refrigerator (0–5°C) for low-temperature aging over several days to weeks. (If the refrigerator is unavailable due to a power outage, bury it underground or in the coolest basement to minimize room-temperature exposure). |
