## 3. Growing Conditions

### 3-1. Limits and Optimal Values

| Item | Limit | Optimum | Outside This Range |
|---|---|---|---|
| Growth temperature | 15-38°C | 25-35°C | The range is narrow, just 10°C. Growth effectively stops below 15°C |
| Low-temperature limit | 10°C | - | Growth stops at or below 10°C. The above-ground parts die at 0°C |
| Frost | Cannot tolerate | - | A single frost ends that year's growing season |
| Annual rainfall | 500-1,500mm | 1,000-1,500mm | Survives even below 500mm; only the yield drops |
| Total evapotranspiration | - | about 400mm | Less than the potato's 500-700mm |
| Soil pH | 4.0-8.0 | 5.5-6.5 | Very high tolerance for acidic soil |
| Soil | Sandy to loam | Well-drained sandy loam | In flooded or poorly drained ground the roots rot quickly |
| Elevation | 0-1,500m | Lowland | At higher elevations, lower temperatures slow growth |
| Sunlight | 6-8 hours a day | 8+ hours a day | Relative yield about 80% at 40% shade |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;A temperature range of 10°C and an E for cold hardiness are the starting point for every plan involving cassava in South Korea. In central South Korea, the period when the daily average temperature stays above 20°C runs roughly four months, from June to September. Planting in early May and digging in late October gives a six-month season, but of that, cassava only really grows well for a bit over four months. Getting half the yield that ten months in the tropics would produce counts as doing well.

### 3-2. Choosing a Site in a Disaster

| Condition | Assessment |
|---|---|
| South-facing slope, sheltered from wind | Best choice. Soil warms up quickly, extending the growing season |
| Sandy, well-drained ground | Good. Roots go in deep and are easy to dig up |
| Poor, acidic red soil | Good. Puts to use land other crops cannot |
| Sloped land | Possible, but build contour terraces beyond 15° |
| Lowland where water collects | Avoid absolutely. Roots rot within days |
| Frost pockets, basins where cold air collects | Avoid. The first frost comes early, shortening the season |
| Heavy, clay-rich soil | Avoid. Roots fail to thicken and are hard to dig up |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;If you are planting cassava in South Korea, southern South Korea has the advantage unless you use a greenhouse or plastic tunnel, and even there the season is short. The SS grade for infrastructure needs is based on tropical conditions; in South Korea, mulching to raise soil temperature is effectively required. Without plastic sheeting, a thin scattering of black leaf mold or ash on the surface is used to raise soil temperature.

## 4. Varieties

### 4-1. Bitter and Sweet Varieties - the Most Important Distinction

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The criterion that divides cassava varieties is neither yield nor days to maturity, but cyanogenic glycoside content. By convention they are called sweet varieties (low-bitterness) and bitter varieties (high-bitterness), but what this actually means is lines with low cyanide content and lines with high cyanide content.

| Category | Sweet Variety (Low-Bitterness) | Bitter Variety (High-Bitterness) |
|---|---|---|
| Cyanide content | About 50mg or less per 1kg fresh weight | From 100mg up to 1,000mg or more |
| Distribution | Low concentration in the flesh; concentrated in the skin | Present in significant amounts in the flesh as well |
| Processing | A thick peel and thorough boiling are enough | A multi-step process — grinding, fermenting, drying, soaking, and so on — is essential |
| Yield | Relatively low | High. Starch content is also high |
| Pests & diseases | More damage from animals and insects | Cyanide concentration acts as a defense |
| Theft | Easy to pull up and take from the field | Cannot be eaten without knowing the processing, so theft is low |
| Use | Eating fresh, boiling | Starch, flour, fermented products |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The reason African farmers deliberately plant bitter varieties is in the bottom three rows of the table above. Yield is higher, animals eat less of it, and people cannot steal it. The worse a famine gets, the more the share of bitter varieties tends to rise, and as a result, konzo cases increase during famines. Which variety to choose in a disaster is a trade-off between yield and safety.

> **Bitterness can be used to judge a variety, but the degree of bitterness is not exactly proportional to cyanide content. The rule is to treat any cassava of unknown variety as a bitter variety and process it accordingly.**

### 4-2. Major Varieties

| Variety | Characteristics | Disaster Suitability |
|---|---|:---:|
| Kasetsart 50 | Thailand's most widely grown variety. High starch content and high yield | High |
| Rayong 9 | A major variety in Thailand. High-yielding with a high dry-matter ratio | High |
| KM505, KM140 | Excellent pest and disease resistance. Suited to areas with viral disease | High |
| Traditional sweet varieties (landraces) | Lower yield, but processing is simple | Suited to beginners |

### 4-3. Selection Criteria by Situation

| Situation | Choice | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| No processing knowledge or tools available | Sweet variety | Peeling and boiling alone reach a safe level |
| Children and elderly people eating together | Sweet variety | Their intake per body weight is larger, so the risk is greater |
| Stockpiling as starch or flour | Bitter variety | Starch content is high, and it goes through multi-step processing anyway |
| Heavy animal damage or theft | Bitter variety | Its bitterness itself acts as a defense |
| Areas with viral disease outbreaks | KM505, KM140 | Resistant lines |
| Short growing season (outdoors in South Korea) | Early-maturing lines | The roots must set within six months |

## 5. Field Preparation and Fertilizer

### 5-1. Preparing the Ground

| Item | Standard | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Tillage | 20-30cm deep | Creates room for the storage roots to expand into |
| Ridges | High ridges, 30cm or more | Prevents waterlogging. Cassava's one fatal weakness |
| Drainage channels | One for every ridge | If water pools, the roots rot within days |
| Soil acidity | Tolerates pH 4.5-7.5 | No separate correction needed |
| Sloped land | Contour terraces for 15° or more | Digging up the roots exposes bare soil, causing severe erosion |
| Raising soil temperature | Black mulch or ash | In South Korea, early soil temperature is the key factor |

### 5-2. Standard Fertilizer Rate (per 1,000m², 300 pyeong (about 990 m²))

| Fertilizer | Application Rate | Notes |
|---|:---:|---|
| Nitrogen (N) | 8-12kg | Split between base fertilizer and top-dressing |
| Phosphate (P) | 6-10kg | All as base fertilizer |
| Potassium (K) | 8-12kg | All as base fertilizer. Most important for starch accumulation |
| Compost | 1,000-1,500kg | Well-rotted compost, all as base fertilizer |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;An SS grade for fertilizer demand means a harvest comes in even without applying fertilizer according to this table. In practice, most cassava fields on African smallholdings receive no fertilizer at all. However, growing it continuously with no fertilizer depletes the soil's potassium quickly. As cassava fills its roots with starch it draws off large amounts of potassium, and because the whole root is dug up and carried off the field, that potassium leaves with it. This is why continuous-cropping tolerance is capped at A (2-year break), with the note that it "draws heavily on soil fertility even in poor soil."

### 5-3. When There Is No Chemical Fertilizer - Disaster Substitutes

| Substitute | Nutrients Supplied | How to Make and Use It | Caution |
|---|---|---|---|
| Wood ash | Potassium, phosphate, calcium | The single most important substitute for cassava. One or two handfuls per m² | It is alkaline, so too much raises the pH |
| Cassava stems and leaves | Potassium, nitrogen, organic matter | Return the stems and leaves left after harvest to the same spot | The most reliable way to cycle potassium back |
| Peels and processing waste | Potassium, organic matter | Turn into compost | Cyanide is still present, so do not feed it to livestock directly |
| Well-rotted compost | Comprehensive nutrients | Let fallen leaves, grass, and manure rot for 6 months or more | If underripe, it damages the roots |
| Diluted urine | Nitrogen, potassium | Dilute tenfold with water and apply between the ridges | Keep it from touching the leaves directly |
| Bone meal | Phosphate, calcium | Burn, crush, and mix into compost | Slow to take effect |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The key to resource cycling in cassava growing is not taking the stems, leaves, and peel off the field. Taking only the harvested roots and returning everything else to the field can cut potassium loss substantially. In particular, the peel makes up 10-20% of the root's weight, and returning it as compost yields a significant amount of nutrients. However, since the peel has the highest cyanide concentration, do not feed it to livestock directly — let it rot thoroughly first.

## 6. Stem Cuttings

### 6-1. Timing

| Region | Planting Time | Harvest Time | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| South Korea (central and southern regions) | Early May | Late October | Only the six months between frosts are available |
| Tropics | Any time the temperature is 20°C or above | 8-18 months later | Timed to the start of the rainy season |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In South Korea, "early May" means right after the last late frost. Cassava cannot survive even one frost, so planting even a single day too early wipes it out entirely. Planting too late, on the other hand, shortens the season and the roots never set properly. Not missing this narrow window is the first condition for growing cassava in South Korea.

### 6-2. Preparing Cuttings

| Item | Standard | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Where to cut | The middle part of the stem | The tip is too soft and the base is too heavily woody |
| Stem thickness | 2-3cm diameter | Establishment is good only when stored nutrients are sufficient |
| Cutting length | 15-20cm | Too short and nutrients run short; too long and it is wasteful |
| Number of nodes | 3-5 | Buds can sprout from each node |
| Stem condition | Firm and healthy | A virus-infected cutting cuts yield by 30-40% |
| Cut surface | Cut at an angle | Widens the area from which roots can emerge |
| Top and bottom | Must always be told apart | Planted upside down, no shoot will emerge |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The top and bottom of a cutting are told apart by the direction of the leaf scars on the nodes. The leaf scar is a crescent shape that opens upward, with the bud just above it. If it is hard to tell, plant the cutting on its side at an angle. Laid on its side, shoots and roots will emerge from the nodes regardless of orientation.

### 6-3. Disinfecting Cuttings

| Method | Procedure | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Heat treatment | Soak in 50-55°C water for 10-15 minutes | Kills pathogens on the surface |
| Ash treatment | Coat the cut surface with wood ash | Serves as both disinfection and drying |
| Drying | Dry the cut surface in the shade for about a day | Prevents rot |
| Culling | Do not use stems showing disease symptoms | The most effective control there is |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Temperature matters when doing the hot-water treatment. Above 60°C the cutting itself dies, and below 45°C the pathogens survive. Without a thermometer, the water is at roughly 50°C when you can hold your hand in it for a few seconds but cannot keep it in there for long. However, if your judgment is off, this method can cost you the whole batch of planting material, so if you are not confident, it is safer to simply cull cuttings by eye instead.

### 6-4. Planting

| Item | Standard | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Planting depth | 5-10cm | So that one-third to one-half of the cutting is buried |
| Planting angle | 15-45° tilt, or vertical | Planting at an angle concentrates the roots on one side, making digging easier |
| Plant spacing | 80-100cm | Looks wide, but is necessary since it grows into a shrub |
| Row spacing | 80-100cm | Ensures airflow and photosynthesis |
| After planting | Firm the soil in and water thoroughly | Keeps it from drying out until it takes root |
| Thinning | When plants are 15-20cm tall | Leave only the 1-2 strongest shoots per hole |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There is a reason for planting at an angle. Planted vertically, the roots spread out deep in every direction, which means more digging and cut-off roots at harvest. Planted on a slant of around 45°, the roots come out concentrated on one side, so at harvest, levering it up from the opposite side brings the whole thing out in one motion. When you have to dig by hand, this difference matters a great deal.
