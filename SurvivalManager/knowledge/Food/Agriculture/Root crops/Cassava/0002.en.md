## 1. Disaster-Preparedness Evaluation

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Cassava's overall grade is S, with a score of 53.8. By scenario: short-term S (51.9), long-term S (55.8), supplementary B (48.1). The fact that long-term outranks short-term sums up cassava's character. Because you have to wait eight to ten months after planting, it is of little use for filling the stomach right away, but once established it holds on in the ground for a long time, producing calories year after year. The B for supplementary means this crop is meant to be used as a staple, not as a side dish.

### 1-3. How to Read the Strengths

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Cassava's SS grade for calories per area is the most striking number on this table. At 4,000kcal per m² — 1.53× the self-sufficiency line — the arithmetic works out to just over 65m² covering one person's calories for a year. Compare that to the 130m² potatoes need or the 190m² Jerusalem artichokes need, and it is less than half. On top of that comes SS for fertilizer demand. No fertilizer is needed at all. It does not matter whether the soil is acidic, mixed with gravel, or has no organic matter. It is a common sight across Africa and Southeast Asia: on red laterite soil where no other crop yields anything, cassava alone produces a harvest.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;SS for seed saving matters as well. Cassava propagates not from seed but by cutting and planting pieces of stem, and the stems from a single plant can produce many new plants. The multiplication rate is very high, so even when the seed supply is cut off, the planted area can still be expanded using the crop's own material. This is a decisive difference from grain crops, which require buying seed.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The A grade for perenniality means something a little different here than it does for other crops. Cassava is a perennial shrub, but once its roots are dug up, that plant is finished. It still earns an A because its harvest window is extremely flexible. It becomes edible after eight months, yield peaks after twelve months, and after that it can simply be left in the ground for a year or two and dug up whenever it is needed. The field itself becomes the storehouse. That is completely unlike a grain crop, which is ruined if the harvest window is missed, and it is a tremendous advantage when there is no way to store food.

### 1-4. How to Read the Weaknesses

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Five criteria carry an E grade: protein density, cooking & processing, cold hardiness, storability, and non-food value. Add seed longevity and that makes six. Three of these are things you absolutely must plan around when working with cassava.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;First, the E for protein density is a fundamental limitation of this crop. Protein's share of calories is only 3.4%, just a third of the 10% requirement line. Eating cassava alone fills the stomach while the body wastes away. This is why protein deficiency is common among children in cassava-dependent regions of Africa. And this problem connects directly to the toxicity issue covered in Section 14: the body needs sulfur-containing amino acids to detoxify cyanide, and when protein is short, those amino acids are missing, so the body's detoxification capacity itself drops. A person who eats nothing but cassava is hurt more badly by the same dose of cyanide.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Second, the E for cooking & processing is because of the procedure for removing cyanogenic glycosides. Cassava is the only crop on this table with an E in this criterion. It requires a multi-step process: peeling, soaking in water, grinding and fermenting, drying completely, and heating thoroughly. It costs fuel, water, time, and labor at every step, and skipping any one of them hurts people.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Third, the E for cold hardiness is the most practically limiting factor when growing cassava in South Korea. Growth stops below 10°C, and a single frost kills the plant. Since it cannot overwinter outdoors in South Korea, the only viable schedule is a single-season crop: plant cuttings in May and dig in late October. The yield that the tropics build up over eight to twelve months has to be produced in just six months here, so the actual yield is much lower. And the stem cuttings have to be kept alive and unfrozen through the entire winter — which becomes the hardest task of all once heating is no longer available.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Storability E has to be considered together with this. Within 2-3 days of being dug up, a cassava root starts to spoil, turning bluish inside. This is a phenomenon called physiological deterioration, and it happens faster than in any other planting material. Because of this, cassava has almost no concept of "post-harvest storage" — once dug, it must be eaten immediately or processed the same day. Leaving it in the ground is, in effect, the only storage method there is.

> **Cassava is the crop that produces the most calories from the least input — and, at the same time, the crop that demands the most processing labor and carries the greatest risk.**

## 2. What Kind of Plant Is This?

### 2-1. Taxonomy and Origin

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Cassava is a perennial shrub of the genus Manihot in the spurge family (Euphorbiaceae). It belongs to the same family as the rubber tree, castor bean, and poinsettia. Plants in the spurge family share the trait of oozing white latex when cut and, in most cases, being toxic — and cassava is no exception. Keeping this family history in mind makes it easier to understand that cassava's toxicity is not some freak accident but a defense the plant evolved on its own. Cyanogenic glycosides are a chemical weapon aimed at insects that chew the leaves and animals that dig up the roots. Humans are the only species that learned how to process it and eat it anyway.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Its native range is the savanna belt of west-central Brazil. There the wet and dry seasons are sharply separated, no rain falls for months during the dry season, and the soil is acidic and leached of nutrients. Every trait cassava has comes from this origin. When the dry season arrives, it sheds its leaves, stops growing, and lives off the reserves stored in its roots. In nutrient-poor soil it sends roots deep to scavenge even the smallest amounts of phosphate. But because it has never experienced frost, its growth stops at temperatures as mild as 10°C, and it dies at 0°C.

### 2-2. Appearance

| Organ | Characteristics |
|---|---|
| Leaves | Palmately compound, palm-shaped leaves. Divided into 5-7 lobes, with long petioles |
| Stem | Grows upright, up to 2-4m tall. Nodes are distinct, and buds sprout from them |
| Root (edible part) | A storage root. 20-50cm long, 1-5kg in weight. 5-10 per plant |
| Skin | Divided into a thin outer brown layer and a thick inner white layer. The part with the highest cyanide concentration |
| Latex | White sap oozes out when the plant is cut |
| Flowers and fruit | Unisexual flowers on a monoecious plant. It forms capsule fruit, but this is not used for propagation |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The fact that the edible part is a root is what sets cassava apart from the potato. A potato is a swollen stem, so it has eyes that can be cut and planted, but cassava's storage root has no eyes, so planting it produces no sprout. That is why propagation must always be done through the stem. There is one more point: because it is a root, the moment it is pulled away from the parent plant at digging, it starts to spoil. This is the anatomical reason behind the E grade for storability.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The structure of the skin also matters in practice. Cassava's skin has two layers: a thin, rough, brown outer layer, and a thick, white inner layer beneath it. Cyanogenic glycosides are concentrated in these two layers, and the concentration in the inner white layer runs several to several dozen times higher than in the flesh. When people talk about "peeling" cassava, scraping off just the outer brown layer is not enough — the thick inner white layer has to be removed as well. This is covered again in Section 14.

### 2-3. Growth Stages

| Stage | Timing | Key Physiology | Key Nutrient |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cutting & establishment | 1-2 months after planting the cutting | Roots and shoots emerge from the nodes at the same time | Phosphate, moisture |
| Vegetative growth | 2-6 months | Stems and leaves expand. Early growth is slow | Nitrogen |
| Storage root bulking | 6-10 months | Storage roots thicken and starch accumulates | Peak potassium demand |
| Maturity & harvest | 10-12 months | Roots fill out fully and the skin hardens | Irrigation stopped |
| Delayed harvest | 12-24 months | Stays in the ground. Fiber increases and the roots become tough | - |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The slow early growth matters in practice. For the first three to four months after planting, cassava loses out in competition with weeds. If weeds are not controlled during this period, the roots will not thicken. Conversely, once the leaves have covered the ground after about four months, weeds are hardly a problem anymore. The labor of growing cassava is concentrated at the front end.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Calories are the highest of any tuber crop (tubers/storage roots), and protein is the lowest. These two facts define cassava's character. Cassava is a calorie source, not a source of nutrition. Building a diet on cassava alone will damage the body within a few months. It must always be eaten together with legumes, fish, or the cassava leaves themselves.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Cassava leaves contain up to 5-10g of protein on a fresh-weight basis and are rich in lysine, which can make up for much of the protein the root lacks. This is why eating cassava leaves as a vegetable is a widespread custom in Africa and Southeast Asia. However, the leaves have a higher cyanide content than the root, so they must be processed strictly by the procedure. This is covered separately in Section 14.
