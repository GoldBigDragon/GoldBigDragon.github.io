## 15. Storage Without Refrigeration

### 15-1. Cassava Has No Live Storage

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The E grade for storability is among the lowest on this table. Potatoes hold for four months in a root-cellar pit, and Jerusalem artichokes last all winter in the ground, but a cassava root turns bluish inside within two to three days of being dug up and starts to spoil within three to four days. It happens faster than in any other tuber crop (tubers/storage roots). The reason lies in anatomy. Cassava is a root, and a root triggers a wound response when it is separated from the parent plant. This is fundamentally different from the potato and Jerusalem artichoke, which are stems and so form their own cork layer to seal a wound.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This means there are only two branches to a cassava storage strategy: leave it undug in the ground, or dig it and process it immediately. There is effectively no such thing as a way to store a dug, raw root.

| Storage Factor | Potato | Cassava |
|---|---|---|
| Edible part | Stem (tuber) | Root (storage root) |
| Wound sealing | Forms its own cork layer | Cannot form one |
| Limit after digging | 1-2 months | 2-3 days |
| Main storage method | Root-cellar pit storage | Leaving it undug in the ground |
| Long-term storage method | Starch and drying | Drying and starch. Effectively the only method |

### 15-2. In-Ground Storage - the Tropical Standard

| Item | Details |
|---|---|
| Principle | Leave it unharvested, still in the ground. As long as the root stays attached to the parent plant, it does not spoil |
| Period | From the 8th month up to 24 months |
| Method | Keep the above-ground parts alive and dig up only the plants you need |
| Advantage | Requires no power, no infrastructure, no labor. The highest freshness there is |
| Disadvantage | Over time, fiber increases and it toughens, while the starch ratio drops |
| Risk | Wild boar and rodents. Bitter varieties suffer relatively less |
| In South Korea | Not viable. Once frost comes, the above-ground parts die and the roots soon spoil as well |

> **If you grow cassava in South Korea, storing it by leaving it in the field is not possible. Everything must be dug up in late October and processed immediately. The fact that the storage strategy that works in the tropics does not work here is the single biggest burden of growing cassava in South Korea.**

### 15-3. Short-Term Storage After Digging

| Method | How | Duration | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Burying in wet sand or soil | Rebury the whole root in soil | 1-2 weeks | A way of imitating the condition of being left in the field |
| Burying in wet sawdust or rice husks | Layer it in a box | 1-2 weeks | Manage so it does not dry out |
| Wax or clay coating | Seal the cut surface and skin with clay | 2-4 weeks | Blocks the wound and slows deterioration |
| Cool indoor spot | Spread out in a well-ventilated, shaded place | 2-5 days | Effectively just temporary holding |
| Soaking in water | Submerge it completely | 3-7 days | Change the water. Can double as the detoxification soak |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Clay coating is a usable method when no tools are available. Sealing the cut surfaces and wounded areas of a dug root with a fine clay paste reduces contact with air and slows deterioration. However, even this does not last beyond a few weeks and is not a fundamental solution.

### 15-4. Long-Term Storage Through Processing - Effectively the Only Path

| Method | Procedure | Duration | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dried chips | Peel, slice to 5mm or less, dry completely for 4-7 days | 1-2 years | Solves detoxification and storage at the same time |
| Flour | Grind the fully dried chips in a mortar or millstone | 1-2 years | Seal it and add ash or charcoal as a desiccant |
| Starch (tapioca) | Grind, mix into water, rinse the sediment several times, and dry it | Several years | The strongest detoxification effect. Barely attracts insects |
| Fermented product | Grind, ferment for 3-5 days, press, roast | 6 months-1 year | The West African gari method |
| Dried granules | Steam the pressed, fermented mass into granules and dry them | 1 year or more | Soak in water and eat right away |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Extracting starch is especially useful in a disaster. It has the longest storage life, greatly reduces bulk and weight, is barely touched by insects, and above all has the most reliable detoxification effect. The procedure is as follows. Peel the root thickly and grind it completely with a grater or a stone mortar. Wrap it in cloth and knead it underwater, and white liquid comes out. Let it stand for 30 minutes to an hour to let the sediment settle, pour off the water on top and discard it, then pour in fresh water and let it settle again, repeating this three or four times. Through this process, the cyanide compounds dissolved in the water keep leaving along with the water poured off the top. Spread the white sediment that remains at the end out thinly and dry it completely, and you have tapioca starch. From 100kg of roots comes roughly 20kg to 25kg.

| Caution | Details |
|---|---|
| The water poured off | Poison is dissolved in it. Do not drink it and do not give it to livestock either |
| The pressed-out solids | Contain fiber and residual starch. Ferment them thoroughly before using as animal feed |
| Drying | Dry it completely. If moisture remains, mold develops |
| Storage | Store in a sealed container with ash or charcoal |

## 16. Cooking and Processing

### 16-1. Fuel-Saving Cooking

| Method | Fuel Use | Procedure | Notes |
|---|:---:|---|---|
| Cooking starch or flour | Very low | Stirred into boiling water, it cooks instantly | The best fuel efficiency |
| Boiling finely cut pieces | Low | Cut to 2cm or less, 15-20 minutes | The larger surface area also speeds up detoxification |
| Steaming | Low | With a little water, 25-30 minutes | Leave a path open for the gas to escape, though |
| Boiling whole | Medium | 20-30 minutes | Not used for bitter varieties |
| Residual-heat cooking | Very low | Boil 15 minutes, then turn off the heat, wrap, and let sit 1 hour | Use only for material that has already finished the earlier detoxification steps |
| Baking in ash | Very low | Bury in hot ash for 30-40 minutes | Only for sweet varieties |

> **Cutting heating time to save fuel is a saving with other crops, but with cassava it is a danger. Save fuel at the earlier steps instead. Grind, soak, and dry it in advance, and the final heating can be short.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This is the core principle of cooking cassava. Boiling a sweet variety fresh to eat takes 20 to 30 minutes' worth of fuel, but if it has been dried and made into flour in advance, all it takes is stirring it into boiling water. In other words, concentrating fuel and time into processing at harvest time saves a great deal of fuel at every meal for months afterward. Given how scarce firewood becomes during winter, this upfront investment is especially valuable.

### 16-2. Cooking Precautions

| Item | Details |
|---|---|
| Skin | Never eaten. Has the highest cyanide concentration |
| Central core | Tough, and relatively high in cyanide too. Remove it |
| The boiling water | Always discard it. Do not use it as broth |
| Lid | Leave it off. The gas must be able to escape |
| Cooking large quantities indoors | Ventilate |
| Taste test | If any bitterness or astringency remains, do not eat it |
| Discolored roots | If blue streaks or browning appear inside, cut that part out. If it is severe, discard the whole thing |
| Children | Even the same amount is far more dangerous for them. Give them only food that has been reliably processed |

### 16-3. Processing That Doubles as Storage

| Product | Procedure | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Cassava flour | Peel, slice thin, dry completely, then grind | Used for porridge, dough, or rice cakes. Since it has no gluten, it works well mixed with grain flour |
| Tapioca starch | Grind, mix into water, rinse the sediment several times, and dry | Lasts the longest and is the safest |
| Fermented granules | Grind, ferment, press, roast | Soak in water and eat right away. West African gari |
| Steamed dough | Pound the boiled root in a mortar into dough | West African fufu |
| Chips | Slice thin, dry, then fry in oil or bake in ash | Both a snack and an emergency food |
| Braised leaves | Braise pounded, long-boiled leaves with oil and salt | Becomes a protein source |

### 16-4. Principles of a Cassava Diet

| Principle | Reason |
|---|---|
| Do not eat cassava alone | Protein shortage lowers the body's ability to detoxify cyanide |
| Plant and eat legumes alongside it | Secures both calories and protein from the same field |
| Do not discard the leaves | The easiest way to make up for the protein the root lacks |
| Secure salt and seaweed | If iodine deficiency compounds it, thyroid problems grow worse |
| Do not rely on a single processed product | So that one processing failure does not ruin the entire food supply |

## 17. Annual Work Calendar

| Month | Task | Key Point |
|---|---|---|
| January | Overwintering care for cuttings, check processed stock | Keep it from dropping below 10°C. The most dangerous period |
| February | Check the condition of cuttings, plan the field | Cull any stems that are dried out or soft |
| March | Prepare the field, build ridges, add compost | High ridges and drainage channels are key |
| April | Cut and disinfect cuttings, raise soil temperature | Watch for late frosts and wait |
| Early May | Plant the cuttings | Right after the last late frost. Missing this window leaves too short a season |
| May-June | Water for establishment, thin, first weeding | Weed management in the first four months determines yield |
| July | Second weeding, hilling, begin leaf harvest | Pick only a few young upper leaves at a time |
| August | Prepare for typhoons, harvest leaves | Mound soil up around the base |
| September | Storage root bulking, water during drought | This is when the roots thicken |
| October | Stop irrigation, prepare for harvest, check processing tools | Check the frost forecast daily |
| Late October | Harvest everything, collect cuttings, process immediately | Peel, slice, and dry on the same day it is dug |
| November | Finish drying and starch processing, prepare cuttings for winter | Return the leftover stems, leaves, and peel to the field |
| December | Overwintering care for cuttings, check flour storage | Keep it from freezing or drying out |

### 17-1. Five Things Never to Forget

| Item | Details |
|---|---|
| Do not cut corners on processing | The hungrier you are, the more you must follow the procedure. Konzo always comes together with famine |
| Boiling alone is not enough | First break it down, soak it, and dry it, then heat it. The order is a matter of life and death |
| Do not eat cassava alone | Without protein, the body cannot detoxify cyanide |
| Process it the same day it is dug | It starts to spoil within two days. Dig a little and process a little at a time |
| Lose the cuttings, and it is over | In South Korea, whether cassava growing succeeds or fails is decided indoors in winter |
