## Introduction

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Cassava (Manihot esculenta) originated in west-central Brazil, in South America. It is a shrub of the spurge family that forms thick storage roots underground and fills them with starch. Today more than 800 million people across Africa, Southeast Asia, and South America depend on this crop for calories, and it is one of the most widely grown tuber crops in the world. Even in a year when drought wipes out the grain harvest, cassava holds on underground. It grows even in poor, unfertilized red soil. It can be left in the ground for a year or two past harvest time without rotting, and dug up whenever it is needed. That is why it is called the last line of defense against famine.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In the disaster-preparedness evaluation, cassava scored an overall grade of S, 53.8 points. Calories per area are SS, 4,000kcal per m², 1.53× the self-sufficiency line. That is higher than any tuber crop (tubers/storage roots) on this list. It also carries SS for fertilizer demand, SS for infrastructure needs, S for water use, and SS for seed saving. In other words, just push a stem cutting into poor soil, and a year later you get enough calories to live on. In a disaster, few ways of producing food are simpler than this.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;But cassava carries one condition that no other crop on this list carries. Eaten the wrong way, this plant kills people. Every part of cassava contains a cyanogenic glycoside called linamarin, and when the tissue is broken, an enzyme acts on it to produce hydrogen cyanide — prussic acid gas. In strongly bitter varieties, the skin alone can contain more than 1,000mg of cyanide equivalents per 1kg fresh weight. Given that a lethal dose for an adult starts in the tens of milligrams, a single meal of unprocessed bitter cassava can kill a person. That is the one and only reason the cooking & processing criterion is graded E on the evaluation table.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;What is more frightening than acute poisoning is chronic poisoning. Eating carelessly processed cassava for weeks to months brings on a disease in which, one day, both legs suddenly go stiff and walking becomes impossible. It is called konzo, and once it strikes, it does not go away. In Africa this disease always arrives together with famine. When people are hungry they rush; when they rush, three days of soaking becomes one day of soaking; and after months of that, the children of the village can no longer walk. If you plan to treat cassava as disaster food, the thing you need to know before the growing methods is this processing procedure. That is why Section 14 of this document is unusually long. What it contains is not a recipe — it is information that is a matter of life and death.
