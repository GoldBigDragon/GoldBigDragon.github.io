## Introduction
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Cassava (Manihot esculenta) is a spurge-family shrub from central-western Brazil that fills thick tuberous roots with starch underground. It holds on in years of drought when grains are wiped out, grows even in poor red soil without fertilizer, and can be left in the ground for a year or two past harvest time, which is why it is called the last bulwark against famine. In the disaster preparedness evaluation it scores an overall S (53.8 points, 7th of 183 valid species), and its calories per area of SS (4,000 kcal per ㎡, 1.53 times the self-sufficiency line) are the highest among roots and tubers. However, every part of the plant contains cyanogenic glycosides, so an untreated bitter variety can kill a person in a single meal, and eating poorly processed cassava for weeks to months causes konzo, which stiffens the legs until the person can no longer walk. Konzo always comes with famine, because when people are hungry they soak for one day what should be soaked for three. So learn the detoxification procedure in Section 10 before you learn how to grow it.

| Weaknesses | E: protein density, cooking and processing requirement, cold tolerance, storability, non-food value, seed longevity. D: harvest speed, domestic cultivation spread. C: temperature range, pest and disease pressure, pest-control self-reliance, taste |

> **Cassava is a crop that gives the most calories for the least input, and at the same time demands the most processing labor and carries the greatest risk.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Calories per area SS means that a little over 65 ㎡ can supply one person's calories for a year, which is half of potato (130 ㎡), and it yields without fertilizer. Seed saving SS means it propagates by planting stem cuttings, so area can be expanded even when the seed supply is cut off, and perenniality A means it can be eaten eight months after planting and then left in the ground for a year or two. Protein density E means protein is only 3.4% of calories, one third of the 10% requirement, and cooking and processing E is because of the cyanide removal procedure; cassava is the only crop with E in this item. Cold tolerance E means it dies from a single frost, so in Korean open fields only a one-year season, planting cuttings in May and digging in late October, works, and storability E means it spoils 2-3 days after digging. The grade for each item is in the Crop Evaluation Results document.

## 2. Plant Overview
| Item | Details |
|:---|:---|
| Classification | Perennial shrub of the spurge family, genus Manihot |
| Leaves and stem | Palmately compound leaves divided into 5-7 lobes. The stem stands upright and grows to 2-4 m |
| Tuberous roots | 5-10 per plant, 20-50 cm long and 1-5 kg each. They have no eyes and do not sprout when planted, so propagation is by stem |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;About 1-2 months after planting cuttings, roots and shoots emerge from the nodes; at 2-6 months the stem and leaves expand (early growth is slow); at 6-10 months the tuberous roots thicken (peak potassium demand); and at 10-12 months the skin hardens. Per 100 g there are 160 kcal (112 kcal boiled), the highest among roots and tubers, with 38 g of carbohydrate that is almost all starch, 1.4 g protein, 0.3 g fat, 1.8 g dietary fiber, and 20.6 mg vitamin C. The leaves have 5-10 g of protein per 100 g fresh weight and are rich in lysine, which makes up a good part of the roots' protein shortage.

## 3. Growing Environment and Varieties
| Condition | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Temperature | Growth 15-38℃ (optimum 25-35℃), a range of only 10℃. Growth stops at 10℃ or below, the aboveground part dies at 0℃, and a single frost ends the season |
| Water and light | Annual rainfall 500-1,500 mm (below 500 mm only the yield falls). 6-8 hours of sunlight a day or more (about 80% relative yield under 40% shade) |
| Soil | pH 4.0-8.0 (optimum 5.5-6.5). Well-drained sandy loam to loam is optimum, and in flooded or clayey soil the roots rot |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In central Korea the period when the daily mean temperature exceeds 20℃ is only a little over four months, so planting in early May and digging in late October gives a good result if half the tropical yield comes out. Sunny south-facing, wind-sheltered, well-drained sandy ground is good; avoid waterlogged lowlands and basins where cold air collects.

| Category | Sweet varieties | Bitter varieties |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Cyanide content (per 1 kg fresh weight) | About 50 mg or less | 100 mg to over 1,000 mg |
| Processing | Peel thickly and boil thoroughly | Multi-step processing such as grating and fermenting, drying, and soaking is essential |
| Yield and defense | Low yield; heavy damage from animals and thieves | High yield and starch content; the bitter taste serves as a defense |

> **Bitterness and cyanide content do not match exactly, so treat every cassava of unknown variety as a bitter variety and process it accordingly.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Choose sweet varieties when you lack the knowledge and tools for processing or when children and the elderly will eat it, and bitter varieties when you will stock starch and flour or animal damage is heavy. Early-maturing strains suit Korean open fields.

## 4. Field Preparation and Planting Cuttings
| Item | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Land preparation | Plow to 20-30 cm and make high ridges at least 30 cm tall, with a drainage ditch for every ridge (if water collects, roots rot within days). In Korea secure early soil warmth with black mulch or ash |
| Fertilization (1,000 ㎡) | Nitrogen 8-12 kg, phosphorus 6-10 kg, potassium 8-12 kg (most important for starch accumulation), fully matured compost 1,000-1,500 kg. Split the nitrogen into base and top dressing and apply the rest entirely as base fertilizer. It yields without fertilizer, but potassium is depleted |
| Disaster substitute fertilizers | Wood ash (a handful or two per ㎡, the most important source of potassium; too much raises pH), stems, leaves, and peels returned to the field after harvest (peels contain cyanide, so rot them first), compost aged 6 months or more, urine diluted 10 times |
| Cuttings | Cut firm, healthy stem from the middle, 2-3 cm in diameter, into 15-20 cm pieces with 3-5 nodes, cut at a slant, coat the cut ends with wood ash, and dry them in the shade for a day. Tell top from bottom by the leaf scars (a half-moon shape facing up with the bud above it), and plant lying down if unsure. Virus-infected cuttings cut yield by 30-40% |
| Planting | Early May, right after the last frost (a single day early wipes them out, and late leaves too short a season). Depth 5-10 cm; planting at a 15-45 degree slant gathers the roots to one side and makes digging easy. 80-100 cm between plants and rows. Thin to the sturdiest 1-2 stems per hole when 15-20 cm tall |

## 5. Water Management and Cultivation Management
| Stage | Water management |
|:---|:---|
| Right after planting cuttings | Soak thoroughly (before rooting they live on stored nutrients alone, so drying out kills them) |
| Rooting to vegetative growth | Leave to natural rainfall |
| Root swelling | Add water only in severe drought (dryness in this period directly affects yield). Stop irrigating 2-3 weeks before harvest (the skin hardens and starch fills out) |

| Care | Method |
|:---|:---|
| Weeds | The biggest variable deciding yield is early weed control. Weed 2-3 times during the first 3 months after planting; after 4 months the leaves cover the ground and little is needed |
| Hilling | Once or twice during growth, mound soil around the base to prevent lodging, raise the ridges again in rainy periods, and cover exposed roots with soil |
| Leaf harvest | From mid growth on, pick only a few young top leaves at a time to use as a vegetable (picking too many shrinks the roots). Always follow the leaf procedure in Section 10 |
| Excess water | If lower leaves turn yellow and the base turns black, clear the drainage ditches at once; if roots turn soft and smell foul, give up that plot |

> **Cassava does not die of drought; it dies of water. Do not plant where water collects.**

## 6. Pests, Diseases, and Nutrient Problems
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Nearly all cassava diseases enter on cuttings, so the core of control is not chemicals but selecting cuttings (take no cuttings from plants with symptoms; this is 80% of control). Pull and burn infected plants on the spot when symptoms appear, do not grow cassava in the same field in succession, and protect natural enemies such as parasitoid wasps and ladybugs.

| Pest or disease | Symptoms and conditions | Control without chemicals |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Cassava mosaic disease, cassava brown streak disease | Mosaic patterns on leaves and curling, cutting yield by 30-70% / Brown streaks on stems and brown rot inside the roots, which can fail the whole crop | No cure, so use resistant varieties and healthy cuttings, and pull and burn infected plants |
| Whiteflies, aphids, spider mites | Whiteflies and aphids suck sap and carry viruses / Spider mites make leaves yellow and mottled and fall in dry weather | Yellow sticky traps, clearing nearby weeds, washing with water, soapy water / Spraying water on leaf undersides |

| Symptom | Cause and action |
|:---|:---|
| Whole leaves turn yellow and plants stay small / Only stems and leaves grow lush and roots are thin | Nitrogen deficiency. Matured compost and urine diluted 10 times / Nitrogen excess. Cut off nitrogen and supplement potassium with wood ash |
| Leaf edges scorch brown (during root swelling) | Potassium deficiency, the most common and most costly (leaves look fine but roots stay thin). Wood ash |
| Leaves turn deep purple | Phosphorus deficiency (common in acid soil). Bone meal and wood ash |

## 7. Intercropping and Crop Rotation
| Compatibility | Companions |
|:---|:---|
| Good | Legumes (they fix nitrogen and give a harvest from the empty space in the first four months, when cassava has not yet covered the ground), maize (harvested first while cassava's early growth is slow), pumpkin and sweet potato (cover the ground and reduce weeds and erosion) |
| Fair | Leafy greens (planted between the ridges and harvested early) |
| Poor | Continuous cropping in the same field (pathogens build up and potassium is depleted), spurge-family crops (they share the same diseases) |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Cassava grows on poor soil but scrapes out the remaining nutrients to the end, so if it is planted in the same field for several years in a row, not even cassava will grow. The order is cassava in year 1, legumes in year 2 (to restore nitrogen), leafy greens or grains in year 3, and cassava again in year 4, keeping an interval of 3-4 years.

## 8. Harvest and Seed Saving
| Harvest | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Timing | For immediate eating, 6-8 months after planting; for starch and flour processing, 8-12 months; at 12-24 months it is tough but still edible. In Korean open fields harvest in late October, and dig even if not fully mature when a first-frost forecast comes |
| Method | On a clear, dry day cut the aboveground part leaving 30-50 cm as a handle, dig around the plant to loosen it, and lever it up from the opposite side. Take care not to break the roots (rot starts at broken surfaces). Only brush off the soil and do not wash, and gather the remaining stems for cuttings |
| After harvest | The white flesh turns to bluish streaks in 24-48 hours, to brown with a bitter taste in 48-72 hours, and to rot in 3-5 days. Because you must peel, slice, and dry on the day of digging, dig only what you will eat and process this week |

| Seed saving | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Cuttings | The stem is the seed. At the late-October harvest keep stems 2-3 cm thick from the middle section uncut as long pieces of about 1 m, in a place at 10℃ or higher that neither freezes nor dries out, for up to 2-3 months (the longer, the lower the rooting rate) |
| Overwintering | This is the substance of seed longevity E. Left in an open field they certainly die. Burying the bases in wet sand or heeling them into a greenhouse or plastic tunnel has a high success rate; standing them in bundles somewhere cool indoors (10-15℃) or keeping them in an earth cellar or dugout (fails below 5℃) is middling; and potting them and carrying them through as living plants in a bright room at 15℃ or higher is the surest. Use several methods at once |

> **In Korea the success of cassava growing is decided not in the field but indoors in winter. If you lose the cuttings, there is no cassava next year.**

## 9. Storage
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Cassava is a root and cannot seal wounds with a cork layer, so it spoils 2-3 days after digging; the storage strategy therefore has only two branches: leave it undug in the ground, or dig it and process it at once.

| Method | How | Period |
|:---|:---|:---|
| In-ground storage | Do not harvest; keep the aboveground part alive and dig only the plants you need. Needs no electricity, facilities, or labor and keeps the freshest, but gets tougher over time and wild boar and rodents target it. In Korea it does not work, because frost kills the aboveground part and the roots soon spoil | From month 8 to month 24 |
| Short-term storage after digging | Bury whole roots in wet sand or soil (1-2 weeks). Sealing cut surfaces and wounds with a clay paste gives 2-4 weeks. Submerged in water that is changed, 3-7 days | 2 days to 4 weeks |
| Dried chips, flour | Peel, slice to 5 mm or less, and dry completely for 4-7 days (this solves detoxification and storage together). Keep milled chips as flour in a sealed container with ash or charcoal as a desiccant | 1-2 years |
| Starch (tapioca) | Mash thickly peeled roots completely, wrap them in cloth and knead them in water, let it settle for 30 minutes to 1 hour, pour off the top water and add fresh water, repeating 3-4 times, then dry the sediment. 100 kg of roots gives 20-25 kg; insects hardly touch it and the detoxifying effect is greatest. The poured-off water holds poison, so discard it | Several years |

> **In Korea in-ground storage is impossible, so everything must be dug in late October and processed at once.**

## 10. Cooking and Precautions
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Cyanogenic glycosides (mostly linamarin) are present in every part: the peel has several to dozens of times more than the flesh, and the leaves often have more than the roots. Fresh roots have a cyanide equivalent of about 50 mg or less per kg in sweet varieties and 100-1,000 mg or more in bitter varieties; the lethal dose for an adult is about 30-200 mg, so 200 g of a 1,000 mg-class bitter root (one meal) reaches the upper end of the lethal range. Never eat it raw, whatever the variety. The substance itself is not a poison; when tissue is broken (by peeling, cutting, or chewing), the enzyme linamarase breaks it down and makes hydrogen cyanide (boiling point 26℃). So first break the cells so that the enzyme can work, give it time to carry the process through to hydrogen cyanide, let that blow off into the air or wash it out with water, and heat last.

> **Boiling drives off hydrogen cyanide, but linamarin that has not yet become hydrogen cyanide stays as it is. And that linamarin turns into poison in the human gut.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;If a large chunk is boiled whole, the enzyme dies (at about 72℃) before the heat reaches the inside, the heat-stable linamarin remains, and after eating, gut bacteria break it down and release cyanide inside the body. Bitter varieties stay dangerous however long they are boiled if the steps below are skipped.

| Step | Method |
|:---|:---|
| Peeling | Cyanide is concentrated in the peel (2-3 mm), so peel off the outer brown layer and the inner white layer together thickly and remove the tough core as well. The peeled surface must be smooth white, and if any pink or pale layer remains, it is not peeled enough. Do not eat the peel; rot it and use it as compost |
| Soaking | Sweet-variety pieces 6-12 hours, bitter-variety pieces 24-72 hours, whole bitter roots 3-6 days (fully submerged). Change the water at least once a day (if not changed, no more comes out) and it goes faster when warm. Do not use a sealed container because gas collects, and do not drink the soaking water, feed it to animals, or pour it onto the field |
| Grating and fermenting | Most effective (95% or more removed). Mash the peeled roots completely and ferment them in the shade for 3-5 days, press the juice out with stones and discard it, then fry or steam the pulp to eat. The surest method for processing bitter varieties in bulk (the West African gari method) |
| Complete drying | Slice to 5 mm or thinner and dry in the sun for 4-7 days until it snaps with a crack (60-90% removed). If undried material is put in a sack, it molds and the poison remains |
| Thorough heating | After the earlier steps, boil 2-3 cm pieces in boiling water for 20-30 minutes with the lid open to let gas out, and always discard the cooking water. No bitter or astringent taste must remain, and ventilate when boiling large quantities indoors |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Sweet varieties take one day, with peeling, 6-12 hours of soaking, and 20-30 minutes of boiling; bitter varieties take 1-2 weeks for home use and 1 week for bulk processing (grating, fermenting, pressing, frying), and if the variety is unknown, follow the bitter-variety procedure. Dried and milled flour often still holds linamarin, so mix it with an equal volume of water, spread it thinly to 1 cm or less, leave it in the shade for 5 hours or more, and then cook it. For leaves, strip off the thick stalks, pound them thoroughly in a mortar, boil them for 30 minutes or more with the water changed, and discard the cooking water; quickly blanching them like greens is dangerous.

| Risk | Details |
|:---|:---|
| Acute poisoning | Within tens of minutes to 6 hours after eating, it begins with dizziness, headache, nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, and diarrhea, breathing becomes rapid and shallow and the person grows confused, and in severe cases it leads to convulsions, cyanosis, loss of consciousness, respiratory paralysis, and death. Children are far more at risk from the same amount because their intake per body weight is larger. If suspected, stop further eating, remove the leftover food, lay the person down somewhere with fresh air, and keep them calm; if conscious, give water in small sips but do not force vomiting, and if consciousness drops, lay them on their side to secure the airway. Where medical care is out of reach, prevention is the only measure |
| Chronic poisoning | Konzo is a disease in which, after eating inadequately processed bitter varieties for several weeks or more, the legs stiffen within hours to days until the person cannot walk (common in children and women of childbearing age); tropical ataxic neuropathy is a disease in which over years the gait becomes unsteady and eyesight and hearing decline (age 40 and over). Neither recovers and neither has a treatment, and both rise sharply when protein deficiency is added |
| Protein and iodine | Cyanide becomes thiocyanate with sulfur from protein in the liver and leaves in the urine, so eating only cassava, with protein just 3.4% of calories, weakens detoxification. Eat beans, fish, eggs, or cassava leaves together with it, and since thiocyanate blocks the thyroid's iodine uptake, secure salt and seaweed too |

| Cooking | Method |
|:---|:---|
| Saving fuel | Shortening the heating time is dangerous with cassava, so do the saving in the earlier steps. If you grate, soak, and dry it in advance and keep it as flour, it cooks just by being stirred into boiling water. For detoxified material, boiling for 15 minutes and then turning off the heat and wrapping the pot for 1 hour is also possible |
| How to eat | Mix the flour with grain flour for porridge, dough, and rice cakes. Cut out the part of any root that has blue streaks or brown inside, and discard the whole root if severe |

## 11. Annual Work Schedule
| Month | Work |
|:---|:---|
| Jan-Feb | Manage cuttings through winter (keep them from falling below 10℃; the most dangerous period) and check processed products. In February sort out dried or soft stems and plan the field |
| Mar-Apr | In March prepare the field, make high ridges and drainage ditches, and add compost. In April cut the cuttings and secure soil warmth |
| May-June | In early May, right after the last frost, plant the cuttings. Irrigate to root, thin, and do the first weeding |
| July-Aug | In July do the second weeding, hill, and start picking young top leaves. In August prepare for typhoons (mound soil at the base) |
| Sep-Oct | In September irrigate in drought during root swelling. In October stop irrigating and check frost forecasts. At the end of October harvest everything, collect cuttings, and peel, slice, and dry on the day of digging |
| Nov-Dec | In November finish drying and starch processing, return stems, leaves, and peels to the field, and prepare for overwintering the cuttings. In December manage the overwintering and check the stored flour |

> **Cassava is a crop where, the hungrier you are, you must not cut the procedure; you must not stop at boiling; you must not eat only cassava; you must process on the day of digging; and you must keep the cuttings alive indoors through winter.**
