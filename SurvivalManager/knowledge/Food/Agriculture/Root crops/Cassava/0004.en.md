## 7. Water Management

### 7-1. Stage-by-Stage Standards

| Stage | Water Management | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Right after planting | Water thoroughly | Before establishment, it survives on stored nutrients alone. It dies if it dries out |
| Establishment to vegetative growth | Rely on natural rainfall | Drought tolerance is strong enough to withstand most dry spells |
| Storage root bulking | Supplement only during severe drought | Dryness at this stage directly affects yield |
| 2-3 weeks before harvest | Stop irrigation | The skin hardens and starch fills out |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Cassava's drought tolerance comes from several mechanisms working together. It sheds its leaves to reduce transpiration, closes its stomata quickly, and sends roots down more than 2m deep. If no rain falls for months, it drops all its leaves and stands like a dry stick, then puts out leaves again once rain returns. Root starch is consumed during this period, so yield drops, but the plant does not die. This is a decisive difference from grain crops, which are wiped out by a single drought.

### 7-2. Symptoms of Water Shortage and Excess

| Category | Symptom | Progression | Action |
|---|---|---|---|
| Shortage | Lower leaves turn yellow and drop first | Normal response | No action needed. This is a survival strategy |
| Shortage | Almost all leaves drop, leaving only the stem | Serious | Root starch is being consumed. Recovers once rain comes |
| Shortage | Cutting dries out before establishment | Fatal | Does not recover. Must be replanted |
| Excess | Lower leaves yellow and the base turns black | Dangerous | Dig drainage channels immediately |
| Excess | Dug-up roots are mushy and foul-smelling | Fatal | Unrecoverable. Abandon that section |

> **Cassava does not die from drought — it dies from water. Do not plant it where water collects.**

## 8. Crop Management

### 8-1. Weed Management - the First Four Months Are Everything

| Period | Task | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Planting to 3 months | Weed regularly, 2-3 times | Early growth is slow and loses out to weeds |
| 3-4 months | Weed as needed, reinforce mulch | The ground is not yet covered |
| After 4 months | Almost no longer needed | The leaves cover the ground and suppress weeds |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The single biggest variable determining yield in cassava growing is neither fertilizer nor water, but early weed management. A field that loses out to weeds during the first three months after planting never fully recovers afterward. Conversely, once that period is past, very little hands-on work is needed after that. This means labor has to be concentrated at the front end, which is important information when allocating labor in a disaster.

### 8-2. Hilling

| Timing | Task | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| 1-2 times during growth | Mound soil up around the base | Prevents lodging and helps root development |
| During rainy periods | Build the ridges back up | Prevents waterlogging |
| When roots are exposed | Cover them with soil | Exposed roots turn green and become tough |

### 8-3. Other Tasks

| Task | Timing | Details |
|---|---|---|
| Thinning | At 15-20cm tall | Leave only 1-2 strong shoots per hole |
| Leaf harvesting | From mid-growth onward | Pick the young upper leaves to use as a vegetable. Picking too many shrinks the roots |
| Raising soil temperature | Early stage | In South Korea, mulch or ash is used to raise soil temperature |
| Windbreak | Typhoon season | At 2-4m tall, it topples in wind. Mound soil up around the base |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Leaf harvesting carries special significance in growing cassava. While waiting eight months for the roots to mature, the leaves keep growing the whole time, so they can be picked and used as a vegetable in the meantime. They contain far more protein than the root, which makes up for cassava's biggest dietary weakness. However, picking too many leaves reduces the photosynthetic area and lowers root yield. The right amount is to leave the lower leaves on each plant and pick only a few young upper leaves at a time, and processing the leaves must always follow Section 14.

## 9. Pests and Diseases

### 9-1. Major Diseases

| Disease | Symptoms | Damage | Response Without Chemicals |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cassava mosaic disease (CMD) | Mosaic patterning appears on the leaves, which curl up | 30-70% yield loss | Resistant varieties, selecting healthy cuttings. No cure |
| Cassava brown streak disease (CBSD) | Brown streaks on the stem; the inside of the root rots brown | Can cause total crop failure | Resistant varieties. Pull up and burn infected plants |
| Bacterial blight | Water-soaked spots on the leaves, wilting stems | Moderate | Healthy cuttings, crop rotation, burning diseased residue |
| Root rot | Roots turn mushy and smell foul | Fatal | Improving drainage is the only countermeasure |
| Anthracnose | Sunken spots on stems and leaves | Moderate | Ensure airflow, remove infected parts |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Almost all of cassava's diseases enter through the stem cuttings. A stem cut from a virus-infected plant carries that virus with it unchanged, and spreads it across the whole field the following year. That is why the core of cassava pest control is not chemicals but selecting cuttings. This is why the evaluation table's basis for pest-control self-reliance reads "mostly manageable just by selecting healthy stem cuttings." Even with no chemicals available, culling by eye is still something you can do.

### 9-2. Major Pests

| Pest | Damage | Response Without Chemicals |
|---|---|---|
| Whiteflies | Suck sap and spread the mosaic virus | Resistant varieties, yellow sticky traps, removing surrounding weeds |
| Aphids | Suck sap and spread viruses | Washing with water, soapy water, protecting ladybugs and parasitic wasps |
| Mites | Leaves develop yellow blotches and drop | Spraying water on the undersides of leaves; worst during dry spells |
| Scale insects | Attach to the stem and stop its growth | Cull at the cutting stage. Protect parasitic wasps |
| Termites | Gnaw on cuttings and roots | Mix wood ash in at planting |
| Wild boar and rodents | Dig up and eat the roots | Fencing. Bitter varieties suffer relatively less damage |

### 9-3. Five Principles for Control Without Chemicals

| Principle | Details |
|---|---|
| Selecting cuttings | Never take cuttings from a plant showing disease symptoms. This is 80% of pest control |
| Resistant lines | Use resistant lines whenever they can be obtained |
| Removing diseased plants immediately | If mosaic symptoms appear, pull the plant up and burn it on the spot |
| Crop rotation | Do not grow it continuously in the same field. Leave a gap of 2 years or more |
| Protecting natural enemies | Preserve the parasitic wasps and ladybugs that prey on whiteflies and aphids |

## 10. Nutrient Deficiency and Excess

| Symptom | Cause | How to Confirm | Natural Remedy |
|---|---|---|---|
| Whole leaves turn yellow and the plant stays small | Nitrogen deficiency | Starts from the lower leaves | Well-rotted compost, urine diluted tenfold |
| Leaf margins scorch brown | Potassium deficiency | Common during storage root bulking | Wood ash. The most important remedy for cassava |
| Growth is poor overall and leaves are deep purple | Phosphorus deficiency | Common in acidic soil | Bone meal, wood ash |
| New leaves are small, deformed, with dying tips | Boron deficiency | Appears first in young leaves | Diversify compost sources, apply a small amount of ash |
| Yellowing between the leaf veins | Magnesium deficiency | Starts from the lower leaves | Dolomite, compost |
| Lush stems and leaves but thin roots | Nitrogen excess | Leaves are excessively dark | Cut off nitrogen and reinforce potassium with wood ash |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The most common and most costly problem in cassava is potassium deficiency. Because potassium is directly involved in carrying starch into the roots, when potassium is short the leaves can look fine while the roots stay thin. On top of that, cassava carries off a substantial amount of potassium every time it is harvested, so growing it continuously without fertilizer depletes potassium first. This is why wood ash is a more valuable resource in cassava growing than in any other crop.

## 11. Intercropping and Crop Rotation

### 11-1. Companion Planting

| Compatibility | Partner | Reason |
|:---:|---|---|
| Good | Legumes (cowpea, peanut, soybean) | Fix nitrogen and supply it to cassava. Makes use of the empty space during the first four months |
| Good | Maize | Harvested first, while cassava's early growth is still slow |
| Good | Squash, sweet potato | Cover the ground, reducing weeds and erosion |
| Fair | Leafy vegetables | Planted between the ridges and harvested early |
| Avoid | Continuous cropping in the same field | Pathogens build up and potassium is depleted |
| Avoid | Other spurge-family crops | Share the same diseases |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Intercropping cassava with legumes is a widely used method in Africa. For the first four months after planting, cassava barely covers the ground, wasting that space in between, so planting cowpea or peanut there produces a harvest in just four months. On top of that, some of the nitrogen the legumes fix stays in the soil and is used by cassava's later growth. This also matters a great deal in a disaster, because it secures a protein source and a calorie source from the same field at the same time.

### 11-2. Rotation Sequence

| Order | Crop | Role |
|---|---|---|
| Year 1 | Cassava | Draws heavily on potassium and phosphate |
| Year 2 | Legumes (soybean, cowpea, adzuki bean) | Restores nitrogen |
| Year 3 | Leafy vegetables or grain | Uses the remaining nitrogen |
| Year 4 | Cassava again | Keeps a 3-4 year interval |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The A grade for continuous-cropping tolerance carries the note "draws heavily on soil fertility even in poor soil." The fact that cassava grows on poor land does not mean it does not deplete that land. In fact, its ability to fill its roots with starch even on land where nothing else grows also means it scrapes together the soil's remaining nutrients down to the last bit. Plant cassava in the same field for several years running, and that land ends up unable to grow even cassava. Always returning the stems, leaves, and peel to the field, and either resting it for 2 years or more or working in a legume, is the only answer to this problem.

## 12. Harvest

### 12-1. Judging the Timing

| Purpose | Harvest Timing (after planting) | Judgment Criteria |
|---|:---:|---|
| Eating fresh | 6-8 months | Lower starch, less fibrous |
| Processing into starch or flour | 8-12 months | Starch content and yield are at their peak |
| Delayed harvest | 12-24 months | Fiber increases and it toughens, but it is still edible |
| Outdoors in South Korea | Plant in early May, harvest in late October | Must always finish before frost arrives |
| Visible signs | Lower leaves yellow and drop; the base of the stem becomes woody | Dig up one plant as a test to check root thickness |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In South Korea there is no choice in the matter. Once frost comes the above-ground parts die, and once they die the roots soon start to spoil as well. Everything must be dug up before the end of October, which means compressing the tropics' eight-to-twelve-month season down to six months. Yield drops accordingly. Once a first-frost forecast comes out, it is better to dig even if the roots are not fully mature yet.

### 12-2. Harvesting Technique

| Step | Details |
|---|---|
| 1 | Pick a clear, dry day. Wet roots spoil immediately |
| 2 | Cut the above-ground stem, leaving 30-50cm. This stub becomes a handle |
| 3 | Use the remaining stem as a lever, lifting from the side opposite the roots |
| 4 | If the soil is hard, dig around the plant first to loosen it |
| 5 | Take care not to break the roots. Rot starts at any broken surface |
| 6 | Shake off the soil lightly. Do not wash with water |
| 7 | Collect the remaining stems separately for use as cuttings |

### 12-3. Post-Harvest Handling - There Is No Time to Spare

| Time Elapsed | Condition |
|---|---|
| Right after harvest | White flesh, normal |
| 24-48 hours | Bluish streaks start to appear in the flesh |
| 48-72 hours | Turns brown; bitterness and off-odors develop |
| 3-5 days | Spoilage begins |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This change is called physiological deterioration. It is not caused by mold or bacteria but is the root's own wound response, and it proceeds irreversibly from the moment of digging. It happens faster than in any other tuber crop (tubers/storage roots). That is why the rule for cassava is not "process within a few days of harvest" but "process on the same day you dig it." Digging, transporting, peeling, and slicing and drying all have to be finished within a single day. Because of this labor burden, the realistic approach with cassava is to dig a little and process a little at a time.

> **The field is the storehouse. Dig only as much as you will eat and process this week. Dig it all at once, and half of it will be ruined within three days.**

## 13. Maintaining Stem Cuttings and Seed Saving

### 13-1. The Cutting Is the Seed

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Cassava's grade for seed saving is SS. Cutting the 2-4m of stem from a single plant into 15cm pieces yields ten or more cuttings, and each one becomes a full plant the following year. The multiplication rate is so high that even starting from just a handful of seed stock, a substantial area can be filled within two or three years.

| Item | Standard |
|---|---|
| Where to collect | Middle of the stem, 2-3cm diameter |
| Collection time | At the same time as harvest, late October |
| Storage unit | Keep uncut, as long sections of about 1m |
| Storage temperature | 10° or above, somewhere that does not freeze |
| Storage humidity | Enough to keep it from drying out. Complete drying is forbidden |
| Storage period | 2-3 months maximum. The longer it goes, the lower the establishment rate |

### 13-2. The Hardest Problem in South Korea - Overwintering Cuttings

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This is the substance behind the E grade for seed longevity. Cassava's stem is living tissue: it dies if frozen, dies if it dries out, and rots if kept too damp. In the tropics this is not a problem, because you can simply cut a stem whenever you need one from cassava still standing in the field. In South Korea, a person has to keep a living stem alive for more than five months, from November to April. With no heating available, this is the single biggest bottleneck in growing cassava.

| Method | Conditions | Success Rate | Notes |
|---|---|:---:|---|
| Standing upright in a cool indoor spot | 10-15°C, moderate humidity | Medium | The simplest method. Stand in bundles so they do not dry out |
| Burying the base in wet sand | 10°C or above | High | Water occasionally so the sand does not dry out |
| Burying in an earth cellar or root-cellar pit | Somewhere that stays at 10°C or above | Medium | Fails if the temperature drops below 5°C |
| Heeling in inside a greenhouse or plastic tunnel | 10°C or above | High | The most reliable option, if available |
| Potting and overwintering indoors | 15°C or above, a bright spot | High | Suited to small quantities. Carries the plant through as a living specimen |
| Left outdoors, untended | - | 0 | Guaranteed to die |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The most reliable method is not storing cuttings at all, but carrying a living plant through indoors. Plant one or two in a small pot and keep it in a bright, warm room, and it will survive the winter while dropping some leaves, and when spring comes you cut stems from that plant to use as cuttings. Keeping just one plant alive turns into ten plants the following year. The rule is to use several methods at once to spread the risk.

> **In South Korea, whether cassava growing succeeds or fails is decided not in the field but indoors in winter. Lose the cuttings, and there is no cassava the following year.**

### 13-3. Propagation by True Seed

| Item | Details |
|---|---|
| Fruiting | Capsule fruit forms and produces seed |
| Germination | Low and irregular germination rate |
| Traits | Offspring show traits different from the parent plant |
| Growth | Seedlings take longer for the roots to mature |
| Practical use | Not used for propagation. Used only for breeding purposes |
