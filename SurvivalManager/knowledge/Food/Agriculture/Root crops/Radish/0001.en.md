## Introduction
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Radish (Raphanus sativus) is a biennial root vegetable of the cabbage family native to the region from the Mediterranean coast to Central Asia, and the white part we eat is a nutrient store formed when the root and the lower part of the stem swell so that the plant can flower the following year. That is why it stores well and why it becomes inedible once the flower stalk rises. Its disaster preparedness evaluation is overall S (51.8 points, 11th of 183 valid species), short term S (52.6), long term S (51.0), and supplementary food SS (62.2 points), all high. It takes 55-70 days from sowing to harvest (evaluation basis), keeps 3-4 months buried in a pit and more than a year when sliced and dried, its leaves (greens) are a separate food, and it can be planted in the same place in succession without major problems. On the other hand, the root freezes at 0℃, it is very weak against waterlogging, and if the sowing and harvest windows are missed, the harvest itself is lost.

> **Radish is not a crop that excels in any single item; its score is high because it has few decisively bad items.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Calories per area B means that, although the calorie density is low at 21 kcal per 100 g, 7,000 kg per 1,000 ㎡ is harvested, which gives 1,470 kcal per ㎡ (0.56 times the self-sufficiency line); a single plant weighs 1.3-1.6 kg, so the weight that reaches the kitchen is more than twice that of leafy vegetables. The greens are 30-50% of the root weight and are a separate food. Continuous-cropping tolerance SS means that the Rural Development Administration places radish in the group for which continuous cropping does no harm; it does not mean that clubroot cannot infect it. Cold tolerance C means that the root freezes at 0℃ and cannot be left in the field in winter, and storability C means that the storage period is B but the grade dropped one step because of a loss rate of 8.94%. Land-preparation and digging energy C means that deep tillage of 22 cm costs 239 kcal per ㎡, more than twice that of leafy vegetables. The grade for each item is in the Crop Evaluation Results document.

## 2. Plant Overview
| Item | Details |
|:---|:---|
| Classification | Biennial of the cabbage family (Brassicaceae), genus Raphanus. Because it belongs to Raphanus, not Brassica, it does not cross naturally with Chinese cabbage, cabbage, leaf mustard, or kale |
| Flowers, fruit | White or pale purple flowers. The pod is plump and does not burst easily, holds 4-10 seeds, and young pods are edible |
| Edible part | The upper third is the enlarged lower part of the stem (hypocotyl), and the lower part is the true root. The upper part is sweeter and firmer and goes into raw salads, the lower part into soups and braises. The leaves (greens) are food too |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;After sowing, the first 2 weeks are the germination and seedling stage, weeks 2-6 the vegetative stage when leaves increase, weeks 6-10 the root-swelling stage, and weeks 10-13 the maturing stage; after that, exposure to cold makes it bolt. Around week 6 is the boundary at which the root shifts from growing in length to growing in thickness. The shape of a straight root is set by the soil it meets in the first few days, so if it hits a stone, hard soil, or unrotted compost it splits, and later management cannot undo it. Per 100 g there are 21 kcal, protein 0.77 g (14.7% of calories), sugars 2.96 g, vitamin C 20-25 mg, and calcium 23 mg; the greens contain beta-carotene, which the root has almost none of, and much more calcium and iron than the root. The amylase-type enzymes that aid digestion are weak against heat.

## 3. Growing Environment and Varieties
| Condition | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Temperature | Optimal growth 17-23℃, optimal root swelling 21-23℃ (slower below that, and above it only the leaves grow and the root turns pithy), germination 15-30℃. At 0℃ the root tissue freezes and is destroyed |
| Water, light | Total evapotranspiration about 500 mm. Likes strong light; under 40% shade the relative yield is about 80%, and in shade only the leaves grow leggy |
| Soil | Well-drained sandy loam to loam, tillage depth 20-30 cm, bed height 15-20 cm, pH 6.5-7.5 (suppresses clubroot). Without lime, apply a little over a handful of wood ash per ㎡ 2 weeks before sowing, but leave a few days between it and the compost (applied together, the compost's nitrogen escapes as ammonia) |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Excess moisture is the greatest risk. Once water stands, the root cannot breathe and soft rot bacteria spread, and within a few days the whole root turns to mush with a foul smell. If a dry spell is followed by a sudden large amount of water, the skin splits lengthwise (root splitting) and the root cannot be stored, so water evenly rather than heavily, and put drainage ahead of all other management.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;A light frost of around -1℃ turns starch into sugar and raises the sweetness, but a cold wave of -3 to -4℃ or below freezes the cells and bursts them, and when the root thaws it turns soft and rots. Pull autumn radish before the first strong cold wave in mid-November. Because radish senses cold (0-13℃) even as a seed or a very young seedling, sowing too early in spring makes the flower stalk rise.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Autumn radish (70-90 days) is the center of disaster preparedness. It swells slowly in cool conditions, so its tissue is firm and rich in sugar, and it keeps 3-4 months in a pit. Spring radish (50-60 days) has soft, watery tissue and stores poorly, while yeolmu (20-40 days) and chonggak radish (40-60 days) are strains grown for their leaves and supply the first food in the early disaster. Much of the commercial seed is first-generation hybrid, so seed saved from it does not come true; if the disaster is likely to last, secure fixed or landrace varieties.

## 4. Field Preparation and Sowing
| Item | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Land preparation | Dig 20-30 cm deep and clear stones, wood chips, and hard clods. Apply well-rotted compost at least 2 weeks before sowing and mix it in deeply (unrotted compost is the most common cause of splitting) |
| Fertilization (1,000 ㎡) | Nitrogen 10-12 kg, potassium 8-10 kg, compost 1,000-1,500 kg. Basal fertilizer 1-2 weeks before sowing; top dressing is nitrogen at 3-4 true leaves and potassium at 6-7 true leaves. Nitrogen during swelling only grows leaves and softens the tissue, so stop it a month before harvest |
| Disaster substitute fertilizers | Well-rotted compost (1-1.5 kg per ㎡), wood ash (supplies potassium and corrects acidity, a little each year), urine diluted 10-15 times (first top dressing only), and an infusion made by soaking ash for a day and straining it (potassium during swelling) |
| Cropping types | Autumn radish is sown from mid-July to early September and harvested from mid-October to late November, and the best time is 70-90 days before the first frost (mid to late August in the central region, early September in the south). If sown late, cold arrives before the root swells. Spring radish is sown from mid-March to late April and harvested from mid-May to early July, finishing before the temperature exceeds 20℃. Overwintering radish is sown from late September to October and harvested in early spring of the next year (south, coast) |
| Sowing | Always sow directly (transplanting bends the taproot and it splits). Depth 1-2 cm, spot sowing at 1-2 seeds per hole, row spacing 60-70 cm, final plant spacing 25-30 cm (8,000-9,000 plants per 1,000 ㎡) |
| Seed treatment | Select large, plump seeds and coat them with wood ash to disinfect and dry them. Hot-water immersion (50-55℃ for 10-15 minutes; above 60℃ the seeds die and below 45℃ the pathogens survive) is the most reliable way to cut seed-borne disease without chemicals |

## 5. Water Management and Cultivation Management
| Stage | Water management |
|:---|:---|
| Germination to seedling | Water lightly and often so the topsoil does not dry (the roots are shallow) |
| Root swelling | Evenly and sufficiently (fluctuating moisture causes splitting). Reduce gradually from 2 weeks before harvest to firm the tissue |
| Rainy season | Secure drainage ditches (excess moisture is a greater risk than drought) |

| Care | Method |
|:---|:---|
| Mulching | Spreading straw or dry grass between the plants narrows the swings in moisture and is the cheapest way to prevent splitting |
| Thinning | Leave one plant per hole at 3-4 true leaves and thin to 25-30 cm spacing at 6-7 true leaves. Pulling shakes the roots of the plants to be kept and makes them split, so cut near the ground with scissors or a fingernail. If thinning is late, many thin radishes remain, and the thinned young radishes are eaten as yeolmu |
| Weeding, shoulders | The first 3-4 weeks are the contest with weeds, and once the leaves cover the ground it is almost unnecessary. Do not put the hoe in deeply, so as not to cut the fine roots. The upper part of the root rising above the ground is normal; if much is exposed, cover it lightly with soil |
| Picking greens | Until swelling begins, pick only 2-3 old lower leaves and always leave 8 or more. Do not pick them after the swelling stage |
| Water shortage | If the leaves wilt at midday and recover in the evening, it is temporary. If it continues, the roots become thin and the pungency stronger |

## 6. Pests, Diseases, and Nutrient Problems
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Pest and disease pressure and pest-control self-reliance are both C (estimated unprotected loss rate 51.7%). The main problems occur underground, where they cannot be seen, and by the time they show, the plant is already lost. Control is not an after-the-fact treatment but a set of preconditions: drainage, acidity, rotation, and sound seed. Unless these four are in place, there is almost nothing that can be done afterward.

| Pest or disease | Symptoms and conditions | Prevention and response |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Soft rot | The root turns to mush with a foul smell and the juice spreads to neighboring plants (excess moisture, wounds, excess nitrogen). It continues in storage as well | High beds and drainage ditches, no wounding. Pull diseased plants at once and discard or bury them far away; do not put them in compost |
| Clubroot, nematodes | Galls form on the root and the plant wilts (acidic soil, continuous cropping of the cabbage family) / small bead-like galls form in rows | Keep pH 6.5-7.0 with wood ash, rotate with non-cabbage-family crops, intercrop marigolds. Even with continuous-cropping tolerance SS, pathogens accumulate |
| Downy mildew, powdery mildew | Leaves turn yellow with mold on the underside (humidity) / white powder on the leaves (alternating dryness and humidity) | Thin for airflow and remove diseased leaves / spray milk diluted 10 times on a clear morning (on a cloudy day the milk spoils), and baking soda at a little over 1 g per 1 L of water (strong solutions harm the leaves) |
| Viral diseases, black rot | Mosaic and curling (spread by aphids) / leaf edges dry in a V shape (hot and humid, wounds) | Control aphids and pull and burn diseased plants / hot-water immersion of seed and removal of diseased plants |
| Pests | Flea beetles (fatal from just after germination to 3-4 true leaves), aphids, diamondback moth and cabbage white caterpillars (chew the leaves), grubs and cutworms (chew the roots) | Insect netting just after germination and wood ash dusting / soapy water at 20 g per 1 L sprayed onto the leaf undersides every few days, and protect ladybugs / hand picking |

| Symptom | Cause and action |
|:---|:---|
| Leaf edges dry brown | Potassium deficiency. Apply wood ash or an ash infusion |
| Inside of the root black or brown (internal blackening) | Boron deficiency (sandy soil low in organic matter, high-pH fields, drought years). Apply plenty of compost, and do not overapply wood ash (boron gets locked in the soil) |
| Only leaves flourish and the root is thin | Excess nitrogen (the most common cause of failure; the tissue is soft and rots in storage). Cut off nitrogen and add potassium |
| Root splits or forks | Caused by unrotted compost, stones, and hard soil, so improve field preparation next season. If the inside is hollow, it is pithiness (Section 8) |

## 7. Intercropping and Crop Rotation
| Compatibility | Companions |
|:---|:---|
| Good | Lettuce, spinach (different root depths so little competition, and they cover the ground. Lettuce takes 40-50 days and radish 70-90, so two crops come from the same area), Welsh onion, garlic chives (the smell confuses pests), marigold (suppresses nematodes) |
| Poor | Chinese cabbage, leaf mustard, cabbage and other cabbage-family crops (share pests and diseases), carrot, burdock and other root vegetables (compete for the same depth) |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Rotation runs on a 4-year cycle: year 1 radish or other cabbage-family crops such as Chinese cabbage, year 2 legumes (fix nitrogen), year 3 leafy vegetables or alliums, and year 4 grasses such as maize or sorghum (deep roots bring up nutrients from the lower layer). If radish is planted right after beans, the abundant nitrogen can make only the leaves flourish, so put in another crop once in between or plant after the bean residue has fully decomposed. Because the thick root goes deep and leaves a hole for water and air where it was pulled, radish is sometimes put in the middle of a rotation to loosen hard ground without machinery.

## 8. Harvest and Seed Saving
| Harvest | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Timing | Pull spring radish before the temperature exceeds 20℃ and autumn radish before it falls below -3℃ in mid-November. Waiting a few more days does not thicken it and only makes it pithy, so pull it when the shoulder diameter reaches the variety standard or the outer leaves begin to droop |
| Method | On a clear, dry day, gather the leaves and pull straight up (if it will not come out, dig beside it with a hoe). Do not throw or drop it (wounds are the entrance for soft rot). Shake off only the soil and do not wash it. Cut the greens cleanly where the leaves attach and handle them separately, and let the surface moisture dry in the shade for half a day |
| Pithiness | The inside of the root becomes spongy and hollow, losing taste and storability. It arises from passing the best harvest time, bolting, heat and dryness, sudden swelling (excess nitrogen, sudden rain), thawing after freezing, and leaf damage; it cannot be reversed, so timing is the prevention. Pithy radish is used for broth or dried strips and is not put in the pit |

| Seed saving | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Traits | It is biennial and self-incompatible, so a single plant flowers but sets almost no seed. Bees and wind carry the pollen (cross-pollination), and the seed keeps 5 years sealed at room temperature. Seeds of wild radish growing along roadsides and banks also germinate normally and are insurance when the seed supply is cut off, but their roots are thin and tough |
| Methods | The seed-to-seed method sows in spring and gets seed that same year, so it is fast and easy, but the roots cannot be seen and chosen, and after a few years the roots become thin and tough. The root-to-seed method selects thick, straight, non-pithy roots in autumn, overwinters them, and replants them in spring, which keeps quality. In the first year of a disaster secure seed by the seed-to-seed method, and when there is room, protect quality with the root-to-seed method |
| Crossing | All radish varieties (autumn radish, spring radish, yeolmu, chonggak radish, red radish) and wild radish cross completely, and they do not cross with Chinese cabbage, cabbage, or leaf mustard |
| Isolation, number of plants | Keep 500 m or more (strictly 1 km) from other flowering radishes or flower only one variety a year, and cut wild radish around the field before it flowers. Use at least 10 seed plants, preferably 20 or more (fewer lowers seed set and causes inbreeding depression). When covering with netting, put several plants together and move the pollen with a brush |
| Procedure | Overwinter the selected roots (Nov-Feb) by burying them in a pit or replanting them in the field with a little leaf left and covering with straw, and replant them in Feb-Mar so that the shoulder shows slightly. Bolting in Mar-Apr (stakes), flowering in Apr-May, and when the pods dry brown in Jun-Jul and rattle when shaken, cut the whole stalk, dry it in the shade for 1-2 weeks, put it in a sack, and beat or trample it (the pods burst poorly, so threshing is hard) |

## 9. Storage
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Storability C reflects a storage period of B and a loss rate of 8.94%, so radish keeps long but the amount lost in storage is not small. Store only sound, unwashed roots; a single radish that is wounded, pithy, or showing signs of soft rot spoils its neighbors all winter. Plan the winter diet in two tracks: vitamin C from raw radish in the pit and from fermented foods, and calories and bulk from the dried product.

| Method | How | Period |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Pit storage | Dig 60 cm wide and 75 cm deep in a place where water does not stand, line it with dry straw or sand, and stand the radishes upright with the leaf end down. Cover with thick straw and then 40-60 cm of soil in 2-3 passes, and dig a ditch around it. 0-4℃, humidity 85-95%. Make several pits, and when one is opened take out only what is needed and cover it again | 3-4 months |
| Burial, indoor | Lay in the field and cover with straw and soil / bury in slightly damp sand without the roots touching / wrap each in paper and keep in a cool place | 2-3 months / 1-2 months / 1-2 months |
| Dried strips | Slice to 0.5-1 cm and spread without overlap in a sunny, breezy place, turning once or twice a day (3-5 days in clear weather, until it bends without breaking). 10 kg shrinks to 1 kg, but most of the vitamin C is lost | 6 months to over 1 year |
| Dried greens (siraegi) | Shake the wilted leaves and soil off the greens, tie them unwashed, and hang them upside down in a breezy shade for one to two months. When using, soak overnight and boil, discard the cooking water and rinse, then use as a seasoned side dish or in soup | Several months |
| Dongchimi, pickling | Dongchimi is whole radish submerged in 2-3% brine and fermented (eaten with the broth, so a good amount of vitamin C remains), jjanji-style is salting at 10-15% (soaked in water before eating), and jangajji is radish packed in soy sauce or doenjang. If the material is fully submerged beneath the brine and the salt concentration does not fall below 2%, lactic acid bacteria grow, not spoilage microbes | Several months to over 1 year |

## 10. Cooking and Precautions
| Caution | Details |
|:---|:---|
| Spoiled radish | Radish with soft rot looks sound outside but is mushy inside and smells foul when cut, so do not eat it and throw it away. Check one or two during storage and remove them |
| Goitrogens | Some breakdown products of glucosinolates interfere with the thyroid's use of iodine. Ordinary amounts are no problem, but eating large amounts raw for a long time on a diet low in iodine is dangerous, so in a disaster where seaweed is gone, mix in cooked radish (heating reduces them) |
| Other | Eating a lot of raw radish at once can irritate the stomach, and radish given a lot of nitrogen accumulates nitrate. There is no acute toxicity and no detoxification procedure is needed |

| Cooking | Method |
|:---|:---|
| Raw | It needs only washing and slicing, so no fuel is used, and its high water content helps with thirst. Vitamin C and fiber are concentrated just under the skin, so use it with the skin on. Grated radish keeps its digestive enzymes alive, which helps digest the starch when eaten with rice, rice cakes, or potatoes |
| Cooked, processed | Boiled long, the pungency leaves and sweetness appears, so radish, salt, and water alone make a complete soup. Use it dried and rehydrated or fermented, and the young pods of radish that has bolted can be used as a seasoned side dish or jangajji |

## 11. Annual Work Schedule
| Month | Work |
|:---|:---|
| Jan-Feb | Check the radish in the pit and pick out spoiled ones, and inspect the dried strips and dried greens. In February, turn the compost, prepare the spring radish field, and check the overwintering seed plants |
| Mar-Apr | Sow spring radish from mid-March and replant the overwintered seed plants. In April, thin the spring radish and top dress, and set stakes for the seed plants |
| May-June | In May, start harvesting spring radish while the seed plants flower (manage isolation). In June, finish harvesting spring radish and clear the field ahead of summer |
| July-Aug | Harvest seed in early July, and from mid-July prepare the field and start sowing autumn radish. In August, continue sowing, guard against flea beetles at germination, and do the first thinning |
| Sep-Oct | In September, do the second thinning and top dressing, manage drainage for the autumn rains, and finish sowing by early September. In October, manage the swelling stage and start early harvesting from mid-October |
| Nov-Dec | Main harvest in mid-November, tidying the greens, making the pit and storing, drying the dried strips and dried greens, and kimjang (because work piles up, split the sowing at ten-day intervals to spread the harvest). In December, manage the stored goods, check the ripening of the dongchimi and kimchi, and tidy the field |

> **Radish is a crop where you keep the sowing window (mid-July to early September) and the harvest window (mid-November, before -3℃), put drainage first, and store only sound roots.**
