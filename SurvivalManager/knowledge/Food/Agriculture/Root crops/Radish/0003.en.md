## 2. What Kind of Plant Is This?

### 2-1. Classification and Origin

| Item | Details |
|:---|:---|
| Scientific name | Raphanus sativus |
| Taxonomy | Magnoliophyta Magnoliopsida / Brassicales / Brassicaceae / Raphanus |
| Life form | Biennial. Depending on conditions, it may also flower in the same year, like an annual |
| Origin | The region from the Mediterranean coast to Central Asia |
| Edible parts | The swollen root and the lower part of the stem, plus the leaves (radish greens) |
| Genus | Raphanus. A different genus from Brassica (the cabbage genus) |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The fact that radish belongs to the genus Raphanus rather than the genus Brassica is important in practice. Napa cabbage, cabbage, leaf mustard and kale all belong to Brassica, so the possibility of crossing among them has to be considered, but radish is of a different genus and does not cross with them under natural conditions. Even if napa cabbage flowers next to radish, the radish seed stays pure. Radish does, however, cross freely with other radish varieties of the same genus and with feral radish. This distinction is the heart of seed saving in Section 13.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The white part we eat is not purely root. The upper third or so is the thickened lower part of the stem, called the hypocotyl, and the lower part is the true root. If you pull up a radish, there are no fine roots on the upper part, while rows of fine roots are attached only to the lower part; that boundary is the boundary between stem and root. The upper part rises above the ground, takes sunlight and sometimes turns green, and it is sweeter and firmer than the lower part. The custom in cooking of using the upper part for saengchae (raw seasoned vegetable dish) or salads and the lower part for soups or braised dishes rests on this difference in tissue.

### 2-2. Appearance

| Organ | Characteristics |
|:---|:---|
| Leaf | Pinnately lobed, with coarse teeth. Leaves grow in a cluster from the top of the root |
| Stem | Almost absent normally; once the flower stalk rises, it grows to 60-100cm |
| Root (edible part) | Varies by variety from cylindrical to round. White, red, black or green |
| Flower | White or pale purple. The typical four-petaled cruciferous flower |
| Fruit | A silique (pod). Contains 4-10 seeds |
| Seed | Large for a Brassicaceae crop, with a hard coat |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The radish pod looks different from those of other Brassicaceae crops. The pods of leaf mustard or napa cabbage are thin and long, and when ripe they split open lengthwise and spill their seed, whereas the radish pod is plump, filled with spongy tissue, and does not split easily. To get the seeds out, the pods have to be crushed or beaten. This trait is a double-edged sword in seed saving: the risk of losing seed to pods bursting by themselves in the field is low, but in exchange threshing is hard work.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Young pods are edible. Picked while still green and tender, they are crisp with a pungent taste, and are used as namul (seasoned vegetable side dish) or jangajji (pickled vegetables in soy sauce or paste). This is why a radish whose flower stalk has risen and whose root can no longer be eaten does not have to be thrown away entirely. It is a resource that comes at the time in spring when stored foods run out.

### 2-3. Growth Stages

| Stage | Timing (from sowing) | Key physiology | Nutrients used most |
|:---|:---|:---|:---|
| Germination and seedling stage | Sowing to week 2 | Cotyledons open and the taproot grows down | Phosphorus, nitrogen |
| Vegetative growth stage | Weeks 2-6 | Leaves multiply and the root grows in length | Nitrogen |
| Root-swelling stage | Weeks 6-10 | The root thickens and sugars accumulate | Potassium |
| Maturation and harvest stage | Weeks 10-13 | The root fills out and gains storability | Reduce watering |
| Bolting and flowering stage | After exposure to cold | The flower stalk rises and the root's nutrients drain into the stalk | Phosphorus, potassium |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The decisive turning point in this table is around week 6. Until then radish grows in length, and after that it grows in thickness. If enough leaves are not produced early on, there is too little photosynthetic capacity to thicken the root; conversely, if there is too much nitrogen early on, only the leaves grow lush and nutrients do not go to the root. This turning point is the reason fertilization is split into base fertilizer and top-dressing, and why the character of the top-dressing is changed from nitrogen to potassium.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The most important thing is the way the taproot descends in the early stage. After emerging from the seed, the main root of radish goes straight down vertically, and if its tip hits a stone, hard soil or a lump of half-rotted compost, it turns aside or splits at that spot. That trace then becomes the shape of the harvest as it is. Deformities in which a radish splits, bends or forks into two are almost all determined at this stage.

> **The shape of a radish is determined by the condition of the soil the root meets in its first few days. However well it is managed later, a root that has already split cannot be made straight. This is why the preparation work of tilling the field deeply and picking out stones is so especially important for radish.**

### 2-4. Nutrition per 100g

| Component | Content (root, raw) | Notes |
|:---|:---:|:---|
| Calories | 21kcal | 13-21kcal depending on variety and part |
| Carbohydrates | 2.8-3.4g | |
| Protein | 0.77g | Share of calories 14.7% |
| Fat | 0.1-0.2g | |
| Sugars | 2.96g | Clearly higher than in leafy vegetables |
| Vitamin C | 20-25mg | A major winter source |
| Calcium | 23mg | |
| Phosphorus | 34mg | |
| Potassium | Abundant | |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The most valuable item in the nutrition of the radish root is vitamin C. At 20-25mg per 100g it is not as high as in leafy vegetables, but the strength of radish lies not in content but in storage. Radish buried in a root-cellar pit retains a considerable part of its vitamin C even after three or four months. Other vegetables cannot last that long, and even if they do, they lose their vitamin C in the process of drying or boiling. Once you consider that the only channels for supplying vitamin C in the middle of winter are radish, fermented foods and a few leafy vegetables that survive in the field, the weight of this item changes.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The radish greens have a different nutrition profile from the root. Beta-carotene is almost absent from the root but abundant in the leaves, and calcium and iron are also much higher in the leaves. The leaves are also richer in dietary fiber. Eating root and leaves together keeps the nutrient makeup from tilting to one side. Throwing away the greens means throwing away half of what this crop gives.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The pungency of radish and its digestion-promoting action come from two different components. The pungency is a substance released when the mustard-oil glycosides common to the Brassicaceae break down, while what aids digestion is an amylase-type enzyme. Because this enzyme breaks down starch, eating radish with rice or tteok (rice cake) makes digestion easier. It is, however, sensitive to heat and disappears when cooked. Grating raw radish as an accompaniment is the cooking method that preserves this enzyme.

## 3. Growing Conditions

### 3-1. Temperature

| Category | Temperature | Description |
|:---|:---:|:---|
| Optimal growth temperature | 17-23°C | Leaves and root grow well together |
| Optimal root-swelling temperature | 21-23°C | Thickening is fastest in this range |
| Growth range | 15-27°C | Range 12°C |
| Germination temperature | 15-30°C | Does not germinate at 40°C or above |
| Frost-damage limit | 0°C | Root tissue freezes and is destroyed |
| Bolting-inducing cold | 0-13°C for a certain period | The flower stalk rises once days lengthen afterward |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The narrowest and strictest of radish's temperature requirements is the optimum for root swelling, a narrow band of 21-23°C. Below it the root thickens more slowly; above it only the leaves grow lush and the root becomes pithy. This is why autumn radish is the basic cropping pattern for a disaster. If it is sown in South Korea from late August to early September, early growth is fast in the warm conditions, and by the time the root begins to thicken, the air temperature has fallen to around 20°C, matching the swelling optimum exactly. This is a stretch where the natural temperature curve and the crop's physiology mesh, and it cannot be created artificially.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Bolting is an especially tricky problem in radish. Radish senses cold even as a seed or as a very young seedling. It is common for radish sown early in spring to throw up a flower stalk partway through growth, and the cause is the seed having experienced cold underground. This differs from crops such as kale or cabbage, which respond to cold only after they have grown to some size. It is the reason spring sowing must not be done too early.

| Condition | What happens to the radish |
|:---|:---|
| Light frost of about 1°C below zero | To keep from freezing, the cells convert starch to sugar, and sweetness rises |
| Cold snap of 3-4°C below zero or colder | Water inside the cells freezes and expands, and the tissue bursts |
| After freezing and thawing | Juice leaks from the burst cells, and the root turns soft and rots |

> **A light frost is a tonic; a hard freeze is poison. The boundary lies at roughly 3°C below zero, and judging when to harvest radish comes down to pulling it before this line is crossed.**

### 3-2. Light

| Item | Standard | Notes |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Sunlight requirement | Likes strong light | A crop with a high light saturation point |
| Partial-shade tolerance | Limited | Relative yield of tuber crops (tubers/storage roots) & root vegetables at 40% shade about 80% |
| Symptoms when light is lacking | Only the leaves grow long and leggy, and the root does not thicken | |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Radish demands more light than leafy vegetables because it has to build a storage organ. A leafy vegetable can simply leave its photosynthetic products in the leaves, but radish has to move what it makes in the leaves into the root for storage. This process takes extra energy. When light is short, the plant first maintains its leaves and only then sends what is left over to the root, so radish grown in shade has lush leaves and a thin root.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The relative yield under shade of 80% in the evaluation table is lower than the 86% of leafy vegetables. This difference can be used when laying out positions in an actual field. Give the sunniest spots to storage-organ crops such as radish and potato, and put leafy vegetables such as lettuce and Swiss chard where buildings or trees cast shade. The total harvest from the same field changes as a result.

### 3-3. Water and Soil

| Item | Standard | Notes |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Total evapotranspiration | About 500mm | More than leafy vegetables |
| Soil moisture | Keep even | Repeated drying and wetting causes cracking |
| Excess moisture | The greatest risk | Direct cause of soft rot and root deformity |
| Soil | Well-drained sandy loam to loam | Deep soil that drains well |
| Soil acidity | pH 6.5-7.5 | Adjusting to pH 6.5-6.8 is a safe choice |
| Bed height | 15-20cm | Key to preventing waterlogging |
| Deep tillage | 20-30cm | Space for the root to go straight down |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Radish is one of the crops most vulnerable to waterlogging. The reason lies in its structure. The thick root has a large volume relative to its surface area and is packed with water and sugar. When water stands in the soil and the oxygen is gone, the root cannot respire, and if soft-rot bacteria enter through a wound or the skin in that state, they feed on the sugar inside and spread quickly. Within a few days a whole root turns to liquid and gives off a foul smell.

> **In growing radish, drainage comes before every other kind of management. Building the beds high and keeping the drainage ditches open, and especially preparing for the autumn monsoon rains (gaeul-jangma) of September in South Korea, decides the success or failure of the year's crop.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Conversely, a shortage of water also causes trouble. When radish goes through a dry spell and then suddenly receives a lot of water, the skin cannot keep pace with the swelling and cracks lengthwise. This phenomenon is called yeolgeun (root cracking). A cracked radish cannot be stored, and disease enters through the cracks. Water management for radish is therefore not a question of giving much or little but of giving evenly. Mulching with straw or dry grass is the cheapest means of achieving this evenness.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Soil acidity is aimed at pH 6.5-7.5, slightly on the higher side compared with other Brassicaceae crops. This is to suppress clubroot. Where there is no lime, wood ash can stand in for it, but because wood ash is strongly alkaline and fast-acting, it should not be applied in large amounts at once. A little over a handful per m² is applied 2 weeks before sowing and mixed well into the soil. However, if wood ash and compost are applied at the same time, the compost's nitrogen is lost as ammonia because of the alkalinity, so it is better to separate the timing by a few days.

## 4. Varieties

### 4-1. Characteristics by Type

| Type | Growing period | Size | Characteristics |
|:---|:---:|:---:|:---|
| Spring radish | 50-60 days | 1,200-1,600g | Bred to resist bolting strongly. Low storability |
| Summer radish | Short | 1,000-1,100g | Developed to withstand high temperatures, but quality is poorer |
| Autumn radish | 70-90 days | 1,300-1,400g | Best storability and high sugar content |
| Winter and overwintering radish | Long | Varied | Excellent cold hardiness, so it can be grown over winter in southern South Korea |
| All-season radish | Medium | Medium | Can be grown year-round but is not the best in any season |
| Yeolmu (young leafy radish) | 20-40 days | Small | A type grown for its leaves. The root is thin |
| Chonggangmu (altari, small whole radish) | 40-60 days | Small | Root and leaves are eaten together. For kimchi |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In this table, the core of disaster preparedness is the autumn radish type, because storability is decisive. Spring radish grows fast, but its tissue is soft and watery, so it does not last long even in a root-cellar pit. Autumn radish thickens slowly in cool conditions, its cell walls harden and sugars accumulate. This difference accounts for a difference of several months in storage time.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Yeolmu and chonggangmu are resources of a different character. They are types grown for their leaves rather than the root, and the time from sowing to harvest is extremely short, 20-40 days. In the early days of a disaster, when the field holds nothing, they are the option that puts food on the table first. This contrasts with radish as a root vegetable, which has to wait 70-90 days. It is possible to sow yeolmu in a corner of the field and eat its leaves while the main radish grows.

### 4-2. Selection Criteria by Situation

| Situation | Choice | Reason |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Early in a disaster, fastest food | Yeolmu, chonggangmu | Leaves and small roots can be harvested in 20-40 days |
| Core of the winter stockpile | Autumn radish type | 3-4 months in pit storage. High sugar content |
| Filling the spring gap | Spring radish type | 50-60 days. Must be harvested before summer, without fail |
| Overwintering in southern South Korea | Winter and overwintering radish type | Relatively strong cold hardiness |
| Securing radish greens and siraegi (dried radish greens) | Leafy type | The amount of leaves matters as much as the root |
| Seed security first | Landraces, fixed (non-hybrid) varieties | Saved seed stays true to type |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The last row becomes decisively important when a disaster drags on. A good share of the radish seed sold on the market is F1 hybrid. It excels in yield and uniformity, but if you save its seed and plant it again, the traits scatter in all directions. If the disaster is going to last several years, it is better to secure fixed or landrace varieties from the start. Even if the first year's yield is a little lower, seed becomes self-supplied from the following year.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Planting several types mixed together is another way of spreading risk. If autumn radish is the mainstay with a little yeolmu alongside, harvest times are staggered, and even if one of them is hit by disease or weather, everything is not lost.
