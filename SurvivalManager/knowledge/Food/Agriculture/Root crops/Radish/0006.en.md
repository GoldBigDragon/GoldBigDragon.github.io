## 13. Seed Saving - Biennial Habit and Self-Incompatibility

### 13-1. Two Barriers

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Radish seed saving has two walls. One is the biennial life form, and the other is the pollination system known as self-incompatibility. This is why the Seed saving grade in the evaluation table stays at A.

| Barrier | Details | Result |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Biennial habit | The flower stalk rises only after the plant has gone through a cold spell | To collect seed, the roots must be carried through the winter |
| Self-incompatibility | Fertilization with its own pollen does not work well | Several plants must be flowered together |
| Cross-fertilization | Bees and wind carry the pollen | It mixes with other varieties |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;However, radish's biennial habit is not as strict as that of napa cabbage or cabbage. Radish is close to the seed-vernalization type, which senses cold even as a seed or as a very young seedling, so radishes sown early in spring commonly flower the same summer. That gives two routes to radish seed.

| Method | Procedure | Strengths and limits |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Direct-sowing seed saving | Sow in spring and leave as is, and it flowers and sets seed that same year | Fast and easy, but the roots cannot be inspected and selected |
| Root-selection seed saving | Grow in autumn, pick good roots after looking at them, overwinter them and replant in spring | Allows quality selection, but is labor-intensive |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The difference between the two is decisive. Direct-sowing seed saving means collecting seed without knowing what the roots look like. Because selection can only be made by looking at the leaves, after a few years the line drifts toward thin, tough roots. That is the direction of reverting to the wild. Root-selection seed saving lifts the roots, picks thick, straight, well-shaped ones and replants them, so the variety's traits are maintained.

> **In the first year of a disaster, secure seed by direct-sowing seed saving, and when there is some leeway, protect quality with root-selection seed saving. If root-selection seed saving is skipped for a few years, radish quickly reverts toward the wild.**

### 13-2. What It Crosses With

| Crop | Scientific name | Crossing with radish |
|:---|:---|:---|
| All radish varieties (autumn radish, spring radish, yeolmu or young summer radish, chonggak radish or ponytail radish, small red radish) | Raphanus sativus | Cross completely |
| Wild radish and sea radish (gaetmu) | Raphanus raphanistrum and others | Cross |
| Napa cabbage, turnip, bok choy | Brassica rapa | Do not cross under natural conditions |
| Cabbage, kale, broccoli | Brassica oleracea | Do not cross |
| Leaf mustard, mustard | Brassica juncea | Do not cross |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;What matters is that radish belongs to the genus Raphanus, not the genus Brassica. It does not matter if napa cabbage, leaf mustard or kale flowers in the year radish seed is being saved. Because the genera differ, no fertilization occurs even if pollen lands. When several Brassicaceae crops are grown in one field, this is a big advantage.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The only problem is among radishes themselves. Autumn radish, yeolmu, chonggak radish and small red radish may look entirely different, but they are all the same species and mix completely. If an autumn radish flowers next to yeolmu, the following year it produces hybrids with thin roots and only lush leaves. And wild radish that grows by itself along roadsides and embankments is also a crossing partner. The plant commonly found on the outskirts of cities and along riverbanks, with white or pale lavender flowers in spring, is the wild form of radish, and seed mixed with it grows into radishes with thin, tough roots.

### 13-3. Isolation and Pollination

| Method | Details | Practicality |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Isolation distance | Keep 500m or more from other flowering radish plants, or 1km if being strict | Hard to control wild radish as well |
| Staggered cultivation | Let only one variety flower in a given year | The most realistic |
| Netting | Cover with netting before flowering to keep bees out | Because of self-incompatibility, pollen must be moved by hand inside |
| Clearing the surroundings | Cut down wild radish around the field before it flowers | Works only if done together |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Radish is a crop with strong self-incompatibility. There is a genetic mechanism that stops pollen tubes from growing even when a plant's own pollen lands on its own pistil. It is the plant's strategy for avoiding inbreeding, and from the human point of view it is a trait that makes seed saving difficult. If only a single plant is isolated, it will bloom profusely but hardly set any seed.

> **Flower at least ten seed plants together, preferably twenty or more. In a self-incompatible crop, a small number of plants lowers the seed-set rate, and after a few generations inbreeding depression weakens vigor.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Even when netting is used, several plants must be placed together under one net. Since there are no bees inside, a person moves pollen back and forth between flowers with a brush. Mid-morning, when the flowers are wide open, is best. It takes a lot of labor, but varietal purity is reliably kept.

### 13-4. Seed-Saving Procedure

| Step | Timing (in South Korea) | Details |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Selecting seed plants | Autumn harvest season | Choose roots that are thick and straight, free of pithiness (spongy, hollow flesh) and clearly show the variety's characteristics |
| Overwintering | November-February | Bury the roots in a root-cellar pit, or replant them in the field leaving a little foliage and cover with straw |
| Replanting | February-March | Replant the overwintered roots with the shoulders slightly exposed |
| Bolting | March-April | The flower stalk rises. Set up stakes to keep it from falling over |
| Flowering | April-May | White or pale lavender flowers open. Keep the isolation during this time |
| Pod set | May-June | Plump pods form |
| Harvest | June-July | Cut the whole stems when the pods have dried to brown and rattle when shaken |
| After-ripening and threshing | Right after harvest | Dry in the shade for 1-2 weeks, then put in a sack and beat or trample |
| Sorting and storage | After complete drying | Winnow off the husks, seal and keep in a cool place |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Unlike other Brassicaceae, radish pods do not shatter easily. Sponge-like tissue surrounds the seeds, so the pods do not split open by themselves even after drying. Thanks to this trait, the risk of losing seed in the field is low, but threshing is hard in return. Put them in a sack and beat or trample them to break them up, and if the seeds still do not come out, put them in a mortar and pound lightly. Pounding hard damages the seeds, so the force must be controlled.

| Item | Guideline |
|:---|:---|
| Seed longevity | 5 years sealed at room temperature |
| Best storage | A cool, dark, dry place, in an airtight container |
| Desiccant | Roasted and cooled rice, charcoal, dry wood ash |
| Germination check | Each year, place 20 seeds on a damp cloth and count the germination rate a few days later |
| Yield per plant | Thousands of seeds |

## 14. Toxicity and Compounds of Concern

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Radish is a safe food at ordinary intake. It can be washed and eaten as is, and the grade SS for Cooking & processing in the evaluation table reflects this. There is no acute toxicity and no special detoxification procedure is needed.

| Item | Details | Response |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Mustard oil glycosides (pungency) | A component common to the Brassicaceae. No problem in small amounts | Eating a lot of raw radish at once can irritate the stomach |
| Goitrogens | Some breakdown products of mustard oil glycosides interfere with iodine uptake | Mix in cooked forms and add seaweed |
| Nitrates | Accumulate in radishes given a lot of nitrogen | Do not over-apply nitrogen |
| Spoiled radish | Foul smell and mushiness in radishes with soft rot | Do not eat; discard |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;A little more on goitrogens. Some of the substances produced when the pungent compounds of Brassicaceae plants break down interfere with the process by which the thyroid uses iodine. For a person who gets enough iodine, eating an ordinary amount of radish is no problem. The condition that makes it risky is eating large amounts of raw Brassicaceae vegetables for a long time on an iodine-poor diet.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In a disaster this condition can actually arise. When distribution is cut off and one is far from the sea, miyeok (wakame), dasima (kelp) and gim (dried laver) disappear. The response is to mix in cooked forms. Heating destroys the enzyme that breaks the compounds down, so goitrogen formation falls. Radish has many cooking methods in which it is eaten cooked, as in soups or braised dishes, so the chance of this problem becoming serious is rather low.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The most practical precaution is picking out spoiled radishes. A radish with soft rot may look fine outside but be mushy like porridge inside and give off a foul smell when cut. Checking a radish or two at a time during storage and removing the spoiled ones is a task needed all winter long.
