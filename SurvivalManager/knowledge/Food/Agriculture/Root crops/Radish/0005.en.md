## 9. Pests and Diseases - Prevention Without Pesticides

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Radish's Pest & disease pressure grade is C, with an estimated yield loss without pest control of 51.7%. Its Pest-control self-reliance is also C, lower than the B of leaf mustard or kale. The reason is that most of radish's main diseases occur underground. Insects on the leaves can be seen and caught, but the bacteria that soften the root, the pathogens that form galls, and nematodes cannot be seen. By the time they show, that plant has already been lost.

> **Pest control in radish is a matter of advance conditions, not after-the-fact treatment. If these four things, drainage, soil acidity, rotation and healthy seed, are not in place, there is almost nothing that can be done afterward.**

### 9-1. Major Diseases

| Disease | Symptoms | Conditions favoring it | Response without pesticides |
|:---|:---|:---|:---|
| Soft rot | The root turns soft, rots and smells foul | Excess moisture, poor drainage, wounds | Raise the beds and secure drainage ditches. Throw diseased plants far away at once |
| Downy mildew | Leaves turn yellow and mold grows on the undersides | High humidity, poor ventilation | Widen the spacing by thinning and remove diseased leaves |
| Clubroot (also called wart disease) | Galls form on the root and the plant wilts | Acidic soil, continuous cropping of Brassicaceae | Raise the pH to near 7.0 with wood ash and rotate crops |
| Viral disease | Mosaic patterns appear on the leaves and they shrivel | Spread by aphids | Keep aphids off, and pull up and burn diseased plants |
| Powdery mildew | A white powder covers the leaf surface | When dry spells and humid spells alternate | Ensure ventilation and sunlight. Spray diluted milk or a baking-soda solution |
| Black rot | Leaf edges dry out in a V shape | Heat and high humidity, wounds | Hot-water soak of the seed; remove infected plants |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Soft rot is the most fearsome disease of radish. The causal bacteria enter through wounds or natural openings and secrete enzymes that dissolve the cell walls of root tissue. When the cell walls dissolve, the tissue turns entirely to mush, and the juice runs out and spreads to neighboring plants. Because it continues to progress during storage, a single diseased radish mixed into a root-cellar pit keeps causing losses all winter.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There are three ways to prevent it. First, keep water from standing: beds 15-20cm high and clear drainage ditches are all it takes. Second, avoid wounding the root. If the root is scraped with a homi (Korean hand hoe) while weeding, or radishes are thrown at harvest, those wounds become entrances. Third, do not give excess nitrogen. Radish grown on excess nitrogen has soft tissue and thin cell walls, which bacteria penetrate easily.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Diluted milk works against powdery mildew because the protein and fat in the milk dry on the leaf surface into a film and, in the process, physically crush the fungal hyphae. Milk is diluted with water to about ten times its volume, sprayed on a clear morning and left to dry in the sun. If it is sprayed on a cloudy day, the milk spoils and stray microbes grow instead. If baking soda is used, mix it weakly, a little over 1g per 1L of water; if it is too strong, the leaves are damaged.

### 9-2. Major Pests

| Pest | Damage | Control without pesticides |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Aphids | Suck sap from the undersides of leaves and spread viruses | Soapy-water spray, wood-ash dusting, protecting ladybugs |
| Flea beetles | Make pinprick-like holes in young leaves | Insect netting right after germination, wood ash, adjusting the sowing time |
| Diamondback moth | Gnaws the leaves | Insect netting, hand-picking |
| Cabbage white butterfly caterpillars | Make large holes in leaves | Rub off the yellow eggs on the undersides of the leaves |
| Root-knot nematodes | Small galls form in rows on the root and growth stops | Rotation, adjusting acidity with wood ash, intercropping with marigolds |
| White grubs and cutworms | Gnaw the root and the base | Pick them out when tilling; hand-pick at night |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Flea beetles are the most common early pests of radish. They are small black insects that follow the scent of Brassicaceae plants and, when touched, spring away like fleas. Full-grown plants endure some holes in the leaves, but if attacked at the cotyledon and first true-leaf stage, the seedlings simply die. Covering with netting from right after germination until the plants have three or four true leaves is the most effective measure, and once this period has passed, the damage drops sharply.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Nematodes are tiny thread-shaped creatures invisible to the eye that invade the root, form galls and live inside them. If the galls of clubroot are large, lumpy masses, nematode galls are small and attached in rows like beads. Where no pesticides are available, rotation is the only response: crops that nematodes cannot feed on are planted for several years to starve them. Plants such as marigold are known to release substances from their roots that suppress nematodes, so planting them along the edge of the field or in between helps.

### 9-3. Natural Control Agents

| Agent | Preparation | Target |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Soapy water | Dissolve 20g of unscented soap in 1L of water | Aphids, spider mites |
| Plant-ash liquid | Soak plant ash in water for 24 hours, strain, and use | Aphids, stink bugs |
| Wood-ash dusting | Sift fine ash and dust it on the leaves while the morning dew is on | Repels flea beetles; supplies potassium |
| Wood vinegar | Dilute 300 to 500 times and spray | Repellent effect, disease suppression |
| Diluted milk | Dilute milk 10 times with water and spray on a clear morning | Powdery mildew |
| Baking-soda solution | A little over 1g per 1L of water | Powdery mildew |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Soapy water works on aphids through a physical action rather than toxicity. The surfactant components dissolve the waxy layer on the aphid's body and block its breathing pores. Only the individuals it touches directly die and there is no residual effect, so the undersides of the leaves must be thoroughly wetted and the treatment repeated every few days. A higher concentration also damages the radish leaves, so keep to the limit of 20g per 1L.

### 9-4. Cultural Control Principles

| Principle | Details |
|:---|:---|
| Drainage | Build the beds high and keep the drainage ditches clear. This is the most important point for radish |
| Soil acidity | Keep the pH at 6.5-7.0 with wood ash to suppress clubroot |
| Rotation | Insert non-Brassicaceae crops, especially legumes, in between |
| Healthy seed | Break seed-borne transmission with a hot-water soak |
| Ventilation | Secure spacing by thinning |
| Residue disposal | Do not put diseased roots and leaves in compost; burn them or bury them far away |
| Timing adjustment | Sow so as to avoid the peak season of pest outbreaks |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Because Continuous-cropping tolerance in the evaluation table is SS, it is easy to read this as meaning radish can be planted in succession. But this grade refers to the relative resistance of radish itself; it does not mean that pathogens do not build up. In a field that clubroot and nematodes have already entered, radish too is eventually hit. If the field allows, a rotation with legumes in between is still the best option.

## 10. Nutrient Deficiency and Excess

| Symptom | Cause | Remedy with natural materials |
|:---|:---|:---|
| The whole leaf turns yellow and the plant is small | Nitrogen deficiency | Work well-rotted compost in between the plants, or apply urine diluted 10-15 times |
| Leaf edges dry out brown | Potassium deficiency | Scatter wood ash or apply plant-ash infusion |
| Growth is poor and the leaves take on a purplish tint | Phosphate deficiency | Add bone meal or wood ash. The same symptom appears when soil temperature is low |
| Yellowing between the leaf veins | Magnesium deficiency | Increase the compost |
| The inside of the root turns black | Boron deficiency | Add plenty of organic matter and keep the pH from getting too high |
| The root does not thicken and only the leaves are lush | Nitrogen excess | Stop the nitrogen supply and supplement potassium |
| The root splits or forks in two | Half-rotted compost, stones, hard soil | Not a nutrition problem. Improve field preparation for the next crop |
| Hollow cavities form inside the root | Pithiness | Covered in Section 12 |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Boron deficiency is a problem characteristic of radish. It is the symptom in which a radish that looks sound from the outside turns out black or brown inside when cut; it is called black heart (heuksim). Boron is a trace element that plants need only in very small amounts, but because it takes part in building cell walls, the tissue collapses when it is lacking. It tends to appear on sandy soil short of organic matter, in fields with too high a pH, and in years of severe drought. The response is to apply plenty of compost. Well-rotted compost contains trace elements in good balance.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;If too much wood ash is applied and the pH rises too far, trace elements including boron are instead held fast by the soil and the roots cannot absorb them. This means that raising the pH and supplying trace elements can work against each other, which is why the rule for wood ash is a little every year rather than a lot at once.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Nitrogen excess is the most common cause of failure in radish. It may seem that the more fertilizer put into the field the better, but with radish it is the opposite. When nitrogen is plentiful, the plant keeps trying to make leaves and does not send sugar to the root. The leaves are lush and deep green, but when pulled up, the root turns out to be no bigger than a finger. Moreover, radish grown under high-nitrogen conditions has soft tissue and rots easily in storage.

## 11. Intercropping and Crop Rotation

| Compatibility | Companion | Effect |
|:---:|:---|:---|
| Good | Legume crops (pea, kidney bean, cowpea) | Fix nitrogen and leave it behind |
| Good | Lettuce, spinach | Different rooting depths mean little competition, and they cover the ground |
| Good | Welsh onion, garlic chive | Their smell confuses pests |
| Good | Marigold | Suppresses root-knot nematodes |
| Poor | Napa cabbage, leaf mustard, cabbage and other Brassicaceae | They share the same pests and diseases |
| Poor | Carrot, burdock and other root vegetables | They compete for the same depth |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Planting lettuce and radish together is an especially efficient use of space. Lettuce has shallow roots that spread near the surface and is finished in 40-50 days, while radish sends its roots deep and runs 70-90 days. While the radish leaves are still small, lettuce grows between them and covers the ground, holding back weeds, and by the time the lettuce is harvested the radish has grown large and taken over the space. Two crops come from the same area.

| Example rotation order | Year | Crop | What happens in the field |
|:---|:---:|:---|:---|
| 4-year cycle | Year 1 | Radish, napa cabbage and other Brassicaceae | Consume a lot of nitrogen and break up the deep soil |
| | Year 2 | Legumes (soybean, pea, cowpea) | Nitrogen is fixed and accumulates |
| | Year 3 | Leafy vegetables such as lettuce and Swiss chard, or onion-family crops | Use nutrients in the shallow layer |
| | Year 4 | Grass-family crops such as maize and sorghum | Deep roots bring nutrients up from the lower layers |
| | Year 1 again | Radish | Starts with pathogen density lowered |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Radish plays a special role in a rotation, because its thick roots go deep and physically break up the soil. After a radish is pulled, a long vertical hole remains, and water and air enter the lower layers through it. Planting radish or turnip has long been used as a way of loosening hard ground without machinery. If a radish is left unharvested to rot in the ground, that spot becomes a channel rich in organic matter.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Placing legumes before radish is especially advantageous. Radish uses a fair amount of nitrogen, and legumes fix nitrogen from the air and leave it in the soil. However, if radish is planted right after the beans are harvested, there may be too much nitrogen and only the leaves may grow lush, so it is better to put another crop in between once, or to plant after the bean residue has fully decomposed.

## 12. Harvest - Pithiness and the Question of Timing

### 12-1. Harvest Timing

| Cropping pattern | Criterion | Notes |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Spring radish | Before the temperature exceeds 20°C | Once the heat comes, it bolts and turns pithy |
| Autumn radish | Mid-November in South Korea, before the first strong cold snap | Pull before the temperature drops below 3 degrees below zero |
| Size criterion | When the shoulder diameter reaches the standard for the variety | Leaving it longer does not thicken it and only makes it pithy |
| Leaf condition | When the outer leaves begin to droop | A sign of maturity |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The point at which judgment divides in harvesting radish is whether to leave it a few more days to thicken or to pull it now. The answer is usually to pull it now. Past a certain point radish no longer thickens and instead starts to turn pithy inside. With radish, it is common for a few days of greed to ruin a year's stored food.

### 12-2. Why Pithiness Occurs

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Pithiness is the phenomenon in which sponge-like hollow cavities form inside a radish. It is also called cavity formation. If a radish that looks sound on the outside is cut and its inside is dry and crumbly, with white holes, it has gone pithy. Such a radish has no flavor and cannot be stored either.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There is not a single cause but several, yet they share a common structure: the supply of contents to fill the cells cannot keep pace with the speed at which the cells swell and enlarge. When only the shell grows and the inside is empty, the cell walls cannot hold up, burst, and become empty space.

| Cause | How it happens |
|:---|:---|
| Harvesting past the best time | Even after swelling has finished, the root keeps absorbing water and the cells stretch |
| Bolting | When the flower stalk rises, the sugars and nutrients stored in the root move to the stalk, leaving the inside empty |
| Heat and dryness | The amount consumed by respiration exceeds the amount photosynthesis replenishes |
| Rapid swelling | Excess nitrogen or sudden rainfall makes the root thicken too fast |
| Thawing after cold freeze injury | Cells that froze burst as they thaw, leaving empty space |
| Leaf damage | When leaves are damaged by disease or insects, the sugar supply is cut off |

> **At its core, pithiness is a signal that the root has stopped serving as a storehouse. Radish is gathering nutrients in order to flower the following year, and when it judges that this plan is complete, it stops storing and moves the contents elsewhere. What people can eat exists only in the interval between.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;So the way to prevent pithiness is, in effect, timing management: pulling on time, choosing a cropping pattern that does not invite bolting, not giving excess nitrogen, keeping moisture even, and keeping the leaves healthy. There is no way to reverse a radish that has already gone pithy.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Even a pithy radish need not be thrown away entirely. Raw, it is dry and crumbly, but it is still usable for making broth or for drying. Cut into strips and dried (as dried radish strips), it actually dries faster because it holds less moisture. It must not, however, be stored in a pit. Rot progresses quickly in the empty spaces and spoils even the sound radishes next to it.

### 12-3. Harvesting Tips

| Item | Tip |
|:---|:---|
| Weather | A clear, dry day. Avoid the time right after rain |
| How to pull | Gather the leaves in one hand and pull straight up. If it does not come out easily, dig alongside with a homi |
| Wounds | Do not throw or drop them. Wounds become the entrance for soft rot |
| Soil | Shake it off, but do not wash with water |
| Handling the greens | Cut at the point where root and leaves meet and handle them separately |
| Pre-drying | Leave in the shade for about half a day to let surface moisture evaporate |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Not washing is the basis of storage. Leaving the soil on in fact preserves surface moisture and covers wounds. Washing with water strips off the thin protective layer on the surface, and mold and bacteria grow in the wet spots. The rule is to wash just before eating.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;When cutting off the greens, do not leave a bit of flesh on the root side, but cut cleanly at the point where the leaves are attached. If any leaf stalk remains, that part rots first and the rot spreads to the root. Conversely, if the cut is made too deep and the inner flesh of the root is exposed, the cut surface dries and cracks, or disease enters through it.
