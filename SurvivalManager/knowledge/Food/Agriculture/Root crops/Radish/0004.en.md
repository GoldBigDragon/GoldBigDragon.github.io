## 5. Field Preparation and Fertilizer

### 5-1. Preparing the Field

| Item | Method |
|:---|:---|
| Tillage | The soil is tilled to a depth of 20-30cm, clods are broken up, and the ground is leveled evenly |
| Stone removal | Stones and wood fragments within the depth the roots reach are cleared away |
| Raised bed | Height 15-20cm. Drainage ditches are dug alongside |
| Soil acidity | Adjusted to pH 6.5-6.8. If the soil is acidic, wood ash is used |
| Organic matter | Well-rotted compost is applied at least 2 weeks before sowing and mixed in deeply |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Field preparation for radish has to be deeper and more thorough than for other vegetables, because the shape of the harvest is decided here. The root has to go straight down nearly 30cm, and if there are stones, hard clods or pieces of half-rotted compost in its path, the root tip turns aside or splits in two. A split radish can still be eaten, but it is hard to store, and peeling and trimming it takes a lot of hands-on work.

> **Half-rotted compost is the most common cause of split roots in radish. When a lump of organic matter that has not finished decomposing touches the root tip, the root divides at that spot. Compost must be fully rotted and applied at least 2 weeks before sowing.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Deep tillage is hard work, and the C for Tillage & digging energy in the evaluation table reflects that burden: 239kcal per m², more than double that of leafy vegetables. This labor, however, is not spent on radish alone. A field that has been tilled deeply once benefits the next crop as well, and where radish has grown, the holes left by the thick roots let water and air pass freely. Putting radish in the middle of a rotation has the effect of improving the soil's physical properties.

### 5-2. Fertilizer Rates

| Fertilizer | Per 1,000m² | Per m² | How it is applied |
|:---|:---:|:---:|:---|
| Nitrogen (N) | 10-12kg | 10-12g | Half as base fertilizer, half as top-dressing |
| Phosphate (P) | 6-8kg | 6-8g | All as base fertilizer |
| Potassium (K) | 8-10kg | 8-10g | Split between base fertilizer and top-dressing |
| Compost | 1,000-1,500kg | 1-1.5kg | At least 2 weeks before sowing |
| Lime | 100-150kg | 100-150g | For correcting acidic soil |

| Application | Timing | Nature |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Base fertilizer | 1-2 weeks before sowing | Compost, all the phosphate, part of the potassium, half the nitrogen |
| First top-dressing | 3-4 true leaves | Mainly nitrogen. Increases the leaves to build photosynthetic capacity |
| Second top-dressing | 6-7 true leaves | Mainly potassium. Helps the root swell |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The key point in fertilizing radish is that the nature of the top-dressing changes midway: the first is nitrogen, the second potassium. There is a physiological reason for this order. Early on, enough leaves must be produced to set up the photosynthetic factory, and once that factory starts running, the sugar it makes has to be moved to the root, and potassium takes part in that movement. Potassium is the element that helps transport sugar within the plant, which is why it is treated as especially important in root vegetables and tuber crops (tubers/storage roots).

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Conversely, giving a lot of nitrogen during the root-swelling stage causes problems. When nitrogen is plentiful, a plant keeps trying to make leaves and spends the sugar that should go to the root on building leaves. The result is a radish with lush leaves and a thin root. Moreover, radish grown on excess nitrogen has soft tissue and rots easily in storage. The rule is to give no nitrogen from one month before harvest.

### 5-3. Substitute Fertilizers in a Disaster

| Substitute | Nutrients supplied | How to use | Caution |
|:---|:---|:---|:---|
| Well-rotted compost | Nitrogen, phosphate, potassium and trace elements in general | Mix 1-1.5kg per m² deeply into the soil as base fertilizer | Half-rotted compost causes the roots to split |
| Wood ash | Potassium, phosphate, calcium | A handful per m². Serves both acidity correction and potassium supply | Too much at once tips the soil toward alkaline |
| Diluted urine | Nitrogen | Dilute 10 to 15 times with water and pour between the plants | Used only for the first top-dressing. Not used during the swelling stage |
| Bone meal | Phosphate, calcium | Mixed into compost and applied as base fertilizer | Decomposes slowly, so it must be applied in advance |
| Plant-ash infusion | Potassium | Soak ash in water, strain it, and apply during the swelling stage | Useful for supplying potassium during root swelling |
| Leaf and grass mold | Organic matter | Worked into the field to improve its physical properties | Use only fully decomposed material |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The substitute resource that is especially valuable for radish is wood ash, because it supplies potassium and at the same time raises the soil pH to suppress clubroot. The pH 6.5-7.5 that radish requires is on the high side even among Brassicaceae crops, and without lime, wood ash is almost the only means of maintaining this level. Wood ash supplies no nitrogen at all, however, so roles have to be shared with compost or diluted urine.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;A practical way to supply potassium during the root-swelling stage is plant-ash infusion. If wood ash is soaked in water for about a day, the clear liquid on top is skimmed off and poured between the plants, the potassium is absorbed right away. This works faster than scattering the ash as a solid, and there is less risk of the alkali changing the soil as a whole.

## 6. Sowing and Thinning

### 6-1. Cropping Patterns

| Cropping pattern | Sowing time (South Korea) | Harvest time (South Korea) | Notes |
|:---|:---|:---|:---|
| Spring radish | Mid-March to late April | Mid-May to early July | Must be harvested before the heat arrives |
| Summer radish | May-June | July-August | Viable only in high-altitude areas |
| Autumn radish | Mid-July to early September | Mid-October to late November | The core cropping pattern for disaster preparedness |
| Overwintering radish | Late September to October | Early spring of the following year | Southern and coastal areas of South Korea |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Of the four cropping patterns, autumn radish is overwhelmingly the most important, for several reasons. First, by the time the root thickens, the temperature has come down to 21-23°C, the optimum for swelling. Second, the temperature keeps falling throughout the growing period, so there is no bolting pressure. Third, the harvest leads straight into storage. Fourth, radish that grows slowly in cool conditions has firm tissue and plenty of sugar, and so stores well.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Spring radish grows under the opposite conditions. Because the temperature is rising, it has already passed the optimum by the time the root thickens, and bolting pressure keeps growing. As a result, spring radish is small, soft and prone to pithiness. Even when sowing in spring, the goal is not storage but obtaining food to eat at that time of year.

> **If the sowing time is missed, the harvest itself fails. Autumn radish has to be sown between mid-July and early September (in South Korea); later than that, the cold arrives before the root has thickened. In a disaster, timing is not up for negotiation.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The timing difference between central and southern South Korea is roughly ten to fifteen days. In central South Korea the focus is mid-to-late August; in southern South Korea, early September. The criterion for judging is to count backward from the expected date of the first frost. 70-90 days before the first frost is the best time to sow.

### 6-2. Sowing Method

| Item | Standard | Notes |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Sowing depth | 1-2cm | Too deep and germination suffers |
| Sowing method | Spot sowing. 1-2 seeds per hole | No transplanting |
| Row spacing | 1-row planting 60-70cm, 2-row planting 60cm | |
| Plant spacing | 25-30cm (final) | Set by thinning |
| Target plant count | 8,000-9,000 plants per 1,000m² | |
| Covering and firming | Cover with fine soil and press lightly | Seed and soil must be in close contact |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Radish must always be sown directly in the field. Seedlings must not be raised and transplanted. The reason is the taproot. If the tip of the main root is broken or bent during transplanting, the root splits at that spot, and the trace becomes the shape of the harvest as it is. For napa cabbage and cabbage, raising seedlings and transplanting them is common knowledge, but radish is the exact opposite.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Recommending spot sowing, with only one or two seeds per hole, follows the same line of thought. If many seeds are put in and thinned out later, the roots of the plants that remain are disturbed during thinning. If seed is plentiful, three or four seeds can be put in as a precaution against germination failure, but when thinning, the extra plants must be cut at ground level, not pulled.

### 6-3. Seed Treatment

| Treatment | Procedure | Effect |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Selection | Choose large, plump seeds of even color | Early vigor improves |
| Hot-water soak | Soak in 50-55°C water for 10-15 minutes, then cool in cold water | Kills pathogens on and inside the seed |
| Ash treatment | Coat the seed with fine wood ash | Serves for both disinfection and drying |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Hot-water soaking is the most reliable method of seed disinfection available when no chemicals are on hand. The seed itself withstands 50-55°C for 10-15 minutes, whereas pathogens on the surface and inside die under those conditions. Above 60°C the seed dies too, and below 45°C the pathogens survive, so temperature matters. Without a thermometer, mix boiling water and cold water at roughly 1 to 1 and use as the guide a temperature that feels hot when you dip a hand in but that you can bear for a few seconds. After the treatment, cool the seed in cold water and dry it in the shade.

## 7. Water Management

### 7-1. Water by Growth Stage

| Stage | Management | Notes |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Sowing to germination | Keep the topsoil from drying out | If germination is uneven, plants differ in size |
| Seedling stage | Lightly and often | The roots are still shallow |
| Root-swelling stage | Evenly and sufficiently | Moisture fluctuations in this stage cause cracking |
| 2 weeks before harvest | Reduce gradually | The tissue firms up and storability improves |
| Monsoon season (jangma, late June to July in South Korea) | Keep the drainage ditches clear | Excess moisture is a greater risk than drought |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There is one principle to remember in water management for radish: evenness matters more than absolute quantity. When a dry spell continues and then rain suddenly falls, the inside of the root swells quickly while the skin cannot keep up with that speed and splits lengthwise. This is yeolgeun (root cracking), and a split radish cannot be stored. Conversely, if water keeps standing, the root rots from lack of oxygen.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The cheapest means of achieving evenness is mulching. When straw or dry grass is spread between the plants, rainwater soaks in slowly when it rains and evaporation is suppressed when it is dry, so the swings in soil moisture are reduced. Where there is no plastic film, this effect is especially great.

### 7-2. When Water Is Short or Excessive

| Condition | Symptom | Result |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Shortage | Leaves wilt during the day and recover in the evening | Temporary. Recovery is still possible |
| Shortage | The root becomes thin and its pungency grows stronger | Mustard-oil glycosides increase |
| Watering after shortage | The root cracks lengthwise | Storage becomes impossible |
| Excess | Lower leaves turn yellow | Oxygen shortage at the root |
| Excess | The root turns soft and smells foul | Soft rot. That plant must be discarded |
| Excess | Fine roots multiply on the root surface | They have stretched out in search of oxygen |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;If a radish with soft rot is found, remove it from the field at once and throw it far away or bury it. It must not go onto the compost pile. The bacteria of this disease survive in the soil for a long time and carry over to the next crop. If juice that has run out of an infected radish touches a neighboring plant, the disease passes straight to it.

## 8. Crop Management

### 8-1. Thinning

| Round | Timing | Spacing left |
|:---|:---|:---|
| 1st | 3-4 true leaves | Leave only one plant per hole |
| 2nd | 6-7 true leaves | Final spacing 25-30cm |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;If thinning is not done on time, radishes do not thicken. When the roots bump into those of neighboring plants, there is no room to grow and they push against each other, leaving many thin radishes. On the same field the plant count is high, but the total weight actually falls.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;When thinning, do not pull; cut close to the ground with scissors or a fingernail. Pulling disturbs the roots of the plant being kept, and the root splits at that spot. If cut off, the root left underground rots and becomes organic matter, with no effect on the neighboring plant.

> **The young radishes thinned out are yeolmu (young leafy radish). The leaves and small roots are used whole in muchim (seasoned side dishes) or kimchi. In growing radish, the first harvest is not the day the roots are pulled but the day of thinning.**

### 8-2. Hilling and Shoulder Care

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;As radish grows, the upper part of the root is pushed up above the ground. This is a normal phenomenon, and its degree varies by variety. The exposed part takes sunlight and turns green; there is no problem with the taste, which is in fact sweeter. If too much is exposed, however, it is hit directly by frost and freezes, swaying in the wind breaks off the fine roots, and the surface becomes rough.

| Situation | Action |
|:---|:---|
| Shoulders slightly exposed | Leave as is. This is normal |
| Shoulders heavily exposed | Cover lightly with soil |
| Plant is leaning | Gather soil around the base to prop it upright |
| Frosts becoming frequent | Cover the shoulders with straw or dry grass |

### 8-3. Weed Management

| Timing | Method | Notes |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Early growth | Pull by hand or scrape shallowly while weeds are small | This is the most important period |
| Together with thinning | Clear the surrounding weeds while thinning | Two jobs at once |
| After the leaves cover the ground | Hardly needed | Shade suppresses weeds |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Radish leaves are large and spread wide, so once the plants are established they smother weeds on their own. The contest is decided in the first 3 to 4 weeks. If weeds take the space first during this period, radish loses the competition for light and nutrients and the root does not thicken.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;When weeding, take care not to push the homi (Korean hand hoe) in deep. Radish's fine roots spread widely near the soil surface, and if these fine roots are cut, the root surface becomes rough or splits. Scrape weeds off shallowly starting from the side farthest from the plant, and pull weeds right next to a plant by hand.

### 8-4. Picking Radish Greens

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Before the root is pulled, a few old leaves from the bottom can be picked and eaten. Because these leaves have lost photosynthetic capacity, they have little effect on root swelling. Many must not be picked at once, however. The leaves are the factory, and what the factory makes is stored in the root.

| Item | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Leaves that can be picked | 2-3 fully grown leaves, starting from the bottom |
| Leaves that must be left | Always at least eight |
| Timing | Until root swelling begins is safe |
| From the swelling stage on | Do not pick. It directly affects root thickness |
