## 15. Storage Without Refrigeration - Root-Cellar Pits and Drying

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Radish's Storability grade is C, while the storage period itself was rated B. Pit storage keeps it for 3-4 months, and thinly sliced and dried it lasts beyond a year. Few vegetables keep this long without electricity or refrigeration. Half of the reason radish is counted as a disaster-preparedness crop lies in this criterion.

### 15-1. Pit Storage

| Item | Guideline |
|:---|:---|
| Temperature | 0-4°C |
| Humidity | 85-95% |
| Pit | About 60cm wide and 75cm deep |
| How to place | Stand the radishes upright with the end where the leaves were facing down |
| Covering order | Cover with straw first, then put 40-60cm of soil on top in 2-3 passes |
| Duration | 3-4 months |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The principle of pit storage is to borrow the ground's ability to buffer temperature. The surface swings widely in temperature with day and night and with the seasons, but the deeper one goes the smaller the fluctuation, and below 1m it is almost constant all year. If a pit is dug 75cm deep and covered with 40-60cm of soil, the place where the radishes sit stays between 0°C and 4°C all winter. These are conditions in which they neither freeze nor sprout.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The condition of 85-95% humidity is also important. Radish is a vegetable that is 94% water, so in a dry place it quickly loses water and becomes wrinkled and dry-fleshed. Humidity stays high underground, so this problem solves itself. Conversely, standing water causes rot, so the pit must be dug where water drains away. The upper part of a slope or well-drained sandy ground is good; paddy floors and low-lying ground where water collects are avoided.

| Step | Method |
|:---|:---|
| Choosing the spot | A place where water does not pool and drainage is good |
| Digging | 60cm wide, 75cm deep |
| Floor | Line with dry straw or dry sand |
| Placing | Stand the radishes upright and pack them neatly. Do not include damaged or pithy ones |
| Covering | Cover thickly with straw, then cover with soil in 2-3 passes |
| Marking | Mark the location and dig a ditch around it so water does not flow in |
| Taking out | Take out only as much as is needed at one time and cover again |

> **Choose only sound radishes for the pit. A single radish that is damaged, pithy or showing signs of soft rot spoils its neighbors all winter. Selecting matters more than burying.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Dividing into several pits is safer than making one large one, because each opening disturbs the temperature and humidity. Bury a month's worth in each pit, and open the next pit only after one pit has been used up.

### 15-2. Burying in Soil and Indoor Storage

| Method | Conditions | Duration |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Burying in the field as is | Lay the dug radishes down at one side of the field and cover with straw and soil | 2-3 months |
| Burying in sand | Layer dry sand and radishes in a box or jar | 1-2 months |
| Wrapping in newspaper or paper | Wrap one by one and keep in a cool, dark place | 1-2 months |
| Keeping in a storeroom with the soil on | Stand them unwashed in a cool storeroom | 3-4 weeks |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Burying in sand is a method used when a pit cannot be dug. The sand fills the gaps between the radishes, reducing contact with air and maintaining humidity. Sand that is too bone-dry draws water out of the radishes, so slightly damp is best, and if the radishes are kept from touching each other, spoilage does not spread sideways even if one goes bad.

### 15-3. Dried Radish Strips

| Step | Method |
|:---|:---|
| Cutting | Cut into sticks 0.5-1cm thick or into thin slices |
| Spreading | Spread out without overlapping in a sunny, breezy place |
| Turning | Turn once or twice a day so they dry evenly |
| Duration | 3-5 days on clear days |
| Done when | They bend without snapping when flexed by hand |
| Storage | Put in an airtight container together with charcoal or roasted rice |
| Storage period | 6 months to 1 year or more |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Dried radish strips are the most reliable of radish's storage methods and the most compact. When radish, which is 94% water, dries, its weight falls below 10%. 10kg of radish becomes 1kg and fits in a single jar. Pit storage lasts 3-4 months while the dried product lasts beyond a year, so the food that carries through winter into spring and summer is in the end the dried kind.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;However, most of the vitamin C is lost in the drying process. What remains is dietary fiber, minerals and sugars. So the winter diet must be built on two tracks. Vitamin C comes from raw radish kept in the pit and from fermented foods, and calories and bulk come from the dried product. Neither alone is enough to get through winter.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The key in drying is to spread the pieces without overlapping. Overlapping parts do not dry and mold grows right there. At night, bring them in or cover them so they do not get dew.

### 15-4. Radish Greens and Siraegi (Dried Radish Greens)

| Step | Method |
|:---|:---|
| Preparing | Shake off wilted leaves and soil. Do not wash |
| Blanching (optional) | A brief blanch in boiling water and a rinse in cold water gives a better color and a softer texture |
| Spreading out | Tie several strands together and hang them upside down under the eaves or in the shade |
| Conditions | A breezy place without strong direct sunlight |
| Duration | One to two months |
| Storage | Once completely dry, put in a sack or box and keep in a cool place |
| Use | Boil, soak in water, peel off the skin, and use in namul (seasoned vegetable side dish) or soup |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Siraegi is dried radish greens. It is a way of turning the leaves that come up along with the radish into winter food rather than throwing them away, and it doubles what a single radish plant yields. The nutritional makeup of the greens differs from that of the root. They contain beta-carotene, which the root has almost none of, and far more calcium, iron and dietary fiber.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Dried siraegi is too tough to eat as it is. Soak it overnight in water, boil it, discard the boiling water and rinse again. In this process the tough fibers soften and the astringency goes away. Stems with a thick outer skin become much softer if the skin is peeled off by hand. Since the process uses fuel, it is efficient to put it on the fire together with something else being boiled.

### 15-5. Kimchi and Pickling

| Style | Salt concentration | Duration | Characteristics |
|:---|:---:|:---|:---|
| Dongchimi (radish kimchi in brine) | 2-3% brine | A few months | Eaten with the broth. A considerable amount of vitamin C remains |
| Kkakdugi and chonggak kimchi (cubed radish kimchi and ponytail radish kimchi) | Seasoning after salting | A few months | Fermentation improves storability and digestion |
| Jjanji-style salting (heavily salted pickle) | 10-15% | 1 year or more | Must be soaked in water before eating |
| Jangajji (pickled vegetables in soy sauce or paste) | Packed in soy sauce or doenjang (Korean soybean paste) | A few months | A salty side dish. Also supplies salt |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Dongchimi is an especially valuable storage method in a disaster. Whole radishes are submerged in brine and fermented slowly at a low temperature; almost no seasoning goes in, so it can be made with just salt and water. The acid that lactic acid bacteria make as they grow keeps stray microbes out, and a considerable amount of vitamin C is preserved in the process. Because the broth is eaten as well, losses are small. This is the historical reason such fermented foods have prevented scurvy in winter when nothing fresh is available.

> **Lactic acid fermentation has two conditions: the material must be completely submerged under the brine, and the salt concentration must not fall below 2%. When both are kept, it is lactic acid bacteria that grow, not stray microbes.**

## 16. Cooking and Processing

| Method | Fuel | Vitamin C retention | Notes |
|:---|:---:|:---:|:---|
| Raw salad and shredded radish (saengchae, muchae) | None | Maximum | Digestive enzymes remain active too |
| Grating | None | Maximum | Helps digest starch |
| Soups and broths | Shared with other cooking | Retained if the broth is eaten too | Radish's most common use |
| Braising | Medium | Low | Long simmering brings out sweetness |
| Fermentation (dongchimi, kimchi) | None | A considerable amount retained | Serves as both storage and cooking |
| Dried, then soaked for use | Sun and wind for drying | Almost none | Minerals and dietary fiber remain |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Where fuel is precious, a great advantage of radish is that it can be eaten without cooking. It only needs washing and slicing, and being so watery it can even stand in for water. When one is thirsty and drinking water is short, a piece of raw radish really does help.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Eating radish grated has a physiological basis. The amylase-family enzymes in radish break down starch, so eating it with cooked rice, tteok (rice cake) or potato makes digestion easier. These enzymes are heat-sensitive and disappear on cooking, and they come out only when the cells are broken by grating. The custom of having raw radish alongside when the stomach feels bloated on a grain-based diet comes from this.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Simmering radish for a long time removes the pungency and brings out sweetness. This is because the breakdown products of mustard oil glycosides, the pungent compounds, volatilize and the remaining sugars show through. That is why radish is good as a broth ingredient. Even in a soup with no other ingredients, adding only radish gives the broth sweetness and umami. This is why a soup boiled from just salt, water and radish can be a complete dish in a disaster.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;It is also worth remembering that there is no need to peel it. Vitamin C and dietary fiber are concentrated right under the skin, so washing it and using it with the skin on is nutritionally better and also cuts preparation time.

## 17. Annual Work Calendar

| Month (South Korea) | Work |
|:---|:---|
| January | Check the condition of the radishes in the pit. Pick out spoiled ones. Check the dried radish strips and siraegi |
| February | Turn the compost. Prepare the spring radish field. Check that the seed plants are overwintering |
| March | Sow spring radish from mid-month. Replant the overwintered seed plants |
| April | Thin spring radish and top-dress. Seed plants bolt; set up stakes |
| May | Start harvesting spring radish. Seed plants flower; manage isolation |
| June | Finish the spring radish harvest. Seed plants set pods. Tidy the field in preparation for summer |
| July | Collect seed in early July. From mid-month, begin preparing the field and sowing autumn radish |
| August | Continue sowing autumn radish. Guard against flea beetles at germination. 1st thinning |
| September | 2nd thinning and top-dressing. Manage drainage in the autumn rains (gaeul-jangma). Finish sowing by early September |
| October | Manage the root-swelling stage. Early harvest begins from mid-month |
| November | Main harvest in mid-month. Gimjang (making kimchi for the winter). Make pits and store. Dry radish strips and siraegi |
| December | Manage stored goods. Check the ripening of dongchimi and kimchi. Tidy the field |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The tightest stretches in this table are the sowing window from mid-July to early September and the harvest window in mid-November. Both windows are narrow, and a miss cannot be made up. If sowing is late, cold arrives before the roots have thickened, and if harvest is late, the roots freeze and become inedible. Radish is a crop strict about timing, and that strictness is this crop's only major drawback.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;It is also necessary to know in advance that November has the heaviest workload of the year. Harvest, tidying the greens, digging and burying pits, drying and gimjang all overlap in a short period. If labor is short, it is realistic to split the sowing at ten-day intervals to spread out the harvest period.
