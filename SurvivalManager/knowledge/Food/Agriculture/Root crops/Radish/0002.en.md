## 1. Disaster-Preparedness Evaluation

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Radish's overall grade is S, with a score of 51.8. Broken down by scenario, it is Short-term S (52.6), Long-term S (51.0) and Supplementary SS (62.2). That all three values are high and close to one another is what characterizes radish. The score comes not from standing out in any single criterion but from having few decisively bad ones.

### 1-3. Why the Calories per Area Grade Is B Even Though Calories Are Low

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Radish has 21kcal per 100g, which puts it close to the very bottom among the crops covered in this document. Yet its Calories per area grade is B, higher than lettuce's D or leaf mustard's C. What makes the difference is yield.

| Crop | Calories per 100g | Yield per 1,000m² | Calories per m² |
|:---|:---:|:---:|:---:|
| Radish | 21kcal | 7,000kg | 1,470kcal |
| Leaf mustard | 36kcal | 3,000kg | 1,080kcal |
| Lettuce | 20kcal | 3,000kg | 600kcal |
| Swiss chard | 18kcal | 4,000kg | 720kcal |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;A single radish plant reaches 1.3-1.6kg, and it does so even when planted at 25-30cm spacing. A leafy vegetable, however well grown, can hardly exceed 300g per plant. The weight that actually reaches the kitchen from the same field differs by more than a factor of two. The weakness of low calorie density is made up for in total quantity.

> **When choosing crops in a disaster, looking only at the calories per 100g leads to the wrong judgment. What really matters is the total calories that a plot of field produces, and in this calculation radish is far ahead of leafy vegetables.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;On top of this come the radish greens. The yield of 7,000kg in the evaluation table is for the root only. The greens amount to 30-50% of the root's weight, and they, too, are food. Moreover, the nutrient makeup of the greens differs from that of the root: beta-carotene and calcium, which the root has almost none of, are present in the leaves in considerable amounts. This is a setup in which planting one crop yields two foods of different character, and it is one of the factors that lifted radish's Supplementary grade to SS.

### 1-4. How to Read the Strengths

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Four criteria are graded SS: Infrastructure needs, Cooking & processing, Continuous-cropping tolerance and Prevalence in South Korea. S went to Speed to harvest, Taste and Seed longevity.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;An SS for Continuous-cropping tolerance is an unusual grade for a Brassicaceae crop. Napa cabbage, cabbage, leaf mustard and kale are all advised a break of 2 years or more before replanting because of clubroot, whereas radish falls in the group of crops unharmed by continuous cropping in the classification of South Korea's Rural Development Administration (RDA). This does not mean that radish never catches clubroot. It is rather because radish is relatively resistant, because its short growing period leaves little time for the disease to progress, and because its root grows straight down, so that even when infected fine roots are cut away a harvest remains. For a small field where rotation is impossible, this grade has great practical value.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The SS for Prevalence in South Korea is also worth noting. Radish is not only grown commercially across all eight provinces of South Korea; there are also populations that grow wild and have become naturalized. Seeds that fall from the field sprout by themselves along roadsides and embankments, and those seeds can be collected and sown again. When the seed supply chain has broken down completely, this trait works as insurance. Feral radish has thin, tough roots, but its leaves are edible and its seeds germinate normally.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The S for Taste is substantive as well. The measured sugar content of 2.96g is clearly higher than that of leafy vegetables, compared with 1.22g for lettuce and 0.00g for Swiss chard. This is why radish tastes sweet and refreshing even raw, and the value rises further after a frost. Whether a food is something people can keep eating in a disaster is hard to see in a nutrition table, but in practice it matters a great deal.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The S for Seed longevity, 5 years sealed at room temperature, is also an item to weigh heavily. Radish seeds are large, hard and long-lived. Because a single plant yields thousands of seeds, even saving seed from only ten plants secures several years' worth. It is among the crops for which self-sufficiency in seed comes easily.

### 1-5. How to Read the Weaknesses

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The criteria graded D are Perenniality and Non-food value; C went to Cold hardiness, Pest & disease pressure, Pest-control self-reliance, Storability, Yield retention without synthetic inputs and Tillage & digging energy.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The C for Cold hardiness is the most practical constraint. At 0°C the root suffers frost damage. The leaves can withstand a few degrees below zero, but when the root underground freezes, its cells burst, and when it thaws, that part turns soft and rots. For that reason radish cannot be left standing in a winter field. This is the exact opposite of kale, which keeps serving as a living storehouse even at 10°C below zero. Radish must be pulled and stored separately, and if that moment is missed, the entire harvest is lost.

> **In South Korea, autumn radish is pulled in mid-November, before the first strong cold snap arrives. A light frost of about 1°C below zero raises the sweetness of radish, but a cold snap colder than 3°C below zero destroys the tissue. The margin of those few days decides the whole year's crop.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The C for Storability is a somewhat complicated grade. The storage period itself was rated B, but it was lowered by one grade once the storage-stage loss rate of 8.94% from the FAO Food Loss Index was factored in. What this figure means is that radish keeps for a long time, yet the amount lost during storage is not small. If a single root with soft rot gets mixed in, the rot spreads to its neighbors; and if humidity is low the roots dry out, while if it is high they rot. For pit storage to succeed, the conditions of 0-4°C and 85-95% humidity must be met, and the techniques for meeting them are the subject of Section 15.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The C for Tillage & digging energy is a burden that arises because radish is a root vegetable. The soil must be tilled deeply to 22cm, which costs 239kcal of digging energy per m². Compared with 10cm and 109kcal for leafy vegetables, that is more than double. Without machinery, this difference is paid for with human labor. An energy return of 5.3× means that a little over five times the energy put into digging is recovered; it is not a losing proposition, but it is not as easy as with leafy vegetables, either.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The C for Pest-control self-reliance means that the means of response are limited when no pesticides are available. The main problems of radish, soft rot, clubroot and nematodes, all occur underground, so they cannot be seen, and by the time they show, it is already too late. The answer lies not in after-the-fact control but in advance conditions: drainage, rotation and soil management. These are covered in Section 9.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The D for Perenniality is a structural property. Since the root is what is eaten, once it is pulled, that is the end. Radish does, however, have one buffer: the leaves can be picked a little at a time until the root is pulled. If too many are picked, the root does not grow large, but a few old leaves from the bottom reach the table without much effect on root thickening.
