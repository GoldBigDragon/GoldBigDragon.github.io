## 12. Harvest

### 12-1. Judging the Timing

| Criterion | Details | Judgment |
|---|---|---|
| Leaves and stems | Lower leaves turn yellow first and the whole plant begins to wilt | Yellowing stage. Harvest is near |
| Stem death | Stems dry out and die completely | Harvest 1-2 weeks after the stems die |
| Skin test | Rubbing the tuber with a thumb does not peel the skin | Skin-set complete. Ready for storage |
| Weather | After 2-3 consecutive clear, dry days | The soil is dry, causing fewer wounds and less soil clinging |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Waiting another 1-2 weeks after the stems die is to let the skin set. On a potato whose skin has not set enough, the surface peels off during harvest and transport, and each of those wounds becomes a starting point for rot in storage. A potato dug a little early may taste good, but it cannot be used for storage.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There is an exception, however. If late blight has spread or the monsoon season (jangma, late June to July in South Korea) is forecast, cut the foliage without waiting and dig 2 weeks later. Leaving the tubers in the ground lets the pathogen ride rainwater down to infect them.

### 12-2. Harvesting Technique

| Step | Details | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Cut the above-ground stems first with a sickle | Pulling by the stem detaches the tubers, leaving them buried |
| 2 | Insert a hoe or spade starting 30cm from the plant | Digging too close stabs the tubers |
| 3 | Dig by lifting the soil and take the tubers out by hand | Dragging them out tears the skin |
| 4 | Rake back through the dug spot once more to find any left behind | Leftover potatoes volunteer the next year and spread disease |
| 5 | Brush off the soil gently, but do not wash with water | Moisture is a direct cause of rot in storage |
| 6 | Spread out in the shade to dry the surface for 2-3 hours | Direct sunlight starts greening |

> **Do not spread potatoes out to dry in direct sunlight. Even two hours of direct sun is enough for the surface to start turning green.**

### 12-3. Sorting After Harvest

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Dug potatoes must be sorted into four groups on the spot. If they are left mixed together, a single spoiled one will drag down all the rest.

| Category | Criterion | Use |
|---|---|---|
| Damaged | Stabbed by the hoe or with large areas of skin torn off | Consume immediately or process into dried potato or starch within a few days |
| Diseased | Has spots, soft rot, or a foul smell | Remove from the field and discard. Keep away from the storage area |
| For storage | Healthy, unwounded, medium size or larger | Main storage after curing |
| Seed potato candidates | Healthy, evenly shaped, 30-50g | Store separately in a dedicated container |

## 13. Seed Potato Maintenance and Seed Saving

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This section covers the weakest link in disaster preparedness with potatoes, and the part deserving the most care. The potato cannot be stored as dry seed. If you fail to carry the seed potatoes for next spring through alive, there is no crop the following year.

### 13-1. Selecting Seed Potatoes

| Criterion | Details | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Size | 30-50g, about egg-sized | Too small and early growth is weak; too large and it wastes seed |
| Shape | One showing the variety's characteristic shape clearly | Malformation can be a sign of virus or a physiological disorder |
| Condition | Free of disease spots, wounds, or soft rot | Otherwise it becomes a source of infection in storage |
| Selection | Mark the healthiest-looking plants during growth, then take seed from those plants | The only field method for screening out virus-infected plants |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Walk the field during the growing season and mark plants with mosaic-patterned or curled leaves, or plants that are unusually stunted, with a stake. Send the potatoes from those plants toward eating and do not keep them as seed, because viruses pass straight through to the next generation via the tuber. This practice is called culling diseased plants, and it is the most effective means of slowing seed potato degeneration when no chemicals are available.

### 13-2. Storing Seed Potatoes

| Condition | Target | Practice |
|---|---|---|
| Temperature | 2-4°C | The coolest spot in a root cellar or basement |
| Humidity | 85-90% | Bury in moist sand or sawdust |
| Light | Diffuse light allowed | Unlike eating potatoes, a little light exposure is actually beneficial |
| Ventilation | Light but continuous | Removes ethylene and moisture |
| Inspection | Once every 2 weeks | Remove any rotten ones immediately |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Light is allowed only for seed potatoes because greening is actually put to use here. A greened potato has more solanine and is unfit for people to eat, but for the same reason mice and insects eat it less, and it resists rot better. Since it is planted in spring without peeling, this is not a problem. It must, however, always be kept in a different spot from eating potatoes and clearly marked.

> **Never store seed potatoes in the same bin as eating potatoes. Accidents where someone, while starving, eats greened seed potatoes really do happen.**

### 13-3. Degeneration and How to Counter It

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;If you keep saving your own seed, aphid-borne viruses accumulate with each generation. Within 3-4 years the yield drops noticeably, and after that it collapses steeply. In normal times you would simply buy fresh virus-free seed potatoes, but when supply is cut off, you have to slow it down with the following methods.

| Method | Details | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Culling diseased plants | Mark symptomatic plants during the growing season and exclude them from seed | The most basic method, and highly effective |
| Highland seed production | Keep only the seed potato field at a cool, high elevation | Lower aphid density slows the rate of infection |
| Early harvest | Dig seed-potato plants 2-3 weeks earlier than the regular harvest | Avoids late-season infection when aphids become numerous |
| Aphid suppression | Light-colored mulch, nasturtium as a trap crop, protecting ladybirds | Fewer vector insects mean less infection |
| Exchanging seed potatoes | Trade with growers in other regions | Changing the lineage dilutes accumulated virus |
| Using true seed | Sow the seeds from potato fruit and raise them as seedlings | Viruses are almost never transmitted this way |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The last method, true seed, needs more explanation. The green fruit that forms after the potato flower fades contains real seeds. These seeds do not inherit most viruses, making them a way to break the cycle of degeneration. However, the potato is genetically very heterozygous, so a plant grown from seed shows completely different traits from its parent. The first year produces small, uneven potatoes; you select the good ones from among them and then continue by tuber from the following year on. It takes 2 more years, but it is the last resort when the seed potato lineage has completely collapsed.

> **The potato fruit itself is highly toxic. Never eat it for any purpose other than collecting seed.**

## 14. Toxicity and Precautions

### 14-1. Solanine and Chaconine

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Potatoes naturally contain glycoalkaloids called solanine and chaconine. These substances originally exist to defend the plant against insects and fungi. Normal potatoes contain trace amounts too, at a level that is not a problem, but the amount rises sharply under the following conditions.

| Condition | Risk Level | Reason | Action |
|---|:---:|---|---|
| Skin has turned green | Very high | Light exposure increases alkaloids along with chlorophyll | Cut the green part out deeply; discard the whole potato if it is extensive |
| Has sprouted | Very high | Alkaloids concentrate in and around the sprouts | Cut out the sprouts and the area around the eyes |
| Tastes bitter or acrid | Very high | The bitterness itself is a sign of alkaloids | Do not eat it |
| Wounded or diseased areas | High | Alkaloids increase as a defense response | Cut them out |
| Small, underdeveloped potatoes | Medium | Immature tubers have higher levels | Eat only fully grown ones |
| Fruit, leaves, and stems | Very high | Not an edible part of the plant | Do not eat them |
| A normal potato | Safe | Levels are not a problem | Can be cooked with the skin on |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Poisoning symptoms begin a few hours after eating, with vomiting, abdominal pain, and diarrhea, and in severe cases progress to headache, dizziness, and neurological symptoms. Fatal cases in adults are rare, but it is dangerous for children and the elderly.

> **Heat does not destroy solanine. Boiling, baking, or frying — the toxin remains no matter what. The only solution is to cut it out or throw it away.**

### 14-2. Storage Ventilation

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;When potatoes rot in bulk in a sealed storage space, the decay process generates harmful gases and consumes oxygen. Before entering a sealed storage room or underground pit, you must always open the door and ventilate it thoroughly first. The old method of lowering a candle in first, and not entering if the flame goes out, still holds true.

## 15. Storage Without Refrigeration

### 15-1. Three Stages of Storage

| Stage | Condition | Duration | Purpose |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Curing | 10-15°C, 85-95% humidity, dark with light ventilation | 1-2 weeks | Forms a cork layer over wounds to block rot and moisture loss |
| 2. Main storage | 3-5°C, 85-95% humidity, total darkness, light ventilation | 4-6 months | Minimizes respiration and suppresses sprouting |
| 3. Inspection | Once a week | Throughout the storage period | Prevents one rotten potato from ruining the whole batch |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Skipping curing cuts the storage period to less than half. If the tiny wounds made during digging are left open, pathogens enter through them and moisture escapes. Leaving the potatoes at a warm 10-15°C for 1-2 weeks lets cork tissue grow over the wounds and seal them. Only after finishing this process should you move them to a cool spot.

### 15-2. Comparing Unpowered Storage Methods

| Method | How to Build | Temperature | Duration | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Root cellar pit | Dig 1.5-2m into well-drained ground, cover with a roof, and add a vent | 0-4°C | 4-6 months | Most stable. Holds up all winter | Heavy digging labor. Not possible where the water table is high |
| Pit/clamp storage | Dig 30cm into the ground and mound it up like a burial mound, layering straw, potatoes, straw, then soil | 0-5°C | 3-5 months | Can be built in a day with no tools | Hard to reseal once opened, so you must take everything out at once |
| Sand-layer storage | Layer dry sand and potatoes alternately in a box | Ambient temperature | 2-4 months | Blocks the spread of rot. Suited to small quantities | Needs a separate cool spot |
| Straw/rice-hull insulation | Wrap and stack in straw in a cool indoor corner, blocking out all light | 5-10°C | 1-2 months | Needs no separate facility | Sprouts if the indoor temperature is high |
| North-facing earth cave/cavern | Uses existing terrain | 4-10°C | 2-4 months | Almost no labor | Humidity and rodents are hard to manage |

### 15-3. How to Build Pit Storage

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This is the most practical method when a spade is the only tool you have.

| Step | Details |
|---|---|
| 1 | Choose a high spot where water does not pool. A north-facing slope is good |
| 2 | Dig shallowly, 1.5m across and 20-30cm deep |
| 3 | Lay 10cm of dry straw on the bottom to cut off ground moisture |
| 4 | Pile the cured potatoes into a mound shape. Keep the height under 60cm |
| 5 | Cover the whole thing with 20-30cm of dry straw |
| 6 | Cover that with 15-20cm of soil and pat the surface so rainwater runs off |
| 7 | Stand a bundle of straw at the top as a vent. Blocking this causes rot inside |
| 8 | Dig a drainage ditch around the perimeter |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The vent is the key element. Potatoes keep respiring even in storage, giving off heat, water vapor, and carbon dioxide. If it is sealed completely, the temperature and humidity inside rise, and the whole batch turns mushy within days. The straw bundle at the top is a structure that lets air through while keeping rain out.

### 15-4. Storage Conditions in Detail

| Condition | Target | If It Deviates |
|---|---|---|
| Temperature | 3-5°C | Below 0°C causes freezing injury and mushiness. 10°C or above causes sprouting |
| Low-temperature caution | Long-term storage below 4°C | Starch converts to sugar, giving a sweet taste, and it blackens when fried |
| Humidity | 85-95% | Too low and it shrivels; too high and mold grows |
| Light | Total darkness | Greening proceeds even under weak light |
| Ventilation | Light and continuous | Blocking it traps heat and moisture; too strong dries them out |

| Caution | Reason |
|---|---|
| Do not store in the same bin as onions | Gas given off by onions accelerates potato rot |
| Keeping apples nearby suppresses sprouting | Ethylene from the apples suppresses sprouts, though it also speeds aging |
| No household refrigerators | Too cold and dry, unsuited to potatoes |
| Inspect once a week | One rotten potato rapidly infects those around it |

### 15-5. Ultra-Long-Term Storage Through Processing

| Method | Procedure | Duration | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Freeze-drying (chuño) | Repeat freezing at night and treading out the moisture by day for a week or more, then dry completely | Several years | A traditional Andean highland method. Possible only where night temperatures drop below freezing |
| Thin-slicing and drying | Slice to 5mm or thinner, blanch in boiling water for 1-2 minutes, then dry completely | Several months to 1 year | Without blanching it browns and spoils in storage |
| Starch extraction | Grate and mix into water, collect only the settled sediment, rinse several times, then dry | Several years | Reduces volume and weight, and pests rarely get into it |
| Flour | Grind fully dried pieces with a mortar or millstone | 1-2 years | Needs sealed storage and a desiccant |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Starch extraction is especially useful in a disaster. That is because scrap potatoes with wounded or diseased parts cut out, and small potatoes that are hard to store, can all be turned into starch. The procedure is as follows. Grate the potatoes, skin and all, wrap the gratings in cloth, and knead them in water. Once white liquid comes out, let it settle for 30 minutes to 1 hour. Pour off the water on top, add fresh water, and repeat settling three or four more times. Spreading out the white sediment left at the end and drying it completely gives you potato starch. About 12-18kg of starch comes from 100kg of potatoes.

> **The standard strategy for disaster storage is to run both in parallel. Put the marketable potatoes in a root cellar for winter food, and turn the scraps and small ones into starch and flour for a multi-year stockpile.**

## 16. Cooking and Processing

### 16-1. Fuel-Saving Cooking

| Method | Fuel Use | Procedure | Notes |
|---|:---:|---|---|
| Boiling whole | Medium | Submerge with skin on for 20-30 minutes | Boiling with the skin on reduces nutrient loss |
| Steaming | Low | Add only a little water and cook with steam. 25-30 minutes | Uses less water and fuel than boiling |
| Boiling in small pieces | Low | Cut to 2cm or smaller: 10-15 minutes | More surface area means it cooks faster |
| Residual-heat cooking | Very low | Boil for 10 minutes, turn off the heat, wrap in thick cloth, and leave for 40 minutes | Heat-retention cooking. Cuts fuel use by more than half |
| Baking in embers | Very low | Bury in hot ash left after a fire for 30-40 minutes | Uses the residual heat of a stove after other cooking is done |
| Cooking with starch | Low | Starch cooks instantly when stirred into boiling water | The most fuel-efficient option |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Heat-retention cooking is the single most valuable technique when fuel is scarce. Boil the pot for just 10 minutes, take it off the heat, and wrap it thickly in straw, a blanket, rice hulls, or the like; the internal temperature then stays above 80 degrees for more than an hour while cooking continues. It suits starchy crops like potatoes especially well. A single wooden box packed with straw is tool enough.

### 16-2. Cooking Precautions

| Item | Details |
|---|---|
| Skin | For a normal potato, eating it with the skin on is better for vitamin C and fiber |
| Greened areas | Must always be cut out deeply. Peeling the skin alone is not enough |
| Cooking water | Potassium and vitamins have dissolved into it, so use it as a soup base too |
| Raw potato | Undigested starch causes stomach upset. Always cook it |
| Cooled potato | Resistant starch increases, making the rise in blood sugar gentler |

### 16-3. Processing That Doubles as Storage

| Processing | Procedure | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Gamja-tteok (potato rice cake) and gamja-ongsimi (potato dumplings) | Squeeze the liquid from grated potato and knead the remaining pulp together with the settled starch | Uses up scrap potatoes completely |
| Fermented potato (ssakhin gamja) | Soak in water and ferment for several weeks, then filter out only the starch | A traditional method from Gangwon Province. The smell is strong, but starch recovery is high |
| Dried potato slices | Blanch, slice thinly, and dry | Rehydrate in water and add to soup or porridge |
| Potato flour | Grind the dried pieces | Mix with grain flour for use in dough |

## 17. Annual Work Calendar

| Month | Task | Key Points |
|---|---|---|
| January | Inspect stored potatoes, check seed potato condition | Remove rotten ones weekly. Keep storage temperature from dropping below 0 degrees |
| February | Select seed potatoes, plan the field, turn the compost | In southern South Korea, sowing begins from late in the month |
| March | Cut and cure seed potatoes, begin sprouting, apply base fertilizer and build ridges | Start sprouting 20-35 days before sowing |
| April | Sow spring potatoes (early-mid month in central South Korea), guard against frost | Cover with soil or straw when a late frost is forecast |
| May | Emergence, 1st hilling, top-dressing, weeding | The period most vulnerable to weed competition |
| June | Focus watering on tuber bulking, 2nd hilling, watch for late blight | Check leaves daily if rain is frequent |
| July | Harvest spring crop, cure, service the storage area | The goal is to finish harvesting before the monsoon |
| August | Sow autumn potatoes (southern South Korea), harvest summer crop (highlands) | Autumn potatoes are planted whole, without cutting |
| September | Manage autumn crop growth, hilling | Manage the seed-potato field separately |
| October | Stop watering the autumn crop, prepare for storage | Finish harvesting before frost |
| November | Harvest autumn crop, cure, root-cellar/pit storage | Store seed potatoes separately |
| December | Check storage, process into starch/dried potato, plan next year | Process the hardest-to-store potatoes first |

### 17-1. Five Things to Never Forget

| Item | Details |
|---|---|
| Greening and sprouts | Green color, sprouts, and bitterness mean solanine. Heat does not remove it, so cut it out or throw it away |
| No continuous cropping | Keep nightshades 3-4 years apart. Collapse hits suddenly in the third year |
| Hilling | Costs nothing while raising yield, safety, and disease resistance all at once |
| Curing | 1-2 weeks of curing after harvest doubles the storage period |
| Seed potatoes | Store separately from eating potatoes, and keep seed only from healthy plants |
