## Introduction

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The potato (Solanum tuberosum) comes from the high Andean plateau of South America. It is a crop shaped over thousands of years by human hands in a place more than 3,000m above sea level — cold, dry, and poor ground, where frost falls at night and harsh ultraviolet light pours down by day. This origin explains almost everything about what the potato does well and what it does not. In a cool climate, it is extremely good at driving starch into the ground in a short period. In the heat of midsummer and in waterlogged ground, by contrast, it is strikingly helpless. Once temperatures exceed 27°C, the tubers stop growing, and if the roots sit in water, they rot within days.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Looked at as a disaster crop, the potato is two-sided. On the good side, it gives you something to eat 80-90 days after planting — the fastest of these six tuber crops. Its calories per area alone cover nearly 80% of the self-sufficiency line. For a tuber crop (tubers/storage roots), it is unusually protein-dense: protein makes up 12% of its calories, and its essential amino acids are well balanced. That means eating potatoes alone, without grain, does not bring on protein deficiency right away. It needs no infrastructure, and seed potatoes can be had anywhere across South Korea. In the first year after a disaster hits, when you need to fill your stomach right away, few crops are as dependable as the potato.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The bad side shows up over time. The potato's most serious weakness is how far its yield collapses once chemical fertilizer and pesticides are gone. In long-term zero-input trials, potato yields fell to 18% of the conventional level — that is, 82% disappeared. There are two reasons. First, the potato is a heavy feeder that draws in large amounts of nutrients over a short period, so cutting off fertilizer brings an immediate drop in yield. Second, there is no cure for late blight. Once it takes hold, nothing can be done, and in a cool, rainy year an entire field can be wiped out within a week. This is the same disease that starved a million people to death in 19th-century Ireland.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There is one more thing: the potato cannot carry itself forward through seed. You have to keep a living tuber called the seed potato alive until the following year, and in that process viruses build up, so yield falls with each generation. The approach used for beans or grain — storing dry seed in a jar for years — does not work here. That is why the potato excels at getting you through the early stage of a disaster, but is a poor pillar for long-term self-reliance on its own. This document is written on that premise. It places special weight on how far you can push potato yields without fertilizer and pesticides, how to carry seed potatoes through without killing them, and how to store them without electricity.
