## Introduction
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Potato (Solanum tuberosum) is a nightshade-family crop from the Andean highlands of South America; it is good at packing starch underground in a short time in a cool climate, but helpless in midsummer heat and on waterlogged ground. In the disaster preparedness evaluation it scores an overall B (48.1 points, 35th of 183 valid species), short term S (50.6), long term B (45.6), and supplementary S (52.5 points), a crop that provides food quickly but does not carry you for long. It yields a harvest 80-90 days after planting and its protein reaches 12% of calories, so it is dependable in the first year of a disaster; but once chemical fertilizer and pesticides disappear the yield collapses to 18% of conventional, late blight has no cure, and because it must be carried on from year to year as living tubers rather than seed, viruses accumulate.

> **The greatest advantage of potato as a staple is not the calories but the protein that comes with them.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Calories per area A is 2,010 kcal per ㎡, 0.77 times the self-sufficiency line, which means a little over 130 ㎡ can supply most of one person's calories for a year. Protein density A means protein is 12% of calories with an amino acid score above 90; sweet potato and cassava are E. Yield retention without synthetic inputs E (17.6%) is a measured value from a 40-year Swiss trial in which the unfertilized plots yielded about one sixth of conventional ones, so be prepared for a field that gave 300 kg in ordinary times to give only a little over 50 kg. Seed longevity E means the seed must be carried over to spring as living tubers rather than dry seed, and repeated self-saving cuts yield within 3-4 years, and pest-control self-reliance D means control of late blight is entirely prevention. The grade for each item is in the Crop Evaluation Results document.

## 2. Plant Overview
| Item | Details |
|:---|:---|
| Classification | Perennial of the nightshade family, genus Solanum. Native to the Andean highlands of Peru and Bolivia |
| Leaves and stem | Odd-pinnately compound leaves with 5-7 leaflets. The stem is 30-100 cm tall and the roots are shallow-spreading |
| Flowers and fruit | In May-June, white, light pink, or purple flowers bloom. The fruit is a green berry resembling a cherry tomato, highly poisonous and inedible |
| Tubers | A storage organ formed by the swollen tip of an underground stem; it is a stem, not a root. So it has eyes, turns green in light, and gains toxic alkaloids |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;For about 30 days after emergence the plant expands its leaves, stems, and roots; around flowering the tips of the underground stems begin to swell; the tubers then enlarge (peak potassium demand); and when the stem withers yellow the skin hardens. Nitrogen is given early and potassium late, and late nitrogen excess makes only the leaves lush and ruins tuber formation and storability. Tuber formation is decided by day length and night temperature, and if nights stay at 20℃ or above it does not begin. Per 100 g there are 66-85 kcal, 14.8-19.6 g carbohydrate (mostly starch), 1.5-2.0 g protein, 2.3 g dietary fiber, 420 mg potassium, and 9.7 mg vitamin C.

## 3. Growing Environment and Varieties
| Condition | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Temperature | Growth 14-23℃ (5℃ and below and 30℃ and above are limits), tuber swelling 14-18℃ (at 27-30℃ and above swelling stops and only leaves grow), night temperature 12-18℃, germination 15-20℃ |
| Water and light | 400-800 mm over the whole growing period, and 24 hours of flooding rots the roots and tubers. 6 hours of sunlight a day or more, and under 40% shade the relative yield is about 80% |
| Soil | Well-drained sandy loam to loam, topsoil 25 cm or deeper, pH 5.0-6.0. Above pH 6.5 common scab (corky scabs on the tuber surface) increases sharply, so do not add lime and do not add more than about 1.8 L of wood ash per 10 ㎡ |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Choose sites in this order: sloping or high ground where water drains (one flood can lose the whole field, and drainage cannot be fixed later), east or southeast-facing ground where dew dries early and a place with wind (this breaks the germination conditions of the late blight spores), land where no nightshade has been grown in the last 3-4 years, and a distance from which water can be carried.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Mealy varieties (Namjak, Dubaek, Haryeong, Russet) are starchy and crumbly and suit boiling and starch extraction, while waxy varieties (Daeseo, Daeji) keep their shape when boiled long and suit braising and broth. Sumi (early, 90-100 days) is Korea's main variety and suits securing food early in a disaster, Chubaek (very early, 80-90 days) gives food the fastest but storage must be given up, and Jopung (very early) is relatively resistant to late blight. To get through winter, Daeji (mid-late, 110-120 days) and Russet, which store well, and for rainy, cool places Kennebec, which resists late blight, are suitable; Dubaek needs 20% or more extra fertilizer, so avoid it when manure is short. Because the weather differs every year and each variety does differently, plant early and mid-late varieties mixed, and on lowlands where summer exceeds 30℃ plant sweet potato instead of potato.

## 4. Field Preparation and Sowing
| Item | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Land preparation | Plow to 20-30 cm and crush clods to fist size or smaller, with beds 20-30 cm high and drainage ditches at least 20 cm deep. Keep pH at 5.0-5.5 and do not add lime |
| Fertilization (1,000 ㎡) | Nitrogen 10-13.7 kg (50% base, 50% top dressing), phosphorus 8.8 kg, potassium 13 kg (phosphorus and potassium all as base), fully matured compost 1,500-2,000 kg. Apply the base 1-2 weeks before sowing so that it does not touch the seed potatoes directly, give the top dressing with the first hilling when the plants are about 20 cm tall, and give none after flowering. Immature compost rots the seed potatoes |
| Disaster substitute fertilizers | Wood ash (sifted white ash at 1-2 L per 10 ㎡; too much raises pH and common scab rises sharply), compost turned over for 6 months or more, urine diluted 10 times or more (matured 2 weeks or more, stopped 3 weeks before harvest, kept off the leaves), bone meal, and hairy vetch or Chinese milk vetch plowed in the previous year |
| Seed potatoes | Prepare 20-35 days before sowing. Exclude those with lesions, wounds, or softness, and cut lengthwise into 2-4 pieces of 30-50 g, each with 1-2 eyes (a crosswise cut leaves pieces without eyes, which rot). Coat the cut surfaces with wood ash and let them heal in the shade for 2-3 days, then sprout them for 25-35 days in diffuse light at 15-20℃ and 80-90% humidity to produce thick, short green sprouts (in the dark, white weak sprouts appear). Hot-water treatment (50-55℃ for 10-15 minutes) kills the buds above 60℃ |
| Planting | Spring crops are sown from early February to late March and harvested from late May to late June; planting as early as possible right after the thaw so that harvest ends before the rainy season is the strongest control of late blight. Highland crops are planted mid-April to mid-May and fall crops on the southern coast in mid to late August. Depth 5-10 cm; single rows 70-80 cm apart with 20-25 cm between plants; sprouts up and cut surface down. 150-200 kg per 1,000 ㎡ |

## 5. Water Management and Cultivation Management
| Stage | Water management |
|:---|:---|
| Sowing to emergence | Only enough to keep the soil from drying (the seed potato lives on stored nutrients, so too much water rots it) |
| Emergence to before flowering | Keep moisture moderate and avoid excess (leaving it slightly short makes the roots reach deep) |
| Tuber swelling | The most important; give plenty (one month of drought in this period halves the yield). Give it in portions so that cracks and secondary growth do not occur |
| 10 days to 2 weeks before harvest | Stop irrigating (the skin hardens and storability rises) |

| Care | Method |
|:---|:---|
| Mulching | Straw at least 5 cm thick is the first substitute for plastic; it cuts evaporation by more than half and blocks soil splash, in effect doing the work of irrigation. It does not raise soil temperature, so apply it after emergence |
| Hilling | The first time, when stems are 10-20 cm, mound 5-10 cm of soil from between the rows around the base; the second, 2-3 weeks later, another 5-10 cm. It prevents greening and solanine formation, increases the number of tubers, prevents falling over, and blocks the path by which blight spores travel down with rainwater, so never skip it under any circumstances |
| Other care | Weeding is essential, and the month after emergence is the most vulnerable to weed competition. If more than 5 stems come from one seed potato, keep 3-4 and pull the rest |
| Water shortage and excess | If leaves droop at midday and recover in the evening it is a warning sign, so run water between the rows; if they do not recover in the evening, irrigate at once but not all at once. If lower leaves turn yellow and roots turn black, dig a drainage ditch; if the stem base softens and smells foul it cannot be saved, so take that plant out of the field |

## 6. Pests, Diseases, and Nutrient Problems
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Late blight, in cool rainy weather, goes from the first spot to a whole field blackened and dried in a week, and since it has no cure, control is all prevention. Arrange resistant varieties, wide spacing, high ridges, and early planting so that the rainy season and late growth do not coincide, and as soon as the first lesion is found, cut the entire aboveground part with a sickle, burn it outside the field, and harvest only the tubers 2 weeks later. The yield falls, but it prevents the fungus from ruining all the tubers and the stored crop.

| Pest or disease | Symptoms and conditions | Prevention and response |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Late blight | Water-soaked blackish-brown spots on leaves, white mold on the undersides, brown rot of tubers (15-20℃ with humidity of 90% or more for two days or more) | Resistant varieties, wide spacing, high ridges, early planting, and removal of the aboveground part when it breaks out. Do not leave diseased residue in the field; burn it or bury it deep |
| Soft rot, blackleg | Tubers soften and smell foul / The stem base rots black and the plant wilts (excess moisture, infected seed potatoes) | Improve drainage, harvest without wounding, and use healthy seed potatoes. Isolate and discard infected plants at once |
| Virus diseases | Mosaic patterns, leaf curl, stunted plants (carried by aphids) | No cure. Virus-free seed potatoes, aphid control, and removal of diseased plants. The main cause of degeneration in self-saved seed |
| Pests | Aphids (suck sap and carry viruses), potato tuber moth, nematodes, field mice | Soapy water and protecting ladybugs / Hilling and not leaving potatoes in the field after harvest / Resistant varieties and rotation of 3 years or more / Clearing weeds around the field and wire mesh on the floor of the store |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The only response to late blight that can be made without chemicals is Bordeaux mixture: dissolve 1 kg of copper sulfate in 80 L of water, dissolve 1 kg of quicklime in 20 L of water, and pour the copper solution into the lime solution (it is a preventive and has no effect after an outbreak, and metal containers are not used). Against aphids use soapy water, 20 g of unscented soap dissolved in 1 L of water.

| Symptom | Cause and action |
|:---|:---|
| Lower leaves turn yellow and plants stay small / Leaf edges scorch brown / Leaves turn from deep green to purplish (cold periods) | Nitrogen deficiency / potassium deficiency / phosphorus deficiency. Matured compost and urine diluted 10 times / a little wood ash in the furrows / bone meal and wood ash |
| Only the leaves grow lush and tubers are small | Nitrogen excess. Cut off nitrogen and supplement potassium with wood ash |

## 7. Intercropping and Crop Rotation
| Compatibility | Companions |
|:---|:---|
| Good | Legumes such as kidney bean and pea (nitrogen fixation), marigold (suppresses nematodes), nasturtium (draws aphids away) |
| Fair | Brassicas, spinach, and lettuce (cover the ground and reduce weeds, with little competition) |
| Poor | Tomato, eggplant, and pepper (same nightshade family, so they share late blight and pests), pumpkin, cucumber, and sunflower (compete for space and nutrients) |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Potato is classed as a two-year fallow crop, but late blight and common scab remain in the soil, so an interval of 3-4 years is safer. The order is potato in year 1, legumes in year 2 (to restore nitrogen), leafy vegetables or onion-family crops in year 3, and grains or root vegetables in year 4. If potatoes are planted in succession in one field, nothing shows in the first year and then in the third year soil-borne diseases pass their critical density and the crop suddenly collapses.

## 8. Harvest and Seed Saving
| Harvest | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Timing | Dig on a clear, dry day after 2-3 days of dry weather, 1-2 weeks after the stems have dried and died, when the skin does not slip when rubbed with a thumb (skin set). If late blight has spread or the rainy season is forecast, cut the aboveground part and dig 2 weeks later |
| Method | Cut the aboveground stems with a sickle, then dig with a hoe or spade starting 30 cm from the plant, lifting the soil, and take the tubers out by hand. Search the dug spot once more for any left. Only brush off the soil gently and do not wash, and spread them in the shade for 2-3 hours to dry the surface (greening begins after even two hours of direct sunlight) |
| Sorting | Sort into four on the spot. Wounded ones are eaten at once or dried or made into starch, diseased ones are taken out of the field and discarded, storage ones go to curing and then main storage, and seed candidates (healthy ones of 30-50 g) are kept separately |

| Seed saving | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Selection | Healthy tubers of 30-50 g (egg-sized) free of lesions, wounds, and softness. During growth mark plants with mosaic or curled leaves and unusually stunted plants with a stick, use their potatoes for eating, and do not keep them as seed potatoes (culling diseased plants; because viruses pass directly through the tubers, this is the most effective way to slow degeneration without chemicals) |
| Storage | 2-4℃, humidity 85-90%, buried in moist sand or sawdust with weak ventilation, and checked every 2 weeks to remove rotten ones. Seed potatoes may have weak light (when greened, mice and insects eat them less). Never keep them in the same bin as eating potatoes, and mark them conspicuously |
| Countering degeneration | Keep a seed-potato field only on cool highland, dig 2-3 weeks earlier than the regular harvest, reduce aphids with light-colored mulch and nasturtium, and exchange seed potatoes with growers in other regions |
| True potato seed | Seeds inside the green fruit do not inherit most viruses, so this is the last means of breaking degeneration. Seedlings differ entirely from the parent and in the first year the potatoes are small and uneven, so pick the good ones and continue them as tubers, which takes 2 more years. The fruit is highly poisonous and must never be eaten except to collect seed |

## 9. Storage
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Storage has three stages. Curing (10-15℃, humidity 85-95%, dark, weak ventilation, 1-2 weeks) builds a cork layer over wounds to block rot and water loss; main storage (3-5℃, humidity 85-95%, complete darkness, weak ventilation, 4-6 months) suppresses respiration and sprouting; and weekly checks remove rotten ones. Skipping curing cuts the storage period by more than half. Below 0℃ they go soft from frost injury and above 10℃ they sprout. Do not keep them in the same bin as onions, and do not use a household refrigerator.

| Method | How | Period |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Cellar storage | Dig ground 1.5-2 m deep that drains well, cover it with a roof, and make a vent (0-4℃). The most stable, but the labor is great and it cannot be done where the water table is high | 4-6 months |
| Pit or clamp storage | Made in a day with one spade. Dig a shallow pit 1.5 m across and 20-30 cm deep, lay 10 cm of dry straw, pile the cured potatoes in a mound no higher than 60 cm, cover with 20-30 cm of dry straw and 15-20 cm of soil, pat it down, and make a vent at the top with a bundle of straw (if blocked, heat and moisture build up and everything softens within days). Once opened it is hard to cover again, so everything must be taken out at once (0-5℃) | 3-5 months |
| Processed storage | Slice to 5 mm or less, blanch in boiling water for 1-2 minutes, and dry completely (unblanched they brown), or mill into flour. Starch, made by grinding, dispersing in water, collecting only the settled sediment, rinsing several times, and drying, can take all the trimmings and small potatoes, reduces volume, and is not attacked by insects (12-18 kg from 100 kg) | Dried pieces several months to 1 year, flour 1-2 years, starch several years |

> **The standard disaster storage strategy is to combine methods. Put the good ones in a cellar as winter food, and turn the trimmings and small ones into starch and flour as a stockpile for years.**

## 10. Cooking and Precautions
| Caution | Details |
|:---|:---|
| Solanine and chaconine | Content is very high in potatoes that have turned green on the skin, have sprouted, or taste bitter or acrid, and also high in wounded or diseased parts and small, immature ones. Do not eat the fruit, leaves, or stems. Cut green parts and the area around sprouts and eyes out deeply, discard the whole potato if the green is extensive, and do not eat anything that tastes bitter. Peeling alone is not enough |
| Symptoms | A few hours after eating, vomiting, abdominal pain, and diarrhea begin, and in severe cases headache, dizziness, and neurological symptoms follow. Death in adults is rare, but it is dangerous for children and the frail |
| Ventilation | When potatoes rot in large quantities in a sealed store or underground cellar, harmful gas is produced and oxygen drops, so before entering, open the door and ventilate thoroughly, put a candle in first, and do not enter if it goes out |

> **Heat does not destroy solanine. Boiled, baked, or fried, the toxin remains. The only remedy is to cut it out or throw it away.**

| Cooking | Method |
|:---|:---|
| Saving fuel | Whole boiling is 20-30 minutes fully submerged in the skin (less nutrient loss), steaming is 25-30 minutes with a little water, and cut to 2 cm or less it takes 10-15 minutes. For retained-heat cooking, boil 10 minutes, take it off the fire, and wrap it thickly in straw, a blanket, or rice hulls for 40 minutes, which uses less than half the fuel; they also cook buried in hot ash for 30-40 minutes, and starch stirred into boiling water cooks at once |
| How to eat | Eating with the skin is better for vitamin C and dietary fiber. The cooking water holds potassium and vitamins, so use it as broth. Raw potato starch is not digested and causes abdominal pain, so always cook it |
| Processing | Squeeze the water out of grated potato and knead the remaining pulp with the settled starch into dough for potato cakes or dumplings, using every scrap. Soaking in water for several weeks to ferment and then straining out only the starch (rotted potato) smells strong but recovers a lot of starch. Blanched and dried pieces are soaked and added to soup and porridge |

## 11. Annual Work Schedule
| Month | Work |
|:---|:---|
| Jan-Feb | Check stored potatoes and seed potatoes (remove rotten ones weekly, keep the store from falling below 0℃). In February sort seed potatoes, plan the field, and turn the compost |
| Mar-Apr | In March cut seed potatoes and cure them, start sprouting (20-35 days before sowing), and apply base fertilizer and make beds. In April sow spring potatoes (early to mid April in central Korea), and cover with soil or straw when a late frost is forecast |
| May-June | In May emergence, first hilling, top dressing, and weeding. In June irrigate heavily during tuber swelling, make the second hilling, and watch for late blight |
| July-Aug | In July harvest the spring crop (the goal is to finish before the rainy season), cure, and prepare the store. In August sow fall potatoes in the south (whole, uncut) and harvest the highland summer crop |
| Sep-Oct | In September manage the fall crop and hill, and manage the seed-potato field separately. In October stop irrigating, prepare for storage, and finish the harvest before frost |
| Nov-Dec | In November harvest the fall crop, cure, and store in a cellar or pit (seed potatoes separately). In December check storage, process into starch and dried pieces (starting with those that are hard to store), and plan for next year |

> **Potato is a crop where you cut out or discard green, sprouts, and bitterness, rotate nightshades on a 3-4 year interval, never skip hilling, cure after harvest, and keep seed potatoes apart from eating potatoes.**
