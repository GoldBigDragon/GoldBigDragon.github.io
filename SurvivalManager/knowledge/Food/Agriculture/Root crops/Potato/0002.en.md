## 1. Disaster-Preparedness Evaluation

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The potato's overall grade is B, with a score of 48.1. Broken down by scenario on the same scale, its short-term ability to get through the early stage of a disaster is S (50.6), its long-term, multi-year self-reliance ability is B (45.6), and its value as a supplementary food beyond the staple is S (52.5). The fact that the short-term score is 5 points higher than the long-term score sums up the potato's character: it hands you food quickly but does not keep you going for long.

### 1-3. How to Read the Strengths

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The criteria that scored A or higher are the three productivity criteria, plus speed to harvest, infrastructure needs, continuous-cropping tolerance, taste, prevalence in South Korea, and seed saving. What this combination means is clear: if you simply stick seed potatoes into bare, unimproved ground, within three months the potato delivers enough calories and protein together for a person to live on. 2,010kcal per m² is 0.77× the baseline for self-sufficiency in one adult's daily calorie needs. In other words, the math works out so that a plot of just over 130m² — under 40 pyeong (about 132 m²) — can supply most of one person's yearly calories from potatoes alone.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;A grade of A for protein density is one that tuber crops (tubers/storage roots) rarely earn. In the same table, sweet potato and cassava both score E on this criterion. The potato's protein-to-calorie ratio is 12%, above the minimum line of 10% that people need. Its amino acid score also exceeds 90, so it lacks the lysine shortfall common in grains. When meat and beans become scarce in a disaster, this difference shows up in the body within a few months. Eating only sweet potatoes fills the stomach but wastes away muscle; the potato does not do this.

> **The biggest advantage of making the potato a staple is not the calories, but the protein that comes along with them.**

### 1-4. How to Read the Weaknesses

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Three criteria received an E: yield retention without synthetic inputs, non-food value, and seed longevity. Of these, the ones that are critical for disaster preparedness are the first and the third.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The 17.6% yield retention without synthetic inputs is a measured figure from a 40-year long-term comparison trial in Switzerland. In the plot given no fertilizer at all, potato yield was about 1/6 the level of the conventionally farmed plot. That figure reflects not just the lack of fertilizer, but also losses from unchecked late blight. In other words, if you plant potatoes once supply lines are cut, you should expect a field that normally yields 300kg to produce only around 50kg. This is why sections 5 and 9 of this document are especially long: how far potassium and phosphorus can be topped up with wood ash and well-rotted compost, and how much Bordeaux mixture and cultural controls can slow the progress of late blight, are what decide whether a potato crop succeeds or fails.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Seed longevity's E grade is a different kind of problem. The potato cannot be stored as dry seed. It has to be carried through to the following spring as a living tuber, and if it freezes, rots, or fully sprouts in the meantime, there is no crop the next year. What's more, saving your own seed year after year lets aphid-borne viruses accumulate, and within 3-4 years the yield drops noticeably. In normal times the state supplies virus-free seed potatoes and papers over this problem, but once that supply is cut, it has to be faced head-on. The apical selection and highland seed production covered in section 13 are the response to this.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Pest-control self-reliance's D grade should also be considered. There is no cure for late blight. In a field where it has already spread, about all you can do is cut off the foliage to stop the pathogen from moving down into the tubers. That is why all disease control in potato farming is preventive: resistant varieties, wide plant spacing, high ridges, early sowing, and burning diseased residue. Apart from these five measures, there is practically nothing else that can be done without chemicals.

## 2. What Kind of Plant Is This?

### 2-1. Taxonomy and Origin

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The potato is a perennial herb of the nightshade family (Solanaceae), genus Solanum, but it is grown as an annual. It belongs to the same family as tomato, eggplant, chili pepper, and tobacco. This is not a piece of trivia but a warning: they share late blight, bacterial wilt, tobacco mosaic virus, and the 28-spotted ladybird beetle. Planting them side by side or in succession in the same field lets them build up each other's diseases.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Its native range is the Andean highlands around Lake Titicaca, spanning Peru and Bolivia. Conditions there are marked by a low annual average temperature, a wide gap between day and night temperatures, acidic soil, and rainfall concentrated in a particular season. This is where the reasons come from for why the potato prefers acidic soil at pH 5.0-6.0, forms tubers well at cool night temperatures, and is extremely vulnerable to waterlogging.

### 2-2. Appearance

| Organ | Characteristics |
|---|---|
| Leaves | Odd-pinnate compound leaves, made up of 5-7 leaflets, with fine hairs on the surface |
| Stem | Upright or slightly vining. 30-100cm tall, branching |
| Roots | Fibrous roots that spread shallowly. This makes them quick to react to drought |
| Flowers | White, pale pink, purple, etc. Bloom in May-June; both self- and cross-pollination are possible |
| Fruit | A berry resembling an unripe cherry tomato. It stays green even when ripe and is highly toxic, so it cannot be eaten |
| Tuber | A storage organ formed by the swelling of the tip of an underground stem (stolon). It is a stem, not a root |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The fact that the edible part is a stem, not a root, matters in practice. Because it is a stem, it has eyes (buds); and because it is a stem, exposure to light makes it produce chlorophyll and turn green, and toxic alkaloids increase along with that green color. This is both why hilling must always be done and why storage must be kept completely dark.

### 2-3. Growth Stages

| Stage | Timing | Key Physiology | Key Nutrients |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sprouting | From sowing to emergence | Germinates using only the seed potato's stored nutrients | Self-nourished |
| Vegetative growth | Up to about 30 days after emergence | Expands leaves, stems, and roots | Nitrogen, phosphorus |
| Tuber initiation | Around flowering | Tips of underground stems begin to swell | Phosphorus, potassium |
| Tuber bulking | From initiation until maturity | Tubers enlarge and starch accumulates | Peak potassium demand |
| Maturity | When the stems turn yellow and wither | The skin sets (skin-set) | No excess nitrogen |

> **Nitrogen early, potassium late. Excess nitrogen late in the season only makes the foliage lush, while ruining both tuber formation and storability.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Day length and night temperature determine tuber initiation. If night temperatures are high, the plant keeps growing leaves and stems instead of forming tubers. This is the fundamental reason potatoes fail on lowland plains in midsummer. It is also why highland summer cultivation exists — because nights there are cool.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The potato's vitamin C content itself falls short of citrus fruit, but because it is enclosed in starch, it survives cooking relatively well. Eating 500g of potatoes a day fills nearly half of the recommended vitamin C intake. This is precisely what wards off scurvy in winter, when fresh vegetables run out. Boiling potatoes with the skin on reduces the loss.
