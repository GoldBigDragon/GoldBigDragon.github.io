## 7. Water Management

### 7-1. Standards by Growth Stage

| Timing | Water Management | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| From sowing to emergence | Only enough that the soil never fully dries out | This is when the seed potato survives on its stored nutrients, so too much water actually causes rot |
| From emergence to before flowering | Maintain adequate moisture, avoid overwatering | It is better to keep it slightly on the dry side, so roots reach deep in search of water |
| Tuber bulking | The most important stage. Water generously | A month of drought during this period cuts the yield in half |
| From maturity to harvest | Stop watering 10 days to 2 weeks before harvest | The skin sets and storability improves |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The potato's total water requirement is 500-700mm over a 120-150-day season. This looks like enough given South Korea's annual rainfall, but the problem is distribution. In spring cultivation, the bulking period from late May to early June often overlaps with the dry spell right before the monsoon season (jangma, late June to July in South Korea). If you have to haul water, concentrate on this period. Water given at other times is mostly wasted effort.

### 7-2. Symptoms of Water Shortage and Excess

| Type | Symptom | Result | Response |
|---|---|---|---|
| Shortage | Leaves droop at midday and recover by evening | A warning sign. Still recoverable | Run water through the furrows between ridges |
| Severe shortage | Leaves do not recover even by evening, and lower leaves wither | Bulking stops | Water immediately, but not too much at once |
| Watering after a shortage | Tubers crack; knobs form from secondary growth | Marketability and storability decline | Avoid sudden change and water in divided portions |
| Excess | Lower leaves turn yellow and roots turn black | Roots die from oxygen deprivation | Dig drainage channels immediately |
| Flooding | Base of the stem turns mushy with a foul smell | Soft rot. Not recoverable | Pull up the affected plant and remove it from the field |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Without irrigation equipment, straw mulch is effectively your irrigation. A 5cm-thick layer of straw mulch cuts topsoil evaporation by more than half, moderates the daily swing in soil temperature, and also stops raindrops from splashing soil, and pathogens, onto the leaves. It is the cheapest, most effective option when plastic film is unavailable.

## 8. Crop Management

### 8-1. Mulching

| Type | Effect | Suited Timing | In a Disaster |
|---|---|---|---|
| Clear plastic film | Raises soil temperature 3-5 degrees, speeding early growth | Spring cultivation, winter cultivation | Does not suppress weeds |
| Black plastic film | Suppresses weeds and retains warmth | Summer cultivation, autumn cultivation | Needs a substitute if plastic is unavailable |
| Straw/hay | Retains moisture, stabilizes soil temperature, suppresses weeds, improves soil | All cultivation types | First choice as a plastic substitute. 5cm thick or more |
| Fallen leaves | Retains moisture, supplies organic matter | All cultivation types | Blows away in wind, so weigh down with a little soil |
| Rice hulls | Retains moisture, maintains aeration | All cultivation types | Temporarily ties up nitrogen, so use together with compost |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Where plastic film is unavailable, straw mulch even has some advantages over film. Soil temperature does not rise excessively, which is favorable during summer bulking, and as it rots it becomes organic matter that improves the soil the following year. However, it does not raise soil temperature the way film does for early spring cultivation, so it is better not to cover the ground until after emergence, then cover it after.

### 8-2. Hilling - A Task You Cannot Skip

| Round | Timing | Method | Purpose |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1st | When stems reach 10-20cm | Rake soil from between the ridges and mound 5-10cm at the base of the plants | Creates space for tubers to form and buries weeds |
| 2nd | 2-3 weeks after the 1st, early in tuber bulking | Mound another 5-10cm | Blocks light from tubers that have risen to the surface |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Hilling has three effects. First, it keeps light off the tubers, preventing greening and solanine formation. Second, as more of the underground stem gets covered with soil, the number of tubers itself increases. Third, it buries the base of the stems in soil, preventing them from being knocked over by wind and rain. There is one more effect: the thickness of the soil blocks the path by which late blight spores ride rainwater down into the ground to infect the tubers.

> **Hilling costs nothing at all, yet it is the one task that raises yield, safety, and disease resistance all at once. Do not skip it under any circumstances.**

### 8-3. Other Management Tasks

| Task | Necessity | Details |
|---|---|---|
| Pinching | Generally unnecessary | Remove the growing tip only when growth is excessively leggy |
| Removing flowers | Optional | Some believe it saves nutrients that would go to flowers and fruit, but the measured difference is small |
| Thinning side shoots | Optional | If more than 5 stems come up from one seed potato, pull all but 3-4 to increase size |
| Staking | Unnecessary | Hilling is enough |
| Weeding | Essential | The month after emergence is the most vulnerable period for weed competition |

## 9. Pests and Diseases

### 9-1. Major Diseases

| Disease | Type | Symptoms | Favorable Conditions | Prevention | Response After Onset | Caution |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Late blight | Oomycete | Water-soaked dark brown spots on leaves, white mold on leaf undersides, brown rot of tubers | Temperature 15-20°C with humidity 90% or more for two days or longer | Resistant varieties, wide spacing, high ridges, early sowing | Cut and burn all foliage, harvest 2 weeks later | No cure. Can lose the whole field |
| Scab | Actinomycete | Corky scabs on the tuber surface | Alkaline soil, dry conditions during bulking | Keep pH 5.0-5.5, maintain moisture during bulking | Cannot be treated. Peel thickly and eat | No lime or immature compost |
| Soft rot | Bacterial | Tubers turn mushy with a strong foul smell | Excess moisture, wounds | Improve drainage, harvest without wounding | Isolate and discard infected tubers immediately | Spreads very fast in storage |
| Blackleg | Bacterial | Stem base rots black and the plant wilts | Excess moisture, infected seed potatoes | Healthy seed potatoes, drainage | Remove affected plants roots and all | Spreads through seed potatoes |
| Black scurf | Fungal | Black sclerotia on the tuber surface, cankers on stems | Cold, damp soil temperature | Sow after soil temperature rises | Seed treatment, crop rotation | Occurs as a trade-off for early sowing |
| Bacterial wilt | Bacterial | Wilts suddenly at midday, vascular tissue browns | High temperature, soilborne | Resistant varieties, crop rotation | Cannot be treated. Remove affected plants | Persists in soil for years |
| Viral disease | Viral | Mosaic patterns, leaf curling, stunted plants | Heavy aphid activity | Virus-free seed potatoes, aphid control | Cannot be treated. Remove affected plants | The main cause of degeneration from saving your own seed |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Late blight is more dangerous than every other problem in potato farming combined. Its progression is overwhelmingly fast. From the first spot appearing on a leaf to the whole field turning black and withered, a week of cool, rainy weather is enough. Without chemicals, there are only three responses.

| Timing | Action |
|---|---|
| Before sowing | Secure resistant varieties; sow early so the monsoon does not line up with late growth |
| During growth | Widen plant spacing so leaves dry quickly, use high ridges, block soil splash with straw mulch |
| The moment the first spot is found | Cut all foliage with a sickle, remove it from the field and burn it, harvest only the tubers 2 weeks later |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The last action is the key. Cutting off the foliage reduces that year's yield, but it prevents the pathogen from riding rainwater down into the soil and rotting the tubers too. If infected tubers go into storage, the entire stored stock collapses over the winter. Weighing the loss in the field against the loss in storage, the answer is clear.

### 9-2. Major Pests

| Pest | Damage | Control Without Chemicals |
|---|---|---|
| Aphids | Suck sap and spread viruses | Spray soapy water, protect ladybirds and lacewings, repel with foil or light-colored mulch, attract with nasturtium |
| Potato tuber moth | Tunnels into leaves and tubers, and keeps damaging them in storage | Block tuber exposure with hilling, leave no potatoes in the field after harvest, seal the storage area |
| Grubs (wireworm larvae) | Bore holes in tubers | Bury potato pieces in the ground to trap and remove them, rotate crops, avoid immature compost |
| 28-spotted ladybird beetle | Skeletonizes leaves into a lace-like pattern | Hand-pick adults and larvae. Do not plant nightshades nearby |
| Nematodes | Cause root galls and stunt the plant | Resistant varieties, 3 years or more of crop rotation, grow marigold and till it under |
| Field mice | Eat tubers both in storage and in the field | Clear weeds around the field, line the storage floor with wire mesh |

### 9-3. Natural Control Agents

| Agent | Preparation | Use | Caution |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bordeaux mixture | Dissolve 1kg copper sulfate in 80L water, dissolve 1kg quicklime in 20L water, then pour the copper solution into the lime solution and mix | Prevents late blight and bacterial diseases | A preventive agent. No effect after infection. No metal containers |
| Soapy water | Dissolve 20g unscented soap in 1L water | Aphids, mites | Spraying in midday heat scorches the leaves |
| Wood ash | Sift dry ash and dust it on dew-covered leaves | Repels pests, supplements potassium | Washes off in the rain |
| Wood vinegar | Dilute 50-100x | Repels pests, assists disinfection | Undiluted solution causes phytotoxicity |
| Diluted milk | Mix milk and water 1 to 9 | Powdery mildew | Spray on a clear morning |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Bordeaux mixture is the only late-blight countermeasure that can be made without commercial chemicals. But it does not work if copper sulfate and quicklime cannot be obtained. In that case, all that is left is cultural control. The longer a disaster drags on, the more weight falls on the skill of avoiding disease through variety choice, layout, and timing.

### 9-4. Five Principles of Chemical-Free Control

| Principle | Details |
|---|---|
| Crop rotation | Keep potato, tomato, eggplant, and chili pepper 3-4 years apart in the same spot |
| Sanitation | Do not leave diseased leaves, stems, or tubers in the field; burn them or bury them deep |
| Airflow and drainage | Wide spacing and high ridges suppress moisture-related disease |
| Securing resistance | Choose varieties matched to the diseases prevalent in your area |
| Healthy seed | Use only disease-free seed potatoes. Half the outcome is already decided here |

## 10. Nutrient Deficiency and Excess

| Symptom | Cause | How to Check | Remedy with Natural Materials |
|---|---|---|---|
| Whole plant yellows starting from the lower leaves, plant is small | Nitrogen deficiency | If it progresses from the older leaves, it is a mobile-element deficiency | Add well-rotted compost, pour urine diluted 10x between the ridges |
| Leaf edges scorch brown | Potassium deficiency | Starts from the leaf tip and edges | A little wood ash in the furrows, compost with fruit peels and grass mixed in |
| Slow growth, leaves turn dark green to purplish | Phosphorus deficiency | Common in cold periods | Bone meal, wood ash; eases naturally as soil warms |
| Growing point of new leaves dries and dies | Calcium deficiency | If it progresses from young leaves, it is an immobile-element deficiency | Burn eggshells, crush them finely, and scatter them |
| Veins stay green while the tissue between them yellows | Magnesium deficiency | Starts from the lower leaves | Dolomite, diversify compost materials |
| Whole new leaves are yellow, even the veins pale | Iron deficiency | Appears when pH is high | Add more organic matter, stop using wood ash |
| Foliage is lush but tubers are small | Nitrogen excess | Leaves are dark green and thick-stemmed | Stop nitrogen, supplement potassium with wood ash |
| Black spots inside the tuber | Calcium shortage and heat stress | Cutting it open reveals brown blotches | Keep moisture steady during bulking |
| Hollow, cracked interior of the tuber | Rapid bulking, uneven moisture | Common in large potatoes | Split up watering and increase planting density |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Judging deficiency symptoms becomes easier if you remember one principle. If symptoms appear starting from the older, lower leaves, a mobile element within the plant — such as nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, or magnesium — is deficient, because the plant has shifted its limited nutrients to the new leaves. Conversely, if symptoms appear starting from the new, upper leaves, an immobile element — such as calcium, boron, or iron, which does not move once it settles in place — is deficient.

## 11. Intercropping and Crop Rotation

### 11-1. Compatibility

| Compatibility | Companion | Effect |
|:---:|---|---|
| Good | Kidney bean, pea, and other legumes | Nitrogen fixation by root-nodule bacteria adds nutrients for the potato |
| Good | Marigold | Root exudates suppress root-knot nematodes |
| Good | Nasturtium | Draws aphids away from the potato |
| Good | Garlic, onion | Their smell repels some pests |
| Neutral | Cabbage family, spinach, lettuce | Covers the ground and reduces weeds. Competition is not significant |
| Caution | Maize | Can provide shade, but competes heavily for nutrients |
| Bad | Tomato, eggplant, chili pepper | Same nightshade family. Shares late blight and pests |
| Bad | Squash, cucumber | Compete for space and nutrients |
| Bad | Sunflower | Competes very heavily for nutrients |

### 11-2. Crop Rotation Sequence

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The potato is classified by the Rural Development Administration (RDA) of South Korea as a 2-year break-group crop, but given that late blight and scab persist in the soil, it is safer to leave 3-4 years. An example of a 4-year rotation is as follows.

| Year | Crop | Effect on Soil |
|---|---|---|
| Year 1 | Potato | Consumes a lot of nutrients and disturbs the soil deeply |
| Year 2 | Legumes (soybean, kidney bean, cowpea) | Fixes nitrogen and restores soil fertility |
| Year 3 | Leafy vegetables or the onion family | Shallow roots draw on nutrients from a different soil layer |
| Year 4 | Grains (foxtail millet, sorghum, buckwheat) or root vegetables | Cleans up leftover nutrients and suppresses weeds |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;If the land is too small for rotation, there is a second-best option. Divide the field into four sections and move the potato planting one section over each year. The total area stays the same, but each section only receives potatoes once every 4 years. Adding a winter green manure on top of this speeds up soil recovery. Sowing rye or hairy vetch in autumn and tilling it under in spring gives you both organic matter and nitrogen at once.

> **If you keep planting potatoes in the same field, nothing shows in the first year, and it collapses in the third. Soilborne disease appears suddenly the moment its density crosses a threshold.**
