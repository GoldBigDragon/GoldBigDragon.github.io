## 3. Growing Conditions

### 3-1. Limits and Optimal Values

| Condition | Optimal | Limit | What Happens Beyond It |
|---|---|---|---|
| Growing temperature | 14-23°C | 5°C or below, 30°C or above | Growth stops below 5°C; foliage dies at 0°C |
| Tuber bulking temperature | 14-18°C | 27-30°C or above | Bulking stops completely and only the leaves keep growing |
| Night temperature | 12-18°C | Sustained 20°C or above | Tuber initiation does not begin at all |
| Germination temperature | 15-20°C | Begins at 5°C or above | Low soil temperature increases black scurf infection |
| Light | 6 hours or more a day | Less than 4 hours | Plants grow leggy and tuber bulking suffers |
| Shade tolerance | Relative yield about 80% at 40% shade | 60% shade or more | Yield falls below half |
| Rainfall | 400-800mm over the whole season | 24 hours of flooding | Roots and tubers rot from oxygen deprivation |
| Soil | Well-drained sandy loam to loam | Clay soil, poorly drained ground | Tubers become misshapen and rot increases |
| Topsoil depth | 25cm or more | Less than 15cm | Tubers get pushed up to the surface and turn green |
| Soil pH | pH 5.0-6.0 | pH 6.5 or above | Scab increases sharply |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Unlike most other crops, which prefer near-neutral soil, the potato does better when the soil is deliberately kept acidic. That is because the actinomycetes that cause scab become active in alkaline conditions. This is why lime is not applied to potato fields. Wood ash is also strongly alkaline, so using too much causes the same problem. When using wood ash on a potato field, it is safe to keep it to about one doe (roughly 1.8 L) per 10m² or less.

> **Adding lime to a potato field is as good as inviting scab in. Keep the pH from exceeding 6.5.**

### 3-2. Choosing a Site in a Disaster

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Without electricity or irrigation equipment, half the battle is decided at the stage of choosing the field. Prioritize in the following order.

| Priority | Condition | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | A sloped site with good drainage, or high ground | A single flood can lose the whole field. Drainage cannot be fixed later |
| 2 | East- or southeast-facing, where morning sun arrives first | Dew that forms overnight dries quickly, breaking the germination conditions late blight spores need |
| 3 | A spot with good airflow | Shortens the time it takes for leaf surfaces to dry |
| 4 | Ground where no nightshade crop has been planted in the last 3-4 years | Lower density of soilborne disease |
| 5 | Within reach for hauling water | A month of drought during tuber bulking determines the yield |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;It is also worth remembering that mountain highlands are advantageous. In cool, poor ground, the potato actually fills with starch better and tastes better. The very conditions that make summer potatoes fail on the plains become the optimal conditions at high elevation.

## 4. Varieties

### 4-1. Two Types by Texture

| Type | Characteristics | Representative Varieties | Suited Uses | Meaning in a Disaster |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Mealy | High in starch, crumbles into a fluffy texture | Namjak, Dubaek, Haryeong, Russet | Boiling, steaming, mashing, starch extraction | High starch yield, good for stockpiling flour and starch |
| Waxy | Low in sugar and starch, holds together without falling apart | Daeseo, Daeji | Braising, stir-frying, soups | Holds its shape even after long boiling, good for soup dishes |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The Sumi potato has intermediate properties between mealy and waxy, so it suits any use. This is why it accounts for most of the cultivated area in South Korea, and it is also the variety most easily found when you have no choice over which one to plant.

### 4-2. Comparison of Major Varieties

| Variety | Maturity | Yield | Storability | Difficulty | Suited Environment | Texture | Notes |
|---|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---:|---|---|---|
| Sumi | Early, 90-100 days | Medium-high | Medium | Easy | Temperate, spring/autumn | Medium | South Korea's main variety. Fast harvest makes it good for securing food early in a disaster |
| Daeji | Mid-late, 110-120 days | High | High | Moderate | Temperate, autumn | Waxy | Good storability makes it ideal paired with winter storage after an autumn harvest |
| Dubaek | Late, 120 days or more | High | High | Moderate | Temperate | Mealy | Requires 20% or more additional fertilizer. Unfavorable when fertilizer is scarce |
| Kennebec | Mid-late | High | High | Easy | Humid, cool areas | Mealy | Resistant to late blight, giving high survival in rainy years |
| Russet | Late | High | High | Moderate | Cool, dry | Mealy | High starch content, good for starch extraction and dry storage |
| Hongyeong / Jayeong | Mid-late | Medium | Medium | Moderate | Temperate | Waxy | Rich in anthocyanins. Useful for adding nutritional variety |
| Jopung | Very early | Medium | Medium | Easy | Temperate | Medium | Suited to early mulch covering and summer cultivation, relatively resistant to late blight |
| Chubaek | Very early, 80-90 days | Medium | Low | Easy | Temperate | Mealy | Gives you food the fastest of all. You have to give up on storing it |

### 4-3. Selection Criteria by Situation

| Situation | Choice | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| You need food right away | Chubaek, Jopung, Sumi | Can be harvested in 80-95 days |
| You need to get through winter | Daeji, Russet | Best storability, lasts until the following spring |
| Rainy, cool region | Kennebec, Jopung | Late blight resistance makes a real difference |
| Almost no fertilizer | Sumi, Jopung | Relatively low fertilizer demand. Avoid Dubaek |
| Stockpiling as starch or flour | Russet, Dubaek, Namjak | Mealy, so starch yield is high |
| High mountains, cool areas | Russet, Andean landraces | Adapted to cool night temperatures |
| Lowlands where summer exceeds 30°C | Sweet potato instead of potato | Potatoes do not form tubers in high heat |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Planting only one variety in a disaster is risky. A year when late blight strikes and a year of drought affect different varieties differently. Mixing early-maturing and mid-to-late varieties, and securing at least one blight-resistant variety where possible, is how you avoid losing the whole field.

## 5. Field Preparation and Fertilizer

### 5-1. Preparing the Ground

| Item | Standard | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Tillage depth | 20-30cm | Secures space for tubers to grow |
| Ridge height | 20-30cm | Since the crop is extremely vulnerable to flooding, drainage comes first |
| Drainage channel | Depth 20cm or more | Prepare a water channel ahead of the monsoon season (jangma, late June to July in South Korea) |
| Soil clods | Break up to fist-size or smaller | Tubers become misshapen in the gaps between large clods |
| Soil pH | Keep at pH 5.0-5.5 | Suppresses scab. Do not apply lime |

### 5-2. Standard Fertilization Rate (per 1,000m²)

| Fertilizer | Amount | Split |
|---|---|---|
| Nitrogen | 10-13.7kg | 50% base, 50% top-dress |
| Phosphorus | 8.8kg | All as base fertilizer |
| Potassium | 13kg | All as base fertilizer |
| Compost | 1,500-2,000kg | Must be fully matured compost |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Converted per m², nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium together come to about 32g. This figure is the basis for the C grade the fertilizer-demand criterion received on the evaluation table. The potato is a crop that eats a lot in a short time.

### 5-3. Base Fertilizer and Top-Dressing

| Type | Timing | Contents | Caution |
|---|---|---|---|
| Base fertilizer | 1-2 weeks before sowing | All the compost, all the phosphorus, all the potassium, half the nitrogen | Keep fertilizer from touching the seed potato directly |
| Top-dressing | Around 20cm plant height, together with the 1st hilling | The remaining half of the nitrogen | Do not apply after flowering |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Using immature compost releases heat and gas during decomposition that rots the seed potato and attracts grubs and root maggots. Compost must always be in a state where it crumbles apart in your hand and smells like soil.

### 5-4. When There Is No Chemical Fertilizer - Disaster Substitute Fertilizers

| Substitute | Nutrients Supplied | How to Make and Use | Caution |
|---|---|---|---|
| Wood ash | Potassium, phosphorus, calcium | Sift fully burned white ash and spread 1-2L evenly per 10m² over the field, then work it in | Strongly alkaline. Excess raises pH and causes scab to surge |
| Well-rotted compost | Nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, trace elements | Pile fallen leaves, grass, human waste, and livestock manure for 6 months or more, turning it at least twice | Immature compost causes harm |
| Diluted urine | Nitrogen, potassium | Dilute at least 10x with water and pour between the ridges. Letting it age 2 weeks or more after collection stabilizes the smell and urea breakdown | Dries out leaves it touches directly. Stop 3 weeks before harvest |
| Bone meal | Phosphorus, calcium | Burn bones, crush them, then mix into compost or apply as base fertilizer | Breaks down slowly, so it is only effective as a base fertilizer |
| Sesame/soybean oil cake | Nitrogen, phosphorus | Soak in water to ferment, then dilute 10x and use as top-dressing | Causes gas damage before fermentation |
| Legume green manure | Nitrogen | Plant hairy vetch or Chinese milk vetch the previous year and till it under in spring | Sow 2-3 weeks after tilling it under |
| Seaweed | Potassium, trace elements | Rinse in fresh water to remove salt, then use as mulch or mix into compost | Leftover salt causes harm |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The potato demands potassium more than anything else. Wood ash is the easiest potassium source to get, but it comes with the dilemma of being alkaline. There are two solutions. One is to mix it into the compost pile to rot together, instead of spreading it directly on the field — mixing with organic matter buffers the alkalinity. The other is to spread it only in the furrows between ridges, avoiding the spot where the seed potato sits.

> **Too much nitrogen only makes the leaves lush while the tubers stay small. In a situation where fertilizer is scarce, the potato actually makes it easy to avoid this mistake.**

## 6. Sowing and Seed Potato Preparation

### 6-1. Timing by Cultivation Type

| Type | Region | Sowing | Harvest | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Spring cultivation | Plains across South Korea | Early Feb-late Mar | Late May-late Jun | Most common. Harvesting before the monsoon season (jangma, late June to July in South Korea) is the key |
| Summer cultivation | Highlands | Mid Apr-mid May | Mid Aug-late Sep | Cool nights favor good tuber bulking |
| Autumn cultivation | Jeju Island, the southern coast of South Korea | Mid-late Aug | Early-late Nov | Moving straight into winter storage after harvest is an advantage |
| Winter cultivation | Southern South Korea, Jeju Island | Late Nov-mid Jan | Mid Apr-mid May | Frost damage management is needed |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The key to spring cultivation is to plant as early as possible right after the ground thaws, finishing the harvest before the monsoon arrives. Late blight explodes under frequent rain and cool conditions, so moving the schedule earlier so that the monsoon does not overlap with the late growth stage is, in itself, the strongest control measure. If frost damage is a concern, sow a little deeper, and after emergence, cover with soil or straw whenever frost is forecast.

### 6-2. Seed Potato Preparation - 20-35 Days Before Sowing

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Seed potato preparation is the stage where potato farming fails most often. The sequence is as follows.

| Step | Details | Standard |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Selection | Exclude any with disease spots, wounds, or soft spots | Exclude under 30g and 250g or more |
| 2. Cutting | Cut into 2-4 pieces so each has 1-2 eyes | Piece weight 30-50g, cut lengthwise |
| 3. Treating the cut surface | Dust with wood ash or dry it in the shade for 2-3 days to heal | Planting it while still damp causes rot |
| 4. Sprouting | 15-20°C, 80-90% humidity, diffuse light, 25-35 days | Induces thick, short, green sprouts |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The reason for cutting lengthwise is that the potato's eyes are concentrated at the apical end, opposite the heel. Cutting crosswise leaves one piece without any eyes, and it simply rots. Splitting lengthwise, working from the heel (the indented spot that was attached to the mother plant) toward the opposite end, distributes the eyes evenly across all the pieces.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Sprouting, that is, chitting, must be done in diffuse light. Sprouting in a dark place produces pale, long, weak sprouts that break during planting. Sprouting in bright shade, in scattered light rather than direct sun, produces short, thick, purplish sprouts that are sturdy. This difference governs the speed of emergence and early growth.

### 6-3. Seed Disinfection - Without Chemicals

| Method | Procedure | Effect | Caution |
|---|---|---|---|
| Wood ash treatment | Coat the cut surface evenly with dry wood ash | Speeds up drying of the cut surface and suppresses surface rot organisms | The most practical method |
| Shade curing | Leave in cool shade for 2-3 days after cutting | A cork layer forms and blocks pathogen entry | Placing it in direct sun dries it out |
| Hot-water treatment | Soak in 50-55°C water for 10-15 minutes | Kills surface pathogens | If the temperature exceeds 60°C, the eyes die |
| Wood vinegar soak | Soak for 10 minutes in a 50-100x diluted solution | Assists in surface disinfection | Undiluted solution damages the tissue |

> **More important than any disinfectant is choosing healthy seed potatoes in the first place. No treatment can revive a diseased potato.**

### 6-4. Planting

| Item | Standard | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Planting depth | 5-10cm | Too shallow causes greening, too deep delays emergence |
| Single-row planting | Row spacing 70-80cm, plant spacing 20-25cm | Most common |
| Double-row planting | Ridge 90-140cm, row spacing 30-40cm, plant spacing 20-25cm | Used when working a wide ridge |
| Planting orientation | Sprouts facing up, cut surface facing down | Applies to pre-sprouted seed potatoes |
| Amount needed per 1,000m² | About 150-200kg | Based on a piece weight of 30-50g |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Change planting density according to your goal. Plant widely spaced if you want large, thick potatoes; plant closely spaced if you want many small, uniform potatoes for seed. If you need to be self-sufficient in seed the following year in a disaster, it works well to plant one side of the field at close spacing as a dedicated seed potato section.
