## 3. Growing Conditions

### 3-1. Limits and Optimum Values

| Item | Limit | Optimum | When outside the range |
|---|---|---|---|
| Growing temperature | 5-28°C | 15-20°C | The range is wide, at 23°C. Prefers a cool climate |
| High-temperature limit | 25°C and above | - | Growth slows and sugar does not accumulate |
| Low-temperature limit | 5°C below zero | - | Below this the roots freeze and turn soft |
| Germination temperature | 5°C and above | 15-20°C | If lower, germination is late and uneven |
| Light | 6 hours or more a day | 8 hours or more a day | In shade, sugar content drops noticeably |
| Soil pH | 6.0-7.5 | 6.5-7.0 | Does poorly in acidic soil. This is the opposite of other tuber crops (tubers/storage roots) |
| Soil | Sandy loam to loam | Deep, soft loam | Many stones and much clay make the roots fork |
| Water | Total ET about 550mm | Ample during root bulking | Excess moisture rots the roots |
| Boron | Needed separately | - | If deficient, the inside of the root rots black |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;What sets sugar beet decisively apart from the other tuber crops (tubers/storage roots) is soil acidity. Potato tolerates pH 5.0-6.0, apios 5.0-6.0 and cassava 4.5-7.5, but sugar beet requires soil near neutral. In acidic soil, growth is visibly poorer and the plant becomes prone to disease. Fields in South Korea generally lean acidic, so correction with lime or wood ash is needed. If lime cannot be obtained in a disaster, wood ash is the alternative, and in that case it is actually more efficient, because it supplies potassium and corrects the pH in one step.

### 3-2. Cropping Types - The Key to This Crop

| Cropping type | Sowing | Harvest | Suitable region | Characteristics |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Autumn sowing, overwintered | Mid-September to early October | April-May of the following year | Southern South Korea, Jeju Island | Both sugar content and yield are at their highest |
| Spring sowing | Mid-March to early April | July-August | Central and northern South Korea | Must be dug before the heat arrives |
| Avoiding summer | - | - | All regions | The hot season must be avoided |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The clear advantage of overwintering is backed by actual trial results. In the same field, roots dug in December gave 5.8 tons per 1,000m²; left in the ground and dug the following April, the yield was 7.8t, an increase of 34%. Sugar content rose to 20.8 Brix. There are two reasons. During winter growth nearly stops, but photosynthesis continues on clear days and sugar builds up, and the cold itself is a signal that makes the plant accumulate sugar. Raising the sugar concentration inside its cells to avoid freezing is the plant's strategy for getting through the winter.

| Condition | Assessment |
|---|---|
| Deep, stone-free field | Best. The taproot grows straight |
| Cool, sunny site | Good. Sugar content rises |
| Near-neutral soil | Good. If acidic, correct it beforehand |
| Low ground where water collects | Avoid. Root rot sets in |
| Stony ground | Avoid. Roots fork and become misshapen |
| Where Swiss chard or beetroot was grown | Avoid. Leave a 3-year interval |
| Places with severe summer heat | Adjust the sowing time |

## 4. Varieties

### 4-1. Main Varieties

| Variety | Characteristics | Disaster suitability |
|---|---|:---:|
| Tener | A variety bred in South Korea. Recorded 20.8 Brix in overwintered cultivation on Jeju Island | High |
| Suez | A variety bred in South Korea that adapts well to the Chungju area | High |
| Tangeum sugar beet | A variety native to the Chungju area | High |
| Fodder lines | Low in sugar, but with large roots and a high yield | Moderate. Advantageous for securing calories |

### 4-2. Selection Criteria by Situation

| Situation | Choice | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| When the goal is sugar production | High-sugar, white-rooted lines | Without red pigment, the syrup stays clear |
| When the goal is securing calories | Large-rooted lines | Even with lower sugar content, the total amount is large |
| Southern South Korea and Jeju Island | Overwintering-adapted lines such as Tener | Sugar content peaks only after the plant has come through the winter |
| Central and northern South Korea | Early-maturing lines | Must be dug before the heat arrives |
| When livestock are kept as well | Fodder lines | Both roots and leaves are used as feed |
| When the seed will be used for many years | Fixed (non-hybrid) varieties | Hybrids segregate in their traits even if you save seed from them |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The last row is the important one. A good share of the sugar beet seed sold commercially is hybrid, and seed taken from a hybrid scatters in its traits in the next generation, so sugar content and yield fall sharply. To make use of the advantage of a 5-year seed longevity, it is better to obtain fixed varieties from the start. If they are hard to tell apart, the only way is to actually save seed from a few plants, sow it, and check whether the offspring are uniform.

## 5. Field Preparation and Fertilizer

### 5-1. Preparing the Soil

| Item | Standard | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Tilling | 20-30cm deep, deeper if possible | Opens a path for the taproot to grow straight |
| Removing stones | Always do it | A root that touches a stone forks |
| Raised bed height | 15-20cm | Prevents excess moisture |
| Drainage ditch | Provide one | The only way to prevent root rot |
| Soil acidity | Correct to pH 6.0-7.0 | If acidic, add lime or wood ash |
| Organic matter | Well-rotted compost, 1,000-1,500kg/1,000m² | Half-rotted compost makes roots fork |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Deep tilling matters especially for sugar beet. The taproot must run straight down to become a large, spindle-shaped root, and if there is a hard layer below 30cm, the root forks there or bends sideways. A forked root yields less, and soil lodges in the fork, making it hard to wash. Where there are no machines, double digging, turning the soil over to the depth of two spades, is effective, and this labor is also why sugar beet's grade for Tillage & digging energy stays at B.

### 5-2. Standard Fertilizer Rates (per 1,000m²)

| Fertilizer | Rate | Timing |
|---|:---:|---|
| Nitrogen (N) | 10-15kg | 50% as base fertilizer, 50% as top-dressing |
| Phosphate (P) | 8-10kg | All as base fertilizer |
| Potassium (K) | 10-15kg | Split between base fertilizer and top-dressing |
| Compost | 1,000-1,500kg | Well-rotted compost, all as base fertilizer |
| Boron | Small amount | Only in deficient areas. Too much is actually harmful |

### 5-3. Base Fertilizer and Top-Dressing

| Type | Timing | Details |
|---|---|---|
| Base fertilizer | 2 weeks before sowing | All the compost, phosphate and potassium, and half the nitrogen |
| Top-dressing | At 5-6 true leaves | The remaining half, mainly nitrogen |
| Prohibited | After the sugar-accumulation stage | Nitrogen applied then makes sugar content fall sharply |

> **Apply nitrogen top-dressing only up to the 5-6 true-leaf stage. Nitrogen applied after that does not raise yield; it wipes out the sugar.**

### 5-4. When There Is No Chemical Fertilizer - Substitute Fertilizers for a Disaster

| Substitute resource | Nutrients supplied | How to make and use it | Caution |
|---|---|---|---|
| Wood ash | Potassium, phosphate, calcium | The best fit for sugar beet. Supplies potassium and corrects pH at the same time | One or two handfuls per m². Too much raises the pH too far |
| Well-rotted compost | All-round nutrients | Apply generously as base fertilizer | Must be fully rotted. If half-rotted, the roots fork |
| Diluted urine | Nitrogen, potassium | Dilute 10 times with water and apply only in early growth | Do not apply after 6 true leaves |
| Bone meal | Phosphate, calcium | Burn, crush and mix into the compost | Acts slowly |
| Seashells and eggshells | Calcium | Burn and crush finely | Used to correct acidity |
| Seaweed | Potassium, trace elements, boron | Dry and crush it, or steep it in water and apply | One of the few routes to boron |
| Sugar beet leaves and processing residue | Potassium, organic matter | Return to the field after harvest | The key to the potassium cycle |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Boron is the trickiest problem in a disaster. It is a trace element needed in only tiny amounts, but without it the inside of the root rots black, and in the other direction too much of it becomes toxic, so the margin is narrow. Where there is no chemical fertilizer, the realistic route to boron is seaweed. Dry and crush wakame, kelp or sea lettuce and mix it into the base fertilizer, or steep it in water and apply it as a soil drench, and along with boron it brings in potassium and other trace elements. Near the sea this method is especially effective. Inland, the second-best option is to use a wide variety of materials in the compost.

## 6. Sowing

### 6-1. Timing

| Cropping type | Sowing time | Harvest time |
|---|---|---|
| Autumn sowing, overwintered | Mid-September to early October | April-May of the following year |
| Spring sowing | Mid-March to early April | July-August |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The timing of autumn sowing must not be missed. If sown too early, the plants grow too large before winter arrives, sense the cold, and send up a flower stalk the following spring. This is called bolting, and in a bolted sugar beet the sugar in the root drains into the flower stalk and the root becomes useless. If sown too late, the plants do not grow enough to get through the winter and freeze to death. The narrow window from mid-September to early October lies between these two risks.

### 6-2. Sowing Method

| Item | Standard | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Sowing depth | 1.5-2.5cm | If too deep, germination is poor |
| Row spacing | 30-40cm | Secures airflow and photosynthesis |
| Plant spacing (final) | 10-15cm | Room for the roots to thicken |
| Seeding rate | 1-1.5kg per 1,000m² | Sow generously, allowing for thinning |
| After sowing | Cover with a thin layer of soil and press lightly so it makes contact | Seed left sitting loose dries out |
| Watering | Keep from drying out until germination | Excess moisture must be avoided |

### 6-3. Seed Treatment

| Method | Procedure | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Soaking in water | Soak for a day, then sow | The shell of the multigerm seed ball softens and germination speeds up |
| Hot-water treatment | Soak in 50-55°C water for 10-15 minutes | Reduces pathogens on the surface |
| Ash treatment | Coat the seed with wood ash | Disinfects and dries at the same time |
| Sorting | Skim off the seeds that float | Removes hollow seeds |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;A common problem with sugar beet is that the shell of the multigerm seed ball is thick and hard, so germination is uneven. Soaking for a day makes germination a few days faster and more uniform. However, seed that has been soaked must be sown right away; drying it again actually lowers its germination ability. If you use hot-water treatment, take care not to exceed 60°C.
