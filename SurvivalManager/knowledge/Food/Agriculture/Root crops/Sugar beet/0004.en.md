## 7. Water Management

### 7-1. Standards by Growth Stage

| Stage | Water management | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Sowing to germination | Keep from drying out | Multigerm seed balls must take up plenty of water for the shell to loosen |
| Early growth | Leave it to natural rainfall and supplement in dry spells | Encourages the roots to go deep |
| Root bulking stage | Water ample, never let it dry out | Drought at this stage lowers both yield and sugar content |
| Last 2-3 weeks before harvest | Stop irrigation | Sugar content rises and storability improves |
| Overwintering period | Do not irrigate | Roots are damaged if they freeze and thaw |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Stopping irrigation just before harvest directly affects sugar content. If watering continues, the roots hold water and gain weight, but the sugar concentration is diluted. Conversely, if water is cut off for 2-3 weeks, moisture falls and the sugar concentration rises. For a crop sold by weight that is a loss, but if the aim is to obtain sugar it is a gain. In a disaster, it is the latter.

### 7-2. Symptoms of Too Little and Too Much Water

| Type | Symptom | Progress | Action |
|---|---|---|---|
| Too little | Leaves droop at midday and recover in the evening | Early | Reinforce the mulch |
| Too little | Lower leaves turn yellow and dry, and growth stops | Middle | Irrigate between the ridges |
| Too little | Roots become thin and hard | Severe | Yield and sugar content fall together |
| Watering after a shortage | Roots split lengthwise | Problem | Moisture supply must be kept even |
| Too much | Lower leaves turn yellow and the top of the root softens | Dangerous | Clear out the drainage ditch at once |
| Too much | Foul smell from the root, brown inside | Root rot | Pull up the plant and dispose of it |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Root splitting is usually caused not by lack of water itself but by abrupt changes in moisture. If a dry spell is followed suddenly by heavy rain, the inner tissue swells quickly, the outer skin cannot keep up, and it bursts. Microbes enter through the crack, so a split root cannot be stored. Reducing swings in soil moisture with mulch is the most effective preventive measure.

## 8. Crop Management

### 8-1. Thinning - A Job You Cannot Skip

| Round | Timing | Task |
|---|---|---|
| 1st | 2-3 true leaves | Thin to a plant spacing of 3-5cm |
| 2nd | 5-6 true leaves | Final thinning to a plant spacing of 10-15cm |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Sugar beet seed is a clump of several seeds joined together, so two or three sprouts come up together at one spot. Left as they are, they push against each other as they grow, and all end up thin and misshapen. Thinning is the most important management task in growing sugar beet, and the later it is done, the more it damages even the roots of the plants you intend to keep. Do not throw away the young plants you thin out; eat them as namul (seasoned vegetable side dish). They taste like Swiss chard.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;When thinning, it is better to cut close to the ground with scissors than to pull. Pulling shakes the roots of neighboring plants as well. The final spacing of 10-15cm looks tight, but if it is widened beyond this, the roots tend to grow too large and the sugar concentration actually falls.

### 8-2. Hilling (Earthing Up)

| Timing | Task | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| 1-2 times during growth | Pile soil up around the top of the root | Keeps the root from being exposed to sunlight |
| After rain | Cover the exposed shoulders | If soil has washed away, cover them again |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;As a sugar beet root grows, its shoulder is pushed up above the ground surface. When that part receives sunlight it turns green, and the green part is low in sugar, tastes bitter, and clouds the syrup during sugar extraction. Hilling is the only way to prevent this, and it is a task that costs nothing at all yet directly affects sugar yield.

### 8-3. Other Care

| Task | Timing | Details |
|---|---|---|
| Weeding | Early growth | Early growth is slow, and the plants are crowded out by weeds |
| Mulching | Once established after sowing | Straw or fallen leaves. Reduces moisture swings and prevents splitting |
| Harvesting leaves | From mid-growth | Pick the lower leaves a few at a time. Overdoing it lowers sugar content |
| Winter protection | November-December | Cover the shoulders with straw or fallen leaves |
| Removing bolted plants | Spring | The root of a plant that has sent up a flower stalk is unusable, so dig it first |

## 9. Pests and Diseases

### 9-1. Main Diseases

| Disease | Symptoms | Conditions | Response without chemicals |
|---|---|---|---|
| Root rot | The root softens and gives off a foul smell | Excess moisture, poor drainage | Improving drainage is the only measure |
| Brown spot disease | Brown spots appear on the leaves and spread | Hot and humid | Secure airflow, remove diseased leaves, rotate for 3 years or more |
| Powdery mildew | The leaf surface is covered with white powder | Dry weather with a large day-night temperature range | Milk diluted 10 times, diluted baking soda solution |
| Root decay disease | The inside of the root rots black | Fields continuously cropped with Amaranthaceae | 3-year break. Do not plant after Swiss chard or beetroot either |
| Heart rot | The inside of the root is black and new leaves become misshapen | Boron deficiency | Not a disease. Make up the boron with seaweed and by diversifying the compost |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The last row is the important one. Heart rot sounds like a disease, but there is no pathogen. It is a pure deficiency symptom, caused when too little boron prevents the tissue from forming properly. Spraying chemicals does nothing; supplying boron is the only remedy. It is common to mistake it for a disease and move to another field or change the seed.

### 9-2. Main Pests

| Pest | Damage | Response without chemicals |
|---|---|---|
| Aphids | Suck sap and spread viruses | Spray soapy water, protect ladybugs and parasitic wasps, avoid excess nitrogen |
| Cyst nematode | Parasitizes the roots and growth stops | No cure. Rotate for several years with non-host crops |
| Root-crop pests | Bore holes in the roots | Resistant lines and early sowing. If harvested before October, damage is about 1% |
| Cutworm | Cuts through the base of young plants | Catch them at night. Ring the base with ash |
| Leaf beetles | Gnaw the leaves | Wash off with water, pick by hand |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The basis for the Pest-control self-reliance grade of A is in the third row of this table. There is empirical evidence that using resistant lines and bringing sowing forward can cut the occurrence of the main pests by more than 90%, and that if harvest is finished before October, damage stays at around 1%. This means that even without chemicals, adjusting the planting time and the digging time alone can solve most of the problem, which is fundamentally different from diseases such as potato late blight, where prevention is the only course.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Cyst nematode, however, is an exception. Once it gets in, it stays in the soil for years and is hard to control even with chemicals. The only measure is not to plant Amaranthaceae crops in that field for several years. This is why you must check the history of the field before planting sugar beet.

### 9-3. Pest-Control Principles Without Chemicals

| Principle | Details |
|---|---|
| Rotation | Leave an interval of 3 years or more between Amaranthaceae crops. Swiss chard and beetroot belong to the same family |
| Drainage | The only way to prevent root rot. Build the raised beds and drainage ditches properly |
| Timing | Avoid the pest season with early sowing and early harvest |
| Healthy seed | Use hot-water treatment and sorting |
| Airflow | Thin so that air moves between the plants |
| Residue disposal | Do not leave diseased leaves and roots in the field; burn them or bury them deep |

## 10. Nutrient Deficiency and Excess

| Symptom | Cause | How to check | Natural-material remedy |
|---|---|---|---|
| Whole leaf turns yellow and the plant is small | Nitrogen deficiency | Starts from the lower leaves | Well-rotted compost, urine diluted 10 times. Only in early growth |
| Leaf edges scorch brown | Potassium deficiency | Common during root bulking | Wood ash |
| Poor growth, leaves deep purple | Phosphate deficiency | Common in cold periods and acidic soil | Bone meal, wood ash |
| New leaves misshapen and the inside of the root rots black | Boron deficiency | Known as heart rot | Dried seaweed, diversified compost |
| Yellowing between the leaf veins | Magnesium deficiency | Starts from the lower leaves | Dolomite, compost |
| Young leaves yellow with only the veins green | Iron deficiency | When soil pH is too high | Use less ash and add more organic matter |
| Lush leaves only, small roots, low sugar content | Nitrogen excess | Leaf color is too dark | Cut off nitrogen and reinforce potassium with wood ash |
| Roots split | Abrupt moisture change, half-rotted compost, stones | The split surfaces are smooth | Even out moisture with mulch |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The thing to guard against most in sugar beet is nitrogen excess. In other crops, lush leaves are no flaw, but in sugar beet they translate directly into lost sugar. As long as the plant judges that it should keep growing, sugar does not move down into the root. In particular, if a fast-acting nitrogen source such as diluted urine is used late in the season, only the leaves grow lush and dark green, and when you dig the roots in autumn the sugar content has dropped by half.

> **For sugar beet, nitrogen fertilizing ends at 5-6 true leaves. Nitrogen after that is not fertilizer but a loss.**

## 11. Intercropping and Crop Rotation

### 11-1. Compatibility

| Compatibility | Partner | Reason |
|:---:|---|---|
| Good | Legumes | They fix nitrogen and leave it for the sugar beet of the next season |
| Good | Onion, garlic | Repel pests, and their root depths differ, so there is little competition |
| Good | Lettuce, spinach | Cover the space between ridges early and suppress weeds |
| Fair | Napa cabbage, radish | There is competition for nutrients |
| Avoid | Swiss chard, beetroot, fodder radish | Same family and same species. They share diseases and cross-pollinate |
| Avoid | Planting in the same spot year after year | Root decay disease and nematodes build up |

### 11-2. Rotation Order

| Order | Crop | Role |
|---|---|---|
| Year 1 | Sugar beet | Takes up a lot of nitrogen and potassium |
| Year 2 | Legumes (soybean, adzuki bean, pea) | Restore nitrogen |
| Year 3 | Leafy vegetables or the onion family | Use the remaining nitrogen. Not Amaranthaceae |
| Year 4 | Sugar beet again | Keep to an interval of 3 years or more |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The Continuous-cropping tolerance grade of B means a 3-year rotation break. If this interval is not kept, root decay disease and cyst nematode build up, and after a few years the whole field suddenly collapses. And when counting this interval, Swiss chard and beetroot must be treated as the same crop. The three are a single species with the same scientific name, so to a pathogen they are indistinguishable. It is common in a home garden to plant sugar beet the following year where Swiss chard was grown, and this is no different from planting sugar beet in the same spot twice in a row.

## 12. Harvest

### 12-1. Judging the Timing

| Use | Timing | Criterion |
|---|---|---|
| Young leafy greens | 30-40 days after sowing | Leaves 15-20cm long |
| Thinned plants | 2-6 true leaves | Used as namul |
| Roots (spring sowing) | 90-150 days after sowing | Root diameter 5-10cm |
| Roots (overwintered) | April-May of the following year | Sugar content and yield are at their highest |
| When signs of bolting appear | Immediately | Once the flower stalk rises, the sugar drains away |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In overwintered cultivation, the benchmark for deciding the harvest time is the flower stalk. When spring arrives and temperatures rise, sugar beet enters its second year and begins to send up a flower stalk, and from that moment the sugar stored in the root is used to build the stalk and seed and declines rapidly. When you see the signs, with leaves reaching upward from the center of the plant and the stem thickening, that is the last moment. The reason the April harvest came out best in the trial on Jeju Island is also that it falls just before this limit.

### 12-2. Harvesting Procedure

| Step | Details |
|---|---|
| 1 | Choose a clear, dry day. Roots stored wet will rot |
| 2 | Dig beside the plant with a hand hoe to loosen the soil |
| 3 | Gather the leaves in your hand and pull straight up. Do not twist |
| 4 | Cut off the leaves. Leave 1-2cm above the top of the root |
| 5 | Leave the fine roots and the tail as they are. If cut, microbes enter there |
| 6 | Knock off loose soil lightly. Do not wash with water |
| 7 | Use the leaves as a vegetable or return them to the field |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;If the leaves are cut too close, the crown of the root is exposed and decay starts there. Conversely, if too much is left, the leaves keep growing and use up the sugar in the root. Leaving 1cm to 2cm is the point between these two risks.

### 12-3. Sorting After Harvest

| Type | Handling |
|---|---|
| Large, undamaged roots | Send to pit storage |
| Split or damaged roots | Use first for sugar extraction. Do not store |
| Shoulders that have turned green | Cut off. They cloud the syrup and taste bitter |
| Small or misshapen roots | Use as food or livestock feed |
| The straightest, largest roots | Collect separately as seed roots |
| Leaves | Namul, soup, pickles. Return any surplus to the field |
