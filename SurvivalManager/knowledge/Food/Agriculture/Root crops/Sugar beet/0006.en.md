## 16. Sugar Extraction and Cooking

### 16-1. Eating Sugar Beet as Food

| Method | Fuel use | Procedure | Notes |
|---|:---:|---|---|
| Boiling | Medium | Put the roots in water with the skin on and boil for 30-40 minutes | The skin slips off by hand once cooked |
| Steaming | Low | Add a little water and steam for 35-45 minutes | The sugar does not leach into the water |
| Cutting small and boiling | Low | Cut into 1-2cm pieces and boil for 15-20 minutes | Add to soups or porridge |
| Residual-heat cooking | Very low | Boil for 15 minutes, take off the heat, wrap the pot in cloth and leave for 1 hour | Cuts fuel use to half or less |
| Baking in ashes | Very low | Bury in hot ashes for 40-50 minutes | The sweetness deepens |
| Dried pieces | Low | Soak in water and use right away | Drying them in advance saves fuel at every meal |
| Leaves | Very low | Blanch in boiling water for 1-2 minutes | Discard the blanching water |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The reason steaming beats boiling is especially clear with sugar beet. The sugar in the root dissolves readily in water, so when the roots are boiled, a large share of it leaches out into the water. If the cooking water is used as broth it does not matter, but if it is thrown away, the sweetness and the calories are thrown away with it. Steaming avoids that loss.

### 16-2. How Sugar Is Obtained

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Of the weight of a sugar beet root, 14-20% is sucrose, which is to say sugar itself. Most of the rest is water, and some is fiber. Getting sugar is therefore a physical separation, not a chemical conversion. There are only three things to do: take the sugar out of the cells, drive off the water, and set what remains. This is the decisive difference from grains and potatoes, where starch must first be turned into sugar.

| Step | What happens | Difficulty |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Slicing | Opens the cells to give the sugar a way out | Easy |
| 2. Extraction | Draws the sugar out with water | Needs temperature control |
| 3. Pressing | Squeezes out the remaining juice | Hard work |
| 4. Clarification | Skims off impurities | Needs materials |
| 5. Boiling down | Drives off the water | Uses the most fuel |
| 6. Crystallization | Sets the sugar into crystals | Needs skill and time |
| 7. Molasses separation | Separates the crystals from the remaining liquid | Needs tools |

### 16-3. Slicing and Extraction

| Item | Standard | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Preparation | Wash off the soil and cut off the green shoulder and the fine roots | They cause bitterness and an earthy taste |
| Skin | Peel thinly or use as it is | Peeling gives a clearer juice; leaving the skin on raises the yield |
| Cut thickness | Thin strips 2-3mm thick | The thinner they are, the faster the sugar comes out |
| Extraction water temperature | 70-75°C | The most important number |
| Extraction time | 40 minutes-1 hour | Until the strips soften and the water turns sweet |
| Amount of water | Just enough to cover the strips | More water means more water to boil off later |
| Stirring | Stir now and then | Extracts evenly |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The temperature of 70-75°C is not an arbitrary choice. At this temperature the cell membranes are denatured and the sugar escapes freely, yet the tissue itself has not yet collapsed. Above 80°C, pectin dissolves out and the juice turns sticky and will not filter. Below 60°C, the cell membranes stay intact, so little sugar comes out, and unwanted microbes multiply as well. Without a thermometer, the range is roughly where steam rises from the water but it has not yet boiled, and a finger dipped in is so hot that it is pulled out at once. It must not be boiled.

> **The water used to draw out the sugar is never boiled. Boiling makes the juice sticky so that it will not filter, and it also spoils the color and smell.**

### 16-4. Countercurrent Extraction to Save Water

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Here comes a trick that saves a decisive amount of fuel. If plain water is poured over freshly cut strips, a thin juice comes out, and all the water in that thin juice has to be boiled off later. If instead the juice from the previous batch is used as the extraction liquid for the next batch, the concentration keeps rising.

| Order | Details |
|---|---|
| 1 | The first batch is extracted with plain water. A thin juice comes out |
| 2 | Do not throw that juice away: pour it over fresh strips for the next batch and heat it again to 70-75°C |
| 3 | Repeat with the same juice three to four times |
| 4 | The juice gets richer with each batch |
| 5 | Once it is rich enough, boil down only that juice |
| 6 | Rinse the strips left at the end once more with fresh water and use that water for the next first batch |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This method follows the same principle as the industrial process. It yields the same amount of sugar while cutting the amount of water to be boiled off to half or less, and the firewood needed falls by the same proportion. It adds no tools and merely changes the order of work, so it is a trick that must be applied in a disaster.

### 16-5. Pressing and Handling the Residue

| Item | Details |
|---|---|
| Pressing | Wrap the extracted strips in cloth and wring them out hard. Pressing with a lever or a stone also works |
| Recovery | A considerable amount of the remaining juice comes out. Skipping this step means a large loss |
| Residue | Fiber and residual sugar remain |
| Livestock feed | Cattle, pigs and chickens all eat it readily. It makes excellent feed |
| Compost | If it is not used as feed, return it to the field |
| Fermentation | Lactic-acid fermentation lets it be kept as winter feed |

### 16-6. Clarification - Skimming Off Impurities

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Besides sugar, the pressed juice contains protein, pectin, organic acids, soil and pigments. If they are not removed, the syrup is dark and bitter and crystals form poorly. There are three methods usable at home.

| Method | Procedure | Effect | Materials |
|---|---|---|---|
| Skimming foam | Skim off the brown foam that floats on the surface with a ladle throughout the boiling | The basic and essential step | None needed |
| Lime treatment | Dissolve a small amount of slaked lime to raise the pH, heat, let it settle and filter it off | The most effective | Burn seashells or oyster shells hard to make quicklime, then soak it in water to make slaked lime |
| Egg white | Stir egg white into the cold juice and heat slowly; the white sets, traps impurities and floats up. Skim it off | Medium | Needs eggs |
| Cloth filtering | Strain several times through a fine cloth | Removes soil and residue | None needed |
| Ash lye | Use a small amount of the clear liquid from wood ash steeped in water | Supplementary | Easy to obtain |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Lime treatment is the core of the industrial process and is also possible at home. Heating seashells or oyster shells red-hot in a wood fire turns them into quicklime, and pouring water on this makes it give off heat and turn into slaked lime. Dissolve this in water, take only the clear liquid from the top, and add it to the juice a little at a time; protein and pectin clump together and settle. However, too much leaves a bitter, soapy taste, so add it in small amounts and watch the result. After the lime is added, if smoke from a charcoal fire is led through a pipe and passed through the liquid, calcium carbonate forms and settles out carrying the impurities with it; this is the carbonation step of the industrial process.

| Caution | Details |
|---|---|
| Too much ash lye | Potassium salts remain and hinder crystallization. Use only a small amount |
| Too much lime | A bitter taste and off-flavor remain |
| Handling quicklime | It becomes hot on contact with water. Keep it away from eyes and skin |
| Limitation | Fully white sugar is hard to make at home. Some earthy taste and color remain |

### 16-7. Boiling Down - The Step That Uses the Most Fuel

| Item | Details |
|---|---|
| Vessel | A shallow, wide pot or cauldron. The larger the surface area, the faster it evaporates |
| Lid | Leave it off. Evaporation is the goal |
| Fire | Strong at first, weak once it thickens |
| Stirring | Keep stirring once the concentration rises. The bottom burns |
| Fill level | No more than two-thirds of the pot. It suddenly boils over |
| Foam | Keep skimming it off |
| Syrup done | It stretches like a thread as it falls from a spoon. Sugar content about 70% |
| Burn warning | Boiling sugar liquid is hotter than water and more viscous, so the burns are deep |

#### Fuel Calculation

| Item | Figure |
|---|:---|
| Heat needed to boil off 1L of water | About 620kcal (80 for heating + 540 for evaporation) |
| Heat content of dry firewood | About 3,500-4,000kcal per kg |
| Thermal efficiency of an open hearth | 5-15% |
| Usable heat from 1kg of firewood | About 175-600kcal |
| Firewood to boil off 1L of water | About 1-3.5kg |
| Water to be boiled off to obtain 1kg of sugar | About 4.5-5L |
| Firewood per 1kg of sugar | About 5-15kg |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This table is the reality of processing sugar beet. 5-15kg of firewood for 1kg of sugar. Considering the firewood needed for heating and cooking through a single winter, this is by no means a light amount. That is why ways of saving fuel are as important as the procedure itself.

| Saving method | Details |
|---|---|
| Countercurrent extraction | Halves the amount of water to be boiled off. The most effective |
| Shallow, wide vessel | A larger evaporation area boils off more with the same heat |
| Windbreak | Wall in the hearth with stones or earth to reduce heat loss |
| Several pots | Place them side by side over one fire and boil down at the same time |
| Residual heat from other cooking | Keep the pot on the fire left over after cooking rice |
| Pre-concentration in the sun | Pour the juice into a shallow black vessel, cover with cloth and leave in the sun. It thickens by itself over several days |
| Freeze concentration | See the section below |

### 16-8. Concentrating Without Fuel - Freeze Concentration

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This is a method usable in winter and uses no firewood at all. The principle is simple. When sugar water freezes, what freezes first is pure water, and the sugar stays in the liquid that has not frozen. So if the ice is removed, the sugar concentration of the remaining liquid rises.

| Order | Details |
|---|---|
| 1 | Pour the juice into a shallow, wide vessel |
| 2 | Leave it outdoors during a night below freezing |
| 3 | Ice forms on the surface and around the edges. This is almost pure water |
| 4 | In the morning, remove the ice and discard it |
| 5 | The remaining liquid is richer |
| 6 | Repeat over several nights |

| Item | Details |
|---|---|
| Advantage | No fuel is needed at all |
| Limitation | As the concentration rises, the freezing point drops and it no longer freezes. Roughly up to about half |
| Afterwards | From then on, boil it down over a fire. The water left to boil off is greatly reduced |
| Condition | Possible only in regions with several consecutive nights below freezing |
| Caution | The ice that was removed still holds a little sugar. Melt it and use it as extraction water for the next batch |

> **If sugar beet is processed in a region with winters, the method that uses the least fuel is to harvest in autumn, press the juice, freeze-concentrate it on winter nights, and boil it down in spring.**

### 16-9. Crystallization and Molasses Separation

| Order | Details |
|---|---|
| 1 | Boil the syrup down further, until a spoonful dropped into cold water gathers into a soft lump |
| 2 | Take it off the fire and leave the vessel in a cool place |
| 3 | If you already have sugar, sprinkle in a few grains. They act as seeds for the crystals |
| 4 | Stir slowly while it cools. The crystals grow evenly |
| 5 | Crystals form over several hours to several days |
| 6 | Put the crystals and liquid in a cloth bag and hang it up. Over several days the molasses drains out |
| 7 | Pressing squeezes it out faster, but the crystals are broken too |
| 8 | Spread the remaining crystals out and dry them completely |

| Product | Properties and uses |
|---|---|
| Crystalline sugar | Has a brownish tint. Easiest to store and compact in volume |
| Molasses | A dark, thick syrup. 20-30% sugar remains |
| Uses of molasses | Seasoning, livestock feed, raw material for alcohol and vinegar, yeast culture |

### 16-10. Going All the Way to Crystals or Stopping at Syrup

| Comparison | Syrup | Crystalline sugar |
|---|---|---|
| Extra fuel | None | Needs further boiling down |
| Skill | Easy | Needs control of temperature and cooling |
| Tools | A pot is enough | Needs bags, a straining frame and time |
| Loss | None | 20-30% of the sugar drains off as molasses |
| Storage | One year or more if sealed | Several years |
| Volume and weight | Large | Small |
| Uses | Sweetening, preserving fruit, keeping food and fermenting are all possible | The same |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In a disaster, the answer is generally syrup. Syrup can do everything: sweetening, preserving fruit in sugar, preservation and fermentation, with no loss, and it needs less skill and fewer tools. Crystalline sugar is better when it has to be carried a long way or stockpiled for a very long time. Note, however, that syrup ferments or molds once its sugar content falls below 60-65%, so it must be boiled down enough to bring it up to around 70%.

### 16-11. Yield and Uses of the Sweetener

| Raw material | Product |
|---|---|
| 100kg of sugar beet | 70-80L of juice, 12-15kg of sugar |
| That juice | About 18-21kg of syrup (70% sugar content) |
| That syrup | 8-12kg of crystalline sugar and 5-8kg of molasses |
| Residue | Livestock feed or compost |

| Use | Details |
|---|---|
| Calories | The fastest-absorbed source of calories |
| Preservation | At a sugar content of 60% or more, bacteria and molds cannot grow. Fruit preserves and jam |
| Fermentation | Raw material for alcohol and vinegar. Vinegar in turn is a means of preserving other foods |
| Morale | In a time of hunger, sweetness is a comfort that nothing else can replace |
| Barter | When supply chains are cut off, a sweetener has high exchange value |

## 17. Annual Work Calendar

| Month (South Korea) | Work | Key points |
|---|---|---|
| January | Check stored roots, check seed roots, winter freeze concentration | Pick out rotten ones once every 2 weeks |
| February | Plan the field, turn the compost, correct acidity with lime or wood ash | Sugar beet likes neutral soil |
| March | Plow the field and pick out stones, spring sowing (after mid-month), replant seed roots | The deeper the plowing, the straighter the roots |
| April | Harvest the overwintered crop (sugar content at its highest), check germination, first thinning | Dig before the flower stalks rise |
| May | Second thinning, top-dressing (this is the last nitrogen), weeding | Stop nitrogen after 5-6 true leaves |
| June | Hilling, start harvesting leaves, manage flowering of seed plants | Stake the seed plants |
| July | Harvest the spring-sown crop, watch seed ripening | Dig before the heat arrives |
| August | Seed harvest and seed drying, sugar processing | When the seed heads dry to brown, strip off the seed |
| September | Autumn sowing (from mid-month), base fertilizer | Miss the timing and the plants will not survive the winter |
| October | Finish autumn sowing, thinning | Early in the month is the limit |
| November | Prepare for winter, straw mulching, curing and pit storage of the harvest | Label the seed roots and store them separately |
| December | Concentrated sugar processing, check storage | Use the residual heat from other cooking as well |

### 17-1. Five Things Not to Forget

| Item | Details |
|---|---|
| Nitrogen only until 6 true leaves | Nitrogen after that only grows leaves and wipes out the sugar |
| Do not put off thinning | The seed is a multigerm cluster, so several seedlings come up together. Left unthinned, all of them stay small |
| Overwintering is advantageous | In southern South Korea, planting in autumn and digging in the following spring gives the maximum in both sugar content and yield |
| Keep twice as many seed roots | Some always spoil over the winter. Seed saving requires at least ten plants |
| Concentrate before boiling down | Reducing the water to be boiled off with countercurrent extraction and freeze concentration halves the firewood |
