## Introduction
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Sugar beet (Beta vulgaris) is a biennial of the amaranth family, the same species as Swiss chard and beetroot, and a strain bred to pack sugar into the root. It was created in late 18th-century Europe as a sweetener source to replace sugarcane, and it grew in France when the Napoleonic Wars cut off sugar imports. Its disaster preparedness evaluation is overall S (52.4 points, 9th of 183 valid species), short term S (53.7), long term S (51.2), and supplementary food B (47.4 points); calories per area is S (3,850 kcal per ㎡), and dry seed keeps 5 years sealed at room temperature. But the real reason to grow it is sweetness. Sugar is the fastest-absorbed calorie, a preservative in which bacteria and mold cannot grow above 60% concentration, and the raw material for alcohol and vinegar. The price is fuel. The sugar in the root is only about 15%, so 1 kg of sugar costs 5-15 kg of firewood, and in a disaster the answer is often to stop at syrup (Section 10).

> **Sugar beet is both a calorie crop and a sweetener crop, and in a disaster a sweetener itself widens the range of storage techniques.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Seed longevity S means that, unlike the other roots and tubers whose seed potatoes, tubers, and cuttings must be kept alive every winter, dry seed can be kept in a jar for 5 years. Temperature range SS and cold tolerance A mean that it grows at 5-28℃ and withstands -5℃, and pest-control self-reliance A rests on evidence that resistant strains plus early sowing cut the main pests by 90% or more and that damage is about 1% if harvested before October. Seed saving D means that, being biennial, the root must be overwintered and replanted the next year to get seed (Section 8), and micronutrients D means that even when eating enough to fill the calories, only 7 nutrients exceed 10% of the recommended amount, so it cannot be a staple. The grade for each item is in the Crop Evaluation Results document.

## 2. Plant Overview
| Item | Details |
|:---|:---|
| Classification | Biennial of the amaranth family (Amaranthaceae), genus Beta. The same species as Swiss chard, beetroot (red sugar beet), and fodder beet. Likes a cool climate, and growth slows above 25℃ |
| Leaves, stems | Leaves like Swiss chard; while the root is growing, the lower leaves can be picked and eaten as a vegetable |
| Root | White and spindle-shaped, 10-15 cm in diameter and 30 cm or more long. The red pigment stains the syrup dark red, so for sugar a white-rooted strain is required |
| Flowers, seed | In the second year the flower stalk rises to 1 m or more, and small green flowers are pollinated by wind. The seed is a seed ball of several fused seeds, so two or three sprouts come from one ball |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;After sowing, the first 4 weeks are the germination and seedling stage, weeks 4-10 the vegetative stage (nitrogen), weeks 10-16 the root-swelling stage (potassium, boron), and weeks 16-22 the sugar accumulation stage (potassium); if it bolts in spring of the second year, the sugar in the root is used up. If nitrogen remains, the plant keeps making leaves and does not send sugar to the root, so leftover nitrogen in the sugar accumulation stage is the most common failure. Per 100 g there are 43-77 kcal, sucrose 14-20 g (over 20 Brix when overwintered), and protein 0.8-1.8 g; it stores sucrose itself rather than starch, so pressing out the juice and evaporating the water gives a sweetener. The leaves are rich in vitamins A and K, iron, and magnesium and make up for the root's weak points.

## 3. Growing Environment and Varieties
| Condition | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Temperature | Growth 5-28℃ (optimum 15-20℃); above 25℃ sugar does not accumulate; germination from 5℃. Below -5℃ the root freezes and turns soft |
| Water, light | Total evapotranspiration about 550 mm. Needs 6 hours or more of sun a day (8 or more is optimal), and in shade the sugar content drops |
| Soil | Deep, stone-free sandy loam to loam, pH 6.0-7.5 (optimum 6.5-7.0). Unlike other roots and tubers it is weak in acid soil, so correct it with lime or wood ash (wood ash supplies potassium and corrects pH at once) |
| Cropping types | Autumn sowing with overwintering: sow mid-September to early October and harvest April-May of the next year (south, Jeju). Spring sowing: sow mid-March to early April and harvest July-August (central, north), digging before the heat arrives. Avoid the summer heat |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Overwintering cultivation is advantageous. In the same field, the yield of 5.8 t per 1,000 ㎡ when dug in December rose 34% to 7.8 t when left in place and dug the following April, and the sugar content reached 20.8 Brix. This is because the cold itself is the signal to accumulate sugar. The window for autumn sowing is narrow: sown too early, the plant senses cold before winter and bolts the next spring, and sown too late, it does not grow enough to get through winter and dies of freezing.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Varieties include Tener (20.8 Brix in Jeju overwintering cultivation), Suez, and Tangeum sugar beet (native to Chungju). For sugar, choose a high-sugar white-rooted strain; for calories, a large-rooted strain. Much of the commercial seed is hybrid and does not come true when saved, so secure fixed varieties from the start.

## 4. Field Preparation and Sowing
| Item | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Land preparation | Dig 20-30 cm or deeper and clear stones without fail (if the root hits a stone or a hard layer it splits or bends). Make beds 15-20 cm high with drainage ditches (the only prevention of root rot) |
| Fertilization (1,000 ㎡) | Nitrogen 10-15 kg (50% basal, 50% top dressing), phosphorus 8-10 kg (all basal), potassium 10-15 kg (basal and top dressing), well-rotted compost 1,000-1,500 kg (unrotted compost makes the root split). Basal fertilizer 2 weeks before sowing; top-dress mainly with nitrogen at 5-6 true leaves and stop there. Nitrogen after that does not raise yield but destroys the sugar |
| Disaster substitute fertilizers | Wood ash (a handful or two per ㎡), well-rotted compost, urine diluted 10 times (forbidden after 6 true leaves), bone meal, and leaves and processing residue returned to the field (potassium cycling) |
| Boron | A shortage turns the inside of the root black, and an excess is toxic, so the margin is narrow. The realistic source is seaweed (dried wakame, kelp, and green laver mixed into basal fertilizer or soaked in water and applied), and inland, diversify the compost materials |
| Sowing | Depth 1.5-2.5 cm, row spacing 30-40 cm, final plant spacing 10-15 cm, 1-1.5 kg per 1,000 ㎡ (allowing for thinning). Cover thinly with soil, press it down firmly, and do not let it dry before germination |
| Seed treatment | The hard seed coat makes germination uneven, so soak the seed in water for a day and sow it at once (drying it again lowers germination). Coat it with wood ash or soak it in 50-55℃ water for 10-15 minutes to reduce surface pathogens |

## 5. Water Management and Cultivation Management
| Stage | Water management |
|:---|:---|
| Sowing to germination | Keep from drying (the seed ball must take up plenty of water for the coat to soften). In early growth, leave it to rainfall and add water only when dry |
| Root swelling | Plenty, never dry (dryness lowers both yield and sugar content) |
| 2-3 weeks before harvest | Stop irrigation (as moisture falls the sugar concentration rises and storability improves). Do not irrigate during winter either |

| Care | Method |
|:---|:---|
| Thinning | It cannot be skipped, because the seed ball sends up two or three sprouts together. Leave plants at 3-5 cm at 2-3 true leaves and the final 10-15 cm spacing at 5-6 true leaves. The later it is, the more the roots of the plants to keep are damaged, so do not pull; cut near the ground with scissors. Eat the thinned young plants as a seasoned side dish |
| Hilling | Once or twice during growth, and after rain cover the exposed shoulders. A shoulder that turns green in the sun has lower sugar, a bitter taste, and clouds the syrup |
| Other care | Weeding is essential because early growth is slow, and a mulch of straw or leaves narrows the swings in moisture and prevents splitting. Pick only a few lower leaves at a time to eat. In winter cover the shoulders with straw or leaves, and dig first the plants whose flower stalk has risen in spring, since their roots are useless |
| Water shortage, excess | If the leaves droop at midday and recover in the evening, reinforce the mulch, and if the lower leaves dry up, run water between the ridges. A sudden watering after a shortage makes the root split lengthwise and it cannot be stored. In excess, the upper part softens and foul-smelling root rot sets in, so discard that plant and dig a drainage ditch |

## 6. Pests, Diseases, and Nutrient Problems
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Pest and disease pressure is C (estimated unprotected loss rate 50.6%), but pest-control self-reliance is A, so adjusting sowing and digging times alone, without chemicals, solves most problems. The exceptions are beet cyst nematodes and root rot, which stay in the soil for years, so check the field's history before planting.

| Pest or disease | Symptoms and conditions | Prevention and response |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Root rot, cyst nematodes | The inside of the root rots black (fields continuously cropped with amaranth-family crops) / parasitizes the root and growth stops | Rest the field 3 years or more and do not follow with Swiss chard or beetroot either / there is no cure, so rotate with non-host crops for several years |
| Cercospora leaf spot, powdery mildew | Brown spots spread on the leaves (hot and humid) / white powder on the leaf surface (dry, large day-night swings) | Ensure airflow, remove diseased leaves, rotate 3 years or more / milk diluted 10 times, baking soda solution |
| Pests | Aphids (carry viruses), root-crop pests (holes in the root), cutworms (base of young plants), leaf beetles (chew the leaves) | Soapy-water spray, protect ladybugs and parasitoid wasps / resistant strains and early sowing, harvest before October / catch at night, ring the base with ash / hand picking |

| Symptom | Cause and action |
|:---|:---|
| New leaves deformed and the inside of the root rotting black | Boron deficiency (called heart rot, but with no pathogen it is a pure deficiency, so chemicals are useless). Dried seaweed, diversified compost |
| Only leaves flourish, the root is small, and the sugar content is low | Excess nitrogen (the thing to guard against most; diluted urine used late halves the sugar content). Cut off nitrogen and add potassium with wood ash |

## 7. Intercropping and Crop Rotation
| Compatibility | Companions |
|:---|:---|
| Good | Legumes (fix nitrogen and leave it for the next crop), onion, garlic (repel pests), lettuce, spinach (cover the ground between ridges early and suppress weeds) |
| Fair | Chinese cabbage, radish (compete for nutrients) |
| Poor | Swiss chard, beetroot, fodder beet (the same species, so they share diseases and cross), and continuous cropping in the same place (root rot and nematodes build up) |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Rotation runs year 1 sugar beet (takes a great deal of nitrogen and potassium), year 2 legumes (restore nitrogen), year 3 leafy vegetables or alliums (not the amaranth family), year 4 sugar beet again, keeping an interval of 3 years or more. If the interval is not kept, root rot and nematodes build up and within a few years the whole field collapses. Swiss chard and beetroot are one species with sugar beet and indistinguishable to pathogens, so planting sugar beet the next year where chard grew in the vegetable garden is also continuous cropping.

## 8. Harvest and Seed Saving
| Harvest | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Timing | Dig the root 90-150 days after spring sowing (5-10 cm in diameter), and in overwintering cultivation in April-May of the next year. In spring, when signs of bolting appear (leaves reaching up from the center of the plant and the stem thickening), that is the last moment, because after that the sugar in the root drains into the flower stalk |
| Method | On a clear, dry day, loosen the soil beside the plant, gather the leaves, and pull straight up. Cut the leaves leaving 1-2 cm above the root (cut too close and rot enters through the crown, too long and the leaves consume sugar). Do not cut the fine roots and tail (germs enter), shake off only the soil, and do not wash with water. Do not store split or wounded roots; use them first for extracting sugar |

| Seed saving | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Structure | To get seed, the first-year root must not be eaten but overwintered and replanted in spring (keeping the seed root), and this is the only bottleneck of sugar beet self-reliance |
| Selecting and storing seed roots | Choose straight, unsplit, unwounded roots of medium size or larger and taste-test one by cutting it for sweetness, cut the leaves leaving 3-5 cm at the crown (protects the growing point), and do not cut the fine roots. Some will spoil over winter, so keep two to three times the amount needed. Bury them upright, crown up, in moist sand or soil in a cellar pit (0-4℃) and inspect every 2 weeks. In the south it is surest to leave them undug, covered with 20-30 cm of straw, to overwinter in place (risky north of the central region) |
| Replanting, flowering | Plant in mid-March to early April at 50-60 cm spacing with the crown just showing above the ground, and stake the flower stalks that grow over 1 m and fall over. They flower in May-June, and when the spikes dry brown, strip them off and dry them thoroughly in the shade |
| Isolation, number of plants | Wind pollination means they cross with Swiss chard, beetroot, and other sugar beet varieties. Isolation is hard, so flower only one variety in the same year and do not save seed from chard or beetroot that year. Because they suffer from inbreeding, take seed from ten or more plants, preferably 20 or more, and mix it |
| Storage | Dry it completely, put it in a sealed container with wood ash or charcoal wrapped in cloth, and keep it in a cool, dark place. It keeps about 5 years sealed at room temperature; check germination every year with a few seeds, and do one seed-saving round before the fifth year |

## 9. Storage
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Storability C reflects a storage period of B and a storage loss rate of 5.09%. The root keeps breathing in storage and burns its own sugar as fuel, so the sugar falls the longer and the warmer it is kept. After curing (10-15℃, humidity 85-95%, dark, light ventilation, 1-2 weeks) it goes into main storage (0-4℃, humidity 85-95%, complete darkness); below freezing it freezes and turns soft, and at 8℃ or above it sprouts.

| Method | How | Period |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Cellar storage | Dig 1.5 m into well-drained ground and make a roof and a vent (0-4℃). The most stable, but the digging is heavy work | 3-4 months |
| Pit or clamp storage | Dig a shallow pit 1.5 m across and 20-30 cm deep, lay 10 cm of dry straw, and pile the cured roots in a mound no higher than 60 cm. Cover with 20-30 cm of straw and 15-20 cm of soil, make a vent at the top with a bundle of straw (if blocked, heat and moisture build up and it rots), and dig a drainage ditch around it (0-5℃) | 2-3 months |
| Processed storage | Syrup (over 1 year if sealed), crystal sugar (years), dried pieces (6 months to 1 year), lactic fermentation in 2-3% brine (1-2 weeks at room temperature), alcohol and vinegar | Several months to years |

> **The surest way to keep sugar beet long is not to protect the root but to turn it into syrup. The root lasts four months, but the syrup carries over the year.**

## 10. Sugar Extraction, Cooking, and Precautions
| Caution | Details |
|:---|:---|
| Root | It has no toxins like the cyanogenic glycosides of cassava or the solanine of potato, so it is safe in ordinary amounts. It can be eaten raw, but it is hard and tastes of earth, so cooking is better (boiled whole 30-40 minutes or steamed 35-45 minutes, and steaming is better because the sugar does not leach into the water), and eating a lot can upset the stomach because of the sugar. Throw away any that is discolored or smells off, and cut out the black part inside (boron deficiency); the rest can be eaten |
| Leaves | Like chard, they contain oxalic acid, and leaves from heavily nitrogen-fed fields accumulate nitrate (watch out for small children), so blanch them in boiling water for 1-2 minutes and discard the water. People with a history of kidney stones should not eat much |
| Sugar processing | Boiling sugar liquid is hotter than water and thick, so burns are deep, and it suddenly boils over, so fill the pot no more than two-thirds, and keep stirring so the bottom does not burn. Keep lime away from eyes and skin. Dilute sugar liquid ferments in a few days, and sealing a fermenting liquid bursts the container |

| Sugar extraction | Method |
|:---|:---|
| Slicing, extraction | Wash off the soil, cut off the green shoulder and fine roots, slice into thin strips 2-3 mm thick, and steep in just enough water to cover them at 70-75℃ for 40 minutes to 1 hour. Do not boil it: above 80℃ the pectin dissolves and the juice turns sticky and will not filter, and below 60℃ the sugar comes out poorly and spoilage microbes multiply. Without a thermometer, the guide is water that steams but is not boiling |
| Counter-current extraction | Pour the juice from the previous round over fresh strips for the next round and reheat, repeating 3-4 times, and the juice gets richer; rinse the last strips with fresh water and use that water for the first round of the next batch. The water to be evaporated falls to half or less, so less firewood is needed |
| Pressing, clarification | Wrap the strips in cloth and press hard to recover the remaining juice, and use the residue as feed or compost. Skim off the brown foam throughout the boiling (essential); the most effective step is lime treatment. Burn clam or oyster shells red-hot in a wood fire to make quicklime, add water to make slaked lime, add the clear upper liquid to the juice a little at a time, let it settle, and filter it (too much leaves a bitter, soapy taste) |
| Boiling down, fuel | Boil down in a shallow, wide pot with the lid off; the syrup is done when it drips from a spoon in a thread at about 70% sugar. To get 1 kg of sugar, 4.5-5 L of water must be evaporated, which takes 5-15 kg of firewood on an open fire (thermal efficiency 5-15%). Ways to save are counter-current extraction (the biggest), the residual heat of other cooking, and pre-concentration by putting the juice in a shallow black dish covered with cloth in the sun |
| Freeze concentration | On sub-zero nights, put the juice out in a shallow, wide dish and remove the ice that forms by morning (nearly pure water), repeating over many nights. No fuel at all is used, but as the concentration rises the freezing point drops, so it works only to about half. In regions with winter, pressing the juice in autumn, freeze-concentrating it on winter nights, and boiling it down in spring uses the least fuel |
| Syrup, crystals | In a disaster the answer is usually syrup. It needs no extra fuel or tools, has no loss, and works for sweetening, pickling, preserving, and fermenting. Below 60-65% sugar it ferments or molds, so boil it down to about 70%. For crystal sugar, boil further until a drop in cold water gathers into a soft lump, cool it while stirring slowly, hang the crystals that form in a cloth bag to drain the molasses over several days, and dry them, which suits long transport or very long stockpiling. 100 kg of root yields about 18-21 kg of syrup |

## 11. Annual Work Schedule
| Month | Work |
|:---|:---|
| Jan-Feb | Inspect the stored roots and seed roots (pick out rotten ones every 2 weeks) and freeze-concentrate in winter. In February, turn the compost and correct acidity with lime or wood ash |
| Mar-Apr | In March, plow and clear stones, spring sowing (from mid-month), and replant the seed roots. In April, harvest the overwintered crop (before the flower stalk rises, sugar at its peak), check germination, and do the first thinning |
| May-June | In May, second thinning, top dressing (the last nitrogen), and weeding. In June, hilling, start picking leaves, and stake the seed plants |
| July-Aug | In July, harvest the spring-sown crop (before the heat) and watch the seed ripen. In August, when the spikes dry brown, collect and dry the seed, and process sugar |
| Sep-Oct | From mid-September, autumn sowing and basal fertilizer (miss the timing and it cannot get through winter). Finish sowing by early October and thin |
| Nov-Dec | In November, prepare for winter (straw mulch), cure the harvest and store it in the cellar, and keep the seed roots separate. In December, concentrate on sugar processing (using the residual heat of other cooking) and inspect the stored goods |

> **Sugar beet is a crop where you stop nitrogen at 5-6 true leaves, never put off thinning, sow in autumn and overwinter in the south, keep twice the seed roots you need, and concentrate before boiling down to cut the firewood.**
