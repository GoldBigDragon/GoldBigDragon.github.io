## 13. Keeping Seed Roots and Saving Seed

### 13-1. A Job That Takes Two Years

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Sugar beet's Seed saving grade is D. The reason is that it is a biennial: it makes its root in the first year and flowers and sets seed in the second. To collect seed, you must not eat the edible root but keep it alive through the winter and replant it the following spring. This process is called seed-root keeping. It is work of the same kind as keeping seed potatoes for potato and overwintering cuttings for cassava, and it is the one bottleneck in making sugar beet self-reliant.

| Step | Timing | Details |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Selecting seed roots | Harvest time | Choose the straightest, healthiest roots |
| 2. Storing seed roots | Winter | Bury them in a root-cellar pit or in sand and carry them through the winter unfrozen |
| 3. Replanting | Following spring | Plant them again with the crown just showing above the ground |
| 4. Bolting and flowering | Spring to early summer | The flower stalk rises to 1m or more |
| 5. Seed ripening | Summer | Leave until the seed spikes dry brown |
| 6. Seed harvest and drying | Summer | Shake off the seed and dry it completely in the shade |
| 7. Storage | The following 5 years | Airtight, cool, dark place |

### 13-2. Selecting Seed Roots

| Item | Standard | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Shape | Straight, not forked | The trait is passed on to the offspring |
| Size | Medium or larger, but not excessively large | Excessively large roots rot easily in storage |
| Sugar content | Sweet when cut and tasted | Cut and check one plant, then choose the rest from around that plant |
| Wounds | None | A wounded root rots in storage |
| Leaf cutting | Leave 3-5cm above the crown | Leave more than for eating, to protect the growing point |
| Fine roots | Do not cut | Microbes enter through the cut surface |
| Quantity | Two to three times the amount needed | Some are bound to spoil over the winter |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Roots to be kept as seed roots are cut with the leaves left long. For eating you leave 1-2cm, but for seed roots you leave 3-5cm. This is because if the growing point at the crown is damaged, no shoots come up the following spring.

### 13-3. Storing Seed Roots

| Method | Conditions | How |
|---|---|---|
| Pit storage | 0-4°C, humidity 90-95% | Bury upright in moist sand or soil, with the crown facing up |
| Clamp storage | 0-5°C | Pile in the order straw, roots, straw, soil in a mound shape, and make a vent |
| Overwintering in place | Southern South Korea, Jeju Island | Leave in the field and cover thickly with straw. Takes the least work |
| Indoor sand box | 0-5°C | Suitable for small amounts |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In southern South Korea, the surest method is to leave the seed roots in the field without digging them. Sugar beet withstands 5°C below zero, so with 20-30cm of straw laid over it, it will generally get through a winter in southern South Korea. A big advantage is that there are no wounds or losses from digging and storing. In central South Korea and farther north this method is risky, so the roots must be dug and put into a pit.

| Inspection during storage | Details |
|---|---|
| Interval | Once every 2 weeks |
| What to check | Soft roots, mold, strange smells |
| Action | Remove spoiled roots immediately. One infects its neighbor |
| Dryness | If the sand has become too dry, sprinkle it lightly with water |
| Temperature | Keep it from dropping below 0°C |

### 13-4. Replanting and Seed Harvest

| Item | Standard |
|---|---|
| Timing | Mid-March to early April in South Korea, after the ground has thawed |
| Planting depth | With the crown just showing above the ground |
| Spacing | 50-60cm. The flower stalk grows large, so space them widely |
| Support | The flower stalk grows to 1m or more and falls over, so set up stakes |
| Flowering | May-June. Pollinated by wind |
| Isolation | Keep well away from Swiss chard, beetroot and other sugar beet varieties |
| Ripening | Leave until the seed spikes dry brown |
| Seed harvest | Strip the spikes by hand and dry completely in the shade |
| Number of plants | Save seed together from at least 10 plants |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Two points matter especially: isolation and the number of plants. Sugar beet is a wind-pollinated, cross-fertilizing plant, so if Swiss chard or beetroot flowers nearby, the plants cross, and the offspring turn out to be something quite different. Commercially an isolation distance of several km is used, but in a disaster it is hard to separate plants that far. The realistic alternative is to stagger the timing: let only one variety flower in a given year, and do not save seed from Swiss chard and beetroot that year.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;With few plants, growth worsens noticeably within a few generations, because cross-fertilizing plants are vulnerable to inbreeding. The rule is to save seed together from ten or more plants, preferably 20 or more, and mix it. If this one rule is not kept, a situation arrives five years later in which there is seed to plant but it does not grow properly.

### 13-5. Seed Storage

| Item | Standard |
|---|---|
| Drying | Dry completely in the shade, until it snaps by hand |
| Container | Airtight container or glass jar |
| Desiccant | Wrap wood ash, charcoal or quicklime in cloth and put it in together |
| Temperature | A cool place with little fluctuation |
| Light | A completely dark place |
| Longevity | About 5 years sealed at room temperature |
| Germination check | Each year, wrap a few seeds in a damp cloth and check the germination rate |

> **A seed longevity of 5 years is sugar beet's greatest asset. It means seed does not have to be saved every year, and this nearly offsets the weakness of being a biennial. However, one two-year seed-saving cycle must be carried out before the fifth year arrives.**

## 14. Toxicity and Precautions

### 14-1. The Root Is Safe

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Sugar beet is a food that is safe at ordinary amounts of intake. It has no toxic compounds like the cyanogenic glycosides of cassava or the solanine of potato. Eaten raw it does not poison, and it can be given to children.

| Item | Details |
|---|---|
| Eating raw | Possible. It is hard and earthy-tasting, so cooking is better |
| Overeating | The high sugar content can upset the stomach. It is not toxicity |
| Spoiled roots | Discard if discolored or smelling strange |
| Shoulders turned green | Bitter and low in sugar. Cut them off |
| Black core | Boron deficiency. Cut out that part and the rest can be eaten |

### 14-2. A Little Caution with the Leaves

| Item | Details |
|---|---|
| Oxalic acid | Like Swiss chard, the leaves contain oxalic acid. Blanching and discarding the water reduces it |
| Kidney stones | People with a history of kidney stones should not eat much |
| Nitrate | Leaves from a field given a lot of nitrogen accumulate nitrate. Take particular care with young children |
| Treatment | Blanching in boiling water for 1-2 minutes and discarding the water reduces oxalic acid and nitrate together |
| Feeding livestock | Suddenly feeding a large amount of leaves causes problems for cattle and sheep. Increase the amount gradually |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The nitrate problem is worth remembering in a disaster in particular. With no chemical fertilizer, diluted urine ends up being used often, and if too much nitrogen goes in, nitrate accumulates in the leaves. Nitrate itself is not a big problem, but when it is converted to nitrite in the body, it reduces the blood's ability to carry oxygen. It is more dangerous for young children. Since simply discarding the water after blanching removes a considerable part of it, it is safer to eat the leaves blanched than raw.

### 14-3. Precautions in the Sugar-Processing Stage

| Item | Details |
|---|---|
| Boiling sugar solution | Its boiling point is higher than water's and it is more viscous, so burns are deep. Take extra care |
| Boil-over | While being reduced it suddenly boils over. Do not fill the pot more than 2/3 full |
| Scorching | As the concentration rises, the bottom starts to burn. Keep stirring |
| Using lime | If lime is used in refining, keep it away from the eyes and skin |
| Fermentation | A dilute sugar solution ferments within days. If that is not wanted, reduce it quickly |
| Sealing | A sealed container holding fermenting liquid will burst |

## 15. Storage Without Refrigeration

### 15-1. Three Stages of Storage

| Stage | Conditions | Duration | Purpose |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Curing | 10-15°C, humidity 85-95%, dark with gentle airflow | 1-2 weeks | Seals wounds and reduces water loss |
| 2. Main storage | 0-4°C, humidity 85-95%, complete darkness | 3-4 months | Reduces respiration and slows the use of sugar |
| 3. Inspection | Once every 2 weeks | Throughout the storage period | Prevents one rotten root from ruining the whole lot |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;What must be especially remembered about storing sugar beet is that sugar decreases during storage. The root is living tissue, so it respires even in storage, and as fuel it burns the sugar stored in its own body. The higher the temperature, the faster the respiration and the faster the sugar disappears. The condition of 0-4°C is the point at which respiration is slowed as far as possible without freezing. If sugar is the aim, it is better to keep the storage period short and process the roots as soon as possible after harvest.

### 15-2. Comparison of Unpowered Storage Methods

| Method | How to make | Temperature | Duration | Advantage | Disadvantage |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Overwintering in place | Leave in the field and cover with 20-30cm of straw | Soil temperature | All winter | Almost no labor, and sugar content rises | Risk of freezing in central South Korea and farther north |
| Pit storage | Dig 1.5m into well-drained ground and add a roof and a vent | 0-4°C | 3-4 months | The most stable | Digging is heavy labor |
| Pit clamp | Dig 30cm and pile in the order straw, roots, straw, soil | 0-5°C | 2-3 months | Made in a day | Hard to cover again once opened |
| Sand-layer storage | Layer dry sand and roots in a box | Ambient temperature | 1-2 months | Prevents the spread of rot | A cool place is needed separately |
| Straw or rice-hull insulation | Wrap in straw and pile in a cool room | 5-10°C | 1 month | No facilities needed | Sprouts if the temperature is high |

### 15-3. How to Make a Clamp

| Step | Details |
|---|---|
| 1 | Choose a high spot where water does not collect. A north-facing slope is good |
| 2 | Dig shallow, 1.5m in diameter and 20-30cm deep |
| 3 | Lay 10cm of dry straw on the bottom |
| 4 | Pile the cured roots in a mound shape. Keep the height within 60cm |
| 5 | Cover the whole with 20-30cm of dry straw |
| 6 | Cover that with 15-20cm of soil and pat the surface so rainwater runs off |
| 7 | Stand a bundle of straw at the top to make a vent. If blocked, it rots inside |
| 8 | Dig a drainage ditch around it |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The vent is the key. Roots respire even in storage, giving off heat, water vapor and carbon dioxide. If the clamp is fully sealed, the temperature inside rises and moisture builds up, and within a few days the whole lot turns soft. The straw bundle at the top is a structure that lets air through while keeping rainwater out.

### 15-4. Storage Conditions in Detail

| Condition | Target | When outside the range |
|---|---|---|
| Temperature | 0-4°C | Below freezing, roots freeze and turn soft. At 8°C or above, they sprout and sugar drops quickly |
| Humidity | 85-95% | If lower, they shrivel; if higher, mold grows |
| Light | Complete darkness | Exposed to light, the crown turns green |
| Airflow | Gentle and continuous | If blocked, heat and moisture build up; if strong, they dry out |
| Leaves | Cut leaving 1-2cm | If long, they keep growing and consume sugar; if short, rot enters the crown |
| Inspection | Once every 2 weeks | One rotten root quickly infects those around it |

### 15-5. Very Long-Term Storage Through Processing

| Method | Procedure | Duration | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Syrup | Extract the juice and boil it down | 1 year or more | The most practical. Covered in detail in Section 16 |
| Crystallized sugar | Boil the syrup down further to crystallize it | Several years | Uses a lot of fuel, but is the easiest to keep |
| Dried slices | Slice thin and dry completely | 6 months to 1 year | Soak in water to use, or chew as they are |
| Lactic fermentation | Soak in 2-3% brine at room temperature for 1-2 weeks | Several months | Becomes a side dish |
| Vinegar | Ferment the juice into alcohol, then ferment that into acetic acid | Several years | Becomes a means of preserving other foods |
| Alcohol | Ferment the juice as it is | Several months | Serves both calorie preservation and disinfection |

> **The surest way to keep sugar beet for a long time is not to protect the roots but to turn them into syrup. The root lasts four months, but syrup carries over into the next year.**
