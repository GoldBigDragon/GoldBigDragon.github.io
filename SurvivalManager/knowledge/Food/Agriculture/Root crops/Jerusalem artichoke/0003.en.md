## 3. Growing Conditions

### 3-1. Limits and Optimal Values

| Item | Limit | Optimum | Outside the Range |
|---|---|---|---|
| Growing temperature | 5-30°C | 18-25°C | The range is a very wide 25°C. It survives in almost any climate |
| Overwintering temperature | Survives in the soil down to -20 to -30°C | - | Overwinters across all of South Korea with no special measures |
| Germination temperature | 5-10°C or above | - | Can be planted as soon as the ground thaws |
| Light | 6+ hours a day | 8+ hours a day | Under 40% shade, yield stays at about 80%. Relatively shade-tolerant |
| Soil pH | 5.0-8.0 | 5.8-7.0 | Adapts broadly, from acidic to mildly alkaline |
| Soil | Almost unfussy | Well-drained sandy loam to loam | In clay, tubers become misshapen and hard to dig |
| Moisture | Can be grown without irrigation | Keep just moist only during tuber bulking | Actually vulnerable to waterlogging; standing water rots the roots |
| Total evapotranspiration | - | About 100L/m² | About 20% of the potato's level |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The most striking cell in this table is water use. The potato uses 500-700mm over its whole season, while the Jerusalem artichoke uses only about 100mm. Since South Korea's annual rainfall is around 1,200mm, this means natural rainfall alone is enough even in the driest year. When there is no electricity to run a pump and no one to carry water by hand, not needing irrigation is a decisive advantage. In practice, wild Jerusalem artichokes on riverbanks grow taller than a person every year with no one watering them at all.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;However, it is surprisingly vulnerable to waterlogging. Being drought-tolerant and being fine underwater are different matters. If the roots stay submerged for several days, oxygen is cut off, the tubers turn soft, and soft rot sets in. This is also the one condition that needs real attention when choosing where to grow it.

### 3-2. Choosing a Site in a Disaster

| Condition | Verdict |
|---|---|
| Riverbanks, levees, mounds beside ditches | Best of all. Close to water, but where it does not pool |
| Abandoned fields, poor ground mixed with gravel | Good. Turns land other crops have given up on into a food source |
| Behind the house, outside the fence, at the foot of a hill | Good. Makes use of ground that is not a field |
| Field edges doubling as a windbreak | Good. Its height lets it work as a windbreak |
| The middle of a kitchen garden | Avoid. It spreads and crowds out other crops |
| Low ground where water pools | Avoid. This is its one weak point |
| A main field that must be tilled regularly | Avoid. It cannot be undone |

> **The Jerusalem artichoke is not a crop for good farmland; it is a crop that turns non-farmland into farmland. And once planted, it is hard to undo.**

## 4. Varieties

### 4-1. Comparison by Strain

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The Jerusalem artichoke is a crop with a loose formal variety system. Most of what circulates in South Korea is either gathered from the wild or a native strain that spread long ago, while named cultivars are usually ones brought in from abroad.

| Strain | Yield & Vigor | Ease of Growing | Suitable Conditions | Texture | Disaster Suitability |
|---|:---:|:---:|---|---|:---:|
| Wild/native strains | Explosive; regenerates even when left untended | Can be left unattended | Wide range, from cool-temperate to temperate | Earthy and coarse | Top priority |
| Fuseau | Stable | Easy | Temperate, cool summers | Smooth and crisp | High. Practical, since it is easy to clean |
| Stampede | Early-maturing, with large tubers | Very easy | Cold regions, short summers | Mild and crisp | High. First choice for regions with a short growing season |
| Dwarf Sunray | Medium | Easy | Temperate, windy areas | Tender and soft | Moderate. Its short height keeps it from lodging |
| Red Fuseau | Stable | Easy | Temperate | Nutty | Moderate. The red pigment is an antioxidant compound |
| Skorospelka | Early-maturing | Extremely cold-hardy | Extremely cold regions, very short summers | Mild | High. Specialized for severe cold |

### 4-2. Selection Criteria by Situation

| Situation | Choice | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| When you cannot obtain planting tubers | Wild strains | Just dig them up along riverbanks or roadsides in autumn |
| When you want it to spread as fast as possible | Wild strains | They have the strongest vigor |
| When you will be cleaning and cooking it often | Fuseau | Smooth, so it is easy to wash and peel |
| Highlands with early frost | Stampede, Skorospelka | Early-maturing, so tubers fill out even in a short summer |
| Windy sites | Dwarf Sunray | Short, so less prone to lodging |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In a disaster, the Jerusalem artichoke's strength lies not in its varieties but in its Prevalence in South Korea SS rating. Because it grows wild and naturalized across all eight provinces, even if seed distribution is completely cut off, you can simply remember a spot where yellow flowers bloomed in autumn, dig it up after the frost, and it becomes your seed tubers, ready to plant. It starts from a different line entirely than crops for which you must buy seed or buy seedlings.

## 5. Field Preparation and Fertilizer

### 5-1. Preparing the Ground

| Item | Standard | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Tilling | 20-30cm deep | The deeper the tilling, the larger the tubers and the easier they are to dig |
| Bed height | 30cm or more | Prevents waterlogging, its one weakness |
| Drainage furrows | 30cm or more | Standing water softens the tubers |
| Soil pH | 5.8-7.0 | It grows even without adjusting this |
| Location | Keep in its own separate area | Because of allelopathy and overgrowth |
| Boundary barrier | Boards or a trench, 40-60cm deep | Stops underground stems from spreading sideways |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;A boundary barrier is not optional; it is essential. In a single year, the Jerusalem artichoke's underground stems extend nearly 1m, and a new tuber forms at the end of each one. Plant it with no measures at all, and within two or three years it will have taken over the field next door. Boards, slate, sheet metal—almost anything set upright and buried at least 40cm deep will stop most of it. You can also dig a trench to form a boundary, but in that case you must dig along the trench every year and cut off any roots that have crossed over.

### 5-2. Standard Fertilization Rate (per 1,000m²)

| Fertilizer | Amount | Notes |
|---|:---:|---|
| Nitrogen (N) | Minimize | Too much produces lush leaves but fewer tubers |
| Phosphate (P) | 3-4kg | Helps root development |
| Potassium (K) | 3-5kg | Most important for tuber bulking |
| Compost | 1,000-1,500kg | Well-rotted compost, all applied as base fertilizer |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Compared with the potato's standard fertilization rate of roughly 32g/m² combined for nitrogen, phosphate, and potassium, the Jerusalem artichoke's is 25% or less of that—and it grows even without that much. The Fertilizer demand SS grade does not mean "a little is enough"; it means "none at all is fine." In practice, wild populations grow taller than a person every year with no fertilizer whatsoever. So in a disaster, it raises the total harvest more to keep your limited compost away from the Jerusalem artichoke and put it instead toward crops that actually demand fertilizer, like the potato or napa cabbage.

### 5-3. Base Fertilizer and Top-Dressing

| Type | Timing | Details |
|---|---|---|
| Base fertilizer | Before planting | Compost plus phosphate and potassium. Minimize nitrogen |
| Top-dressing | Mid-July (optional) | Only if aiming for a bigger harvest. Fine to skip entirely |
| Mulching | After planting | Fallen leaves or straw, 5-10cm. Just enough to suppress early weeds |

### 5-4. When There Is No Chemical Fertilizer - Disaster Substitutes

| Substitute | Nutrients Supplied | How to Make and Use It | Caution |
|---|---|---|---|
| Wood ash | Potassium, phosphate, calcium | Ash from broadleaf trees is high in potassium. A handful per m² |
| Well-rotted compost | Complete nutrients | Age fallen leaves, grass clippings, and manure for 6 months or more | If underripe, it damages the roots |
| Fallen-leaf mulch | Organic matter, moisture retention | Cover the bed thickly | Left in place, it turns into soil by the next year |
| Bone meal | Phosphate, calcium | Burn, crush, and mix into compost | Slow to take effect |
| Diluted urine | Nitrogen | Better not to use it | Nitrogen is harmful for the Jerusalem artichoke |
| Above-ground residue | Organic matter, potassium | Cut the stems in autumn and leave them on the spot as cover | The easiest self-sufficient fertilizer of all |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;That last row is the real trick to growing the Jerusalem artichoke. Stems and leaves that have grown up to 3m in a single year are a substantial amount of organic matter, and they contain the potassium the plant drew up from the field. If you cut them and leave them on the spot instead of burning or removing them, the nutrients cycle back within the field. In winter they serve as mulch, keeping the ground from freezing deep, and by spring they rot down into organic matter. This is how a field keeps itself going without bringing in anything from outside.

## 6. Seed Tuber Preparation and Planting

### 6-1. Planting Time

| Planting Type | Timing | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Spring planting | March-May, as soon as the ground thaws | Most common |
| Autumn planting | October-November | Left to overwinter as is; sprouts the following spring |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;That autumn planting is possible matters in a disaster. Tubers dug in autumn begin to shrivel after 2-4 weeks at room temperature, so if there is nowhere suitable to store them, simply planting them right away is the most reliable way to keep them. They spend the winter in the ground and sprout on their own in spring. This entirely skips the labor and risk of building a storage facility.

### 6-2. Preparing Seed Tubers

| Item | Standard | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Size | Egg-sized, 30-50g | Smaller ones grow more slowly at first |
| Cutting | Cut large ones into pieces with 2-3 eyes each | Conserves seed tubers |
| Cut surface | Dry in the shade for a day to let it heal over | Prevents rotting in the soil |
| Ash treatment | Dust the cut surface with wood ash | Serves as both disinfection and drying |
| Selection | Exclude diseased or softened tubers | Disease usually comes in along with the seed tuber |
| Storage | Best to plant immediately after digging | Drying out delays sprouting |

### 6-3. Planting

| Item | Standard | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Planting depth | 5-10cm | Too shallow is vulnerable to drying out; too deep delays emergence |
| Plant spacing | 30-45cm | It grows even if closer, but the tubers become smaller |
| Row spacing | 60-70cm | Ensures airflow and photosynthesis |
| Planting orientation | Eyes facing up | Unlike apios, it is not very sensitive to orientation |
| After planting | Cover with soil and press down lightly | No watering needed |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The concept of seed disinfection effectively does not apply to the Jerusalem artichoke. It does not propagate by seed but by tuber, and not many diseases travel in along with the seed tuber. Letting the cut surface dry and heal for a day and dusting it with wood ash is enough. In a situation with no chemicals available, this is no small advantage.
