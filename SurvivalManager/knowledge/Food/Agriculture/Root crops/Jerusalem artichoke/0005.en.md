## 12. Harvest

### 12-1. Judging the Time

| Category | Timing | Criteria |
|---|---|---|
| Optimal time | After the first frost | The above-ground parts die back, and inulin content and sweetness peak |
| Harvest window | Mid-November to March of the following year | Can be dug any time over more than four months |
| For seed tubers | March of the following year | Freshly dug tubers are in the best condition |
| Too-early harvest | Before September | Tubers have not yet formed. Most of the yield is lost |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There are two reasons to wait for the first frost. One is that the leaves keep making sugar and sending it down into the ground right up until frost falls. The other is that cold exposure breaks part of the inulin down into shorter chains, which increases sweetness. Jerusalem artichokes dug before frost are bland, while those dug after frost are sweet. This change continues to progress through the winter.

### 12-2. Harvesting Technique

| Step | Details |
|---|---|
| 1 | Cut the above-ground stem at its base. Leave the cut stem on the spot as cover |
| 2 | Start digging from at least 30cm away from the plant, working inward |
| 3 | Lift the soil with a hoe or garden fork to expose the tubers |
| 4 | Do not stab down with a spade. A wounded tuber soon turns soft |
| 5 | Shake off the soil lightly. Do not wash with water |
| 6 | Dig only as much as you need, and leave the rest covered with soil |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The single most important rule of harvesting is not to dig it all up at once. From the moment it is dug, the Jerusalem artichoke starts losing moisture and shrivels within 2-4 weeks at room temperature. While still in the ground, on the other hand, it stays no different from freshly dug even after months. So digging only what you will eat this week, and digging again next week, is not laziness with the Jerusalem artichoke—it is the best storage strategy there is.

### 12-3. Sorting After Harvest

| Category | Treatment |
|---|---|
| Large, unblemished ones | Eat right away or put into the root cellar |
| Medium-sized ones | Slice and dry into chips and flour |
| Small ones (30-50g) | Set aside separately as seed tubers |
| Damaged ones | Eat first. Do not store |
| Soil | Just shake it off. Washing halves the storage life |

## 13. Seed Tuber Maintenance and Seed Saving

### 13-1. The Ground Itself Is the Seed Bank

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The Jerusalem artichoke's Seed longevity grade is E. That means storing it for several years as dry seed is impossible, but for this crop specifically, that is not a real weakness. The field itself is the seed bank, and that bank grows larger on its own every year. If you let winter kill the potato's seed potatoes, next year's crop is gone; but the Jerusalem artichoke's seed tubers do not disappear unless you dig up the entire field.

| Item | Standard |
|---|---|
| Seed tuber size | 30-50g, egg-sized |
| Collection time | March, dug just before planting |
| Selection | Firm, with live eyes, and no signs of disease |
| Storage | Best to plant immediately after digging |
| Multiplication rate | Dozens from one. Multiplies dozens of times over in a single year |
| Degeneration | Propagated vegetatively, yet there are almost no reports of viral degeneration |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This very high multiplication rate matters a great deal in practice. Plant ten tubers, and by that autumn you will have hundreds. The following year, thousands. Even if seed tubers are hard to obtain early in a disaster, securing just a handful is enough to fill a substantial area within two years. This is a fundamentally different trait from a crop for which you must buy seed every year.

### 13-2. Propagating by True Seed

| Item | Details |
|---|---|
| Seed set | Seeds rarely ripen fully in South Korea's climate |
| Germination | Germination rate is low and irregular |
| Traits | Offspring do not come out the same as the parent plant |
| Practicality | Not used for propagation |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;For the Jerusalem artichoke, propagation by true seed is effectively meaningless. In South Korea, autumn frost often falls before the seed has fully ripened, and even when it does ripen, the germination rate is low. Propagation happens entirely through tubers. However, this does cause the Jerusalem artichoke in a given area to become genetically almost uniform, which could become a vulnerability if a particular disease were ever to appear. Bringing in seed tubers from several different places and planting them mixed together can reduce that risk to some extent.

## 14. Inulin and Precautions

### 14-1. It Is Not Toxic. But It Will Make You Sick the First Time

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The Jerusalem artichoke contains no toxic substances. There is no danger comparable to cassava's cyanogenic glycosides or the potato's solanine, and eating it raw causes no poisoning. Even so, this section exists on its own because someone eating it for the first time who eats a full meal of it will almost certainly experience severe abdominal bloating and pain. In a disaster, a hungry person finishing a bowl of Jerusalem artichoke and suffering all night is something that actually happens. This is a matter of digestive physiology, not poison, but to the person experiencing it, there is no telling the difference.

| Stage | What Happens in the Body |
|---|---|
| 1 | Inulin is a polysaccharide made of fructose linked in chains |
| 2 | Human digestive enzymes cannot break this bond |
| 3 | It passes through the stomach and small intestine unchanged and reaches the large intestine |
| 4 | Bacteria in the large intestine ferment the inulin |
| 5 | This process produces large amounts of hydrogen, carbon dioxide, and methane |
| 6 | The abdomen swells and fills with gas, bringing cramping pain and diarrhea |

### 14-2. Symptoms and Adaptation

| Amount Eaten | Someone Unaccustomed | Someone Adapted |
|---|---|---|
| 50g or less | Usually no symptoms | No symptoms |
| 100-150g | Mild bloating | No symptoms |
| 200-300g | Noticeable bloating, frequent gas, abdominal discomfort | Mild bloating |
| 500g or more | Severe abdominal pain, diarrhea, lasting several hours | Bloating and gas |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;What matters is that this reaction adapts over time. Eating inulin consistently changes the composition of the gut bacteria so that fermentation becomes gentler, and within a few weeks, symptoms drop sharply even at the same intake. So if you plan to rely on the Jerusalem artichoke as food, your body should be trained beforehand—or from early in a disaster—by starting with small amounts, not after the disaster has already struck. Starting at 50g a day and increasing over 2 weeks works well.

| Group | Caution |
|---|---|
| Children | Intake per unit of body weight is larger, so start with even smaller amounts |
| The elderly | If gut function is weak, symptoms last longer |
| People with irritable bowel syndrome | Symptoms are especially severe. Limit to small amounts |
| Someone already having diarrhea | Do not feed it to them. It worsens dehydration |
| People with diabetes | Actually advantageous, since it does not spike blood sugar quickly |

### 14-3. Ways to Reduce Gas

| Method | Mechanism | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Long boiling | Heat and acid break some of the inulin chains | Medium |
| Boiling with vinegar added | The acid speeds up hydrolysis | Medium |
| Lactic fermentation | Bacteria break down the inulin beforehand | Large |
| Cooking after cold storage | Natural breakdown proceeds during storage | Medium |
| Long-term drying | Some breakdown occurs during drying and rehydration | Small |
| Small amounts, often | The gut bacteria adapt | Largest |

> **The most reliable method is fermentation. Soak it in brine and let it lactic-ferment for 1-2 weeks, and bacteria break down the inulin beforehand, greatly reducing the amount that reaches your large intestine. It becomes a side dish and solves the digestive burden at the same time.**

### 14-4. Other Precautions

| Item | Details |
|---|---|
| Raw consumption | Possible, but bloating is worse. Better to eat it cooked |
| Leaves and stem | Not eaten by people. Can be used as livestock feed |
| Spoiled tubers | Discard if softened and foul-smelling |
| Vigor | Not toxic, but the field layout collapses without management |
| Allelopathy | Suppresses the growth of neighboring crops |

## 15. Storage Without Refrigeration

### 15-1. The Ground Itself Is the Storehouse

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Unlike the potato, the Jerusalem artichoke has no thick cork layer. Dug up and left at room temperature, it loses moisture quickly from its surface and shrivels within a few days to two weeks. This is why its Storability grade is C. Yet that same tuber stays fine for months on end while it remains in the ground, because the soil holds both temperature and humidity steady. So the first principle of storing the Jerusalem artichoke is not building a storehouse, but not digging it up.

| Storage Factor | Potato | Jerusalem Artichoke |
|---|---|---|
| Biggest enemy | Rot, sprouting, greening | Drying out |
| Room-temperature limit | 1-2 months | 2-4 weeks |
| Best method | Root-cellar pit storage | Leaving it undug in the ground |
| Target humidity | 85-95% | 90% or more |

### 15-2. Overwintering Storage in Place

| Item | Details |
|---|---|
| Principle | Leave it undug, in the ground. A living storehouse |
| Method | After the first frost, cut the above-ground growth and cover with 20-30cm of straw or fallen leaves |
| Duration | All winter, effectively until the following spring |
| Temperature | Held at soil temperature. The tubers survive even at -20°C outside air temperature |
| Advantages | Needs no electricity, no facilities, and no labor. Freshness is at its highest |
| Drawbacks | Hard to dig once the ground freezes deep. Becomes a target for rodents and wild boar |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The purpose of mulching is not to keep the tubers from freezing. Jerusalem artichoke tubers do not die even if they freeze. The purpose is to prevent a situation where the soil above them freezes as hard as stone and a spade cannot get in. A layer of straw or fallen leaves 20 to 30cm deep keeps the soil beneath it soft even in the depths of winter. You can also simply cover it with the stems cut in autumn.

### 15-3. Power-Free Storage After Digging

| Method | How To | Temperature | Duration | Caution |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Root-cellar/pit storage | Bury in layers of moist sand, soil, or sawdust | 0-4°C | 2-3 months | Maintain 85-95% humidity |
| Pit storage | Dig 30cm and mound up in layers—straw, tubers, straw, soil—like a burial mound | 0-5°C | 1-2 months | Make an air vent at the top with a bundle of straw |
| Sand box | Layer dry sand and tubers in a box | Cool indoor space | 1-2 months | If the sand gets too dry, sprinkle it lightly with water |
| Wrapping in damp cloth | Wrap in cloth or newspaper and keep in a cool place | Cool indoor space | 2-3 weeks | Use only for small quantities |
| Room temperature, as is | Not recommended | - | 1-2 weeks | Shrivels soon |

### 15-4. How to Build Pit Storage

| Step | Details |
|---|---|
| 1 | Choose a raised spot where water will not pool. A north-facing slope is good |
| 2 | Dig shallow: 1-1.5m across, 30cm deep |
| 3 | Lay 10cm of dry straw on the bottom |
| 4 | Pile the shaken-off tubers into a mound shape, no more than 50cm high |
| 5 | Fill the gaps with moist sand or leaf mold to prevent drying out |
| 6 | Cover with 20-30cm of straw, then 15cm of soil on top, and pat it down |
| 7 | Stand a bundle of straw upright at the top to make an air vent |
| 8 | Dig a drainage ditch around it |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The difference from a potato pit is step 5. Dry straw alone is enough for the potato, but doing the same for the Jerusalem artichoke will let it dry out inside. You need to pack the gaps with sand or leaf mold dampened just enough to clump when squeezed in your hand, without water seeping out. However, this also raises the risk of rot, so be sure to make the air vent and open it to check once every two weeks.

### 15-5. Very Long-Term Storage Through Processing

| Method | Procedure | Duration | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dried chips | Slice thin, 2-3mm, and dry completely in the sun or with residual heat from a stove | Months to years | Volume and weight are greatly reduced |
| Flour | Grind fully dried chips in a mortar or millstone | 1-2 years | Seal airtight, with ash or charcoal added as a desiccant |
| Lactic fermentation | Soak in 2-3% brine at room temperature for 1-2 weeks | Months | Greatly reduces the digestive burden |
| Jangajji | Bury in soy sauce or doenjang | Months to 1 year | Becomes a preserved side dish |
| Vinegar pickling | Soak in boiled vinegar water | Months | The sourness helps break down the inulin |

> **The standard storage strategy is to combine several methods. Leave most of it in the ground, turn some into dried chips and flour as a hedge against animals and severe cold, and ferment some into a side dish with a reduced digestive burden.**

## 16. Cooking and Processing

### 16-1. Fuel-Saving Cooking

| Method | Fuel Use | Procedure | Notes |
|---|:---:|---|---|
| Raw | None | Wash, slice, and eat | Crisp and sweet, but causes severe bloating |
| Boiling | Little | In water for 10-15 minutes | Cooks faster than the potato, since it has less starch |
| Steaming | Little | With a little water, for 15 minutes | Richer flavor and less nutrient loss |
| Residual-heat cooking | Very little | Boil 5 minutes, turn off the heat, wrap in cloth for 30 minutes | Saves a great deal of fuel |
| Baking in ashes | Very little | Bury in the leftover ashes of a stove for 20-30 minutes | Bake unpeeled, skin and all |
| Cooking as flour | Little | Stir into boiling water and it cooks instantly | The most fuel-efficient of all |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The reason Cooking & processing is graded A is that 10 minutes of heating is enough, and it can even be eaten raw. In a situation where fuel is extremely scarce, this is a considerable advantage. That said, for the reasons covered earlier, eating it raw causes the worst bloating, and the longer it boils, the more the inulin breaks down and the lighter the burden becomes. It amounts to a trade-off between fuel and your stomach. Boil it longer before your body is used to it, and cook it briefly once it is.

### 16-2. Taste and How to Eat It

| Item | Details |
|---|---|
| Taste | Raw, it is crisp and sweetish, like a cross between pear and lotus root |
| Cooked taste | Softens and becomes sweeter. Unlike the potato, it has no starchy texture |
| Skin | No need to peel. Just scrub off the soil with a brush |
| Discoloration | Browns quickly once cut. Soaking in vinegar water reduces this |
| Pairings | Works in doenjang soup, braises, tempura, and pickles alike |
| Broth | The boiling water holds dissolved sugars and potassium, so use it too |

### 16-3. Processing That Doubles as Storage

| Processing | Procedure | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Jerusalem artichoke chips | Slice thin and dry completely | Eat as is, or soak in water and add to soup |
| Inulin flour | Grind the dried chips | Mix into grain flour, or cook into porridge |
| Tea | Slice, dry, then roast and steep | Has a savory, roasted flavor. A winter drink |
| Lactic-fermented pickles | Soak in brine for 1-2 weeks | Becomes a side dish, with less digestive burden |
| Jangajji | Bury in doenjang or soy sauce | Keeps for months, and doubles as a salty source of salt |
| Syrup | Simmer for a long time to break down the inulin | Becomes a fructose syrup. Used for sweetening |

## 17. Annual Work Calendar

| Month | Task | Key Point |
|---|---|---|
| January | Dig as much as needed, check stored stock | Pull back the straw, dig, then re-cover |
| February | Plan the field, turn the compost | Decide where the new plot and its boundary barrier will go |
| March | Collect and select seed tubers, begin planting | Plant as soon as the ground thaws |
| April | Finish planting, mulch | No watering needed |
| May | Check emergence, first pinching, weeding | Cut the top shoot once the plant is about 1m tall |
| June | Finish weeding | Weeds disappear on their own after this |
| July | Second pinching, prepare against lodging | Keep the height to around 1.5m |
| August | Pinch off flower buds, prepare for typhoons | Tubers have not formed yet |
| September | Tuber bulking begins; water only in severe drought | Tubers start to fill out from this point |
| October | Watch for flowering, prepare for harvest | No need to rush |
| November | Cut above-ground growth after the first frost, first harvest, straw mulch 20-30cm | Dig only as much as you need |
| December | Harvest as needed, dry and ferment for processing | Move some indoors as a precaution against animal damage |

### 17-1. Five Things to Remember

| Item | Details |
|---|---|
| Small amounts the first time | Inulin causes severe gas and abdominal pain. Start at 50g and increase over 2 weeks |
| The planting spot cannot be undone | Once planted, eradication is nearly impossible. Plant it on a border or scrap of land, together with a boundary barrier |
| Wait for the first frost | Before frost, tubers are small and bland. Harvesting before September is a loss |
| Not digging it up is storage | Left in the ground, it stays fine for months; dug up, it shrivels within 2 weeks |
| Do not fertilize it | Nitrogen just makes it taller while the tubers get smaller. Give your compost to other crops instead |
