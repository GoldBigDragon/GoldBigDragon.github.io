## Introduction

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The Jerusalem artichoke (Helianthus tuberosus) is also known in Korean as ttungttanji. It is a perennial herb in the daisy family, in the same genus as the sunflower, and has no relation to the potato. It originated in central North America and was introduced in the late Joseon dynasty to be grown as livestock feed, from which it escaped into the fields. Today it grows taller than a person along roadsides, riverbanks, and abandoned fields across South Korea, putting out yellow flowers in autumn. No one plants it and no one fertilizes it, yet it comes up again in the same spot every year. Most people in South Korea who have seen a Jerusalem artichoke have seen it by the roadside, not in a field.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;As a semi-wild plant, the Jerusalem artichoke scores SS Overall, 58.8 points, in the disaster-preparedness evaluation. All three axes—Short-term SS, Long-term SS, and Supplementary SS—rank at the very top, ahead of most grain crops. The reason is clear from the table: Fertilizer demand SS, Water use SS, Infrastructure needs SS, Temperature range SS, Cold hardiness SS, Perenniality SS, Continuous-cropping tolerance SS, Prevalence in South Korea SS, and Tillage & digging energy SS. Eight of these nine SS grades are criteria that ask "what does a person have to put in?" The Jerusalem artichoke answers every one of them with "nothing." It needs no fertilizer, no water, no infrastructure, no pesticides, no seed purchase, and no tilling. If ordinary agricultural evaluation is a contest over how much a crop yields under good conditions, the disaster-preparedness evaluation asks what is left when no conditions are met at all. Change the event, and the winner changes too.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;But before taking this score at face value, there is something you should know. There are no official national yield statistics for the Jerusalem artichoke. No one tracks the area planted, the yield per unit area, or how much it varies from year to year. The yield used in this evaluation, 4,000kg per 1,000m², is an assumption, not an observation. The pest-and-disease loss rate of 50.6% is not a figure measured on the Jerusalem artichoke itself but a reliability-L6 estimate borrowed from the class Magnoliopsida (the dicots) as a whole, and the zero-input yield retention of 45.1% is likewise an imputed value, taken from the potato's organic-to-conventional yield ratio and applied across tuber crops (tubers/storage roots) in general. Even the calorie count differs between sources. The Korean Food Composition Table (national standard) puts it at 35kcal per 100g, while other sources give 68-76kcal—twice as much. This gap will be covered in detail later, but the point is this: that the Jerusalem artichoke grows with no help at all is something anyone can confirm by looking. How many kg and how many kcal come out of that field is something no one has yet properly counted.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;And the Jerusalem artichoke has two problems that the grade table does not show well. One happens inside the body of the person eating it. Jerusalem artichoke starch takes the form of inulin, which humans cannot digest. It passes unchanged down to the large intestine, where bacteria ferment it, producing large amounts of gas in the process. Someone eating it for the first time who finishes a whole plate will feel bloated and suffer severe stomach pain a few hours later. In a starving situation, eating a full meal of it and then suffering all night is something that actually happens. The other problem happens in the field. Once planted, it cannot be eliminated. If even a piece the size of a fingernail is left in the soil, it comes back in the same spot the following year. This is the flip side of that Perenniality SS grade.
