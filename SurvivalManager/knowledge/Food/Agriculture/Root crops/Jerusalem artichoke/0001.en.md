## Introduction
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Jerusalem artichoke (Helianthus tuberosus), also called ttungttanji in Korean, is a perennial of the sunflower genus in the aster family and has nothing to do with potato. It came from central North America, was brought to Korea in the late Joseon period as fodder and escaped from the fields, and now grows taller than a person along roadsides, riverbanks, and abandoned fields all over the country. In the disaster preparedness evaluation it scores an overall SS (58.8 points, 3rd of 183 valid species), and the short-term, long-term, and supplementary scores are all SS as well. Most of the nine items rated SS ask what people must put in. If ordinary evaluation is a contest of how much a crop yields under good conditions, the disaster preparedness evaluation is a contest of what is left when no conditions exist at all, and when the event changes the winner changes too. However, this score rests on shallow evidence, a first-time eater suffers severe gas and abdominal pain from inulin, and once planted it cannot be removed.

> **Jerusalem artichoke ranks high not because it yields a lot but because it is never wiped out in any year.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Calories per area B is 1,400 kcal per ㎡, 0.54 times the self-sufficiency line, which means one person would need nearly 200 ㎡ of field to live on it, lower than potato. It is still SS because it needs no fertilizer, irrigation, or facilities, it comes back by itself from leftover tubers so that digging energy is amortized over 10 years (22 kcal a year; calories returned 134 times, potato 10.6 times), and a temperature range of 25℃ together with cold tolerance below -20℃ means that in a bad year it only shrinks and never disappears. What is needed in a disaster is not the maximum yield of a good year but the minimum guaranteed in a bad year. The grade for each item is in the Crop Evaluation Results document.

| Item | Displayed value | Actual basis |
|:---|:---|:---|

Filling 2,100 kcal a day would take 6 kg, so Jerusalem artichoke is not a staple but a crop that supplements a staple and keeps people from starving in a year without one.

## 2. Plant Overview
| Item | Details |
|:---|:---|
| Classification | Perennial of the sunflower genus in the aster family. Native to central and eastern North America |
| Leaves and stem | Broadly ovate leaves with pointed tips and toothed edges. The stem stands upright, grows to 1.5-3 m, and has bristly hairs |
| Flowers | In August-October, yellow flower heads 5-8 cm across (like small sunflowers) bloom |
| Tubers | Knobby, ginger-shaped, in red, white, or purplish colors. They form at the root tips and the tips of rhizomes |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In April, when the ground thaws, shoots rise from the eyes of the tubers, in May-July the stem grows several centimeters a day, and in August-October it flowers. The tubers swell late: all summer the plant grows only stems and leaves, tubers begin to set from late September as the days shorten, and they are largest and sweetest after frost. Per 100 g there are 35 kcal (Korean Standard Food Composition Table), about 17 g carbohydrate (mostly inulin, almost no starch), 2.18 g protein, 0.01 g fat, 6.37 g sugars (which rise as inulin breaks down during storage and heating), and about 630 mg potassium, more than potato or sweet potato. Inulin is a polysaccharide of long chains of fructose that human digestive enzymes cannot cut.

## 3. Growing Environment and Varieties
| Condition | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Temperature | Growth 5-30℃ (optimum 18-25℃), a range of 25℃. It survives underground at -20 to -30℃ and overwinters throughout Korea with no protection. Germination needs 5-10℃ or more, so it can be planted as soon as the ground thaws |
| Water and light | Can be grown without irrigation and needs moisture only during tuber swelling. 6 hours of sunlight a day or more (optimum 8 hours or more), and under 40% shade the yield is about 80% |
| Soil | pH 5.0-8.0 (optimum 5.8-7.0), hardly selective. Well-drained sandy loam to loam is optimum; in clay the tubers deform and are hard to dig |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;It is in fact weak against excess moisture: if water stands for a few days the tubers soften and soft rot sets in, so this is the one condition to watch in the growing environment. Good places are riverbanks, levees, and mounds beside ditches (close to water but never standing in it), abandoned fields and gravelly poor ground, behind houses, outside fences, and foothills, and field edges that double as windbreaks; avoid the middle of a vegetable plot, waterlogged lowlands, and main fields that must be plowed often.

> **Jerusalem artichoke is not a crop for good fields but a crop that turns land that is not a field into a field. And once planted, it is hard to undo.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The variety system is loose, and wild and native strains have the strongest propagation and the highest suitability for disasters; if dug in autumn from a riverbank or roadside they become seed tubers at once. There are also Fuseau, which is easy to trim, early Stampede and Skorospelka for cold regions, and Dwarf Sunray, which resists wind.

## 4. Field Preparation and Planting
| Item | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Land preparation | Plow to 20-30 cm (the deeper, the larger the tubers and the easier the digging), with beds at least 30 cm high and drainage ditches at least 30 cm deep. Separate it into its own plot and bury boards or ditches 40-60 cm deep as a boundary barrier, which is essential (rhizomes spread nearly 1 m a year and take over the neighboring field within two or three years) |
| Fertilization (1,000 ㎡) | Minimize nitrogen (too much makes only leaves grow lush and the tubers fewer), phosphorus 3-4 kg, potassium 3-5 kg (most important for tuber swelling). It is 25% or less of potato's amount and grows without any, so give limited compost to potato or Chinese cabbage instead. Mulch with fallen leaves or straw 5-10 cm |
| Disaster substitute fertilizers | Wood ash (a handful per ㎡), fallen-leaf mulch, and the stems cut in autumn laid back on the spot (the easiest self-supplied fertilizer; potassium circulates and in winter it acts as mulch). Do not use diluted urine, because nitrogen is harmful |
| Seed tubers | Egg-sized, 30-50 g (if small, early growth is late). Cut large ones so that each piece has 2-3 eyes, coat the cut surface with wood ash, and dry it in the shade for a day to heal. Exclude diseased or soft ones, and planting right after digging is best |
| Planting | In spring, as soon as the ground thaws in March-May; in autumn, October-November (they overwinter as they are and sprout the next spring, so planting them instead of storing them is the surest). Depth 5-10 cm, 30-45 cm between plants, 60-70 cm between rows, eyes up. Cover with soil and press lightly; no irrigation is needed |

## 5. Water Management and Cultivation Management
| Stage | Water management |
|:---|:---|
| Right after planting | Water once thoroughly |
| Vegetative growth (May-July) | Leave to natural rainfall (excess moisture is actually harmful) |
| Tuber swelling (September-October) | Add water only in severe drought (only dryness in this period affects yield) |
| Maturity (November onward) | Stop irrigating |

| Care | Method |
|:---|:---|
| Topping | Cut the top shoot in May when the plant is about 1 m tall and again in July, and pick off flower buds in August-September, to hold the height to about 1.5 m (this prevents nutrient loss and lodging in autumn typhoons and sends nutrients to the tubers). Use the cut shoots as mulch |
| Lodging prevention | Hilling at the base, a cord around several plants tied together, dense planting, and a fence or another crop on the windward side |
| Other care | Weeding once or twice in May-June is enough (once the plants are tall, weeds disappear on their own). When cutting the aboveground part in autumn, push a spade deep along the plot boundary to cut rhizomes that have crossed over; this boundary check is the only task that must never be skipped in any year |
| Signs of excess water | If lower leaves turn yellow and the stem base turns black, clear the drainage ditches at once; if the base softens and white mycelium appears (southern blight), pull that plant and burn it |

## 6. Pests, Diseases, and Nutrient Problems
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The diseases below rarely wipe out a field. Because wild populations have persisted for decades with no pest control, drainage, ventilation, and pulling and burning any diseased plant on the spot as soon as it is found are enough. In a disaster what threatens Jerusalem artichoke is not germs but animals.

| Pest or disease | Symptoms and conditions | Control without chemicals |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Southern blight, sclerotinia rot | White mycelium spreads at the base of the stem and the plant suddenly wilts (hot and humid) / The stem softens and white cottony mycelium and black sclerotia form (cool and damp) | Improve drainage, remove and burn infected plants, and put wood ash on the spot / Ensure ventilation and rotate on a 3-4 year cycle |
| Rust, powdery mildew, bacterial soft rot | Orange-brown powder on leaf undersides (rainy season) / White powder on leaf surfaces / Tubers soften and smell foul (wounds at harvest, excess moisture) | Ensure ventilation and remove infected leaves / Milk diluted 10 times or baking soda solution (5 g per 1 L of water) / Dig without wounding and dry; discard diseased tubers at once |
| Aphids | Suck sap from new shoots | Avoid excess nitrogen and protect ladybugs; wash with water, soapy water, wood ash |
| Field mice, wild boar | Eat large numbers of tubers underground | Clear weeds around the field, bury wire mesh or boards around the plot, and fence it. It is the greatest risk of in-ground storage, so split the crop across several plots instead of keeping it all in one place |

| Symptom | Cause and action |
|:---|:---|
| Only leaves and stems grow lush and tubers are small (height over 3 m) | Nitrogen excess, the most common problem. Cut off nitrogen and balance with topping |
| Leaf edges scorch brown (during tuber swelling) / Leaves turn purplish (cold periods) | Potassium deficiency / phosphorus deficiency. Wood ash / bone meal and wood ash |

## 7. Intercropping and Crop Rotation
| Compatibility | Companions |
|:---|:---|
| Good | Windbreak border crops (tall enough to block wind), nectar plants (the autumn flowers gather bees), fodder plots (leaves and stems are used as animal feed) |
| Fair | None. Because of allelopathy, intercropping itself does not suit it, and growing in its own plot is the rule |
| Poor | Beans, tomatoes, and most vegetables (allelopathic compounds suppress the growth of neighbors within 1 m), and any crop in the middle of the field (the rhizomes invade and push them out) |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Continuous-cropping tolerance SS means it may be left in the same place permanently, and that is the basic way to grow it; move it only when southern blight becomes severe. To move a plot you must remove the shoots that keep coming up in the old place for 3-5 years. The flip side of perenniality SS is that once planted it cannot be removed: one plant scatters dozens of tubers over more than 1 m in every direction in a year, so even a fingernail-sized piece left behind sprouts the next year. Digging out once is of low effect, and the most effective way is to starve it. If you keep cutting the shoots before the leaves open so that the tubers cannot refill their stores, density falls sharply in 2-3 years, and covering the ground completely with thick cloth or boards for 1-2 years or letting pigs graze on it is also highly effective. Even so, complete eradication is realistically difficult.

> **Planting Jerusalem artichoke is an irreversible decision. Plant it at a boundary or on odd corners of land, not in the middle of a field, and burying a barrier from the start is the only easy remedy.**

## 8. Harvest and Seed Saving
| Harvest | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Timing | The best time is after the first frost (the aboveground part dies and inulin and sweetness peak, and in the cold part of the inulin breaks into shorter chains and tastes sweeter). They can be dug at any time for more than four months, from mid-November to March, and seed tubers are dug in March. Before September the tubers have not set and most of the yield is lost |
| Method | Cut the aboveground stems at the base and lay them on the spot, then lift the soil with a hoe or three-pronged fork to expose the tubers. Do not strike with a spade (wounded tubers soon soften). Only brush off the soil and do not wash (washing halves the storage time) |
| Sorting | Eat large unblemished ones right away or put them in a cellar, slice medium ones and dry them into chips and flour, and set aside small ones of 30-50 g as seed tubers. Moisture starts leaving the moment they are dug, so the best storage strategy is to dig only what you will eat this week |

| Seed saving | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Seed tubers | Seed longevity is E, but the tubers in the ground are themselves the seed store. Dig firm tubers of 30-50 g with live eyes and no symptoms in March just before planting and plant them at once |
| Multiplication | Plant ten tubers and there are hundreds by autumn and thousands the next year, enough to fill a considerable area in two years. Virus degeneration is almost never reported |
| Propagation by seed | In Korea's climate seeds seldom ripen and germinate poorly, so they are not used. Because propagation is by tubers alone, the stock tends to become genetically uniform, so bring seed tubers from several places and plant them mixed |

## 9. Storage
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Unlike potato, Jerusalem artichoke has no thick cork layer, so dug tubers shrivel in 2-4 weeks at room temperature (within days at the fastest); the greatest enemy is therefore drying, not rot. The first principle of storage is not to build a store but not to dig.

| Method | How | Period |
|:---|:---|:---|
| In-ground overwinter storage | Do not harvest; leave in the ground. After the first frost cut the aboveground part and cover with 20-30 cm of straw or fallen leaves (tubers do not die when frozen; the cover is to keep the ground from freezing rock-hard so a spade cannot enter). The drawback is that it becomes a target for field mice and wild boar | All winter, until the next spring |
| Cellar or earth cellar storage | Layer in moist sand, soil, or sawdust, and keep at 0-4℃ with 85-95% humidity | 2-3 months |
| Pit storage | Dig 30 cm and pile straw, tubers, straw, and soil in a mound; unlike potato, fill between the tubers with moist sand or leaf mold so they do not dry out, cover with 20-30 cm of straw and 15 cm of soil, make a vent at the top with a straw bundle, and open it to check every 2 weeks | 1-2 months |
| Dried chips, flour | Slice 2-3 mm thin and dry completely in the sun or on the residual heat of a stove. Volume and weight fall sharply. Keep flour sealed with ash or charcoal as a desiccant | Chips several months to years, flour 1-2 years |
| Lactic fermentation, pickles, vinegar pickling | Room temperature in 2-3% salt water for 1-2 weeks / buried in soy sauce or doenjang / soaked in boiled vinegar water (the sourness helps break down inulin). They become side dishes and ease digestion | Several months to 1 year |

> **The standard storage strategy is to combine methods. Leave most in the ground, turn some into dried chips and flour to guard against animals and severe cold, and ferment some into side dishes that ease the digestive burden.**

## 10. Cooking and Precautions
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Jerusalem artichoke contains no toxic substance and does not poison even when eaten raw, but a first-time eater who eats a full portion almost always suffers severe abdominal bloating and pain. Human digestive enzymes cannot cut the bonds of inulin, so it passes through the stomach and small intestine and reaches the colon, where bacteria ferment it and release large amounts of hydrogen, carbon dioxide, and methane. However, if you eat inulin steadily the composition of the colonic bacteria changes and symptoms fall sharply within a few weeks, so starting at 50 g a day and increasing over 2 weeks is a safe approach, and you should accustom the body with small amounts before a disaster hits or from its early days.

| Amount eaten | Person not used to it | Person who has adapted |
|:---|:---|:---|
| 100-150 g | Mild bloating | No symptoms |
| 200-300 g | Clear bloating, frequent flatulence, abdominal discomfort | Mild bloating |
| 500 g or more | Severe abdominal pain and diarrhea lasting several hours | Bloating and flatulence |

| Precaution | Details |
|:---|:---|
| Who | Children should start with less because their intake per body weight is larger, people with irritable bowel syndrome should limit it to small amounts, and it should not be given to someone who already has diarrhea. For people with diabetes it is actually favorable because it does not raise blood sugar quickly |
| Reducing gas | Eating small amounts often has the greatest effect, and lactic fermentation in salt water for 1-2 weeks is the surest because bacteria break down the inulin in advance. Long boiling, boiling with vinegar, cooking after cold storage, and long drying also help somewhat |
| Other precautions | Raw is possible but bloating is worse, so it is better eaten cooked; the leaves and stems are not eaten by people but can be used as animal fodder. Discard tubers that turn soft and smell foul |

| Cooking | Method |
|:---|:---|
| Saving fuel | A heating time of 10 minutes is enough. Boil 10-15 minutes in water, steam 15 minutes with a little water, for residual-heat cooking boil 5 minutes, turn off the heat, and wrap in cloth for 30 minutes, roast in the skin buried in hot ash for 20-30 minutes, and flour stirred into boiling water cooks at once. The longer it is boiled the more the inulin breaks down and the lighter the burden, so boil it long until accustomed and cook it short afterward |
| Taste and how to eat | Raw it is crisp and sweetish, like pear mixed with lotus root; cooked it softens, becomes sweeter, and has no starchy feel. Do not peel it; scrub off the soil with a brush, and since it browns when cut, keep it in vinegar water. Doenjang soup, braising, frying, and pickling are all possible, and the cooking water holds sugar and potassium, so use it as broth |
| Processing | Eat dried chips as they are or soak them and add to soup; mix flour milled from the chips into grain flour or cook it as porridge; roast and brew for tea. Long simmering breaks down the inulin and makes fructose syrup |

## 11. Annual Work Schedule
| Month | Work |
|:---|:---|
| Jan-Feb | Dig only what you need to eat (remove the straw, dig, and cover again) and check stored tubers. In February turn the compost and decide on the spot and barrier for a new plot |
| Mar-Apr | In March collect and sort seed tubers and start planting as soon as the ground thaws. In April finish planting and mulch (no irrigation needed) |
| May-June | In May check emergence, make the first topping (at about 1 m tall), and weed. In June finish weeding (afterward weeds disappear on their own) |
| July-Aug | In July make the second topping and prepare against lodging (about 1.5 m tall). In August pick flower buds and prepare for typhoons |
| Sep-Oct | In September tubers begin to thicken, and irrigate only in severe drought. In October watch flowering and prepare for harvest (do not rush) |
| Nov-Dec | In November cut the aboveground part after the first frost, make the first harvest, and mulch with 20-30 cm of straw (dig only what you need). In December harvest what is needed and dry and ferment it (move some indoors against animal damage) |

> **Jerusalem artichoke is a crop to start eating in small amounts from 50 g, to plant at a boundary or on odd corners of land with a barrier because it cannot be undone, to dig only what you need after the first frost, to treat leaving it undug in the ground as storage, and to grow without fertilizer.**
