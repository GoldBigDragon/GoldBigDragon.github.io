&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The Jerusalem artichoke's overall grade is SS, 58.8 points. By scenario, it is Short-term SS (57.7), Long-term SS (60.0), and Supplementary SS (71.7). The Supplementary score is unusually high, because the calculation weighs nutrient density relative to calories more heavily than the calories themselves. As covered later, this figure of 71.7 needs some interpretation.

### 1-3. Why a Near-Wild Plant Outranks the Grain Crops

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The Jerusalem artichoke's Calories per area is only a B. At 1,400kcal/m², that is 0.54× the self-sufficiency line, meaning a person living on this alone would need close to 200m² of field. That is lower than the potato and a third of cassava's figure. Speed to harvest is also a D. Yet the reason it still scores SS Overall is that this evaluation measures something other than yield.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The first reason is inputs. Fertilizer demand SS means no fertilizer at all is needed. Water use SS means the crop can be grown on natural rainfall alone, with no irrigation; total evapotranspiration runs about 100L/m², only around 20% of the potato's. Infrastructure needs SS means no plastic film, no greenhouse, and no stakes are needed. In a disaster, these three are not mere cost savings but the line between possible and impossible. When nitrogen fertilizer cannot be obtained, there is no electricity to run a pump, and plastic film cannot be bought, a heavy-feeding grain crop simply cannot be planted at all. The Jerusalem artichoke is one of the few crops that can still be planted under those conditions.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The second reason is perenniality. An annual grain crop must be tilled, sown, and its seed protected every single year. If even one of these three tasks fails, that year's entire crop is lost. The Jerusalem artichoke comes back on its own from leftover tubers. Because the digging energy is amortized over 10 years, it comes to only 22kcal a year, and the energy returned per unit of digging energy invested is 134×. For the potato, it is 10.6×. When the human body is the only source of power, a twelvefold difference translates directly into a difference in how much land can be worked.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The third reason is environmental adaptation. A temperature range of 25°C, cold hardiness down below -20°C, tolerance of poor soil, and naturalized growth across all of South Korea: put these four together, and the result is that "whatever kind of year it was, the Jerusalem artichoke survived it." A single cold spell or a single drought can empty an entire grain field. The Jerusalem artichoke only shrinks in a bad year; it does not disappear. What a disaster situation calls for is not the maximum yield of a good year but the guaranteed minimum of a bad one.

> **The Jerusalem artichoke ranks so high not because it yields a lot, but because it never fails completely, in any year.**

### 1-4. But the Evidence Behind This Score Is Thin

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The Jerusalem artichoke lies outside official statistics. It is not a crop covered by the national crop-condition survey, and there is no long-term trial data for it. Many cells in the evaluation table are filled not with observations but with estimates and imputed values.

| Item | Value shown | What the evidence actually is |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Calories per area | 4,000kg/1,000m² | There is no official yield statistic. This is an assumed figure for good conditions |
| Calories per 100g | 35kcal | Other sources give 68-76kcal—twice as much |
| Pest & disease pressure | Estimated yield loss 50.6% | Not a figure measured on the Jerusalem artichoke, but a reliability-L6 estimate borrowed from the class Magnoliopsida as a whole |
| Pest-control self-reliance | C | Marked down together with the loss-rate figure because its basis is weak. In practice, almost no pest control is needed |
| Yield retention without synthetic inputs | 45.1% | An imputed value, taken from the potato's organic-to-conventional yield ratio and applied across tuber crops in general |
| Corroboration coefficient | 0.986 | Means 1 of the 25 cells was filled by a lower-tier imputation |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The difference in calorie figures matters in particular. Most of the Jerusalem artichoke's carbohydrate is inulin, and inulin is not broken down by human digestive enzymes. It is not absorbed in the small intestine but passes down to the large intestine, where bacteria ferment it, and in that process the body gains far fewer calories than the same weight of starch would give. The Korean Food Composition Table's 35kcal reflects this fact, while the 68-76kcal figures are calculated by treating the carbohydrate as if it were starch. The calories the body can actually use are closer to the lower figure.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;And this fact calls for a second look at the Supplementary SS (71.7) grade. The S grade for protein density comes from 24.9%, that is, 2.18g of protein divided by 35kcal of energy. It is a ratio that is high because the denominator is small, not because the numerator is large. The absolute amount of protein gained from eating 100g of Jerusalem artichoke is 2.18g, only a little more than the potato. It looks excellent relative to calories, but that has to be read together with the fact that the calories themselves are low. Filling 2,100kcal a day with Jerusalem artichoke alone would require eating 6kg, which is not an amount a person can actually eat. That is what "6.0kg actually needed" in the Micronutrients row of the evaluation table means.

> **The Jerusalem artichoke is not a staple food. It is a crop that supplements the staple, and keeps you from starving in a year when the staple fails.**

## 2. What Kind of Plant Is This?

### 2-1. Taxonomy and Origin

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The Jerusalem artichoke is a perennial herb in the daisy family (Asteraceae), genus Helianthus (sunflower). It is in the same genus as the sunflower, and its flower looks like a small sunflower. It belongs to a different family and order from the potato altogether. The word "potato" appears in its Korean name, dwaejigamja (literally "pig potato"), only because it produces a tuber underground. The English-speaking world's name "Jerusalem artichoke" is just as groundless: the plant has nothing to do with Jerusalem, and it is a different plant from the artichoke.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;It is native to central and eastern North America, where it grew on disturbed ground at the edges of prairies, riverbanks, and woods. "Disturbed ground" here means places swept by floods, burned over by wildfire, or dug up by animals. Plants that survive in such places share a fixed strategy: grow tall fast to monopolize the light, build an underground storage organ to prepare for the next year, and regenerate even from a single leftover piece. Every trait of the Jerusalem artichoke—its height of up to 3m, its underground tubers, its fearsome capacity to spread—comes from this strategy. And this strategy is enormously advantageous to people in a disaster, because disturbed ground is exactly the kind of ground that is most common after a disaster.

### 2-2. Appearance

| Organ | Characteristics |
|---|---|
| Leaves | Broadly egg-shaped with a pointed tip and a toothed edge. The lower leaves are opposite, the upper ones alternate |
| Stem | Grows upright, up to 1.5-3m tall. Covered all over with rough, stiff hairs |
| Roots | Extend deep into the soil. Tubers form at the tips of the roots and the underground stems |
| Flowers | Bloom in August-October. Yellow flower heads, 5-8cm across, shaped like small sunflowers |
| Tubers | Knobby, shaped like ginger. Some are red, some white, some tinged purple |
| Seeds | Achenes form, but they are not used for propagation |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The knobby shape of the tubers, with their many fine roots, matters in practice. Unlike the potato's smooth skin, peeling them is tedious, and soil lodges in the crevices, making them hard to wash. For this reason, they are treated commercially as a labor-intensive crop. But they can be eaten unpeeled—the skin, in fact, is where much of the nutrition is—so this is not a problem in a disaster. Scrubbing off the soil with a brush is enough.

### 2-3. Growth Stages

| Stage | Timing | Key Physiology | Key Nutrients |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sprouting & establishment | April | Once the ground thaws, shoots rise from the eyes of the tubers | Phosphate, a small amount of nitrogen |
| Vegetative growth | May-July | The stem grows several cm a day | Nitrogen, potassium |
| Flowering | August-October | Yellow flowers bloom. The tubers begin to thicken around this time | Potassium |
| Tuber bulking | September-November | Sugars made in the leaves convert to inulin and accumulate underground | Potassium, phosphate |
| Maturity & storage | Around the first frost | The above-ground parts die back, leaving only the tubers. Inulin content peaks at this point | Stop irrigation |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;It matters that tuber bulking starts late. The plant spends the whole summer just growing stems and leaves, and only starts storing reserves underground once the days begin to shorten. So if you dig in August you find nothing; the tubers only start to form around late September, and they are largest and sweetest after frost has fallen. Digging too early, out of impatience, loses most of the yield. Waiting for the first frost is the governing rule of harvesting the Jerusalem artichoke.

### 2-4. Nutrition per 100g

| Nutrient | Amount | Notes |
|---|:---:|---|
| Calories | 35kcal (Korean Food Composition Table) | Other sources give 68-76kcal. The difference comes from how inulin's calories are calculated |
| Carbohydrate | About 17g | Mostly inulin. Almost no starch |
| Protein | 2.18g | Not a small amount for a tuber crop |
| Fat | 0.01g | Effectively none |
| Sugars | 6.37g | Rises as inulin breaks down during storage and cooking |
| Potassium | About 630mg | More than in the potato or sweet potato |
| Iron | Present | Relatively high among tuber crops |
| Vitamin C | Present | A significant amount is lost on cooking |
| Inulin | Abundant | A polysaccharide that humans cannot digest |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The key to understanding the Jerusalem artichoke is inulin. Inulin is a polysaccharide made of long chains of fructose, chemically different from the potato's starch. Human digestive enzymes can break down starch but cannot break down inulin. So inulin passes straight through the stomach and small intestine unchanged, reaches the large intestine, and becomes food for the bacteria living there. When this trait works in your favor, the result is more beneficial gut bacteria and a slower rise in blood sugar. When it works against you, the result is large amounts of gas and stomach pain. As covered in detail in Section 14, the first thing anyone planning to rely on the Jerusalem artichoke as disaster food needs to know is not how to grow it, but this trait.
