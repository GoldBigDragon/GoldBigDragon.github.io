## 7. Water Management

### 7-1. Standards by Growth Stage

| Timing | Water Management | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Right after planting | Water thoroughly once | Helps the roots establish. Almost no water is needed after this |
| Vegetative growth (May-July) | Leave it to natural rainfall | Waterlogging is actually harmful |
| Tuber bulking (September-October) | Supplement only in severe drought | Only dryness at this stage affects yield |
| Maturity (from November on) | Stop watering | Improves storability |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In growing the Jerusalem artichoke, water management effectively means "give it no water." Total evapotranspiration is only about 100L/m², which South Korea's rainfall comfortably covers. What actually needs attention is drainage. Because its deep roots draw on moisture from underground, it withstands drought well, but it cannot survive even a few days of standing water.

### 7-2. Symptoms of Too Little and Too Much Water

| Type | Symptom | Stage | Action |
|---|---|---|---|
| Deficiency | Leaves droop at midday and recover by evening | Normal range | No action needed. A common sight |
| Deficiency | Lower leaves dry up and drop | Moderate | Reinforce the mulch |
| Deficiency | Growth stops completely in September-October | Severe | Tuber bulking halts. This is the only time to water |
| Excess | Lower leaves turn yellow and the base of the stem blackens | Dangerous | Dig drainage furrows immediately |
| Excess | The base softens and white mycelium appears | Southern blight | Pull up and burn the affected plant |

## 8. Crop Management

### 8-1. Pinching

| Timing | Method | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| May | Once the plant is about 1m tall, cut off the top shoot | Side branches emerge, increasing leaf area and limiting height |
| July | Cut off the top shoot again | Prevents lodging and redirects nutrients to the tubers |
| August-September | Pinch off the flower buds | Nutrients that would go to flowering go to the tubers instead |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Pinching is optional, but its effect is clear. Left alone, the Jerusalem artichoke grows up to 3m, and the nutrients spent supporting that height are simply lost. What's more, a tall plant can be knocked flat entirely by an autumn typhoon, and once it falls over, photosynthesis stops and tuber bulking halts. Cutting off the top shoot once in May and once in July keeps the height to around 1.5m. Do not discard the cut shoots; use them as mulch material.

### 8-2. Support and Preventing Lodging

| Method | Details |
|---|---|
| Pinching | The most fundamental measure. Lowers the plant's height |
| Hilling | Mound soil up around the base. Improves root anchorage |
| Perimeter twine | Tie several plants together in a group with a length of twine around them |
| Dense planting | Planted close together, plants lean on each other and lodge less |
| Windbreak placement | Put a fence or another crop on the side the wind comes from |

### 8-3. Other Care

| Task | Timing | Details |
|---|---|---|
| Weeding | May-June, 1-2 times | Needed only early on. Once the plant is tall, weeds disappear on their own |
| Mulching | After planting | Fallen leaves and straw, 5-10cm |
| Boundary check | Once a year, in autumn | Cut off any underground stems that have extended outside the plot |
| Stem disposal | After the first frost | Cut and leave on the spot as cover, or use as mulch material |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The one piece of upkeep that must never be skipped, year after year, in growing the Jerusalem artichoke is the boundary check. Skipping any other task only reduces the yield a little, but skip the boundary check for a few years and the entire layout of the field collapses. It is enough, when cutting the above-ground growth in autumn, to push a spade in deep once along the plot boundary and sever any underground stems that have crossed over.

## 9. Pests and Diseases

### 9-1. Major Diseases

| Disease | Symptoms | Conditions | Response Without Chemicals |
|---|---|---|---|
| Southern blight | White mycelium spreads at the soil line, and the plant suddenly wilts and dies | Hot, humid conditions; poor drainage | Improve drainage, remove and burn infected plants, apply wood ash to the spot |
| Sclerotinia rot | The stem softens, and cottony white mycelium and black sclerotia form | Cool, damp weather | Ensure airflow, rotate crops every 3-4 years, remove diseased plants |
| Rust | An orange-brown powder forms on the underside of the leaves | During the monsoon season (jangma, late June to July in South Korea) | Ensure airflow, remove infected leaves. Its effect on yield is small |
| Powdery mildew | The leaf surface is covered with a white powder | Dry weather with large day-night temperature swings | Spray milk diluted 1:10, or diluted baking soda solution |
| Bacterial soft rot | The tuber softens and gives off a foul smell | Wounds at harvest, waterlogging | Dig without wounding the tubers and let them dry. Discard diseased tubers immediately |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The diseases listed in the table rarely wipe out a field in practice. Pest-control self-reliance was graded C, but that is a conservative figure assigned because the underlying data has low reliability, and in the field, spraying anything on the Jerusalem artichoke is rarely done. The fact that wild populations have persisted for decades with no pest control at all is the proof of this. The best response to disease is to secure drainage and open up airflow, and to pull up and burn a diseased plant on the spot as soon as you find one.

### 9-2. Major Pests

| Pest | Damage | Response Without Chemicals |
|---|---|---|
| Aphids | Suck sap from new shoots | Avoid excess nitrogen and protect ladybugs. Rinse with water, soapy water, wood ash |
| Field mice & rodents | Dig up and eat large quantities of the underground tubers | Clear weeds around the field, bury wire mesh or boards around the plot |
| Wild boar | Dig up the ground and eat the tubers | Fencing. The biggest risk for in-ground storage |
| Grubs | Bore holes into the tubers | Turn over the field in autumn to expose them |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In a disaster, what threatens the Jerusalem artichoke is not pathogens but animals. In a year when people go hungry, wild boar and field mice go hungry too. Leaving the tubers in the ground all winter is a power-free storehouse for people, but it is the same storehouse for animals. The realistic response is to fence the storage area, or to spread the risk by dividing the crop across several plots instead of keeping it all in one place.

### 9-3. Natural Control Agents

| Material | How to Make It | Target |
|---|---|---|
| Diluted milk | Dilute 1:10 with water and spray on a clear morning | Powdery mildew |
| Diluted baking soda | 5g baking soda per 1L water, with a little soapy water as a spreading agent | Powdery mildew |
| Wood ash | Dust it on dry leaves, or steep it in water and spray | Aphids, soft-bodied pests |
| Wood vinegar | Collected from charcoal-kiln smoke, diluted 200-500x | General repellent effect |
| Sulfur | Apply a small amount around the base | Suppresses Southern blight |

## 10. Nutrient Deficiency and Excess

| Symptom | Cause | How to Check | Natural Remedy |
|---|---|---|---|
| Lower leaves yellow first, plant stays small | Nitrogen deficiency | Rarely appears even on poor soil | A little diluted liquid fertilizer or well-rotted compost |
| Leaf edges scorch brown | Potassium deficiency | Common during tuber bulking | Wood ash. The easiest source of potassium |
| Leaves turn from dark green to purplish | Phosphate deficiency | Common in cold weather | Bone meal, wood ash |
| Yellowing only between the veins, veins stay green | Magnesium deficiency | Appears starting from the lower leaves | Dolomite, more varied compost |
| Only leaves and stem grow lush, tubers stay small | Nitrogen excess | Leaf color is excessively dark, height exceeds 3m | Stop supplying nitrogen. Rebalance with pinching |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The problem you actually run into with the Jerusalem artichoke is usually excess, not deficiency. This plant is built to live on poor soil, so it barely shows symptoms even under most deficiencies. Give it plenty of fertilizer, on the other hand, and it will simply shoot up to 3m, and when you dig in autumn you will be disappointed to find small tubers. Fertilizing the Jerusalem artichoke is, as a rule, a net loss.

## 11. Intercropping and Crop Rotation

### 11-1. Compatibility

| Compatibility | Target | Reason |
|:---:|---|---|
| Avoid | Soybean, tomato, most vegetables | Releases allelopathic substances that suppress the growth of neighboring crops |
| Avoid | Any crop in the middle of a field | Its underground stems invade and crowd them out |
| Possible | A border crop for windbreak use | Its height blocks the wind |
| Possible | Placed as a nectar plant | Its autumn flowers draw bees |
| Possible | A feed-production plot | Leaves and stems are used as livestock feed |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The Jerusalem artichoke is an allelopathic plant. Its roots and leaves release substances that suppress the germination and growth of other plants. This trait works in its favor for suppressing weeds, but it works the same way on crops you plant nearby. Soybeans or tomatoes planted within 1m of a Jerusalem artichoke plot noticeably slow their growth. So the very concept of intercropping does not suit the Jerusalem artichoke well, and growing it in its own separate plot is the governing rule.

> **The Jerusalem artichoke is not a good neighbor. Keep it separate.**

### 11-2. On Crop Rotation

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;A Continuous-cropping tolerance of SS means it is fine to leave it permanently in the same spot; if anything, that is the standard way to grow it. Even so, if you are concerned about pathogens building up, moving the plot every 3 to 4 years is ideal—though in practice, moving the plot at all turns out to be extremely difficult.

| Situation | Response |
|---|---|
| Permanent cultivation in the same spot | The standard method. Move it only once Southern blight becomes severe |
| When moving the plot | You must keep removing the shoots that keep coming up in the old spot for 3-5 years |
| The vacated spot afterward | Leafy vegetables that use a lot of nitrogen do well there. The allelopathic substances disappear within 1-2 years |
| When trying to eliminate it entirely | See the explanation below |

### 11-3. Once Planted, It Cannot Be Eliminated

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This is the flip side of the Perenniality SS grade. A spot where you plant Jerusalem artichoke effectively becomes a Jerusalem artichoke field forever. The reason lies in how it propagates. A single plant produces dozens of tubers in a year, and these scatter more than 1m in every direction along its underground stems. No matter how carefully you dig at harvest, fingernail-sized fragments and tiny tubers on the tips of fine roots are left behind. Every single one of them becomes a new shoot the following spring.

| Removal Method | Effectiveness | Time Required |
|---|:---:|---|
| Digging deep once | Low | It comes back the following year |
| Pulling up shoots every year | Medium | Must be repeated every year for 3-5 years or more |
| Cutting every shoot as it appears | High | Blocking photosynthesis exhausts the tubers' reserves. 2-3 years |
| Heavy light-blocking cover | High | Cover completely with thick fabric or boards for 1-2 years |
| Livestock grazing | High | Pigs dig up the ground and eat it all |
| Boundary barrier | Prevention | Doing this beforehand, at planting time, is the only easy method |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The most effective method is not digging but starving. Shoots emerge using the nutrients stored in the tuber, and if you keep cutting those shoots before the leaves unfold, the tuber cannot replenish its reserves and instead depletes them. Repeat this for 2-3 years, and the density drops sharply. Still, complete eradication has to be considered unrealistic in practice.

> **Planting Jerusalem artichoke is a decision you cannot undo. Plant it on a border or on odd scraps of land, not in the middle of a field, and burying a boundary barrier from the start is the only easy safeguard.**
