## 1. Disaster-Preparedness Evaluation

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The sweet potato's Overall grade is SS, with a score of 57.6 points. Broken down by scenario, its Short-term capacity in the early days of a disaster is S (56.7), its Long-term self-reliance is SS (58.4), and its Supplementary value is S (53.2). In sharp contrast to the potato, the Long-term result is higher than the Short-term one. That means that, although it takes 120-150 days from planting until there is anything to eat, once established the crop asks for almost no fertilizer or pesticide and yields calories reliably year after year.

### 1-3. How to Read the Strengths

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The combination to note in the sweet potato is that Calories per area (S) and Fertilizer demand (S) sit side by side. Normally the two do not go together, because a big harvest requires heavy feeding. The sweet potato produces 3,675kcal from 15g of nutrients per m². Compared with the potato, which takes up 32g to deliver 2,010kcal, the output per unit of input differs by nearly a factor of four. In a situation where supply chains are cut and fertilizer cannot be bought, this difference translates directly into whether people survive. Indeed, under zero-input conditions the potato falls to 17.6% of the conventional yield, while the sweet potato holds at 45.1%.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The SS for Continuous-cropping tolerance also deserves a mention. Most tuber crops (tubers/storage roots) and root vegetables have to be rested for several years at a time because of soil-borne diseases, but the sweet potato can be planted in the same spot year after year without serious trouble. Where land is scarce, this is a great advantage. Without the burden of splitting the field and working out a sequence for rotation, the sunniest, best-drained spot can be dedicated permanently to sweet potatoes.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The substance behind the S for Micronutrients is beta-carotene. 100g of a deep orange sweet potato contains several times the daily recommended amount of vitamin A in the form of beta-carotene. In a long-term disaster, when fresh vegetables run out and animal foods disappear, vitamin A deficiency leads straight to night blindness and weakened immunity, and this one crop solves the whole problem. In addition, the leaves and stems can be eaten as namul (seasoned vegetable side dish), so the plant also serves as a practical source of greens.

> **One ridge of sweet potatoes is a staple, a source of vitamins, and a vegetable garden all at once. Few crops combine all three.**

### 1-4. How to Read the Weaknesses

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The criteria marked E are Protein density, Cold hardiness, Non-food value, and Seed longevity. Of these, the one tied directly to survival is the first.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;A protein share of 3.0% of calories is a serious figure. A person who covers 2,100kcal a day with sweet potatoes alone takes in only about 16g of protein, far short of the 55g an adult man needs per day. If this continues for months, muscle wastes away, wounds do not heal, edema develops, and the body becomes vulnerable to infection. The protein-energy malnutrition that appeared in the early 20th century among children in tropical regions who lived on sweet potatoes and cassava as their staples arose by exactly this route.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The solution is combination. Planting and eating sweet potatoes together with beans is the right answer, and this is not a supplementary recommendation but an essential element of the design. Be sure to assign 25-33% of the field area to legumes such as soybean, cowpea, and kidney bean. Sweet potato leaves and stems help too. The leaves contain far more protein than the roots, so eating them regularly as namul fills part of the shortfall.

> **No one can live on sweet potatoes alone. Planting beans alongside them is not optional but part of the sweet potato growing plan.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The E for Cold hardiness and the E for Seed longevity come down to a single problem. Sweet potatoes are killed outright by frost, and even harvested roots suffer chilling injury within a few days below 9°C. To make slips the following year, the seed sweet potatoes must be kept alive at around 13°C all winter, and with heating gone this is the biggest obstacle to being self-sufficient in sweet potatoes. Potatoes can simply be left somewhere cold, but sweet potatoes need a warm place. Sections 13 and 15 deal with this problem in depth.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The B for Infrastructure needs belongs to the same context. Seed sweet potatoes are not planted directly in the field. Instead, they are first sprouted in a seedling bed to produce slips, and those slips are then planted as cuttings. This means a seedling bed has to be built every year, and in April (in South Korea) that bed must be able to hold its soil temperature. Without plastic sheeting, a hotbed has to be built. Section 6 covers this method.

## 2. What Kind of Plant Is This?

### 2-1. Classification and Origin

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The sweet potato is a vining plant of the morning glory family (Convolvulaceae), genus Ipomoea. It belongs to the same family as morning glories and bindweeds. Despite the similar name, it is a completely different lineage from the potato, and so most pests and diseases are not shared either. Even when potato late blight sweeps through a field, the sweet potatoes next to it are unharmed. This fact has important implications for disaster preparedness. Dividing plantings among crops of different families is how to avoid losing everything at once, and the potato and sweet potato make an ideal pair for that.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Its place of origin is the tropical lowlands stretching from Central America to northern South America. Its homeland is sandy ground that is warm and strongly sunlit, with rain that does not leave water standing. This explains why the sweet potato likes 20-30°C, grows especially well in sandy soil, and is killed outright by frost. It reached Korea (today's South Korea) by way of Japan in the 18th century, in the late Joseon period, and became established as a famine-relief crop as soon as it arrived.

### 2-2. Appearance

| Organ | Characteristics |
|---|---|
| Leaf | Heart-shaped or palmate (hand-shaped). Lobing and color vary by variety. Edible |
| Stem | A creeping, ground-hugging vine that grows to 1-3m. Roots form at every node |
| Root | Divided into fibrous roots that absorb water and nutrients, and storage roots that thicken to become the part we eat |
| Flower | Trumpet-shaped, pale pink to purple. Rarely blooms in temperate climates |
| Fruit | A round capsule holding 2-4 blackish-brown seeds |
| Storage root | A thickened root. It has no eyes, but can produce adventitious buds from any part |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The sweet potato rarely flowers because it is a short-day plant. Flower buds form only when the days grow shorter, but by the time the days become short enough in South Korea, frost has already fallen and killed the plant. In the tropics, flowers are a common sight. In a disaster, this has an important practical meaning. Sweet potatoes cannot be carried on by seed; each generation must be passed on through living roots.

### 2-3. Growth Stages

| Stage | Timing | Key physiology | Key nutrition |
|---|---|---|---|
| Establishment | Up to 2 weeks after planting | Roots emerge from the nodes and take hold | Water is absolutely essential |
| Vine elongation | 2-8 weeks | Leaves and stems spread, building the photosynthetic apparatus | Nitrogen, potassium |
| Storage-root initiation | 30-50 days after planting | Some roots begin to thicken | Potassium, moderate dryness |
| Storage-root bulking | 50-120 days after planting | Starch accumulates and the roots thicken rapidly | Maximum potassium demand |
| Maturity | After 120 days | Sugars accumulate and the skin hardens | Stop irrigation |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Understanding the physiology of the storage-root initiation stage is the heart of growing sweet potatoes. At first, all of the sweet potato's roots are thin fibrous roots. Some of these thicken into storage roots, but depending on conditions they may fail to thicken and simply remain thin roots. When nitrogen is plentiful and water is abundant, the plant feels no need to store anything and grows only leaves and vines. Conversely, when nutrients and water are moderately scarce, the plant begins storing in its roots to prepare for hard times.

> **A sweet potato stores nutrients in its roots only when it is moderately hungry. In a well-fed sweet potato field, the vines are lush and there are no sweet potatoes.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The sweet potato's sweetness comes not only from the sugars a variety naturally contains but also from the processes of heating and storage. This is because an enzyme in the sweet potato called beta-amylase converts starch into maltose at 50-70°C. The longer the sweet potato is held in this temperature range, the sweeter it becomes. That is why a sweet potato baked slowly over gentle heat is sweeter than one boiled in a hurry. It also means that, where fuel is scarce, residual-heat cooking has an advantage in taste as well.
