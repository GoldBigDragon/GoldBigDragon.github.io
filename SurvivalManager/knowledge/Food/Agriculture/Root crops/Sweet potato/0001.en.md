## Introduction
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Sweet potato (Ipomoea batatas) is a vine of the morning glory family from the tropical lowlands of Central and South America, and the part we eat is not a stem but a tuberous root, a root that has thickened. Its disaster preparedness evaluation is overall SS (57.6 points, 4th of 183 valid species), short term S (56.7), long term SS (58.4), and supplementary food S (53.2 points), with the long term higher than the short term. It takes 120-150 days after planting, but once established it asks for almost no fertilizer or chemicals and yields 3,675 kcal per ㎡, is rich in beta-carotene, tolerates poor soil and drought, and can be grown in succession. Its decisive flaw is that protein is only 3.0% of calories (E), so it must go together with legumes. It is also killed outright by frost, the harvested roots suffer chilling injury below 9℃, and if the seed sweet potatoes are not kept alive at around 13℃ all winter, farming breaks off the next year. The contest is decided not in the field but in the winter store.

| Category | Grade | Score |

| Short term | S | 56.7 |
| Long term | SS | 58.4 |
| Supplementary | S | 53.2 |

| Evaluation | Items |
|:---|:---|
| Strengths | SS: Continuous-cropping tolerance, Taste. S: Calories per area, Micronutrients, Fertilization, Domestic cultivation spread, Seed saving. A: Water consumption. B: Facility burden, Sunlight, Cooking and processing requirement, Storability, Yield retention without synthetic inputs, Yield stability |
| Weaknesses | E: Protein density, Cold tolerance, Non-food value, Seed longevity. D: Perenniality. C: Harvest speed, Temperature range, Pest and disease pressure, Pest-control self-reliance, Land-preparation and digging energy |

> **One ridge of sweet potato is a staple, a vitamin source, and a vegetable patch all at once, but you cannot live on sweet potato alone. Planting beans with it is not an option but part of the cultivation plan.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The key is that calories per area S and fertilization S come together. Sweet potato makes 3,675 kcal from 15 g of nutrients per ㎡, nearly four times the output per input of potato (32 g, 2,010 kcal), and its yield retention without synthetic inputs is 45.1%, higher than potato's 17.6%. Continuous-cropping tolerance SS means that the Rural Development Administration places it in the group for which continuous cropping does no harm, an exception among roots and tubers. The substance of micronutrients S is beta-carotene, and the leaves and stems also become seasoned greens. Protein density E means that if 2,100 kcal a day came only from sweet potato, protein would be only about 16 g (an adult man needs 55 g), so within months muscle is lost, wounds do not heal, and infection becomes likely; therefore assign 25-33% of the field area to legumes. Cold tolerance E and seed longevity E are one problem: the seed sweet potatoes must be kept alive and warm (Section 8). The grade for each item is in the Crop Evaluation Results document.

## 2. Plant Overview
| Item | Details |
|:---|:---|
| Classification | A vine of the morning glory family (Convolvulaceae), genus Ipomoea. A completely different lineage from potato, so they mostly do not share pests and diseases, and planting the two crops separately avoids losing both at once |
| Leaves, stems | The stem is a vine creeping along the ground, growing 1-3 m and rooting at each node. Leaves and leaf stalks are edible |
| Flowers, fruit | It is a short-day plant, and in Korea frost comes before it flowers, so it almost never does. It cannot be continued by seed and must be carried on through living roots |
| Tuberous root | A thickened root. It has no eyes but can produce adventitious buds from any part |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Up to 2 weeks after planting is the establishment stage (water is absolute), and weeks 2-8 are the vine-extension stage (nitrogen, potassium); tuberous roots begin to form at days 30-50, pass through the swelling stage at days 50-120 (potassium demand at its maximum), and enter the maturing stage after day 120 (stop irrigation). The roots are at first all thin fibrous roots, and some thicken into storage roots; if nitrogen and water are plentiful, the plant feels no need to store and grows only vines, so it gathers nutrients into the roots only when it is moderately hungry. Per 100 g there are 147 kcal, carbohydrate 31-35 g, sugars 9.83 g, protein 1.09 g, and vitamin C 10-23 mg, and vitamin A (beta-carotene) is higher the deeper the orange. The sweetness arises during heating and storage, when beta-amylase turns starch into maltose at 50-70℃, so the more slowly it is cooked over a gentle fire, the sweeter it gets.

## 3. Growing Environment and Varieties
| Condition | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Temperature | Growth 25-30℃ (stops at 15℃ or below, with 35℃ or above the limit), root swelling 20-30℃ (above 30℃ only the vines grow), nights 18℃ or above (chilling injury at 10℃ or below), soil temperature at planting 18℃ or above (below 15℃ the cutting does not establish). Frost kills the aboveground part in a single night |
| Water, light | Total evapotranspiration about 400 mm, and 24 hours of flooding rots the roots. Needs 6 hours or more of sun a day (under 4 hours only the vines get long), and under 40% shade the relative yield is about 80% |
| Soil | Well-drained sandy loam or sandy ground, tillage depth 30 cm or more. In clay soil and ground with a high water table the roots cannot thicken. It grows at pH 4.2-8.3, so it is practically indifferent to acidity |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Choose the site in this order: ground where water does not stand (poor drainage is the most common cause of failure), a sunny south-facing slope, light soil mixed with sand, and a spot sheltered from wind. Rich ground grows only vines, so ground low in fertility is actually an advantage, and poor, parched land where no other crop can be planted is the place for sweet potato.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Starchy types (chestnut sweet potato and others) are low in water and high in starch, suit drying and flour processing, and rot less in storage; moist types (pumpkin sweet potato and others) are high in water and sugar, suit baking and porridge, and have the most beta-carotene. The most important indicator in choosing a variety is neither yield nor taste but the rot rate in storage. Stored four months under the same conditions, Daeyumi and Gogeonmi had rot rates under 1%, while Sincheonmi reached 36.7%, losing 360 kg out of 1,000 kg. In regions with early frost, chestnut sweet potato, Hopungmi, and Sodammi, harvested in 100-120 days, are suitable.

## 4. Field Preparation and Planting
| Item | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Land preparation | Dig 25-30 cm deep and make high beds 25-35 cm high and 60-75 cm wide with furrows at least 20 cm deep (the cheapest investment, as it improves drainage, warms the soil, and thickens the roots). Break the soil up until it is crumbly |
| Fertilization (1,000 ㎡) | Nitrogen at a minimum (excess is fatal, and on poor ground it is omitted), phosphorus 10-15 kg, potassium 15-20 kg (most important for root swelling), and well-rotted compost 1,000-1,500 kg, all as basal fertilizer 2 weeks before planting. Top dressing is omitted in principle (nitrogen in mid-growth stops storage and makes the plant grow vines again) |
| Disaster substitute fertilizers | Wood ash (1-2 L per 10 ㎡), rotted compost, urine diluted 20 times (only at establishment, forbidden at swelling), bone meal, seaweed rinsed in fresh water to remove the salt, and rice straw and fallen leaves |
| Nursery bed | 50-60 days before planting, dig a pit 50-60 cm deep and 1-1.2 m wide facing south, tread in fresh horse or cow manure mixed with straw and leaves to a thickness of 30-40 cm and sprinkle it with water to ferment (without livestock, use leaves, rice bran, and urine), then cover with 10-15 cm of sandy soil. When the soil temperature settles around 30℃, lay the seed sweet potatoes 3-5 cm apart, cover with 3-4 cm of soil, cover with a straw mat at night, and lower the temperature to 23-25℃ once sprouts appear |
| Slips | In 30-40 days they grow to 25-30 cm; cut leaving 3-5 cm of the base and new shoots come again, so 3-4 cuttings can be taken from one root. Choose healthy slips with 5-7 nodes and pencil thickness or more, leave them in the shade a day to heal the cut surface, then disinfect by soaking in 50-55℃ water for 10-15 minutes or coating with wood ash |
| Planting time | When soil temperature is 18℃ or above and the minimum air temperature is stable at 15℃ or above (temperature, not the calendar, is the standard). South: late April to mid-May; central region: mid-May to early June; after late June the growing period is too short and only small sweet potatoes result |
| Planting | Lay the slip down and bury 3-4 nodes 3-5 cm deep, leaving the 2-3 leaves at the tip above ground (sweet potatoes set at each node, and if planted upright, roots form at only one point and the harvest falls by half). Plant at 25-30 cm in the row and 70-90 cm between ridges and water at once with 100 cc or more per plant |

## 5. Water Management and Cultivation Management
| Stage | Water management |
|:---|:---|
| Right after planting | Water thoroughly (the only condition for a stem without roots to survive). Plant on a cloudy day and for a few days shade the plants with straw or grass, or cut the leaves by about half to reduce transpiration |
| Establishment to root formation | Add water when dry, then leave it to rainfall, and keep it slightly dry during root formation (days 30-50) (with plentiful water only the vines grow, and drought stress promotes storage root formation) |
| Root swelling (days 50-120) | If a drought lasts 2 weeks or more, water thoroughly once a week (in a plot short of water for 60 days, the average weight of one sweet potato fell 75%). If water is limited, concentrate it in July-August |
| 3 weeks before harvest | Stop irrigation (sugar content rises and storability improves) |

| Care | Method |
|:---|:---|
| Lifting the vines | Thin roots form at each node touching the ground and steal nutrients, so from July every 2-3 weeks, on a cloudy day or in the evening, lift the vines slightly, break the node roots, and set them back in place. If too lush, cut off the last 30 cm or so and eat it as a seasoned side dish |
| Weeding | The period up to 40 days after planting, before the vines cover the ground, is what matters (without it the yield differs by 40-50%). After the cover closes, the vines suppress weeds |
| Mulching | Straw or hay at least 5 cm thick is the first choice in place of plastic film, and cut grass is spread before its seeds ripen |
| Picking leaves, stems | From 60 days after planting until frost, pick the young shoots and leaf stalks within 15 cm of the vine tip, no more than one third of all leaves at a time (too much delays swelling). Peel off the outer skin, blanch, and eat as a seasoned side dish or in soup |
| Water shortage, excess | In a shortage the leaves curl at midday and new leaves get small (water at once if in the swelling stage); if severe, irrigate and cover with straw. In excess only the vines flourish, so dig drainage ditches. If flooding turns the roots black and foul-smelling, they cannot recover, so drain at once and dig them if they can be dug |

## 6. Pests, Diseases, and Nutrient Problems
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Pest and disease burden is low. Pest-control self-reliance C means that control is almost unnecessary and natural materials are enough when it is, and there is no disease without a cure such as potato blight. Prevention rests on healthy slips and seed sweet potatoes (half of the result is decided here), high beds, thorough curing, and clearing residue after harvest.

| Pest or disease | Symptoms and conditions | Prevention and response |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Soft rot | The root turns soft and smells foul (wounds, excess moisture, high temperature in storage) | Harvest without wounds, cure thoroughly, remove infected roots at once |
| Black rot | Black spots form on the root surface and it tastes bitter (infected seed sweet potatoes and slips) | Select healthy seed sweet potatoes, hot-water treatment. Do not eat it if it tastes bitter (Section 10) |
| Fusarium wilt, anthracnose, powdery mildew | The stem base splits and wilts (soil-borne) / brown lesions on leaves and stems (excess moisture in the rainy season) / white powder on the leaves (dry weather with large day-night swings) | Resistant varieties, healthy slips, drainage / ensure airflow, remove diseased leaves / milk diluted 1 to 9 |
| Viral diseases | Leaf mosaic, deformity, lower yield (aphids, spread through seed sweet potatoes) | Select seed sweet potatoes only from healthy plants |
| Pests | Wireworms (holes in the root), sweet potato leaf folder moths (leaves and stems), aphids and spider mites, cutworm moths (cut young slips at the base), nematodes (galls on the root), field mice and wild boar (dig up the roots) | No unrotted compost, potato-piece traps, rotation / remove damaged leaves, burn residue / soapy water (20 g of unscented soap in 1 L of water), protect ladybugs / ash or a paper collar around the base / marigold / fences, thorny branches, harvest earlier |

| Symptom | Cause and action |
|:---|:---|
| Leaf edges burn brown and curl | Potassium deficiency. A little wood ash in the furrows |
| Only vines flourish and the roots do not thicken | Excess nitrogen (overwhelmingly the most common problem). Cut off nitrogen, lift the vines, pinch the shoots and eat them as a seasoned side dish, and do not fertilize that spot the next year |

## 7. Intercropping and Crop Rotation
| Compatibility | Companions |
|:---|:---|
| Very good | Legumes (soybean, cowpea, kidney bean): fix nitrogen and supply the protein that sweet potato lacks to people |
| Good | Maize, sorghum (sweet potato covers the ground beneath tall crops), marigold (suppresses nematodes), perilla (its smell repels pests) |
| Fair | Lettuce, spinach (harvested and removed early, before the vines cover the ground) |
| Poor | Potato (shares wireworms and nematodes and competes for space), tomato, chili pepper, eggplant (nematode damage overlaps), pumpkin (competition for space) |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Sweet potato is in the group for which continuous cropping does no harm, so when the field is small it can be fixed in one place with a small amount of compost added every year. When there is room, a 3-year rotation of sweet potato, legumes, and leafy vegetables or grain is recommended; after several years nematode and Fusarium wilt densities can rise, so if nematodes appear, plant marigold or Sudan grass for a year and plow it under.

## 8. Harvest and Seed Saving
| Harvest | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Timing | 120-150 days after planting, but the standard is not "is it fully grown" but "is it before the cold." Finish before the soil temperature falls below 15℃ and before frost (chilling in frosted leaves travels down into the root); sweet potatoes that sat in soil below 10℃ always rot in storage after digging, so dig before frost even if not fully thick. Dig one or two plants beforehand to check the size |
| Method | Clear away the vines 5-7 days before so the skin hardens, then on a clear, dry morning dig 30 cm out from the plant, lift the soil with the roots, and separate them by hand (do not pull). Shake off only the soil, do not wash, and let them dry in the shade for 2-3 hours. The skin is thin, and a single fingernail scratch is where rot begins, so collect wounded ones separately and eat them first |

| Seed saving | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Structure | In temperate regions flowering is rare and seedlings vary in character, so all propagation is vegetative. The genotype is 100% preserved, but the seed cannot be dried and stored, so it must get through winter as living sweet potatoes, and if the seed sweet potatoes freeze or rot there is nothing to plant the next year |
| Choosing seed sweet potatoes | Choose spindle-shaped ones of 150-250 g with no wounds, lesions, or black spots at all (one infects the whole lot) from the best-grown plants. 60-80 kg per 1,000 ㎡ of planting area, and one root yields 20-30 slips |
| Overwintering | After curing (30-33℃, humidity 90-95%, 3-4 days), cool gradually to 13℃ over 4-5 days and keep at 12-15℃, humidity 85-90%. If put in the potato pit (3-5℃) together, only the sweet potatoes are wiped out, so keep them separate; a corner of a living room or behind the kitchen is safest. To make 30℃ without electricity, put them in a box in the warmest spot of a sunny room (the heated end of the ondol floor), wrap with straw or a blanket, and keep the humidity up with a bowl of water |
| Controlling degeneration | Repeated vegetative propagation accumulates viruses, and in 5-6 years the yield visibly falls. Keep seed sweet potatoes only from the most vigorous plants each year, use slips from the end farthest from the mother plant (lower virus concentration), eat plants with mottled or curled leaves, and exchange seed sweet potatoes with another region every 4-5 years |

## 9. Storage
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Success in storage is decided in the 3-4 days of curing right after harvest (30-33℃, humidity 90-95%). A cork layer forms on the wound surface, blocks pathogens, and cuts storage loss to half or less. After cooling gradually it goes into main storage (12-15℃, humidity 85-90%, dark, light ventilation) and keeps 5-6 months, and it is inspected every 2 weeks to remove soft ones at once. At 9℃ or below the inside turns hard and rots, and at 18℃ or above it sprouts.

| Method | How | Period |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Indoor box | After curing, pack in a cardboard box lined with newspaper in layers and place in a corner of the living room. The surest | 4-6 months |
| Rice hull or sand layers, straw insulation | Alternate with rice hulls or dry sand in a box (blocks the spread of rot) / spread thick straw in an indoor corner, pile up the roots, and cover with straw and mats | 3-5 months / 3-4 months |
| Beside the ondol | The space beside the flue. Best if the temperature is right, but it sprouts if too hot | 5-6 months |
| Dried slices | Slice raw to 5 mm and dry completely in the sun for 3-5 days (volume falls to 20%, and it keeps over a year if insects are kept off). Soak to make porridge or grind to flour for rice cakes | 1-2 years |
| Dried strips, starch | Steam, peel, slice lengthwise, and dry / grate, stir into water, and dry the sediment that settles | 6 months to 1 year / years |

> **The best disaster combination is indoor box storage plus dried slices. Slice and dry at once the ones that start to soften in storage, before they rot, and turn them into a long-term stockpile.**

## 10. Cooking and Precautions
| Caution | Details |
|:---|:---|
| Black rot | There are sunken black spots on the skin, the flesh inside is brown when cut, and it is intensely bitter. The ipomeamarone produced then is a liver- and lung-toxic substance not destroyed by heating, and livestock that eat it die of pulmonary edema. If it tastes bitter, spit it out at once and throw all of it away |
| Rotten parts | Mold toxins spread much more widely than the visibly discolored part, so if part has gone soft, do not cut it out; throw the whole thing away |
| Raw sweet potato | It has no constant toxin like solanine, so it can be eaten with the skin, and if it sprouts just remove that part. But raw starch is not digested and ferments in the large intestine, causing gas and stomach pain, so cook it whenever it is eaten as a staple |
| Shoots | The leaves and stems contain oxalic acid, so discard the blanching water and peel off the outer skin of the leaf stalks. Eating large amounts daily risks kidney stones, so alternate with other seasoned greens |

| Cooking | Method |
|:---|:---|
| Baked in ashes | Almost no fuel is used and it takes 40-60 minutes, and the sweetness is the strongest. This is because it stays long at 60-75℃, where beta-amylase turns starch into maltose; cooked quickly over a strong fire, the enzyme is denatured and it is less sweet. Burying it in the embers after cooking is the best use of fuel |
| Steaming, boiling, residual heat | Steaming 25-35 minutes, boiling 20-30 minutes (sugar leaches into the water), and residual-heat cooking, 30 minutes of heating followed by 2 hours of insulation, tastes the same as steaming |
| Processing | Syrup (jocheong) and taffy (yeot) (pour malt water over steamed sweet potato, saccharify at 60℃ for 6-8 hours, then boil down; a sweetener when there is no sugar, and keeps long at room temperature), starch, sweet potato makgeolli (steamed sweet potato, nuruk, and water at 20-25℃ for 5-7 days), and blanched, dried greens |

## 11. Annual Work Schedule
| Month | Work |
|:---|:---|
| Jan-Feb | Inspect the stored sweet potatoes every 2 weeks and remove soft ones |
| Mar-Apr | In March, select seed sweet potatoes, set up the nursery bed, and bury them (50-60 days before planting). In April, raise slips, plow, add compost and wood ash, and make beds |
| May-June | In May, after confirming a soil temperature of 15℃ or above, take the first cutting of slips and plant. In June, check establishment, replant gaps, weed, and use the second cutting of slips |
| July-Aug | In July, lift the vines and begin harvesting sweet potato shoots. In August, manage the vines and keep harvesting shoots (root-swelling stage) |
| Sep-Oct | In September, check size and frost timing with a trial dig. In October, clear the vines, harvest 5-7 days later, and cure at once |
| Nov-Dec | In November, finish curing, cool, begin main storage, and dry the dried slices. In December, inspect the storage and process spoiled ones at once |

> **Sweet potato is a crop that is never taken below 9℃, never skips the 3-4 days of curing, is not eaten if it tastes bitter, keeps the seed sweet potatoes alive through winter, and is planted together with legumes.**
