## Introduction

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The sweet potato (Ipomoea batatas) comes from the tropical lowlands of Central and South America. It is a vine of the morning glory family (Convolvulaceae), and unlike the potato, the part we eat is not a stem but a root. Because it is a thickened root, it has neither eyes nor buds, yet it can put out sprouts from anywhere on its body. This one difference sets the sweet potato apart from the potato in how it is propagated, how it is stored, and how it is used in a disaster.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;From a disaster-preparedness standpoint, the sweet potato ranks among the very top of the six crops. It earns an Overall grade of SS with 57.6 points, and in particular an SS in the Long-term scenario, which looks several years ahead. The reasons are clear. At 3,675kcal per m², it surpasses one person's self-sufficiency line, reaching 1.4 times that level. Eaten in an amount that covers a day's energy needs, it meets 10% or more of the recommended intake for as many as 16 micronutrients. Its beta-carotene content in particular is unmatched by any other root crop. It grows well in poor soil, needs only about 15g of nutrients per m², resists drought, and, rarely for tuber crops (tubers/storage roots), tolerates continuous cropping. On top of that, it tastes good. The SS it received for Taste in the evaluation table is not a matter of mere preference. When people have to eat the same food for months on end, whether they can keep eating it is directly tied to survival.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;However, the sweet potato has one critical flaw that must be understood. It contains almost no protein. Its protein share of calories is 3.0%, less than a third of the 10% minimum that people need. Protein density is the only criterion in the evaluation that received an E. On sweet potatoes alone, a person feels full and keeps their weight, but loses muscle, finds that wounds do not heal, and watches immunity collapse. The sweet potato is an excellent source of energy, but it is dangerous as a sole staple. It must always be paired with something such as beans, perilla, nuts, fish, or eggs. This is why this document stresses combinations with legumes throughout.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Another weakness is cold. A single frost kills the above-ground growth outright, and harvested sweet potatoes left for a few days below 9°C suffer chilling injury: the flesh turns black inside and rots. In sharp contrast to the potato, which wants a cool place at 3-5°C, the sweet potato must be held at around 13°C all winter. Without heating, this is by no means easy. And if the seed sweet potatoes are not kept alive, no slips can be raised the following year and the cycle of cultivation is broken. That is why this document devotes particular space to storage in Section 15 and to overwintering seed sweet potatoes in Section 13. Success with sweet potatoes is decided not in the field but in the winter storehouse.
