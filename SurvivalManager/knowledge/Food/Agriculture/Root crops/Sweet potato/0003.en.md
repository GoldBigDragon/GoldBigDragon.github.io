## 3. Growing Conditions

### 3-1. Limits and Optimum Values

| Condition | Optimum | Limit | What happens beyond the limit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Growing temperature | 25-30°C | 15°C or below, 35°C or above | Growth stops below 15°C |
| Storage-root bulking temperature | 20-30°C | Above 30°C | Only the vines grow and the roots do not thicken |
| Minimum temperature | 18°C or above at night | 10°C or below | Chilling injury stops growth |
| Frost | Cannot tolerate it | 0°C | The above-ground parts die overnight |
| Soil temperature (at planting) | 18°C or above | Below 15°C | The slips fail to establish and rot |
| Light | 6 hours or more a day | Less than 4 hours | Only the vines lengthen and no storage roots form |
| Shade tolerance | Relative yield about 80% at 40% shade | | It holds up to some extent even in shade |
| Total water requirement | About 400mm | Flooding for 24 hours | The roots rot |
| Soil | Well-drained sandy loam, sandy ground | Clay soil, land with a high water table | The roots cannot thicken and become misshapen |
| Topsoil depth | 30cm or more | Less than 20cm | The roots grow only sideways and stay small |
| Soil pH | pH 5.5-6.5 | Grows even at pH 4.2-8.3 | Its tolerance range for soil acidity is extremely wide |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;A soil pH tolerance of 4.2-8.3 is a remarkable figure. Compared with most crops, which grow properly only within a pH range of about 1-1.5, the sweet potato is in effect indifferent to acidity. When neither soil testing nor liming is possible, this trait is decisive. Whatever the ground is like, once planted, it grows.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Conversely, the conditions that cannot be compromised at all are drainage and soil temperature. In ground where water collects, the roots rot, and when the soil is cold, the slips do not establish at all. Poor, sandy ground is, if anything, a good field for sweet potatoes. Places where other crops fail, such as newly cleared land, sandy riverbanks, and sloping gravelly fields, become ideal sites for sweet potatoes.

> **If poor, barren land remains where no other crop can be planted, that is the place for sweet potatoes.**

### 3-2. Choosing a Site in a Disaster

| Rank | Condition | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | A place where water does not collect | Poor drainage is the most common cause of failure with sweet potatoes |
| 2 | A sunny, south-facing slope | The soil warms quickly and the hours of sunshine are longer |
| 3 | Light, sandy soil | The roots thicken easily and are easy to dig |
| 4 | A spot sheltered from the wind | It reduces the number of vines turned over by typhoons |
| 5 | Land low in fertility | If anything, an advantage. Rich land only grows vines |

## 4. Varieties

### 4-1. Types by Texture

| Type | Characteristics | Representative varieties | Suitable uses |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mealy (bamgoguma, chestnut sweet potato) | High in starch and low in water, so crumbly | Bamgoguma, Sincheonmi, Yulmi | Boiling, steaming, flour and starch |
| Moist (hobakgoguma, pumpkin sweet potato) | High in water and sugar, so moist and soft | Hobakgoguma, Daeyumi | Baking, simmering, porridge |
| High-sugar (kkulgoguma, honey sweet potato) | Sweetness rises further in storage | Beniharuka, Hopungmi | Baking, eating raw, snacks |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;From a disaster-preparedness standpoint, the mealy types have some advantages. Because they contain little water, they are suited to drying and milling into flour; their starch yield is high; and they suffer less spoilage in storage. The moist types, by contrast, have a high energy density, but their high water content means more fuel and time for drying and more softening in storage.

### 4-2. Comparison of Main Varieties

| Variety | Growing period | Yield | Storability | Cultivation difficulty | Texture | Characteristics |
|---|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---:|---|---|
| Bamgoguma (lineage) | 100-120 days | Medium-high | High | Easy | Mealy | Short growing period and good storability |
| Hobakgoguma | 120-130 days | High | Medium | Easy | Moist | The richest in beta-carotene |
| Beniharuka | 130-150 days | High | Medium | Moderate | Moist | Sweetness rises greatly in storage. Suited to warm areas |
| Hopungmi | Early-cropping type | Very high | High | Easy | Mealy | Large yield gain under early cropping |
| Sodammi | Early-cropping type | High | High | Easy | Mealy | Suited to early cropping, like Hopungmi |
| Daeyumi | Mid-to-late maturing | High | Highest | Easy | Mealy | Storage rot rate below 1% |
| Gogeonmi | Mid-to-late maturing | High | Highest | Easy | Mealy | The lineage with the best storability |
| Sincheonmi | Mid-to-late maturing | Medium | Very low | Easy | Mealy | High rot rate in storage, unsuitable for stockpiling |
| Purple sweet potato | 110-130 days | Medium | Medium | Easy | Moist | Rich in anthocyanins |
| Yeonhwangmi, Yulmi | Mid-maturing | Medium | Medium | Easy | Mealy | Tender leaves and stems, good for namul (seasoned vegetable side dish) |

### 4-3. Selection Criteria by Situation

| Situation | Choice | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Must get through the winter | Daeyumi, Gogeonmi | Storage rot rate below 1% |
| Areas with early frost | Bamgoguma, Hopungmi, Sodammi | Can be harvested in 100-120 days |
| Boosting vitamin A | Hobakgoguma | Highest beta-carotene content |
| Wanting to eat the leaves and stems too | Yeonhwangmi, Yulmi | Tender leaf stalks and little astringency |
| Stockpiling as flour or starch | Bamgoguma lineage, Daeyumi | Being mealy, they give a high starch yield |
| Poor storage facilities | Daeyumi, Gogeonmi | They tolerate temperature swings |
| To avoid | Sincheonmi | Tasty, but more than a third rots in storage |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The difference in storability is dramatic when seen in actual figures. When stored for four months under the same conditions, Daeyumi and Gogeonmi show a rot rate below 1%, while Sincheonmi reaches 36.7%. Out of 1,000kg in storage, that is the difference between losing 10kg and losing 360kg. In a disaster, the choice of variety alone decides a third of the winter food supply.

> **The most important indicator in choosing a sweet potato variety is neither yield nor taste, but the rot rate in storage.**

## 5. Field Preparation and Fertilizer

### 5-1. Preparing the Soil

| Item | Standard | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Tillage depth | 25-30cm | Creates room for the roots to grow downward |
| Raised bed height | 25-35cm | The higher the bed, the thicker the roots and the better the drainage |
| Raised bed width | 60-75cm | Sized for single-row or double-row planting |
| Furrow | Depth of 20cm or more | The drainage channel in the monsoon season (jangma, late June to July in South Korea) |
| Soil condition | Break it up until crumbly | Roots bend in hard soil |
| Soil pH | pH 5.5-6.5 recommended | Does not need much attention |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Making the raised beds high is the cheapest and surest investment in growing sweet potatoes. A high bed improves drainage, raises the soil temperature, and thickens the soft layer of soil in which the roots grow. Trials repeatedly report cases where raising the bed height to 20-35cm alone produced a noticeable increase in yield.

### 5-2. Standard Fertilizer Rates (per 1,000m²)

| Fertilizer | Rate | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Nitrogen | Minimum | Excess is fatal. Can be omitted on poor land |
| Phosphate | 10-15kg | Helps root development. All as base fertilizer |
| Potassium | 15-20kg | Most important for storage-root bulking. All as base fertilizer |
| Compost | 1,000-1,500kg | Fully matured compost. Never excessive |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Converted to m², this comes to around 15g. That is less than half of the potato's. The fertilizing principle for sweet potatoes can be summed up in one sentence: nitrogen at a minimum, potassium in plenty.

### 5-3. Base Fertilizer and Top-Dressing

| Type | Timing | Details | Caution |
|---|---|---|---|
| Base fertilizer | 2 weeks before planting | All of the compost, phosphate, and potassium | Almost no nitrogen is applied |
| Top-dressing | Omitted in principle | The effect is not clear | Add a small amount of potassium only where losses are heavy in sandy soil |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Not applying top-dressing may feel unfamiliar, but for sweet potatoes this is the standard practice. If nitrogen is added in mid-season, the plant stops storing and starts growing vines again. Even roots that have already begun to thicken stop bulking. As far as growing sweet potatoes goes, a shortage of fertilizer in a disaster is, if anything, a blessing.

### 5-4. When There Is No Chemical Fertilizer - Disaster Substitutes

| Substitute resource | Nutrients supplied | How to make and use it | Caution |
|---|---|---|---|
| Wood ash | Potassium, phosphate, calcium | Sift white ash and scatter 1-2L per 10m² before forming the raised beds, then mix it in | Sweet potatoes tolerate a wide range of soil acidity, so the burden is lighter than for potatoes |
| Well-rotted compost | All-round | Made by piling fallen leaves and grass for 6 months or more. A small amount as base fertilizer | Too much makes only the vines lush |
| Diluted urine | Nitrogen, potassium | Only in the establishment stage right after planting, a small amount diluted 20-fold | Never apply during the bulking stage |
| Bone meal | Phosphate, calcium | Mix it into the soil as base fertilizer | Decomposes slowly |
| Seaweed and seagrass | Potassium, trace elements | Rinse in fresh water and use as mulch on the raised beds | Be sure to remove the salt |
| Rice straw, fallen leaves | Organic matter, potassium | Laid in the furrows, they decompose slowly | Improves the physical properties of the soil |
| Tobacco ash, charcoal powder | Potassium, soil improvement | Mix a small amount into the raised beds | A large amount causes alkalization |

> **The most common mistake in fertilizing sweet potatoes is giving too much. Giving less because it feels like a waste is closer to the right answer for sweet potatoes.**

## 6. Planting Cuttings and Raising Slips

### 6-1. Why Seed Sweet Potatoes Are Not Planted Directly

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Potatoes are cut from seed potatoes and planted straight into the field, but sweet potatoes are not. If a whole sweet potato is buried, several sprouts do come up on the spot, but the new roots do not thicken properly and instead grow as appendages of the original sweet potato, so the yield drops sharply. So the seed sweet potatoes are first sprouted in a seedling bed to grow vines, and those vines are cut and inserted into the field. These cut vines are called planting shoots, or slips.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The advantage of this method is the multiplication rate. A single seed sweet potato yields 20-30 slips. That is, by keeping just ten seed sweet potatoes alive, one can obtain 200-300 slips the following year and fill a plot of field. This is the basis for the S that Seed saving received in the evaluation table.

### 6-2. Building a Seedling Bed - Without Plastic Film or Electricity

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The key to raising sweet potato seedlings is to bring the soil temperature up to 25-30°C from late March to April (in South Korea). Without an electric heating cable, fermentation heat is used. The traditional hotbed method works just as it always did.

| Step | Details |
|---|---|
| 1 | In a sunny, south-facing spot, dig a pit 50-60cm deep and 1-1.2m wide |
| 2 | Mix fresh horse or cow dung with straw and fallen leaves and tread it into the pit to a thickness of 30-40cm |
| 3 | Sprinkle water to wet it and cover it for a few days; fermentation starts and gives off heat |
| 4 | Once the fermentation heat rises, cover it with 10-15cm of soil mixed with sand |
| 5 | When the soil temperature settles at around 30°C, lay the seed sweet potatoes down and cover them with 3-4cm of soil |
| 6 | Cover with a straw mat or transparent cover at night and open it during the day |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Horse dung gives the strongest and longest-lasting fermentation heat. If there are no livestock, piling fallen leaves mixed with rice bran and urine also produces heat. A fermentation-heat hotbed holds a temperature of 20-40°C for one to one and a half months, which is exactly the duration and temperature that sweet potato seedling production needs.

| Seedling-bed condition | Standard |
|---|---|
| Orientation of seed sweet potatoes | Lay them down with the thicker end (opposite the stalk end) pointing slightly upward |
| Spacing of seed sweet potatoes | 3-5cm, so they do not touch each other |
| Bed soil temperature | 30-32°C at first, 25°C once sprouts emerge |
| Duration | Slips can be cut in 30-40 days |
| Water | Only enough to keep it from drying out. Too much moisture makes them rot |

### 6-3. Choosing Slips

| Item | Standard | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Length | 20-30cm | If too short, there are not enough nodes |
| Number of nodes | 5-7 | Sweet potatoes form at every buried node |
| Thickness | At least as thick as a pencil | Thin ones establish poorly |
| Part | The tip end of the vine (the part with the growing point) | It has the best vigor |
| How to cut | Do not pull it up by the roots; cut 2-3cm above the ground | New shoots grow again from the remaining stump, making a second and third cutting possible |
| After cutting | Leave it in the shade for a day so the cut surface heals | The establishment rate goes up |

### 6-4. Planting Time

| Region | Timing | Standard |
|---|---|---|
| Southern South Korea | Late April to mid-May | |
| Central South Korea | Mid-May to early June | |
| Common standard | When a soil temperature of 18°C or above and a minimum temperature of 15°C or above have stabilized | Temperature, rather than the calendar, is the standard |
| Deadline | Avoid planting after late June | The growing period falls short and only small sweet potatoes result |

### 6-5. How to Plant - Laying the Cuttings Down

| Item | Standard | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Orientation | Laid down, close to horizontal | If planted upright, roots form at only one point |
| Number of nodes buried | 3-4 nodes | Sweet potatoes form at every node |
| Depth | 3-5cm | If deep, the soil is cooler and establishment is slower |
| Part left exposed | The 2-3 leaves at the tip left above ground | Photosynthesis helps establishment |
| Plant spacing | 25-30cm | |
| Ridge spacing | 70-90cm | |
| After planting | Water immediately, 100 cc or more per plant | Whether the plant establishes is decided here |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There are several variations of the lay-down method. Horizontal planting, in which the slip is laid straight and flat, yields many sweet potatoes of even size, and they are easy to dig because they do not hang deep. Improved horizontal planting, in which the slip is bent into a sickle shape, establishes somewhat faster. Slanted planting, in which the slip is inserted at an angle, withstands drought well, but fewer sweet potatoes form and their sizes are uneven. Where watering cannot be done often, slanted planting is better; where water is secure, horizontal planting is better.

> **The difference in harvest between a field planted upright and one planted lying down can reach a factor of two. The planting posture alone makes or breaks the year's crop.**

### 6-6. Disinfecting Slips

| Method | Procedure | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Hot-water treatment | Soak in 50-55°C water for 10-15 minutes | Kills pathogens on the surface |
| Wood-ash treatment | Dust the cut surface with dry ash | Prevents rot at the cut surface |
| Wood-vinegar soak | 10 minutes in a 100-fold dilution | Assists sterilization |
| Selection of healthy plants | Discard shoots with spots or wilting | The most effective method |
