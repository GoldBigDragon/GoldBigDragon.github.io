## 7. Water Management

### 7-1. Standards by Growth Stage

| Timing | Water management | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Right after planting | Soak thoroughly. 100 cc or more per plant | The only condition under which a rootless stem survives |
| Establishment stage (2 weeks) | Top up when dry. Planting on a cloudy day helps | Until roots emerge, water keeps escaping from the leaves |
| Vine elongation stage | No irrigation. Leave it to natural rainfall | With plenty of water, only the vines grow |
| Storage-root initiation stage (30-50 days) | Keep slightly dry | Drought stress promotes the formation of storage roots |
| Storage-root bulking stage (50-120 days) | If drought lasts 2 weeks or more, water thoroughly once a week | Water shortage in this stage cuts yield the most |
| 3 weeks before harvest | Stop irrigation | Sugar content rises and storability improves |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Water right after planting matters most. Slips are planted without roots, so there is no way to replace the water evaporating from the leaves. If a slip does not get through this stage, it simply dries out. Where water is short, plant on a cloudy day or just before rain, and for a few days after planting, shade the plants during the day by covering them with straw or grass. Cutting off about half of the leaves to reduce transpiration is another method.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Drought damage during the bulking stage is confirmed by figures. In a trial plot where water was short for 60 days after transplanting, the average weight of a single sweet potato fell from 41.2g to 10.6g, a drop of as much as 75%. If the capacity to haul water is limited, it should be concentrated in this period, roughly July to August in South Korea.

### 7-2. Symptoms of Too Little and Too Much Water

| Condition | Symptoms | Response |
|---|---|---|
| Shortage | Leaves curl inward at midday and new leaves become small | If in the bulking stage, irrigate at once; at other stages, leave it alone |
| Severe shortage | Growth at the vine tips stops and the lower leaves turn yellow and drop | Irrigate and cover with a straw mulch |
| Excess | The vines are excessively lush, with large, dark green leaves | Dig drainage ditches and lift the vines |
| Flooding | The roots turn black and give off a foul smell | Cannot be recovered. Drain the water at once and dig the crop if it can be dug |

## 8. Crop Management

### 8-1. Lifting the Vines

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Sweet potato vines put out roots at every node that touches the ground. These roots do not thicken but stay thin, and by absorbing nutrients and water they rob the sweet potato at the original spot of the nutrients that would have gone to it. As a result, small sweet potatoes are scattered over the whole field and no large ones appear.

| Task | Timing | Method | Effect |
|---|---|---|---|
| Lifting the vines | From July, every 2-3 weeks (in South Korea) | Lift the vines slightly, break the node roots, and put the vines back in place | Nutrients concentrate in the original plant |
| Turning the vines over | Same timing | Turn the whole vine over to the opposite side | More effective than lifting, but the leaves are damaged |
| Pinching | When the vines are excessively lush | Cut off about 30cm of the tip | The cut stems are eaten as namul (seasoned vegetable side dish) |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;It is better to lift the vines on a cloudy day or in the evening. If done at midday, the leaves are turned over and scorched by the sun, and growth stops for a few days from the stress.

### 8-2. Weeding

| Item | Details |
|---|---|
| Critical period | Up to 40 days after planting, before the vines cover the ground |
| Effect | Besides removing weeds, it breaks up the hardened topsoil and improves aeration and water infiltration |
| Yield difference | Yield differences of 40-50% are reported between fields that were weeded and fields that were not |
| Afterward | Once the vines cover the ground, weeds are suppressed on their own |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Early growth in sweet potatoes is slow, so they are easily crowded out by weeds. But once the vines cover the ground, they become a powerful weed suppressant themselves. In other words, if the first 40 days are protected, the rest of the season needs almost no hand labor. In a disaster where labor is short, this trait matters. Effort can be concentrated in the first month and afterward spent on other work.

### 8-3. Mulching

| Type | Effect | In a disaster |
|---|---|---|
| Black plastic film | Raises soil temperature, suppresses weeds, retains moisture | The most effective, but not possible without plastic film |
| Straw, hay | Retains moisture, suppresses weeds, improves soil | First choice as a substitute for plastic film. Thickness of 5cm or more |
| Fallen leaves | Retain moisture, add organic matter | Put soil on top so the wind does not blow them away |
| Cut-grass cover | Retains moisture, suppresses weeds | Cut the weeds around the field and spread them as they are. Do this before the seeds ripen |

### 8-4. Harvesting Leaves and Stems

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The leaf stalks and young stems of the sweet potato are an excellent vegetable. They can be picked repeatedly all summer without greatly affecting the root yield. In a disaster, this is an important side benefit, because it means the field is already producing food during the four months of waiting before the roots can be dug.

| Item | Standard |
|---|---|
| Harvest period | From 60 days after planting until frost |
| Amount picked | No more than a third of all the leaves at one time |
| Part | Young stems and leaf stalks within 15cm of the vine tip |
| Preparation | Peeling the outer skin of the leaf stalks keeps them from being tough |
| Cooking | Blanch and eat as namul, or add to soup. Avoid eating raw |
| Caution | Picking too often delays root bulking |

## 9. Pests and Diseases

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The sweet potato carries a relatively low burden of pests and diseases. Pest-control self-reliance received a C in the evaluation, but the reasoning is that pest control is almost unnecessary and, where needed, natural materials are enough. Unlike the potato, it has no fatal disease for which there is no remedy.

### 9-1. Major Diseases

| Disease | Symptoms | Favorable conditions | Response without chemicals |
|---|---|---|---|
| Soft rot | Roots turn soft and give off a foul smell | Wounds, excess moisture, high temperature in storage | Harvest without wounding, cure thoroughly, remove infected roots at once |
| Black rot (black spot disease) | Black spots on the root surface, bitter taste | Infected seed sweet potatoes and slips | Select healthy seed sweet potatoes, hot-water treatment |
| Fusarium wilt (vine-splitting disease) | The base of the stem splits and wilts | Soil-borne | Resistant varieties, healthy slips, drainage |
| Anthracnose | Brown lesions on leaves and stems | Excess moisture in the monsoon season (jangma, late June to July in South Korea) | Ensure ventilation, remove diseased leaves |
| Powdery mildew | White powder on the leaf surface | Dry weather with large day-night temperature swings | Spray diluted milk (1:9) |
| Virus disease | Leaf mosaic, deformity, reduced yield | Aphid transmission, transmission through seed sweet potatoes | Select seed sweet potatoes only from healthy plants |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The disease to be most careful about in sweet potatoes is black rot. Infected sweet potatoes taste bitter and produce substances harmful to humans and livestock. During storage, black spots appear, and the affected area hardens and tastes bitter. This part must be cut out without fail, and if the spots have spread to several places, the whole sweet potato must be thrown away. Fed to livestock, it causes pulmonary edema.

> **If a sweet potato tastes bitter, do not eat it. Sweet potatoes are not bitter by nature, and bitterness is a sign of disease.**

### 9-2. Major Pests

| Pest | Damage | Control without chemicals |
|---|---|---|
| Grubs (click beetle larvae) | They bore holes in the roots, lowering marketability and storability | No immature compost, potato-piece traps, crop rotation, plowing the field under after harvest |
| Sweet potato leaf folder | Attacks leaves and stems | Remove damaged leaves, burn crop residue |
| Aphids | Suck sap and transmit viruses | Spray soapy water, protect ladybugs |
| Spider mites | The undersides of leaves turn white | Spray water forcefully. They occur in dry weather |
| Cutworms | Cut young slips off at the base | After planting, sprinkle ash around the base or put a paper collar around it |
| Nematodes | Galls on the roots, rough surface | Grow marigolds, crop rotation, resistant varieties |
| Field mice, wild boar | Dig up and eat the roots | Fences, harvesting earlier |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In a disaster, the sweet potato's biggest practical pest is not an insect but wild animals. Wild boar and field mice find the sweet potato field with precision. Realistic responses are to place objects that carry human scent around the field, to lay thorny branches around it, or to hurry the harvest.

### 9-3. Natural Control Agents

| Control agent | Preparation | Use |
|---|---|---|
| Soapy water | 20g of unscented soap in 1L of water | Aphids, spider mites |
| Diluted milk | Milk and water at 1 to 9 | Powdery mildew |
| Wood ash | Sifted dry ash applied to dew-covered leaves | Repels pests, supplements potassium |
| Wood vinegar | Diluted 50-100-fold | Repels pests, assists sterilization |
| Hot-water treatment | 50-55°C, 10-15 minutes | Disinfecting seed sweet potatoes and slips |

### 9-4. Prevention Principles

| Principle | Details |
|---|---|
| Healthy planting stock | Use only slips raised from disease-free seed sweet potatoes. Half the outcome is decided here |
| Drainage | Make the raised beds high |
| Curing | Wounds must be healed after harvest so that disease does not spread in storage |
| Residue disposal | Do not leave vines and leftover roots in the field after harvest |
| Culling diseased plants | Mark plants showing symptoms during the growing season and do not use them as seed sweet potatoes |

## 10. Nutrient Deficiency and Excess

| Symptom | Cause | How to confirm | Remedy with natural materials |
|---|---|---|---|
| Turns yellow from the lower leaves and the vines become thin | Nitrogen deficiency | Progresses from the older leaves | Well-rotted compost; diluted urine (20-fold) only in the establishment stage |
| Leaf edges scorch brown and curl | Potassium deficiency | Starts at leaf tips and edges | A small amount of wood ash in the furrows; compost of grass and fruit peels |
| Slow growth and leaves going from dark green to purplish | Phosphate deficiency | Common in cold periods | Bone meal, wood ash |
| The growing point of new leaves dries up | Calcium deficiency | Progresses from young leaves | Burn eggshells, crush them, and scatter |
| Yellowing only between the leaf veins | Magnesium deficiency | Starts from the lower leaves | Dolomite; more variety of compost materials |
| New leaves pale yellow overall | Iron deficiency | When pH is high | Add organic matter; stop using wood ash |
| Only the vines are lush and the roots stay thin and do not thicken | Nitrogen excess | Leaves are large and deep green, and the vines are thick | Stop supplying nitrogen, lift the vines, supplement potassium with wood ash |
| Roots are only thin and long | Nitrogen excess or excess moisture | Many fine roots | Improve drainage, stop watering |
| Roots crack and are deformed | Soil hardening, uneven moisture | Hard soil or marks from hitting stones | Till deeply and raise the beds |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;By far the most common problem in sweet potatoes is not deficiency but nitrogen excess. The typical situation is vines as tall and lush as a person, and no sweet potatoes when dug up. Once this symptom shows, there is not much that can be done that year. The answer is to cut losses by diligently lifting the vines and pinching the tips to eat as namul, and not to add any fertilizer to that spot the next year.

## 11. Intercropping and Crop Rotation

### 11-1. Compatibility

| Compatibility | Companion | Effect |
|:---:|---|---|
| Very good | Legumes (soybean, cowpea, kidney bean) | They fix nitrogen and supply people with the protein that sweet potatoes lack |
| Good | Maize, sorghum | Under tall crops the sweet potatoes cover the ground, so the space is used twice over |
| Good | Marigold | Suppresses root-knot nematodes |
| Good | Perilla | Its smell repels some pests, and it is a source of oil |
| Fair | Lettuce, spinach | Harvested early, before the sweet potato vines cover the ground, and then removed |
| Poor | Potato | Shares grubs and nematodes and competes for space |
| Poor | Tomato, chili pepper, eggplant | Nematode damage overlaps |
| Poor | Squash | Both creep over the ground, so competition for space is severe |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The pairing of sweet potatoes and legumes means more than simple compatibility. The sweet potato's one fatal flaw is its protein shortage, and beans fill it precisely. At the same time, nitrogen fixation by legume roots naturally supplies the small amount of nitrogen the sweet potato needs. In the field and at the table, the two are partners.

> **Next to a sweet potato field there must always be a bean field. This is not a farming technique but a nutritional design.**

### 11-2. Crop Rotation

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The sweet potato is classified in the group unharmed by continuous cropping. This means that planting it in the same spot repeatedly causes no major problems, a highly exceptional property among tuber crops (tubers/storage roots). However, it is not completely harmless: after several years, levels of nematodes and Fusarium wilt can rise.

| System | When it applies | Details |
|---|---|---|
| Continuous cropping | When the field is small and there is no room to spare | Fix sweet potatoes in one place. Add a small amount of compost every year |
| 2-year alternation | When there is a little room to spare | Alternate sweet potatoes and legumes |
| 3-year rotation | Recommended | Sweet potato → legumes → leafy vegetables or grains |
| When nematodes appear | Essential | Plant marigold or sudangrass for one year and plow it under |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Planting a green manure is better than leaving the field empty over winter. If rye or hairy vetch is sown in October after the sweet potato harvest (in South Korea), it prevents soil loss through the winter, and when plowed under in spring it becomes organic matter and nitrogen. Hairy vetch is a legume and fixes nitrogen, but because that can be too much nitrogen for sweet potatoes, it is safer to regulate the amount or to use rye instead.
