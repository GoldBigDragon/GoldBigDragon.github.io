## Introduction

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Apios (Apios americana) is a legume vine that once grew wild along riverbanks and the damp edges of forests in eastern North America. Native peoples of North America dug and ate it for a long time, and even after Europeans arrived, people kept digging it out of the ground during every famine, yet it never became a large-scale field crop the way wheat or maize did — the reasons being that harvesting it is tedious and the first harvest takes a long time to come. Apios grown in South Korea today is in a similar position: there is only trial cultivation and cultivation on a handful of farms, and it is not a crop that appears in national statistics. In other words, apios is a plant that has not yet fully walked out of the wild.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Yet under the standard this document uses — an evaluation that assumes electricity, logistics, and chemical inputs have all stopped — apios ranks near the very top, with an overall grade of SS and a score of 67.7. That puts it above wheat, above maize, above the potato. If this result feels strange, it is because we normally judge crops by a different standard. Ordinary-times agriculture competes on yield per area on the assumption that fertilizer, pesticides, and machinery can be used freely, and on that field grain crops are overwhelming. But when nothing is supplied, the question changes: when no one applies fertilizer, no one sprays, and no one tills the field, does this plant keep producing food? Set against that question, the rankings are turned completely upside down.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This is exactly what apios is good at. Being a legume, it carries root-nodule bacteria and fixes its own nitrogen, so it does not go hungry even without fertilizer. Being a perennial, once planted it comes back again every year, with no need to store seed and no need to till the field over again each year. Even below -20°C, the tubers survive the winter in the ground. Pests and diseases barely touch it, so pest control itself becomes unnecessary. On top of that, as a tuber crop (tubers/storage roots) it reaches a protein share of calories of 13.9%, rare for the category and higher even than the potato. This combination — fertilizer independence, perenniality, and protein — is a combination no grain can match.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There is one thing, however, that this document wants to make clear from the outset: the high score for apios rests on thin evidence. This crop has no official national yield statistics. The yield per area used in the evaluation, 2,000kg, is far from the 500kg to 800kg reported in domestic trial plantings in South Korea. The pest and disease susceptibility rate is not a value measured on apios itself but an estimate borrowed from other legume crops, and the zero-input yield retention rate of 45.1% is likewise an imputed value calculated by applying the potato's organic-to-conventional yield ratio to tuber crops as a whole. In other words, apios's grade stands on reasonable inference about the plant's nature, not on decades of measured data. This does not mean its strengths are not real; it means the size of those strengths should not be taken as literal numbers. And there is no way to fill this gap except for growers to plant it themselves and find out.
