## Introduction
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Apios (Apios americana) is a legume vine that grew on its own along rivers and at the edges of damp forests in eastern North America. Native peoples of North America dug and ate it for a long time, but harvesting is laborious and the first harvest takes years, so it never became a large-scale field crop, and in Korea it does not appear in national statistics. Yet in an evaluation that assumes electricity, logistics, and chemical inputs are all cut off, it ranks above grains and potatoes with an overall SS (67.7 points, 1st of 183 valid species). Ordinary agriculture competes on yield per area on the premise that fertilizer, pesticides, and machines can be used freely, but the ranking flips the moment the question becomes whether a plant keeps producing food when no one fertilizes, no one sprays, and no one tills the field. Being a legume, it makes its own nitrogen with rhizobia; being perennial, it comes back every year once planted; its underground tubers overwinter even below -20℃; it needs no pest control; and its protein-to-calorie ratio of 13.9% is the highest among roots and tubers. However, there are no national yield statistics, and many cells in the evaluation table are filled with estimates and substitute values rather than measurements, so the size of the strengths must not be trusted at face value.

| Category | Grade | Score |

| Short term | SS | 67.0 |

| Evaluation | Items |
|:---|:---|
| Strengths | SS: perenniality, fertilization, facility burden, temperature range, cold tolerance, pest-control self-reliance, continuous-cropping tolerance, land-preparation and digging energy. S: calories per area, taste, seed saving. A: protein density, water consumption, yield stability. B: cooking and processing requirement, yield retention without synthetic inputs |
| Weaknesses | E: seed longevity. D: micronutrients, harvest speed, sunlight. C: pest and disease pressure, storability, non-food value, domestic cultivation spread |

> **What the disaster preparedness evaluation measures is not how much a crop yields when it is well tended but how much is left when no one tends it. Everything about why Apios beat the grains is in that sentence.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;All eight SS items ask what people must put in, and Apios answers that nothing needs to be put in for any of them. Nitrogen in particular is the first nutrient to be cut off in a disaster and the first reason grains collapse without inputs, but Apios does not pass through that bottleneck. Digging energy is amortized over 10 years, and the calories returned per unit of digging energy invested are 161 times (potato 10.6 times); its yield retention without synthetic inputs is B (45.1%), far higher than potato's E (17.6%). On the other side, read together the harvest speed D, which means waiting two to three years after planting for a proper harvest, micronutrients D, domestic cultivation spread C (seed tubers can be found in only one or two provinces), and sunlight D (yield halves under 40% shade). The grade for each item is in the Crop Evaluation Results document.

| Item | Displayed value | Actual basis |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Calories per area | 2,000 kg/1,000 ㎡ | The reported value from domestic trial cultivation is 500-800 kg; this is close to an upper-bound assumption for good conditions |
| Pest and disease pressure | Loss rate 48.9% | An estimate borrowed from legumes as a whole |
| Yield retention without synthetic inputs | 45.1% | A substitute value that applies potato's organic-to-conventional yield ratio to roots and tubers in general |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;That Apios fixes nitrogen, overwinters underground, and comes back year after year follows from the plant's nature and does not waver, but the yield per area, the yield stability, and the losses when disease spreads have never been properly counted by anyone. So you must not bet all your food on Apios alone, and since it gives no food in the autumn of the year a disaster strikes, you plant it in advance in ordinary times.

## 2. Plant Overview
| Item | Details |
|:---|:---|
| Classification | Perennial vine of the legume family, genus Apios |
| Leaves and vines | Pinnately compound leaves with 5-7 leaflets. The vine grows up to 3-5 m and, with nothing to climb, crawls along the ground and tangles |
| Tubers and nodules | Tubers the size of a quail egg to a hen egg hang along the rhizomes like a string of beads, 20-50 per plant. Millet-grain-sized nodules on the fine roots |
| Flowers and pods | Strongly scented flowers, from reddish purple to pinkish brown, bloom in June-August. The 5-10 cm pods do not set in some years |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;About 15 days after planting, shoots rise from the eyes of the tubers and the vines spread, and the tubers swell in August-September (peak potassium demand). In the first year the plant puts its strength into rooting and spreading rhizomes, so no harvestable size forms; edible tubers appear from the autumn of the second year, and yield stabilizes in the third year. Per 100 g there are 175 kcal (2.5 times potato), about 38 g carbohydrate, 6.10 g protein (three times potato, with all the essential amino acids), and about 1 g fat, and dietary fiber is about 5 times that of potato, calcium about 30 times, iron about 4 times, and vitamin C about 5 times. Because micronutrients are D, pair it with crops rich in carotene such as leafy greens or pumpkin.

## 3. Growing Environment and Varieties
| Condition | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Temperature | Growth 5-30℃ (optimum 25-30℃). Growth stops below 5℃ but the plant does not die, and above 30℃ only the vines grow and tuber swelling slows. Emergence needs a mean temperature of 15℃ or more. It survives underground down to -20℃, so it overwinters throughout Korea with no protection |
| Light and soil | 4-6 hours a day or more (optimum 8 hours or more). pH 4.5-7.0 (optimum 5.0-6.0; in alkaline soil iron and manganese uptake is blocked and the leaves turn yellow). Sandy loam to loam (heavy clay deforms the tubers) |
| Water | Constantly moist is optimum, and it tolerates flooding for a considerable time (very strong against excess moisture among roots and tubers). Dryness makes the tubers small. Total evapotranspiration is about 400 mm (potato 500-700 mm) |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;A temperature range of 25℃ is insurance against a cool year or a hot year ever wiping the crop out completely. The value of Apios lies not in good fields but in turning marginal land that other crops cannot use into food-producing ground, so waterside ground, ditch banks, paddy levees, damp foot slopes below hillsides (organic matter accumulates, so no fertilizer is needed), and field edges and fences (used as supports) are good. Avoid full shade or forest interiors, calcareous alkaline soil (pH 7 or above), and the middle of a field that must be plowed often.

| Strain | Characteristics | Yield (kg per 1,000 ㎡) |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Red-vine strain | Early maturing, with many tubers. Suited to places with early frost | 500-800 |
| Green-vine strain | Late maturing, with fewer but larger tubers, good as seed tubers | 500-700 |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Because domestic spread is C, there is almost no room to choose a strain in a disaster. If you secure a spot and plant in ordinary times, it multiplies by itself every year, so ten tubers now become hundreds of seed tubers a few years later, and this is the cheapest preparation.

## 4. Field Preparation and Sowing
| Item | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Land preparation | In March plow to 20-30 cm (the deeper, the larger the tubers), with beds at least 30 cm high and drainage ditches at least 30 cm deep. Partition with boards or ditches so the rhizomes cannot cross into neighboring fields, and cover with rice straw or fallen leaves |
| Fertilization (1,000 ㎡) | No lime (dolomitic lime 120 kg only to correct strong acidity). Nitrogen 8 kg, phosphorus 12 kg, potassium 12 kg, fully matured compost 1,000 kg (all as base fertilizer). Too much nitrogen makes the rhizobia lazy and lets only the vines grow lush so the tubers stay small, so it hinders rather than helps |
| Disaster substitute fertilizers | Wood ash (a handful per ㎡ spread thinly; too much raises pH and does harm), compost aged 6 months or more, bone meal (as base fertilizer only), urine diluted 10 times, and mulch of fallen leaves and grass 5-10 cm. On first-time ground, mix a handful of soil from a field where Apios or beans grew into the planting hole to inoculate rhizobia |
| Seed tubers | Freshly dug in March (better than those dug in autumn and stored), about 5 g, firm and unwrinkled, with distinct bumps at the eyes. Exclude tubers with black spots or soft parts. Pre-sprouting makes emergence even |
| Planting | Plant in early to mid April after the last frost, at a mean temperature of about 15℃. Depth 5-7.5 cm, 30 cm between seed tubers, 60-75 cm between rows (about 3,300 per 1,000 ㎡). Press lightly, then irrigate. Emergence takes about 15 days (up to a month if late), so do not dig impatiently to check |

> **Planting direction is where failures happen most. The upper end, where the stem emerges, is blunt with clustered bumps, and the lower end, where roots emerge, is smooth and pointed; the pointed end goes down. If you cannot tell, plant the tuber lying on its side. Lying down it finds its own direction, but planted upside down it dies.**

## 5. Water Management and Cultivation Management
| Stage | Water management |
|:---|:---|
| Planting to vegetative growth (May-July) | Water just enough to keep the soil from drying; natural rainfall is sufficient, and supplement it if dry (dryness in this period is recoverable) |
| Tuber swelling (August-September) | Irrigate thoroughly (the only decisive period for yield; in a dry rainy season the tubers cannot thicken) |
| Maturity and harvest (October onward) | Stop irrigating (the skin hardens and storability rises) |

| Care | Method |
|:---|:---|
| Stakes | Set stakes every 2 m along the row and stretch cucumber netting or twine in a grid at 1.8-2 m height. When the vines reach about 30 cm, hang them on once, and drive the stake bases at least 30 cm into the soil. Without cucumber netting, plant maize or sunflower every other row to serve as supports, or plant beside a fence |
| Typhoon preparation | Before the forecast, mound soil around the stake bases and pull diagonal cords from stakes at both ends of the row. An August typhoon is mostly rain damage, so clear the drainage ditches first; a September typhoon brings rain and strong wind together, so securing the stakes comes first |
| Other care | Weed only early, in May-June (once the vines cover the ground, weeds decline). Picking flowers in July-August sends more nutrients to the tubers, and the picked flowers are dried for tea. Once a year, cut off rhizomes that have strayed outside the plot |

## 6. Pests, Diseases, and Nutrient Problems
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Pest and disease pressure is C but pest-control self-reliance is SS. This means no disease is known that, like potato late blight or cassava mosaic disease, wipes everything out once it enters, and when Apios is grown on a large scale, pests and diseases specific to it may appear.

| Pest or disease | Symptoms and conditions | Control without chemicals |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Aphids, spider mites | Suck sap from new shoots and leaf undersides in the summer heat / Leaves turn rough with silvery-white spots in hot, dry weather | Wash off with water, spray diluted soapy water (ash lye or natural soap diluted 500 times), wood vinegar diluted 200-500 times, protect ladybugs / Spray water on leaf undersides to raise humidity |
| Bacterial leaf spot, powdery mildew | Black spots on leaves in the rainy season / White powder on leaf surfaces in late summer | Ensure ventilation, pick off diseased leaves and burn them, and lift the vines on stakes / Spray milk diluted 10 times with water |
| Field mice, moles, wild boar | Eat tubers underground in winter and spring / Dig up the ground and eat in autumn and winter | Clear weeds around the field and bury boards around the plot / Fences, blocking scent |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In a disaster the greatest threat is not fungi or bacteria but animals. Tubers left in the ground are a food store open all winter to wild boar and field mice too, and in a year when people starve animals starve too, so put a fence around the storage area and dig up part of the harvest separately and move it indoors to spread the risk.

| Symptom | Cause and action |
|:---|:---|
| Lush vines but small tubers | Nitrogen excess, the problem seen most often in Apios. Around July dig lightly at the fine roots; if there are pink, millet-grain-sized nodules and the inside is red when cut open, nitrogen fixation is active, so cut off nitrogen and supplement potassium with wood ash |
| Lower leaves fade to light green, no nodules | Nitrogen deficiency (failed symbiosis). Give a small amount of dilute urine and mix in soil from a field where Apios or legumes grew to re-inoculate |
| Leaves turn purple / edges turn brown and scorch / only the veins stay green | Phosphorus deficiency / potassium deficiency / iron or magnesium deficiency (pH 7 or above). Bone meal and wood ash / wood ash / lower the pH with leaf mold and compost |

## 7. Intercropping and Crop Rotation
| Compatibility | Companions |
|:---|:---|
| Good | Maize and sunflower (they serve as living supports and Apios leaves nitrogen behind), leafy greens (the leftover nitrogen is used right the next year), under orchard trees (the vines suppress weeds and supply nitrogen) |
| Fair | Root vegetables (they mix with the rhizomes and make harvesting troublesome) |
| Poor | Fields that are turned over often, other vine crops such as pumpkin and cucumber (they tangle and make harvesting hard for both) |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The greatest gift Apios gives neighboring crops is nitrogen. Because it is perennial, this accumulation builds year after year, and ground where Apios has grown for a few years grows other crops well from the first year. Rotation hardly applies (continuous-cropping tolerance SS) and leaving it in the same place for a long time is the rule; when moving a plot, the order is Apios (5-10 years) → leafy greens or grains → root vegetables → Apios again, and crops that use a lot of nitrogen, such as Chinese cabbage and spinach, do well on cleared ground. Even a single fingernail-sized tuber fragment left behind will sprout the next year, so plant where the ground can be given up for a long time, such as field edges, beside fences, and ditch banks.

## 8. Harvest and Seed Saving
| Harvest | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Timing | From 2-3 years after planting (do not dig in the first year). Tubers can be dug at any time from mid-November, when the vines have dried yellow and died, until March; they do not deteriorate in the ground and in fact part of the starch turns to sugar, improving the flavor. Seed tubers are dug in March of the following year, and flowers in early to mid August (just before full bloom) |
| Method | Dig in from the outside, at least 30 cm from the plant, follow the rhizome by hand, and lift the tubers strung like beads. Do not strike with a spade (wounded tubers cannot be stored). Only brush off the soil and do not wash (they soften within days when wet, so wash just before eating), and dig only what you need and leave the rest covered with soil |
| Handling | Eat large ones right away or store them briefly, process medium ones by drying and milling, set aside ones of about 5 g as seed tubers, and eat wounded ones first |

| Seed saving | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Seed tubers | Seed longevity is E, but the tubers in the ground are themselves a living seed store and multiply by themselves every year. Plant small tubers of about 5 g, freshly dug in March of the following year, within 2 weeks. Although propagated vegetatively, there are no major diseases, so there is almost no generational degeneration (unlike potato) |
| When storage is needed | Buried 5-10 cm deep in a pot of soil and kept somewhere frost-free, they last until spring; wrapped in wet newspaper and kept somewhere cool, 2-4 weeks |
| Propagation by seed | Plants do not breed true to the parent, so it is impractical; but if only vegetative propagation continues, the whole field becomes one individual and collapses all at once when a fatal disease strikes. If the disaster lasts many years, soak seeds from pods that have dried brown for a day or nick the seed coat, and grow a few seed-grown plants separately |

## 9. Storage
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Drying is the greatest enemy of Apios storage. The cork layer is thin, so tubers die when they dry out, and humidity of 90% or more must be maintained; storage at room temperature is practically limited to 2-3 weeks.

| Method | How | Period |
|:---|:---|:---|
| In-ground storage | Leave undug in the ground, and after the first frost remove the vines and cover with 20-30 cm of straw or fallen leaves (to keep the ground from freezing rock-hard so you cannot dig). Needs no electricity, facilities, or labor and keeps the freshest, but becomes a target for field mice and wild boar | All winter, up to several years |
| Sand-layer storage | Layer moist sand and tubers in a box and keep at 0-4℃, sprinkling a little water when the sand dries | 2-3 months |
| Dugout cellar and pit storage | For a cellar, dig at least 1.5 m into well-drained ground, make a roof and a vent, and keep humidity at 85-95% (3-4 months). For a pit, dig 1-1.5 m across and 30 cm deep, lay dry straw, and pile the tubers in a mound, filling between them with moist sand or leaf mold to prevent drying; cover with 20-30 cm of straw and 15 cm of soil, make a vent at the top with a straw bundle, and dig a drainage ditch around it | 2-4 months |
| Dried chips, flour, starch | Slice 3-5 mm thick, blanch in boiling water for 2-3 minutes, then dry completely (unblanched they brown and keep the inhibitors). Store milled flour in a sealed container with ash or charcoal; starch, made by grinding, dispersing in water, settling, rinsing the sediment several times and drying, uses even trimmings and wounded tubers completely | Chips 6 months-1 year, flour 1-2 years, starch several years |

> **The basis of a storage strategy is dispersal. Leave most in the ground as winter food, and turn some into dried chips and flour to guard against animals and severe cold.**

## 10. Cooking and Precautions
| Caution | Details |
|:---|:---|
| Always cook | It is not solanine (a nightshade compound) but the trypsin inhibitors and lectins common to legumes that make raw tubers indigestible, causing abdominal pain, nausea, and diarrhea. Because you cannot tell by eye whether they are undercooked, cook them thoroughly for at least 30 minutes in boiling water or a steamer |
| Saponin | Cause of the bitterness and foam; it decreases if the first cooking water is discarded. A small amount is harmless |
| Allergy | As a legume, test first with a small amount if you are allergic to peanuts or beans |

| Cooking | Method |
|:---|:---|
| Steaming and boiling | Steam with just a little water for 30 minutes (richer flavor, less nutrient loss). Boil fully submerged for 30 minutes; discarding the first water reduces bitterness and removes the inhibitors most reliably |
| Saving fuel | Boiling for only 10 minutes leaves inhibitors inside, so do not shorten the time but trap the heat. After boiling for 15 minutes, wrap the pot in straw or thick cloth and leave it for 1 hour or more to use less than half the fuel. Bury in hot ash and roast for 30-40 minutes, or stir flour into boiling water to cook it at once |
| Taste and how to eat | A nutty flavor like chestnut mixed with potato. After cooking the skin peels off by hand, and it can be eaten with the skin. Boiled it is floury, roasted it is chewy |
| Processing | Mill dried boiled chips into flour and use it in dough and porridge mixed with grain flour; boiled tubers lactic-fermented in 2-3% salt water for 1-2 weeks make pickles that are easier on digestion (do not ferment raw). Flowers are dried for tea |

## 11. Annual Work Schedule
| Month | Work |
|:---|:---|
| Jan-Feb | Dig only what you need to eat (remove straw when the ground freezes), check stored tubers. In February turn the compost, prepare stake materials, and choose a spot for a new plot |
| Mar-Apr | In March collect and sort seed tubers, till, and apply base fertilizer and make beds. In early to mid April plant seed tubers (pointed end down) and mulch |
| May-June | In May check emergence, set up stakes and netting, and weed (hang the vines on the netting when they reach about 30 cm). In June train the vines; flowering begins |
| July-Aug | In July finish weeding, check nodules, irrigate in drought, and start picking flowers. In August harvest flowers, irrigate heavily during tuber swelling, and prepare for typhoons (the most important month for yield) |
| Sep-Oct | In September keep irrigating, prepare for typhoons (secure the stakes), and clear rhizomes outside the boundary. In October stop irrigating and watch the vines yellow (do not rush) |
| Nov-Dec | In November clear the vines, make the first harvest, and cover with 20-30 cm of straw. In December harvest what is needed, and dry and mill (move some indoors against animal damage) |

> **Apios is a crop to plant in advance in ordinary times (the first harvest comes two to three years later), plant with the pointed end down, always eat after cooking for at least 30 minutes, keep in the ground or in wet sand without washing, and, since it is hard to clear, plant within a set boundary on ground you can give up for a long time.**
