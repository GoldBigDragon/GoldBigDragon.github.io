## 1. Disaster-Preparedness Evaluation

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The overall grade for apios is SS, with a score of 67.7. Broken down by scenario, all three axes remain at the very top: short-term SS (67.0), long-term SS (68.5), and supplementary SS (65.7). The fact that the gap between short-term and long-term is only 1.5 points sums up the character of this crop. Most crops either give food quickly but cannot hold out for long, or hold out for long but give nothing in the first year. Apios has a major weakness — a late first harvest — yet its short-term score does not suffer, because while it sits in the ground waiting, the field demands no fertilizer and no human labor.

### 1-3. Why a Near-Wild Crop Outranks Grain Crops

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Gathering up the criteria marked SS in the table reveals one thing in common: perenniality, fertilizer demand, infrastructure needs, temperature range, cold hardiness, pest-control self-reliance, continuous-cropping tolerance, and tillage & digging energy. All eight of these ask the same question — what does a person have to put in? Apios answers all eight questions the same way: nothing has to be put in. By contrast, the criteria where grain crops dominate are calories per area, storability, and speed to harvest, and all three of these hold only on the assumption that fertilizer, pest control, and drying facilities are already in place.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Looking closely, three things are decisive. The first is the SS grade for fertilizer demand. Being a legume, apios hosts root-nodule bacteria on its roots and fixes atmospheric nitrogen directly. Nitrogen is the nutrient that runs out first, and most severely, in a disaster. Phosphorus and potassium can be patched together to some degree with bone meal and wood ash, but nitrogen cannot. The amount collected from urine and compost falls far short of what wheat or maize demands. This is the first reason grain crops collapse under zero-input conditions — and apios simply never passes through this bottleneck.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The second is the combination of the SS grade for perenniality and the SS grade for tillage & digging energy. An annual grain crop must be tilled, sown, and have its seed kept from spoiling until the next year, every single year — this labor and risk repeat annually. Apios, once planted, keeps coming back from the tubers left in the ground. Because its digging energy is amortized over 10 years, the annual figure is 22kcal — 10% of the potato's. The energy returned per unit of digging energy invested is 161×, not even comparable to the potato's 10.6×. When human labor is the only power source available, this difference is exactly the difference in how much area survives.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The third is yield retention without synthetic inputs. Apios scores B (45.1%), while the potato scores E (17.6%). When fertilizer and chemicals disappear, the potato collapses to a sixth of its yield, while apios holds onto just over half. Having originally lived in the wild without any help, it has little to lose. Being less improved is a disadvantage in ordinary times, but in a world where the inputs that sustained that improvement have vanished, it becomes an advantage outright.

> **What the disaster-preparedness evaluation measures is not how much a crop yields when well cared for, but how much remains when no one cares for it at all. Every reason apios outranks grain crops is contained in that one sentence.**

### 1-4. But the Evidence Behind This Score Is Thin

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There is something you must know before trusting apios: no official yield statistics exist for this crop. It is not a subject of national crop-condition surveys, and there is no decades-long trial data either. That is why many cells in the evaluation table are filled not with measured values but with estimates and imputed figures.

| Item | Value Shown | What the Evidence Actually Is |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Calories per area | 2,000kg/1,000m² | Domestic trial plantings in South Korea report 500-800kg/1,000m². The yield used in the evaluation is close to an upper-bound assumption under favorable conditions |
| Pest & disease pressure | Estimated susceptibility 48.9% | Not measured on apios itself; a reliability-L4 estimate borrowed from legumes as a whole |
| Yield retention without synthetic inputs | 45.1% | An imputed value obtained by applying the potato's organic-to-conventional yield ratio uniformly across tuber crops (tubers/storage roots) in general |
| Yield stability | Variation 6.3% | The median of 10 Northern European grain-legume anchor species, assigned to apios |
| Taste | Sugars 3.28g/100g | Not measured; filled in with the median of 19 species in the same family |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;What this table means is this: the facts that apios makes its own nitrogen, overwinters in the ground, and keeps coming back once planted are facts that follow from the plant's own nature, so they do not waver. But how much it yields per area, how far it drops in a bad year, and how much is lost if disease spreads — no one has properly counted any of that yet. If the grade for calories per area drops from S to B, the overall score moves by several points. So you should never stake your entire food supply on apios alone.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The criteria explicitly marked as weaknesses need to be read together with this. Speed to harvest at D means 200 days or more after planting, and in practice you must wait two to three years before a proper harvest comes — apios gives you nothing to eat in the autumn of the year a disaster strikes. Micronutrients at D means that even eating enough to cover your calorie needs, only seven vitamins and minerals exceed 10% of the recommended intake, so deficiency sets in unless you secure vegetables separately. Prevalence in South Korea at C is the practical constraint that, even if you want to plant it, seed tubers can only be obtained in one or two provinces. And sunlight at D reflects a trait common to legumes: yield drops by half with just 40% shade. Although it is a plant that grew at forest edges, it merely tolerates shade — it does not grow well in it.

## 2. What Kind of Plant Is This?

### 2-1. Taxonomy and Origin

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Apios is a perennial vine in the bean family (Fabaceae), genus Apios. It belongs to the same family as the soybean, adzuki bean, pea, and kudzu. This one fact explains almost every property apios has: forming nodules on its roots to fix nitrogen, leaving the soil more fertile, being unsafe to eat raw, and losing half its yield in 40% shade — all of these are traits of the bean family.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;It is native to eastern North America, growing wild from southern Canada to Florida along riverbanks, lakeshores, and the damp edges of forests. Two things follow from this origin. One is that it likes water and tolerates flooding fairly well — very different from most tuber crops (tubers/storage roots), which rot within days if submerged. The other is that it is accustomed to acidic soil: the leaf mold at forest edges generally sits around pH 5, and apios grows best in that range. This means there is no need to pour on lime to bring the soil to neutral, which matters a great deal when lime cannot be obtained.

### 2-2. Appearance

| Organ | Characteristics |
|---|---|
| Leaves | Pinnately compound. Made up of 5-7 leaflets, arranged oppositely |
| Stem (vine) | A climbing vine that grows up to 3-5m long. Without something to climb, it creeps along the ground and tangles with itself |
| Roots | Tubers hang along the underground stems in a row like beads on a string; this is the part that is eaten |
| Flowers | Bloom in June-August. Reddish-purple to pinkish-brown, with a strong fragrance that draws bees well |
| Fruit (pod) | A linear pod 5-10cm long, ripening from August into autumn. Some years it sets pods, other years it does not |
| Root nodules | Millet-grain-sized nodules form on the fine roots; they house the bacteria that fix nitrogen |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The way the tubers form in a chain matters in practice. Plant a single seed tuber, and in that same year the underground stems spread out in every direction, forming 20 to 50 tubers strung along those stems like beads. Their size varies widely, from quail-egg to hen's-egg. At harvest you have to lift out an entire strand, and if even one tuber is left in the ground, a new shoot comes up from that spot the following year. This trait is the substance behind the SS grade for perenniality — and it also means that once planted, apios is hard to get rid of.

### 2-3. Growth Stages

| Stage | Timing | Key Physiology | Key Nutrient |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dormancy/overwintering | November-March | The above-ground parts wither completely and die back; only the tubers survive the winter underground | Relies on its own reserves |
| Sprouting/emergence | April, average temperature around 15°C | Shoots rise from the eyes of the tubers, about 15 days after planting | Phosphorus |
| Vegetative growth | May-July | The vine extends visibly day by day and root nodules form | Nitrogen is self-supplied; phosphorus |
| Flowering | June-August | Flowers bloom. Removing the flowers sends more nutrients to the tubers | Phosphorus, potassium |
| Tuber bulking | August-September | The tips of the underground stems thicken as starch and protein accumulate | Peak potassium demand |
| Maturation/harvest | October-November | The vine yellows and dies back; the tubers finish developing underground | Stop irrigation |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;What is worth noting is that even though the first year's growth follows the stages in the table above, it does not produce tubers of harvestable size. In the first year, most of the plant's energy goes into rooting and extending underground stems. Tubers worth eating begin to appear from the second autumn, and yield stabilizes in the third year. This is the substance behind the D grade for speed to harvest. Put the other way around, from the third year onward, autumn comes and brings a harvest every year without any labor spent on planting.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;A protein share of calories of 13.9% means that eating apios alone, to the point of fullness, is enough to meet protein requirements. Compared with the human minimum of 10%, the potato's 12.0%, and sweet potato and cassava, which are both in the single digits, this is the highest among tuber crops (tubers/storage roots) — a privilege that comes from being the root of a legume. When meat and beans have run out, being able to supply both calories and protein from a single crop is an advantage that is hard to replace with anything else.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;That said, micronutrients score D. Even eating enough to cover your calorie needs, only seven nutrients exceed 10% of the recommended intake. It is weak in vitamin A and the folate group. If apios is made a staple food, it must be paired with crops rich in carotene, such as leafy vegetables or squash. This is less a flaw specific to apios than a limitation of tuber crops in general.
