## 12. Harvest

### 12-1. Judging the Time

| Item | Timing | Criteria |
|---|---|---|
| First harvest | 2-3 years after planting | Do not dig in the first year; it is a year for growing roots |
| Main harvest | Mid-November to March of the following year | After the vine has yellowed and died back |
| Harvest for seed tubers | March of the following year | Tubers dug in March are in better condition as seed stock than those dug in autumn |
| Flower harvest | Early-to-mid August | The fragrance is strongest just before the flowers fully open |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;A major advantage of apios is how wide its harvest window is. You can dig it at any time over more than four months, from November to March of the following year. It does not get worse in the ground — on the contrary, as it passes through winter, some of its starch turns to sugar and the flavor improves. There is none of the pressure, as with potatoes, to rush the digging and rush the storing within a fixed window. You dig only as much as you need and leave the rest as it is.

### 12-2. Harvesting Technique

| Step | Details |
|---|---|
| 1 | Clear the withered vine off the stakes and cut it at the base |
| 2 | Start digging from a point at least 30cm outside the plant and work inward |
| 3 | Find the underground stem and follow it by hand, lifting it out. The tubers strung along it like beads come out together |
| 4 | Do not stab down with the spade. A wounded tuber cannot be stored |
| 5 | Brush off the soil lightly. Do not wash with water |
| 6 | Dig only what you need and cover the rest of the area back over with soil, leaving it as it is |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The reputation of apios as troublesome to harvest is accurate. The tubers are scattered along the underground stems, so it is not as simple as turning up the ground under one plant the way you would with a potato. You have to dig while following a single stem, and because the tubers vary so much in size, the small ones are hard to tell apart from the soil. This work is also one reason apios never became a commercial crop. But when labor is the only resource available in a disaster, the calculation changes: it is a trade-off where you need no tilling, no seed to buy, and no fertilizer to apply, in exchange for spending more time digging.

### 12-3. Post-Harvest Handling

| Category | Handling |
|---|---|
| Large (hen's-egg size or bigger) | Eat right away or put into short-term storage |
| Medium | Process into dried pieces or flour |
| Small (about 5g) | Set aside separately as seed tubers |
| Damaged | Eat these first. Do not store them |
| Soil | Just brush it off. Washing sharply shortens the storage period |

> **Do not wash them. Apios has a thin cork layer, so if left wet it softens within days. Wash it only right before eating.**

## 13. Seed Tuber Maintenance and Seed Saving

### 13-1. The Seed Tuber Is the Seed

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Apios's seed longevity grade is E, meaning the method of keeping dry seed in a jar for several years does not work here. But for apios specifically, this weakness is not a practical problem, because the tubers planted in the ground are themselves a living seed store, and that store keeps growing on its own every year. Unlike the potato, which shares the E grade for seed longevity but also carries the risk that losing the seed potatoes wipes out next year's crop entirely, apios does not lose its seed tubers unless the whole field is dug up.

| Item | Standard |
|---|---|
| Seed tuber size | About 5g. Eat the ones that are too large; use the small ones as seed tubers |
| Collection time | March of the following year. Freshly dug from the ground is best |
| Selection | Firm, without wrinkles, with clearly raised eyes |
| Storage period | Plant within 2 weeks of digging if possible |
| Degeneration | Although propagated vegetatively, there are no major diseases, so generational decline is rarely observed |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;With potatoes, saving your own seed for 3, 4 years running lets aphid-borne viruses accumulate, and yield drops noticeably. There are almost no such reports for apios. This is why its seed saving grade is S, a trait whose value grows the longer a disaster drags on. There are only a few crops that can carry on their own generations without the state supplying disease-free seed.

### 13-2. When You Need to Store Seed Tubers

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The rule is to leave tubers in the ground and dig them just before planting, but there are cases — such as relocating a plot or sharing tubers with others — where storage becomes necessary.

| Method | Conditions | Duration |
|---|---|---|
| Burial in a pot | Bury 5-10cm deep in a pot of soil and keep it somewhere that does not freeze. Water occasionally so the soil does not dry out | Until spring |
| Burial in sand | Layer moist sand and tubers in a box. 0-4°C | 2-3 months |
| Damp newspaper | Wrap in newspaper, sprinkle lightly with water, and keep in a cool place | 2-4 weeks |
| Plain room temperature | Not recommended | Starts shriveling after 1-2 weeks |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There is one common principle: apios tubers die if they dry out — the exact opposite of the potato, which likes dry conditions. Because the cork layer is thin, moisture simply escapes, so the goal of storage is not to prevent rot but to prevent drying. Humidity needs to be kept above 90%, which is why moist sand and soil make the best storage materials.

### 13-3. Propagating from True Seed

| Item | Method |
|---|---|
| Seed-collection plant | The healthiest plant with the thickest tubers |
| Judging maturity | Wait until the pods turn brown and dry |
| Separation | Thresh the pods to collect the seed |
| Drying | Dry completely in the shade |
| Pre-sowing treatment | Soak in water for a day, or nick the seed coat |
| Traits | Offspring do not come out with the same traits as the parent plant |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Propagating from seed is not a practical method. Some years the plant sets pods and some years it does not, germination rates are not high, and tubers take even longer to appear when grown from seed. Even so, it is worth trying. If you propagate vegetatively only, the whole field ends up as a single genetic individual, and if a fatal disease ever appears in that individual, everything collapses at once. Seed propagation reduces that risk, and at the same time creates a chance for individuals to emerge that are better suited to the local environment. If a disaster drags on for several years, growing a few seed-grown seedlings separately in a corner of the field becomes a form of insurance.

## 14. Toxicity and Precautions

### 14-1. Always Cook Before Eating

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Apios must not be eaten raw. This conclusion is certain. However, a commonly mistaken explanation for the reason circulates, so it is worth setting straight.

| Point | Details |
|---|---|
| Widespread explanation | Like the potato, it supposedly contains solanine, making it dangerous raw |
| Actual fact | Solanine is an alkaloid found in nightshade-family plants. Apios, a legume, does not contain it |
| Actual cause | Protease inhibitors (trypsin inhibitors) and lectin-family compounds common to legumes |
| Symptoms | Eaten raw, it is not digested and causes stomach pain, nausea, and diarrhea |
| How to remove it | Heating destroys most of it. Cook thoroughly for 30 minutes or more |
| Conclusion | Whatever the cause, the conclusion is the same: do not eat it raw |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;As the name suggests, trypsin inhibitors are proteins that interfere with human digestive enzymes — the same reason raw beans upset the stomach. They are heat-sensitive and lose their activity when thoroughly boiled or steamed. Since you cannot see by eye how much of this compound remains, it is safer to go by time: 30 minutes in boiling water, or 30 minutes or more in a steamer. Undercooked apios still has an acrid, astringent taste, so it can also be told apart to some extent by flavor.

### 14-2. Other Precautions

| Item | Details |
|---|---|
| Saponins | The cause of the bitter taste and foaming. Discarding the first boiling water reduces them. Small amounts are harmless |
| Bean allergy | Being a legume, anyone allergic to peanuts or beans should first test it in a small amount |
| Overeating | With five times the dietary fiber of the potato, eating a lot suddenly causes bloating |
| Propagative vigor | Not a toxicity issue, but it spreads if left unmanaged. A boundary is needed |
| Flowers and seeds | Flowers are dried and used for tea. Seeds are not used as food |

## 15. Storage Without Refrigeration

### 15-1. The Storage Principle Is Different

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Storing apios works on the exact opposite principle from storing potatoes or sweet potatoes. The goal of potato storage is to keep humidity moderately low to prevent rot, while the goal of apios storage is to preserve moisture. Because its cork layer is thin, water keeps evaporating from the surface, so if left at room temperature it turns shriveled after about two weeks. This is why its storability grade is C, and the method that completely neutralizes this weakness is in-ground storage.

| Storage Goal | Potato | Apios |
|---|---|---|
| Biggest enemy | Rot and sprouting | Drying out |
| Target humidity | 85-95% | 90% or above |
| Room-temperature storage limit | 1-2 months | Under 1 month, effectively 2-3 weeks |
| Best method | Root-cellar pit storage | Leaving it undug in the ground |

### 15-2. In-Ground Storage - The Best Method

| Item | Details |
|---|---|
| Principle | Leave it unharvested, still in the ground. The soil maintains both temperature and humidity |
| Method | After the first frost, clear away the vine and cover with 20-30cm of straw or fallen leaves |
| Duration | All winter long; effectively possible for several years |
| Temperature | Soil temperature rarely drops below freezing. Tubers survive even with outdoor air at -20°C |
| Advantages | Requires no electricity, no facilities, and no labor. Gives the highest freshness |
| Disadvantages | Hard to dig if the ground freezes deep. Becomes a target for voles and wild boar |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In in-ground storage, the role of straw mulch is not to keep things warm but to prevent the ground surface from freezing. The tubers themselves survive even if frozen, but if the ground freezes as hard as stone, a spade cannot get in and you cannot dig them out. A layer of straw or fallen leaves 20 to 30cm thick keeps the soil beneath it soft even in the dead of winter. For the mulch material, you can simply use the apios vine cleared away in autumn.

### 15-3. Power-Free Storage After Digging

| Method | How To | Temperature | Duration | Caution |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sand-layer storage | Layer moist sand and tubers in a box | 0-4°C | 2-3 months | Sprinkle lightly with water if the sand dries out |
| Root-cellar pit storage | Dig 1.5m or deeper into well-drained ground, with a roof and a vent | 0-4°C | 3-4 months | Maintain 85-95% humidity |
| Pit-clamp storage | Dig 30cm and mound up straw, tubers, straw, soil in that order, like a burial mound | 0-5°C | 2-3 months | Make a vent at the top with a bundle of straw |
| Damp newspaper wrap | Wrap in newspaper, sprinkle lightly with water, and keep in a cool place | Cool indoor spot | 2-4 weeks | Use only for small amounts |
| Low-temperature cellar | 7-15°C, bury in sand | 7-15°C | 1-2 months | Duration is shorter because the temperature is higher |

### 15-4. How to Make a Pit Store

| Step | Details |
|---|---|
| 1 | Choose a spot where water does not pool. A north-facing slope is good |
| 2 | Dig shallow: 1-1.5m across, 30cm deep |
| 3 | Lay 10cm of dry straw on the bottom |
| 4 | Pile the brushed-off tubers into a mound shape, no more than 50cm high |
| 5 | Fill the gaps with moist sand or leaf mold to prevent drying |
| 6 | Cover with 20-30cm of straw, then 15cm of soil on top of that |
| 7 | Stand a bundle of straw at the top to make a vent |
| 8 | Dig a drainage ditch around the perimeter |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The difference from pit-clamp storage for potatoes is step 5 — filling the gaps with a moisture-holding material. Dry straw alone is enough for potatoes, but doing the same with apios lets moisture escape from inside and the tubers shrivel. Use whichever of moist sand, damp sawdust, or leaf mold you have. However, if it is wet enough for water to run out, mold will develop, so the standard is that it should clump when squeezed in your hand but not release water.

### 15-5. Very-Long-Term Storage Through Processing

| Method | Procedure | Duration | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dried chips | Slice 3-5mm thick, blanch in boiling water for 2-3 minutes, then dry completely | 6 months-1 year | Without blanching, they brown and the inhibitory compounds remain |
| Flour | Grind fully dried chips with a mortar or millstone | 1-2 years | Store in an airtight container together with ash or charcoal |
| Starch | Grate and mix into water, then rinse the settled sediment several times and dry it | Several years | Uses up scraps and damaged tubers completely, with nothing wasted |
| Lactic fermentation | Soak in 2-3% brine at room temperature for 1-2 weeks | Several months | Boil first, then ferment. Do not ferment it raw |

> **The basic storage strategy is to spread the risk: leave most of the crop in the ground to use as winter food, and dig up a portion to turn into dried pieces and flour as a hedge against animals and extreme cold.**

## 16. Cooking and Processing

### 16-1. Fuel-Saving Cooking Methods

| Method | Fuel Use | Procedure | Notes |
|---|:---:|---|---|
| Steaming | Low | Add a little water and steam for 30 minutes | Less saponin leaches out, so the flavor is richer with less nutrient loss |
| Boiling | Medium | Submerge fully in water and boil for 30 minutes. Discarding the first water reduces bitterness | The most reliable way to remove the inhibitory compounds |
| Residual-heat cooking | Very low | Boil for 15 minutes, turn off the heat, wrap in a thick cloth, and leave for 1 hour or more | Cuts fuel use to less than half |
| Ash roasting | Very low | Bury in the hot ash left in the hearth for 30-40 minutes | Uses the residual heat left over from other cooking |
| Flour cooking | Low | Stirred into boiling water, the flour cooks immediately | The most fuel-efficient method |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Unlike the potato, there is a limit to how much you can cut the cooking time for apios, because thoroughly removing the protease inhibitors requires cooking all the way to the center. If you boil it for 10 minutes and take it out, the outside may be done while the inhibitory compounds remain inside. So the way to save fuel is not to shorten the time but to trap the heat instead. Boil for just 15 minutes, then wrap the whole pot thickly in straw or a blanket and let it sit for an hour or more — the internal temperature stays above 80°C and cooking continues. A single wooden box packed with straw is the entire technique.

### 16-2. Taste and How to Eat It

| Item | Details |
|---|---|
| Taste | A flavor like a cross between chestnut and potato, with a savory, nutty note stronger than sweetness |
| Bitterness | Caused by saponins. Discarding the first boiling water reduces it |
| Skin | Peels off by hand once cooked. It is fine to eat with the skin on |
| Texture | Fluffy and mealy when boiled, chewy when roasted |
| Pairings | Salt alone is enough. It also goes well with doenjang (Korean soybean paste) |
| Flowers | Dried and used for tea. Strongly fragrant |

### 16-3. Processing That Doubles as Storage

| Processing | Procedure | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Apios flour | Grind boiled, dried chips | Mixed with grain flour for use in dough and porridge |
| Starch | Grate, mix into water, collect the sediment, and dry it | Reduces bulk and keeps out insects |
| Simmered | Simmer whole in soy sauce or brine | Allows a few days of storage at room temperature |
| Fermented pickle | Soak boiled tubers in brine for lactic fermentation | Eases digestion and becomes a side dish |
| Apios tea | Slice thinly, dry, then roast | Brewed together with the flowers |

## 17. Annual Work Calendar

| Month | Task | Key Points |
|---|---|---|
| January | Dig from the ground and eat only as much as needed; check stored stock | If the ground freezes, clear the straw and dig |
| February | Plan the field, turn the compost, prepare staking materials | Decide where to open a new plot |
| March | Collect and select seed tubers, till the soil, apply base fertilizer, and make beds | Seed tubers freshly dug in March are better than autumn ones |
| April | Plant seed tubers (early-to-mid month), mulch | The pointed end goes down |
| May | Check for emergence, set up stakes and netting, weed | Once the vine grows about 30cm, hook it onto the netting |
| June | Train the vine, check root nodules, flowering begins | Gently dig the fine roots to check for nodules |
| July | Finish weeding, irrigate if there is drought | From this point on, the vine suppresses weeds on its own |
| August | Harvest flowers, concentrate irrigation for tuber bulking, prepare for typhoons | The single most important month for determining yield |
| September | Continue irrigation, prepare for typhoons, clear underground stems that crossed the boundary | September typhoons knock the stakes down |
| October | Stop irrigation, watch for the vine turning yellow | No need to rush |
| November | Clear the vine, first harvest, straw mulch 20-30cm | Dig only as much as needed |
| December | Harvest what is needed, process into dried pieces and flour | Move a portion indoors as a hedge against animal damage |

### 17-1. Five Things to Remember

| Item | Details |
|---|---|
| The first harvest comes two or three years later | It gives nothing in the year a disaster strikes. This is a crop you plant ahead of time, in ordinary days |
| Planting orientation | The pointed end goes down. Planted upside down, it dies underground |
| Always cook it | Eaten raw it is not digested and upsets the stomach. Heat for 30 minutes or more |
| Dies if it dries out | Do not wash it, do not let it dry out — keep it in the ground or in moist sand |
| Set a boundary | Once planted, it is hard to remove. Plant it only where you can afford to give up the space for a long time |
