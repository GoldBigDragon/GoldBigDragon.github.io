## 7. Water Management

### 7-1. Guidelines by Growth Stage

| Stage | Water Management | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Planting-emergence | Enough to keep the soil from drying out | The tuber needs to absorb moisture for the eyes to activate |
| Vegetative growth (May-July) | Natural rainfall is enough; supplement if dry | Dryness at this stage is recoverable |
| Tuber bulking (August-September) | Water thoroughly | The single decisive period that determines yield |
| Maturation/harvest (from October) | Stop irrigation | The skin firms up and storability improves |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Apios's total evapotranspiration for the whole season is about 400mm, less than the potato's 500-700mm. South Korea's annual rainfall is around 1,200mm, more than half of it concentrated in summer, so in years when tuber bulking overlaps with the monsoon season (jangma, late June to July in South Korea), no irrigation is needed at all. The problem comes in years with a dry monsoon: if there is no rain in August and September, the tubers cannot bulk up, and that year's yield never recovers.

### 7-2. Symptoms of Water Deficit and Excess

| Type | Symptom | Progression | Response |
|---|---|---|---|
| Deficit | Leaves droop during the day and recover in the evening | Early sign | Still fine. Thicken the mulch |
| Deficit | Lower leaves yellow and wither first | Intermediate | Water between the rows and reinforce the mulch |
| Deficit | Vine tips stop growing and leaves become smaller | Severe | Tuber bulking stops; yield loss is locked in |
| Excess | Lower leaves turn yellow and drop | Early | Dig a drainage ditch |
| Excess | Area near the roots softens and smells | Severe | Dig up and remove the affected plant |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;That said, apios's tolerance for excess moisture is among the highest of all tuber crops (tubers/storage roots). Having originally lived along riverbanks and the edges of wetlands, it does not collapse right away even after several days of standing water. Under conditions that would already have wiped out a potato crop, apios holds on with just some yellowing of the leaves. But tolerating it and thriving in it are different things: if water stands for several weeks, oxygen is cut off and the roots eventually rot.

## 8. Crop Management

### 8-1. Staking - A Task You Cannot Skip

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Apios vines grow up to 3-5m long. Without something to climb, they creep along the ground and tangle together, and when that happens the lower leaves get no light, photosynthesis drops sharply, and moisture builds up and causes disease. Putting up stakes is the single most important task in growing apios.

| Step | Details |
|---|---|
| 1 | Set up stakes along the row at 2m intervals. Bamboo, branches, rebar — anything works |
| 2 | Run cucumber netting or twine between the stakes in a grid. Height 1.8-2m |
| 3 | Once the vine has grown about 30cm, hook it onto the netting by hand. Do this once, and it will keep climbing on its own after that |
| 4 | Drive the base of each stake at least 30cm into the soil, and anchor both ends with diagonal guy lines |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;If cucumber netting is hard to obtain in a disaster, there are alternatives. One is to plant a tall, sturdy-stemmed crop such as maize or sunflower every other row and use it as a living stake — the same principle Native American peoples used when planting maize and beans together. Another is to plant right next to a fence or a wall: there is no need to build a separate stake, and it solves the field boundary at the same time.

### 8-2. Typhoon Preparedness

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Typhoons in August and September are the greatest physical risk in growing apios. By this time the vine has covered the entire net, turning it into a sail that catches the wind head-on. If the stakes go down as a whole, the underground stems beneath them get torn up too, and the harvest drops sharply.

| Timing | Preparation |
|---|---|
| Before a typhoon warning | Mound and firm soil around the base of the stakes |
| Before a typhoon warning | Run diagonal guy lines from both ends of the stake row to stakes driven into the ground and pull them taut |
| August typhoons | Mostly rain damage. Dig drainage ditches first |
| September typhoons | Rain and strong wind come together. Securing the stakes comes first |
| After it passes | Stand fallen vines back up and cover any exposed roots with soil |

### 8-3. Other Management Tasks

| Task | Timing | Details |
|---|---|---|
| Weeding | May-June | Only needed early on; once the vine covers the ground, weeds decrease |
| Mulching | Right after planting | Rice straw or fallen leaves, 5-10cm; handles weed control and moisture retention together |
| Flower removal | July-August | Removing the flowers increases the nutrients that go to the tubers. Dry the picked flowers and use them for tea |
| Boundary control | Once a year | Cut off any underground stems that have crossed outside the plot |
| Vine cleanup | Just before harvest | Clear away the withered vine and use it as next year's mulch material |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Removing the flowers is optional, but it is worth doing in a disaster. Forming flowers and pods consumes a considerable amount of nutrients, and since propagation happens through the tubers anyway, the flowers are a net loss unless your goal is to collect seed. What's more, apios flowers are strongly fragrant, so dried flowers can be used for tea, leaving nothing wasted. Pick them just before they fully open, from early to mid-August, and dry them in the shade.

## 9. Pests and Diseases

### 9-1. What "Virtually No Pests or Diseases" Really Means

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In the evaluation table, apios's pest-control self-reliance is graded SS — meaning no pest control is needed. This does not mean apios never gets any disease at all; it means no fatal disease is known that wipes out an entire field. Apios has nothing like potato late blight or cassava mosaic disease — diseases that, once they take hold, destroy everything with no way to stop them. Minor problems like some leaf damage or aphids do occur, but such losses only shave a few percent off the yield and do not ruin the harvest.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;That said, the pest & disease pressure grade is C, with an estimated susceptibility of 48.9%. This figure was not investigated directly on apios; it is a reliability-L4 estimate borrowed from legumes as a whole. It does not come from any observation that apios actually suffers this much loss — with no data available, the value for the same family was conservatively applied. It is possible that once apios begins to be grown on a large scale, pests and diseases specific to it will emerge as a problem from that point on. One reason a near-wild crop resists disease is simply that it has not yet been widely planted.

### 9-2. Problems That Actually Occur

| Problem | Symptom | Timing | Response Without Chemicals |
|---|---|---|---|
| Aphids | Cluster on new shoots and the undersides of leaves and suck sap | Summer, high-temperature period | Rinse off with water, spray diluted soapy water, wood vinegar, protect ladybugs |
| Mites | Leaves become rough with silvery-white spots | Hot, dry period | Spray water on the undersides of leaves. Raising humidity sharply reduces them |
| Bacterial leaf spot | Black spots on leaves, spreading and withering | Monsoon season (jangma) | Ensure airflow, pick off and burn diseased leaves, keep the vine up off the ground on stakes |
| Powdery mildew | White powder on the leaf surface | Late summer | Spray milk diluted 10× with water, or a diluted baking-soda solution |
| Voles and moles | Eat the tubers underground | Winter-spring | Clear weeds around the field, bury boards around the plot's perimeter |
| Wild boar | Dig up the ground and eat the tubers | Autumn-winter | Fencing, blocking scent. The greatest risk to in-ground storage |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In a disaster, the greatest threat to apios is not fungi or bacteria but animals. Leaving tubers in the ground and digging them up as needed is the biggest advantage of power-free storage, but it also makes them an open food store for wild boar and voles all winter long. In a year when people go hungry, animals go hungry too. It is safer to fence the storage area or to make sure a portion of the harvest is dug separately and moved indoors, spreading out the risk.

### 9-3. Natural Control Remedies

| Material | How to Make It | Target |
|---|---|---|
| Soapy water | Lye made from ash, or natural soap, diluted 500× with water | Aphids, mites |
| Wood vinegar | Collected by cooling the smoke from charcoal-making, diluted 200-500× | Aphids, repellent effect |
| Diluted milk | Dilute milk 10× with water and spray on a clear morning | Powdery mildew |
| Wood ash | Sprinkle directly on dry leaves, or steep in water and spray | Soft-bodied pests in general |
| Ginkgo leaf infusion | Soak ginkgo leaves in water for a week, then strain and use | Repels aphids |

## 10. Nutrient Deficiency and Excess

| Symptom | Cause | How to Check | Natural Remedy |
|---|---|---|---|
| Lower leaves fade to pale green first, with poor growth | Nitrogen deficiency | Dig up a root; no nodules means the symbiosis has failed | A small amount of diluted urine. Re-inoculate by mixing in soil where apios has grown well |
| Leaves go past dark green into a purplish tint | Phosphorus deficiency | Common in cold periods | Add bone meal and wood ash to the base fertilizer |
| Leaf edges scorch brown | Potassium deficiency | Often appears during tuber bulking | Wood ash — the easiest source of potassium |
| Only the veins stay green while the tissue between them yellows | Iron or magnesium deficiency | Common when soil pH is 7 or above | Increase organic matter with leaf mold and compost to lower the pH |
| The vine grows lush but tubers stay small | Nitrogen excess | Leaf color is excessively dark | Cut off nitrogen and reinforce potassium with wood ash |
| No root nodules form | Absence of rhizosphere bacteria | No millet-grain-sized nodules on the fine roots | Mix in soil from a field that grew legumes, or from an existing apios bed |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The problem most often seen with apios is not nitrogen deficiency but nitrogen excess. If you forget it is a legume and fertilize it generously like other tuber crops (tubers/storage roots), the vine will spread past its stakes into the neighboring plot, only for you to be disappointed by small tubers when you actually dig in autumn. If root nodules are forming well, nitrogen is already sufficient. Checking is simple: around July, gently dig up the fine roots of one plant and see whether millet-grain-sized pink nodules are attached. If the inside is red when a nodule is cut open, it means nitrogen fixation is actively taking place.

## 11. Intercropping and Crop Rotation

### 11-1. Companion Planting

| Compatibility | Partner | Reason |
|:---:|---|---|
| Good | Maize, sunflower | Serves as a living stake. In return, apios leaves nitrogen behind |
| Good | Leafy vegetables | Use the nitrogen apios leaves behind right away the following year |
| Good | Under fruit trees | The vine covers the ground, suppressing weeds and supplying nitrogen |
| Neutral | Root vegetables | Planted together, the underground stems get tangled with them, making harvest troublesome |
| Avoid | A field that is tilled often | Being a perennial, it cannot be kept in a spot tilled every year |
| Avoid | Other vining crops | Tangles with squash, cucumber, etc., making harvest difficult for both |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The greatest gift apios gives its neighboring crops is nitrogen. The nitrogen a legume crop fixes in a year ranges from a few kg to several tens of kg per 1,000m², and part of it stays in the soil through roots and fallen leaves. Because apios is a perennial, this buildup accumulates year after year. A spot where apios has been grown for several years grows other crops noticeably well from the very first year they take it over. Considering that there is effectively no way to manufacture nitrogen fertilizer in a disaster, this is worth more than the food production alone.

### 11-2. On Crop Rotation

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The concept of crop rotation barely applies to apios — this is why its continuous-cropping tolerance is SS. Keeping it permanently in the same spot is the basic way to grow it, and moving it is actually a loss. Even so, there is a sequence worth keeping in mind when planning the layout of a field.

| Situation | Sequence |
|---|---|
| When relocating the apios plot | Apios (5-10 years) → leafy vegetables or grain (nitrogen-consuming) → root vegetables → apios again |
| When creating a new apios plot | Choose the spot with the poorest soil fertility. Leave the good land for heavy feeders |
| After clearing out apios | Since nitrogen remains, nitrogen-hungry crops such as napa cabbage or spinach benefit |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There is one thing to keep in mind, though: once apios is planted, it is hard to clear out completely. Even a single fingernail-sized fragment of tuber left in the ground will sprout from that spot the following year. To relocate the plot, you have to keep removing the shoots that keep coming up for several years running. So when first deciding on a spot, you need to think carefully about whether it is fine to leave it there for a long time. Places with little other use — the edge of a field, beside a fence, along a ditch — are well suited.
