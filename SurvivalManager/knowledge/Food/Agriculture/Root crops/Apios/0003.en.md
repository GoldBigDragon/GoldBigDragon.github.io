## 3. Growing Conditions

### 3-1. Limits and Optimal Values

| Item | Limit | Optimum | Outside This Range |
|---|---|---|---|
| Growing temperature | 5-30°C | 25-30°C | Below 5°C growth stops but the plant does not die. Above 30°C only the vine grows, and tuber bulking slows |
| Overwintering temperature | Survives in the ground down to below -20°C | - | Overwinters without any protection across all of South Korea. USDA zones 4-9 |
| Emergence conditions | Average temperature 15°C or above | - | Below that, shoots do not come up and wait underground instead |
| Light | 4-6 hours a day | 8 hours a day or more | Yield drops by half at 40% shade. It tolerates shade but does not grow well in it |
| Soil pH | 4.5-7.0 | 5.0-6.0 | In alkaline soil, iron and manganese uptake is blocked and leaves turn yellow |
| Soil | Sandy loam to loam | Sandy loam rich in organic matter | In heavy clay, tubers become misshapen and are hard to dig |
| Moisture | Tolerates flooding for a fairly long time | Consistently moist | In dry conditions, tubers become small. Extremely tolerant of excess moisture among tuber crops (tubers/storage roots) |
| Total evapotranspiration | - | About 400mm for the whole season | Less than the potato's 500-700mm |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The 25°C temperature range is the number most worth noting in this table. The potato has a range of 10°C, cassava also 10°C, while apios tolerates everything from 5°C to 30°C. This means it is not wiped out entirely in either a cool year or a hot year. In a disaster, with no weather forecasts and extreme weather growing more frequent, a wide range is itself a form of insurance. The whole field rarely comes up empty just because one year turned out strange.

### 3-2. Choosing a Site in a Disaster

| Condition | Assessment |
|---|---|
| Waterside, ditch bank, rice-paddy levee | Best choice. Apios can use land that is unusable for other crops because of excess moisture |
| Damp foot of a hillside | Good. Organic matter washes down and accumulates there, so fertilizer is not needed |
| Field edge, fence line | Good. The fence can double as a stake, and it keeps a clear boundary from other crops |
| Full shade, inside a forest | Avoid. It tolerates it, but yield drops to less than half |
| Calcareous alkaline soil | Avoid. Growth visibly worsens above pH 7 |
| The middle of a field that needs frequent tilling | Avoid. Being a perennial, it needs to keep one spot for a long time |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In a disaster, farmland is always in short supply. So the real value of apios lies not in yielding a lot from good land, but in turning leftover scraps of ground that other crops cannot use into food-producing land — a spot with poor drainage where potatoes and sweet potatoes rot, the half-shade at the foot of a hill, a narrow strip along a ditch. Put apios into places like these, and calories come from area that would otherwise produce nothing at all.

> **Apios is not a crop for claiming good farmland — it is a crop that turns bad land into something you can eat.**

## 4. Varieties

### 4-1. Two Strains

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Apios introduced and trial-grown in South Korea falls broadly into a red-vine strain and a green-vine strain. They are distinguished by the color of the vine, and it is more accurate to call them strains rather than varieties, since no formally registered variety system has been established.

| Strain | Characteristics | Yield (kg/1,000m²) | Disaster Suitability |
|---|---|:---:|:---:|
| Red-vine strain | Early-maturing. Produces a large number of tubers per plant | 500-800 | High |
| Green-vine strain | Late-maturing. Tends to produce fewer but larger tubers | 500-700 | Moderate |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The yield of 500-800kg given here comes from domestic trial plantings in South Korea, a large gap from the 2,000kg used in the evaluation in Section 1. To understand this gap, remember that apios yield can vary several-fold depending on how many years have passed since planting, how densely it was planted, and how completely the underground stems were dug up. The first harvest in year two is small, while a five-year-old planting has its ground packed full of tubers. It is safer for growers to plan around the lower figure and treat any higher yield as a margin of safety.

### 4-2. Selection Criteria by Situation

| Situation | Choice | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Areas with early frost, short summers | Red-vine strain | Early-maturing, so tubers fill out before frost |
| Wanting to eat as soon as possible | Red-vine strain | Matures early with more tubers, giving a better second-year harvest |
| Wanting to grow thick seed tubers to propagate | Green-vine strain | Thick tubers make good seed stock |
| Unable to tell the strains apart | Whichever you can obtain | The two strains do not differ much in cultivation method or yield |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In practice, there is almost no room to choose a strain in a disaster, because prevalence in South Korea is C — it can only be obtained in one or two provinces. So what matters more than choosing a variety is claiming a spot and planting it ahead of time, in ordinary days. Since apios multiplies on its own year after year once planted, if you plant ten tubers now, you will effectively have hundreds of seed tubers a few years later. This is the cheapest disaster preparation you can make with apios.

## 5. Field Preparation and Fertilizer

### 5-1. Preparing the Soil

| Item | Standard | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Tillage | March, depth 20-30cm | Creates room for the tubers to spread. The deeper the tillage, the larger the tubers |
| Bed height | 30cm or more | A crop tolerant of excess moisture, but tubers spoil if water pools |
| Drainage ditch depth | 30cm or more | Drains off flooding during the monsoon season (jangma, late June to July in South Korea) |
| Soil acidity | Maintain pH 5-6 | No need to raise it with lime |
| Boundary control | Section off with boards or a ditch | Prevents underground stems from spreading into the neighboring plot |
| Covering | Mulch with rice straw or fallen leaves | Combines weed control and moisture retention |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Unlike other tuber crops (tubers/storage roots), you do not add lime when preparing a field for apios. Most crops prefer a pH near 6.5, so lime is applied to acidic soil, but apios does best at pH 5-6. When lime is scarce, this is no small advantage. The 120kg of dolomitic lime in the fertilizer table below is the amount for correcting a field that has become strongly acidic; if the pH is already 5 or above, it can be left out.

### 5-2. Standard Fertilizer Rates (per 1,000m², i.e. 300 pyeong)

| Fertilizer | Rate | Timing |
|---|:---:|---|
| Nitrogen (N) | 8kg | Base fertilizer. Applied sparingly since it is a legume |
| Phosphorus (P) | 12kg | All as base fertilizer |
| Potassium (K) | 12kg | All as base fertilizer |
| Compost | 1,000kg | Well-rotted compost, all as base fertilizer |
| Dolomitic lime | 120kg | Only when correcting strongly acidic soil |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;What matters is that nitrogen, at 8kg, is lower than phosphorus and potassium. Giving legume crops a lot of nitrogen makes the root-nodule bacteria lazy. When nitrogen is abundant, the plant has no real reason to keep up the symbiosis, so fewer nodules form and, as a result, its self-reliance declines. On top of that, excess nitrogen only makes the vine lush while the tubers stay small. Giving apios plenty of nitrogen is not a help but a hindrance.

### 5-3. When You Have No Chemical Fertilizer - Disaster Substitutes

| Substitute Resource | Nutrients Supplied | How to Make and Use It | Caution |
|---|---|---|---|
| Wood ash | Potassium, phosphorus, calcium | Ash from broadleaf trees has more potassium. Mix in a handful shallowly per m² | Being alkaline, using too much raises the pH and becomes harmful instead |
| Well-rotted compost | All-round nutrients, organic matter | Pile up fallen leaves, plant matter, and manure and let it age for 6 months or more | Under-composted material damages the roots |
| Bone meal | Phosphorus, calcium | Burn bones, crush them, mix into compost, and let sit for a month or more | Slow-acting, so use only as base fertilizer |
| Diluted urine | Nitrogen, potassium | Dilute 10× with water and apply between the ridges | Use only minimally on apios; keep it off the leaves |
| Leaf/plant mulch | Organic matter, moisture retention | Cover the bed 5-10cm deep | Turns into nutrients gradually as it decomposes |
| Root nodules | Nitrogen | Not a separate resource — this is the plant's own ability | For ground planted for the first time, mix in a handful of soil taken from where apios has been dug up |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;That last row is a trick unique to apios. Root-nodule bacteria have to already be present in the soil for infection to occur. In ground where apios or other legumes have never been planted, nodules do not form easily, and first-year growth can be poor. Bringing a handful of soil from a spot where apios is already growing well and mixing it into the planting hole has the effect of inoculation. This is a method you can use when no commercial inoculant is available, and soil from a field that has grown soybeans or adzuki beans also helps to some degree.

> **The cheapest investment you can make in a legume crop is not fertilizer, but a handful of soil where root-nodule bacteria are alive.**

## 6. Seed Tuber Preparation and Planting

### 6-1. Timing

| Item | Timing | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Seed tuber preparation | March | Tubers dug in March are better than ones dug the previous autumn and kept in storage |
| Planting | Early-to-mid April | After the last frost, when the average temperature is around 15°C |
| Emergence | About 15 days after planting | Can take up to a month if delayed. Do not dig it up impatiently to check |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;For seed tubers, it is better to dig them freshly from the ground in March of the following year than to dig them in autumn and store them. Apios tubers have a thin cork layer, so moisture escapes quickly if they are kept at room temperature, but there is no such loss if they are simply left in the ground. The ground itself serves as the storehouse. This principle is the same as the core idea in Section 15, on storage.

### 6-2. Selecting Seed Tubers

| Item | Standard | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Size | About 5g | Too small and early growth is poor; too large and seed tubers are wasted |
| Condition | Firm, without wrinkles | Soft or shriveled tubers have lost moisture and have lower germination rates |
| Eyes | Clearly raised buds | Tubers with dead eyes will not sprout |
| Disease signs | No black spots or soft patches | Otherwise it rots underground after planting |
| Pre-sprouting | Advantageous to pre-sprout before planting | Gives more even emergence and faster early growth |

### 6-3. Planting Orientation

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This is where most failures in growing apios happen. Unlike the potato, apios tubers have a clear top and bottom, and if planted upside down, the shoot loses its way underground and simply dies.

| Side | How to Identify It |
|---|---|
| Top (where the stem will emerge) | Not pointed but blunt, with several bumpy protrusions clustered together |
| Bottom (where roots will emerge) | Smooth and pointed. This end must face down into the ground |

> **The pointed end goes down. If you cannot tell, plant it on its side — laid on its side it will find its own direction, but planted upside down it will die.**

### 6-4. Planting

| Item | Standard | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Planting depth | 5-7.5cm | Too shallow and it is vulnerable to drying out; too deep and emergence is delayed |
| Seed tuber spacing | 30cm | Gives the underground stems room to spread |
| Row spacing | 60-75cm | Space for setting up stakes to train the vine on |
| Seed tubers per pyeong | About 11 | About 3,300 per 1,000m² |
| After planting | Press lightly to firm the soil, then water | If the tuber and soil are not in close contact, rooting is delayed |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Apios is a crop that needs virtually no seed disinfection, because it propagates by tubers rather than by seed, and it has no major diseases. Even where disinfectant is available, there is little reason to use it. Instead, if a cut surface has formed, dry it in the shade for a day to let it heal before planting, and dusting it with wood ash can reduce rot. Only when propagating from true seed do you soak the seed in water for a day or nick the seed coat to help it germinate.
