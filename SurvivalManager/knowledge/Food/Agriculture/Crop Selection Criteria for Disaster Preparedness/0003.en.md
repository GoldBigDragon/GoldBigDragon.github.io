## 3. Productivity
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This looks at three criteria: how much comes out (calories), how much protein is in it, and which nutrients are missing if you eat only this.

---
### 3-1. Calories per Area (g DM/m²/yr) `[E2]`
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Of the 25 criteria, this is **the one with the largest weight**.

```
Edible dry-matter yield D_e = yield (g fresh/m²/yr) × dry-matter fraction
 = calories per area (kcal/m²/yr) ÷ 3.55  ← back-calculated when the dry-matter fraction is unknown
```

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;**3.55 kcal/g DM** is the median dry-matter-basis calorie density across 711 entries in the Korean Food Composition Table. The 10-90% range of this value is 2.78-4.21, a span of only **1.5×**. The range of fresh-weight kcal/100g, by contrast, is 238×. In other words, **83% of fresh-weight calorie variation is explained by moisture content, not calorie density.**

| Grade | Self-sufficiency multiple | Threshold (g DM/m²/yr) | (t DM/ha) | Meaning |
|:--:|:--:|---:|---:|:---|
| SS | 1.50×↑ | **1,106↑** | 11.1 | 50% surplus after self-sufficiency for one person |
| S | 1.00×↑ | **737↑** | 7.4 | **the line where self-sufficiency for one person is reached** |
| A | 0.70×↑ | **516↑** | 5.2 | covers 70% |
| B | 0.50×↑ | **369↑** | 3.7 | covers half |
| C | 0.30×↑ | **221↑** | 2.2 | supplementary calorie source |
| D | 0.15×↑ | **111↑** | 1.1 | Supplementary |
| E | below that | - | - | does not qualify as a food crop |

> **Yield and calorie density must not be scored as separate criteria.**
> Separating them turns moisture content itself into the score, so napa cabbage ties with the potato and rice scores lower than the potato.

---
#### 3-1-1. Harvest Index (HI) — a Diagnostic Indicator
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This is a check against crops that "take a lot of land to grow one plant but yield little edible material," which can score well once tallied per 100g.

```
HI = dry weight of the edible portion ÷ total above-ground dry weight
Required total above-ground dry matter B_req = D_e ÷ HI
```

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;**HI barely changes even when the growing system changes.** Across 8 treatments of DOK winter wheat, HI ran 0.358-0.413 (coefficient of variation 4.4%), while total above-ground dry matter ran 6.65-13.07 t DM/ha, a 97% swing. This means **the entire gap between no fertilizer and conventional farming comes from total biomass, while the harvest index stays the same.**

| Crop group | HI ceiling |
|:---|---:|
| Storage roots & tubers (potato, sweet potato, Chinese yam, cassava, Jerusalem artichoke, kudzu) | 0.80 |
| Heading & leafy vegetables (napa cabbage, cabbage, lettuce, kale) | 0.55 |
| Cereal grain | 0.50 |
| Legume seed | 0.45 |
| Bulbs & stems/leaves (onion, garlic, garlic chive, Welsh onion) | 0.40 |
| Fruit & fungi | 0.35 |
| Fruit vegetables (tomato, cucumber, squash, eggplant) | 0.30 |
| Wild greens & shoots (only the tender part is harvested) | 0.20 |
| Nuts & oilseeds (walnut, pine nut, almond, sesame) | 0.15 |

---
#### 3-1-2. Light-Budget Verification Gate
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There is a physical ceiling on how much matter a plant can produce in one year. An input value above that ceiling means the data is wrong.

```
Physical ceiling on total annual above-ground dry matter
 = annual horizontal global irradiance 4,734 MJ/m² × realized conversion efficiency 1.0% ÷ dry-matter heat of combustion 17.5 MJ/kg
 = 2,705 g DM/m²/yr (27.1 t DM/ha/yr)

If B_req = D_e ÷ HI_max exceeds this value → reject the input (do not cap it)
```

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;**The reason this is a rejection, not a cap,** is that the ceiling is only a ceiling, not an estimate. Capping the value at the ceiling would assign it the groundless, optimistic value of "the physical maximum possible." A rejected crop is imputed with its crop group's median grade and flagged as unconfirmed.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;**This gate does not apply to fungi**, because they do not photosynthesize. Instead, the criterion looks at **"edible dry matter ÷ substrate dry matter = biological efficiency,"** and because productivity from a brought-in substrate cannot count as land-use efficiency, the grade is capped at A.

---
#### 3-1-3. Minimum-Yield Gate
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;If the light-budget gate screens out physical impossibility at the high end, this gate screens out **unit errors at the low end.**

```
Yield < 50 kg/1,000m² (= 50 g/m²) → reject the input
```

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;A fresh weight of 50 g/m² is the same order of magnitude as the seeding rate for cereals (wheat 15-20 g/m², rice 6-9 g/m²). **If yield is at seeding-rate level, reproduction itself cannot work,** so a value in this range is treated not as a measurement but as a unit error — "a per-log or per-bag value written as if it were per 1,000m²." In practice, all 5 species caught by this gate were mushrooms.

### 3-2. Protein Density — Protein:Calorie Ratio `[E1]`

```
P:E (%) = protein (g) × 4 kcal/g ÷ total calories (kcal) × 100
```

| SS | S | A | B | C | D | E |
|:--:|:--:|:--:|:--:|:--:|:--:|:--:|
| 25%↑ | 18%↑ | 12%↑ | **10%↑** | 8%↑ | 6%↑ | below 6% |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;**P:E is a quantity unaffected by moisture basis.** Since the numerator and denominator come from the same sample, the value is the same whether raw or dried. Using protein content per 100g fresh weight would turn moisture content itself into the score, structurally penalizing crops with high water content.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;**B (10%) is set to the WHO/FAO adult protein requirement (about 10-12% of total energy) — "the line at which eating only this crop still meets the requirement."**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;**Bonus:** an essential amino acid score of 90 or above (potato, buckwheat, soybean, amaranth, hemp, quinoa) raises the grade by 1 level.

| Food | Total protein (share of calories) | Available protein | P:E | Grade |
|:---|---:|---:|---:|:--:|
| Potato | 10.0% | **5.9%** | 10.6% | **A** (bonus) |
| Wheat | 13.4% | **5.9%** | 15.4% | A |
| Rice | 9.0% | 4.9% | 8.2% | C |
| Maize | 11.0% | **4.7%** | 10.3% | B |

> **By total protein, wheat > maize > potato — but correcting for amino acid quality flips the order to potato = wheat > rice > maize.**  
> The limiting amino acid in cereals is lysine, and the potato is rich in exactly that lysine.

---
### 3-3. Micronutrients — Based on Actual Intake `[E1]`

```
Intake = min( weight equal to 2,100 kcal, 2,500 g )
Micronutrient grade = the number of nutrients (out of 21) for which that intake meets 10% or more of the RDA
```

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;**The 2,500 g cap** is the physiological limit on an adult's daily food intake by weight. Without this cap, a crop like napa cabbage — which needs 13kg to supply 2,100 kcal — would gain an unfair advantage.

> The 21 nutrients assessed: vitamins A, C, D, E, K, B6, B12, thiamine, riboflavin, niacin, pantothenic acid, folate, calcium, iron, magnesium, phosphorus, potassium, zinc, copper, manganese, selenium. Sodium is excluded, since it carries no deficiency risk.

|:--:|:--:|:--:|:--:|:--:|:--:|:--:|
| 18↑ | 16↑ | 14↑ | 12↑ | 9↑ | 6↑ | below 6 |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Under this standard, the correlation between protein and micronutrients is **-0.056** — the two criteria measure different things. Measured per 100g of edible portion, the potato, at 82% moisture, would be structurally penalized, erasing the fact that **the potato is a source of vitamin C that cereals cannot replace.**
