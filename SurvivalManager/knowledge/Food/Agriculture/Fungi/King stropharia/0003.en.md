## 3. Substrate Materials and Preparation

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The wine cap mushroom's substrate is different in character from the other five species. It is not a substrate bagged and sterilized, but a layer of organic matter spread out on the ground. This difference is what produces the SS grade for infrastructure needs. No jars, no bags, no sterilizing pot are needed — only a shovel, a water container, and materials.

> **Other mushrooms need their substrate made and then sterilized; this one only needs its substrate laid on the ground. In a disaster, this difference is what separates success from failure.**

### 3-1. Materials You Can Use

| Material | Characteristics | Suitability |
|---|---|:---:|
| Hardwood chips | Most recommended. Keeps producing for years | Very high |
| Rice straw, wheat straw | Works alone and fruits quickly. The bed's lifespan is shorter, though | High |
| Corn stalks | Nutrient-rich, giving high yields | High |
| Rice hulls | Good aeration; mixed in with other materials | Medium |
| Fallen leaves | Easy to obtain. Better mixed with wood chips than used alone | Medium |
| Sawdust | Blocks airflow if too fine. Mixed with chips | Medium |
| Spent substrate | Leftover substrate from growing shiitake or wood ear, used as-is | Medium |
| Coffee grounds, bean pods | Mixed in small amounts as a nitrogen source | Supplementary |
| Bamboo scraps | Usable if broken into small pieces | Supplementary |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Having such a wide range of usable materials is a decisive advantage in a disaster. Even without oak, even without sawdust, this mushroom will grow on straw alone. Still, since yield can differ by up to 3.9× depending on the substrate type, it is best, where possible, to use wood chips as the main material and mix in straw as a supplement.

### 3-2. Choosing Wood Chips

| Item | Criterion | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Best species | Maple, willow, poplar, elm | Soft and quick to break down, so mycelium spreads well |
| Usable species | Oak, alder, cherry | Hard, so fruiting comes later, but the bed lasts longer |
| Prohibited species | Pine, Korean pine, and other conifers | Resin and terpenes inhibit the mycelium |
| Prohibited species | Walnut, Manchurian walnut | Allelopathic compounds kill the mycelium |
| Chip size | Fingernail to thumb-sized | Too fine blocks air; too large and mycelium cannot bridge the gaps |
| Freshness | Within 1 year of felling | Chips piled up for a long time already have other fungi established in them |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The rule to choose fresh chips is easy to confuse because it is the exact opposite of what applies to other mushrooms. Shiitake sawdust gets better the longer it ages, but the wine cap mushroom loses the turf war in old chips where other fungi are already living. Avoid piles of chips left outside that have turned white with mold. Freshly chipped material is best; if that is not available, wash the chips in water to reduce surface contaminants before use.

### 3-3. Treating the Substrate Without Electricity

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The wine cap mushroom grows without being sterilized. But not sterilizing does not mean doing no treatment at all. Reducing the number of contaminants noticeably raises the success rate, and it is safer to apply some treatment, especially when establishing your first bed. Choose whichever of the four methods below fits your circumstances.

#### 3-3-1. Water Soaking — the Simplest Treatment

| Item | Method |
|---|---|
| Method | Fully submerge wood chips or straw in a water container or pit for 24-48 hours |
| Principle | Cutting off oxygen weakens aerobic contaminants, and the material soaks up plenty of water |
| Draining | Lift out and drain for 6-12 hours, until squeezing it no longer drips water |
| Moisture content | 65-80%, optimum 75% |
| Fuel | None required |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This is the most widely used treatment for building a wine cap mushroom bed. It uses no fire at all, needs only water, and has the useful side effect of soaking the material thoroughly. If you simply scatter spawn on dry wood chips, the mycelium has no water and cannot spread.

#### 3-3-2. Lime Soaking — Reducing Contaminants Without Fuel

| Item | Method |
|---|---|
| Mix | 1-1.5kg hydrated lime, or 0.5-1kg quicklime, per 100L of water |
| pH | 12-13. A strong alkali that feels slippery on the skin |
| Soaking | Submerge cut straw or wood chips completely for 12-24 hours |
| Draining | Lift out and drain for 6-12 hours |
| Inoculation | Do not rinse. The residual alkali keeps suppressing contaminants |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;A strong alkali destroys the cell membranes of mold spores, while mushroom mycelium withstands it relatively well. What's more, the wine cap mushroom is not very particular about acidity, so it grows well even in a substrate with residual alkali. This method uses no fuel at all and is more reliable than water soaking. If lime is unavailable, wood-ash lye can substitute — stir wood ash into water, let it sit a day, and draw off only the top layer — though its pH is lower and varies widely, so results are inconsistent.

#### 3-3-3. Straw Composting — When Straw Is the Main Material

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Wet the straw and pile it 1-1.5m high; thermophilic microbes generate their own heat, and the core of the pile reaches 60-70°C. Turn it every 2-3 days and leave it for 1-2 weeks, and the easy sugars that contaminants could feed on are used up in advance, turning it into a selective substrate. This works not by sterilizing but by removing the food source first.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;For the wine cap mushroom, do not compost for too long. Button mushroom requires 2-3 weeks of full composting, but for this mushroom it is better to keep it short, at 1-2 weeks. Over-composting also reduces the nutrients the mycelium itself would feed on, lowering yield. Once the temperature inside the pile has passed 60°C and dropped back into the 40s, spread it out to cool.

#### 3-3-4. Boiling-Water Soak or Drum Steaming

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;If you have fuel to spare and want to reduce the chance of failure, boil the straw for 1 hour, or pour 20-30cm of water into the bottom of a 200L drum, stack the material on a rack above it, and steam it for 2-4 hours. It can take less time than for other mushrooms, because sterilization is not essential to begin with, so complete sterility is not the goal. Use this method for indoor bed cultivation, or whenever the first bed absolutely must succeed.

### 3-4. Comparing Treatment Methods

| Method | Fuel | Time Required | Contaminant Suppression | Recommended Situation |
|---|:---:|:---:|:---:|---|
| Water soaking | None | 24-48 hours | Low | Outdoor wood-chip beds. The most common method |
| Lime soaking | None | 12-24 hours | Medium | Straw as the main material, summer bed-building |
| Straw composting | None | 1-2 weeks | Medium | When straw is plentiful and there is time |
| Boiling water, steam | High | 1-4 hours | High | Indoor beds, securing the first bed |

### 3-5. Choosing a Site for the Bed

| Item | Good Conditions | Conditions to Avoid |
|---|---|---|
| Light | Partial shade. 100-500lux | A spot in direct sun all day |
| Source of shade | Shade cast by vegetables, under trees, north side of a wall | Total darkness |
| Drainage | Flat ground or a gentle slope where water does not pool | A low spot where water collects after rain |
| Cultivation | A permanent bed that is not tilled, between orchard trees, alongside a path | The middle of a field that is tilled every year |
| Accessibility | A spot you pass by every day | A remote spot where harvest time is easy to miss |
| Water | A spot you can water at the same time as your vegetables | A spot that needs water hauled there separately |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The edge of a vegetable garden or the space between rows is the best spot. The shade a crop casts substitutes for shade cloth, the bed gets wet along with the vegetables when you water them, and passing by every day means you will not miss the harvest window. And as Section 11 shows, this mushroom actually helps the crops growing beside it.

## 4. Obtaining Spawn

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Mushrooms have no seeds. If the seed shop closes, there is nowhere to buy them, and saving seed in a jar the way you would with a plant does not work either. But fungi have three paths that plants do not: saving spores, taking a piece of living mushroom flesh and growing it on, and moving an already established mycelium wholesale. The wine cap mushroom earned its SS for seed saving especially because the third path is so easy for it.

### 4-1. Dividing the Bed — the Easiest and Most Reliable Method

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;While propagating spawn for other mushrooms requires aseptic technique or splitting a mushroom apart, for this one a single shovel is all it takes. Dig up part of a bed where the mycelium has spread well and bury it among the wood chips at a new site — that piece is now your spawn.

| Step | Method |
|---|---|
| Timing | Spring or autumn. Avoid midsummer and midwinter |
| What to take | A section with thick white mycelial strands and no sign of contaminants |
| Digging it up | Cut out a roughly 20cm square with a shovel. The mycelium binds it so it comes out like a clod of earth |
| Dividing | Break it into fist-sized pieces. Breaking it too fine damages the mycelium |
| Transferring | Bury the pieces 30-40cm apart within the wood-chip layers of the new bed |
| Watering | Soak thoroughly |
| Yield rate | One bed can produce 3-5 new beds |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Fill the spot you dug out with fresh wood chips, and the original bed soon recovers. Repeat this every spring and autumn, and the number of beds grows exponentially. No sterilization, no aseptic technique, and no spawn shop are needed. This is the most practical strength this mushroom has in a disaster.

> **Once the wine cap mushroom is established, spawn from then on can simply be dug up with a shovel and used. No other mushroom offers a simpler way to secure seed stock.**

### 4-2. Collecting Spores and Making a Spore Print

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Just because dividing beds is easy does not mean you should skip preserving spores. A bed can be lost all at once to flood, drought, frost damage, or a contaminant invasion. Spores are the insurance for that moment.

| Step | Method |
|---|---|
| Choosing a specimen | A large individual whose cap has fully opened and whose gills have turned purplish black |
| Cutting the stem | Cut the stem off close, right where it meets the cap |
| Setting it down | Place it gills-down on aluminum foil |
| Covering | Cover with a bowl to block drafts |
| Leaving it | 24 hours in a cool spot |
| Recovering it | Lift the cap away and purplish-black spore powder remains in the exact shape of the cap |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This mushroom's spores are purplish black. That is a decisive difference from shiitake or enoki, which produce white spores, and as Section 12 shows, it is also the most reliable marker for telling it apart from Amanita mushrooms. Making a spore print is both seed preservation and a safety check at the same time.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Aluminum foil is better than paper. Paper absorbs moisture, and contaminants hide between its fibers. Foil does not absorb moisture, and its smooth surface preserves the spores intact. If foil is not available, a glass plate is the next best option.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;After collecting the spores, leave the foil, spores and all, in a shaded, airy spot for one more day to drive off even the last trace of moisture. Then fold the foil in half to protect the spore side on the inside and place it in a zip bag. Add well-toasted salt or a piece of charcoal to the bag to absorb moisture. Gather the zip bags into a glass jar, seal it with beeswax, and keep it in an earthen cellar or basement where the temperature stays steady across all four seasons. It must stay out of light, since ultraviolet rays destroy the spores' DNA.

| Storage Form | Lifespan | Notes |
|---|:---:|---|
| Spore print (dried on foil) | 5-10+ years | With moisture fully excluded, cellular activity stops |
| Liquid culture (suspended in sterile water) | 3-6 months | Without refrigeration it germinates too early or bacteria multiply |
| Ready-inoculated spawn (grain or sawdust) | 6 months-1 year | Being living mycelium, it keeps consuming nutrients |
| An established bed | Years | Essentially unlimited with basic upkeep |

> **In a disaster, spores must not be stored in liquid form. They must be kept as a dried spore print.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The difference in lifespan between spores and plant seeds comes from structure. A tomato seed holds an embryo and stored nutrients, so over time it consumes its own reserves and ages to death — 2-4 years at room temperature. A mushroom spore is just a single cell wrapped in a thick cell wall, with no reserves to consume. Given the right conditions it lasts 5-10 years, and in the scientific literature, cases of reviving spores decades old are not even rare.

| Point of Comparison | Tomato Seed | Mushroom Spore |
|---|---|---|
| Average lifespan (room temperature) | About 2-4 years | About 5-10+ years |
| Vulnerability | Temperature swings, depletion of its own reserves | Mold contamination from moisture |
| Cell structure | A complex embryo with stored nutrients | A single cell with a thick protective wall |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The D for seed longevity on the evaluation table is based on commercial spawn. Switch to spore prints, and that D effectively loses its meaning. In the first year of a disaster, make 20-30 spore prints from your largest, healthiest specimens and store them in an earthen cellar. Even if you lose every bed, you can start over five or ten years later.

### 4-3. Making Spawn from Spores — the Heated Straw Substrate Method

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In theory, scattering spores on a substrate makes the mycelium spread. In practice, though, green mold and bacteria in the air occupy the substrate hundreds of times faster than mushroom spores do. The richer the substrate's nutrients, the more vulnerable it is. Without electricity, use a primitive substrate that relies on nature's own defenses instead of a nutrient-rich artificial one.

| Step | Method |
|---|---|
| Boiling | Boil rice straw or wheat straw thoroughly in water for at least 1 hour |
| Squeezing | Lift it out, squeeze out the water, and let it cool until it can be handled by hand |
| Sowing | Scatter the stored spore powder generously and evenly, without being sparing |
| Covering | Cover with clean cloth to block drafts and airborne spores |
| Incubation | 2-4 weeks in a dark spot at 22-26°C. Success is when white mycelial strands cover the straw |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;If you go sparingly and drop spores in one at a time, only primary mycelium grows before dying out. A mushroom only appears once different mating types meet and become secondary mycelium, so you need to shake in a great many spores at once and let them find their own partners inside the substrate.

### 4-4. Tissue Culture — the Most Reliable Method

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;If you have a fresh, living mushroom in hand, there is almost no reason to use spores. The white flesh where the cap and stem meet, on the inside, is already a mass of secondary mycelium that has completed mating. Take a piece of it and put it in the substrate, and it skips germination and pairing entirely, with mycelium spreading outward right away.

| Step | Method |
|---|---|
| Choosing | A fresh specimen whose cap is just about to open. Do not use one that is old or insect-eaten |
| Disinfecting | Heat the knife over a flame and let it cool, or dip it in boiling water and remove it |
| Splitting | Split the mushroom lengthwise by hand. Cutting with a knife drags surface contaminants into the interior |
| Taking the sample | Pick out a rice-grain-sized piece of the white flesh where the cap and stem meet, on the inside |
| Inoculating | Tuck it between boiled, cooled straw or damp wood chips |
| Incubation | A dark spot at 22-26°C. White mycelium spreads around the piece within 3-5 days |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Splitting matters because the inside of a mushroom is nearly sterile. All kinds of spores sit on its outer skin and gills, but the flesh inside is clean. Splitting it by hand exposes a fresh clean surface, and a piece taken from there becomes pure mycelium even without disinfecting. Flesh from the thick base of the stem works especially well.

### 4-5. Priority Order for Obtaining Spawn

| Priority | Method | Condition |
|:---:|---|---|
| 1 | Dividing a bed | When you already have an established bed |
| 2 | Tissue culture | When you have a fresh mushroom in hand |
| 3 | Straw substrate from a spore print | When you have lost your beds but have stored spores |
| 4 | Commercial spawn | Secure it in advance whenever available |
