### 1-3. What an SS in Sunlight Means

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The sunlight criterion on the evaluation table originally asks how much yield remains under 40% shade. The SS attached to the wine cap mushroom does not mean it won that competition — it means it was never on that axis to begin with. Fungi have no chlorophyll. The carbon this mushroom uses is the cellulose and lignin already locked up in wood and straw, and how bright the sky is does not touch that stockpile at all. A cultivation light level of 100-500lux — about what you get indoors on a cloudy day — is enough, because light serves only as a signal here, not as nourishment.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This difference proves decisive in situations where sunlight is reduced for years on end, as with a major volcanic eruption or nuclear winter. Once aerosols spread through the stratosphere, grains, tuber crops (tubers/storage roots), and vegetables collapse all at once. But straw, fallen leaves, and branches are still there, and mushrooms are the only route for turning them into protein people can eat. The wine cap mushroom is the simplest path along that route.

> **Mushrooms are the only food source whose yield does not fall even in a world without sunlight. The wine cap mushroom is the only species that achieves this without any sterilizing equipment.**

### 1-4. How to Read the Strengths

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Five criteria carry an SS: protein density, infrastructure needs, sunlight, continuous-cropping tolerance, tillage & digging energy, and seed saving. Add to these speed to harvest at A, cold hardiness at S, perenniality at S, taste at S, and non-food value at A.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Infrastructure needs at SS matters most. Every other mushroom must have its substrate sterilized without exception, and sterilization takes fuel or electricity. Sterilized substrate then has to be inoculated under near-aseptic conditions, and a failure means throwing the whole batch out. The wine cap mushroom skips this entire chain. Spread wood scraps on the ground, scatter spawn, cover it again — that is all it takes. It wins the competition against contaminant fungi because its mycelium is thick and vigorous enough to push out Coprinus species and Trichoderma.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Continuous-cropping tolerance at SS is special too. That tolerance usually just means little loss from replanting in the same spot, but this mushroom does not merely avoid loss — it actively improves the land. In a trial with three consecutive years of rotation with rice, soil organic matter rose 27-31.6% and available potassium rose 83.8-421.7%, while the yield of the rice planted afterward climbed higher each year, from 13% in the first year to 19.9% in year three. A combination where growing a mushroom also improves the paddy or field is rare.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The substance behind non-food value at A is practical too. This mushroom's mycelium has star-shaped cells called spiny cells that trap soil nematodes, immobilizing 92.9-95.5% of them within 15 minutes. Even pine wood nematodes get caught at a rate of 80.6-84.3% within two hours. When pesticides run out, this becomes a way to cut damage from root-knot nematodes, directly helping vegetables grown in the same soil.

### 1-5. How to Read the Weaknesses

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There is one E, for storability, and two D's, for prevalence in South Korea and seed longevity.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Storability at E stings the most. At 15°C it loses market quality in 4-6 days, and 95% has rotted by day 12. On top of that, it is hard to just lay it out and air-dry the way other mushrooms are dried. Its thick cap and high water content mean the outside often spoils before the inside has dried during natural drying. That is no reason to give up on drying, though. Slicing it thin, blanching and squeezing out the water, and adding smoke-drying can bring the moisture down to 11-13%, and at that point it keeps for a long time. Section 13 covers the method in detail. Even for this mushroom, the wall of poor storability is ultimately cleared by drying — it just takes more effort than with other mushrooms.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Prevalence in South Korea at D is a real obstacle. Spawn is hard to find, and there are few cultivation cases adapted to South Korea's climate. There are two responses. One is to secure spawn in advance whenever it becomes available; the other is to multiply it yourself by dividing beds. Seed saving at SS covers a large part of this weakness. Once you have one established bed, you can dig it up with a shovel and turn it into several new beds, and this process needs no equipment at all.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Micronutrients at C, meeting 11 nutrients, is lower than other mushrooms. Still, it is worth remembering that its potassium — 3.58g per 100g on a dry-matter basis — is the highest reported value among edible mushrooms. That helps balance out a diet that has been running high in sodium.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;And there is one more weakness that the evaluation table does not record: the risk of misidentification. This mushroom grows out of soil, has the classic mushroom shape with a cap, stem, gills, and ring all present, and its ideal harvest point is the white button stage before the cap opens. These three conditions overlap with the young form of the deadly Amanita mushrooms. Section 12 must be read without exception.

## 2. What Kind of Fungus Is This?

### 2-1. Name and Classification

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The wine cap mushroom belongs to phylum Basidiomycota, class Agaricomycetes, order Agaricales, family Strophariaceae, genus Stropharia. Its Korean name causes confusion: the name literally means "dokcheongbeoseot look-alike," and dokcheongbeoseot (Stropharia aeruginosa) is a separate species with a blue-green, slimy cap for which poisoning cases have been reported. The name was given only because this mushroom resembles that one — the wine cap itself is edible. English speakers call it king stropharia after its crown-shaped ring, or wine cap after the color of its cap.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The species name rugosoannulata means "wrinkled ring." It is worth remembering, since the part this name points to is the key marker for safety verification.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Ecologically, it is a saprotrophic fungus that breaks down fallen leaves and woody debris. Unlike shiitake or wood ear, which grow attached to tree trunks, it grows in the layer of organic matter on the soil surface. In nature it fruits in clusters wherever organic matter has built up — on forest floors, around refuse piles, at the edges of pastures. Because of this habit, cultivation succeeds simply by laying wood chips or straw on the ground.

### 2-2. Appearance

| Part | Characteristics |
|---|---|
| Cap | 4-15cm in diameter; large ones exceed 20cm. Opens from hemispherical to flat |
| Cap color | Reddish brown, purplish brown, wine red. Yellow and mixed forms also occur. Fades and cracks when it dries out |
| Gills | Fully attached to the stem, closely spaced. Start white, pass through grayish purple, and turn purplish black |
| Stem | Club-shaped, thicker at the base. White above, yellowish below |
| Ring | Thick and firm, with deep radial grooves on the upper surface that make it look star-shaped or toothed |
| Volva | Absent |
| Base | White strands of mycelium (rhizomorphs) extend down into the substrate |
| Spores | Purplish black, purplish brown |

> **Gills that turn from white to purplish black, a ring with star-shaped grooves on its upper face, and no volva at the base — these three traits are the wine cap mushroom's identity card.**

### 2-3. Life Cycle — From Spore to Mushroom

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;A mushroom's life cycle differs from a plant's. Unlike a plant, where a single seed becomes a whole tree, a single mushroom spore cannot become a mushroom on its own.

| Stage | What Happens |
|---|---|
| Spore | Released from the gills of a mature cap. A single-nucleus cell carrying only half the genetic material |
| Primary mycelium | The spore germinates and extends as threads. Carrying only half the genes, it cannot form a mushroom and eventually dies out |
| Secondary mycelium | Two primary mycelia of different mating types meet and fuse. Only in this state can a mushroom form |
| Rhizomorph formation | Secondary mycelium forms thick strands and takes over the substrate. This thick mycelium is what pushes out contaminant fungi |
| Primordium formation | Millet-seed-sized primordia form at the boundary between the casing layer and the substrate |
| Fruiting body | The primordium swells like a white egg, then grows as the cap opens |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The wine cap mushroom's mycelium is noticeably thick. Where other mushrooms' mycelium looks like white frost, this one looks like white thread or cord. That thickness is exactly its vigor, and the reason it beats out contaminant fungi even in unsterilized substrate.

> **A single spore will not produce a mushroom. When sowing spores, do not go one spore at a time — shake in plenty of spore powder so they can meet and pair up with each other inside the substrate.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;It is worth noting that protein content runs around 30% on a dry-matter basis. Nearly a third of this mushroom's dried weight is protein — a ratio comparable to soybeans, with a relatively well-balanced amino acid profile besides. When meat and beans have run out, a combination emerges where potatoes or grain supply the calories and this mushroom supplies the protein.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The umami figure is meaningful too. An equivalent umami concentration corresponding to 177-230g mSG per 100g means that broth flavor changes completely even with just a little of this mushroom added. When salt, soy sauce, and doenjang (Korean soybean paste) have grown scarce, this is a major value that does not show up in any calculation. Cooked accounts often describe a mealy texture like potato and a chewy bite like meat, and that is also where the S grade for taste on the evaluation table comes from.
