## Introduction

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The wine cap mushroom (king stropharia, Stropharia rugosoannulata) scores highest in the disaster-preparedness evaluation among the six mushroom species covered in the crop evaluation. Its overall grade is S, 54.4 points. The reason comes down to one thing: this mushroom does not require sterilization.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The point where mushroom farming collapses in a disaster is almost always sterilization. A pressure sterilizer runs on electricity, atmospheric steaming burns firewood for hours on end, and aseptic inoculation demands a clean space and trained hands. Leave out just one of these and green mold takes the substrate. The wine cap mushroom skips this step entirely. Spread wood chips or straw on the ground, scatter spawn over it, cover it again, and you are done. No bags, no jars, no sterilizing pot, no clean room needed. Of the six species, this is the only mushroom with an SS grade for infrastructure needs on the evaluation table.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;It differs in calories as well. While the other mushrooms could not even be calculated for calories per area and were imputed with the median value, the wine cap mushroom shows a measured 1,618kcal/m² from outdoor field cultivation. That is 0.62× the self-sufficiency line — one person's daily calorie need converted to land area. Filling your stomach on mushrooms is no joke here.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;And the fact that it is a fungus is itself decisive. Since it does not photosynthesize, its yield does not fall even if the sky darkens with volcanic ash or soot. An SS grade for sunlight on the evaluation table does not mean it uses light well — it means it stands outside that axis altogether. Under conditions where rice, wheat, and potatoes collapse together, mushrooms are practically the only food source whose output holds up, and this is the one mushroom whose calories are even accounted for.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The weaknesses are just as clear. First, raw storability is E: at 15°C it loses market quality within 4-6 days, and by day 12 most of it has rotted. High moisture and a thick cap also make natural drying difficult. Second, prevalence in South Korea is D: the crop is still at the trial-cultivation stage, spawn is hard to obtain, and reference cases are few. Third, and most importantly, as its name suggests, this mushroom can be confused with deadly poisonous mushrooms. In particular, the young button stage — its ideal harvest point — closely resembles the immature form of the death-dealing Amanita mushrooms. Section 12 of this document is written specifically to teach that distinction.
