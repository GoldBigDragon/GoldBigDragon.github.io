## Introduction
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The king stropharia (Stropharia rugosoannulata) is the highest-scoring of the six mushrooms in the evaluation, with an overall grade of S and 54.4 points (rank 6 of 183 valid species). The reason is that it needs no sterilization. Mushroom farming usually collapses at sterilization (electricity or firewood) and sterile inoculation, but with this mushroom you lay wood chips or straw on the ground, scatter spawn, cover it again, and you are done. Its energy yield measured in the field is 1,618 kcal per ㎡ (0.62 times the self-sufficiency line). It does not photosynthesize, so yield holds even when sunlight falls (sunlight SS). Its weak points are raw storability E (it loses marketability in 4-6 days at 15℃), domestic cultivation spread D, and confusion with deadly poisonous mushrooms. The young button stage, the right time to harvest, resembles the young stage of the deadly amanitas, so read Section 7 and pick only what grew on a bed you made yourself.

## 2. Mushroom Overview
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;It belongs to the genus Stropharia of the family Strophariaceae, order Agaricales, class Agaricomycetes, phylum Basidiomycota, and is a saprotroph that decomposes fallen leaves and woody debris. The species name rugosoannulata means "wrinkled ring".

| Feature | Details |
|:---|:---|
| Cap | 4-15 cm across (large ones exceed 20 cm). Hemispherical, flattening with age. Reddish brown, purple-brown, or wine-colored; fades and cracks when dry |
| Gills | Fully attached to the stem and crowded. White at first, turning gray-purple, then purple-black |
| Stem, ring | Club-shaped and thick at the base; white above, yellow below. The ring is thick and firm, with deep radial grooves on its upper face that look like a star or saw teeth |
| Base, spores | No volva (sac). Bundles of white mycelial cords extend into the substrate. Spores purple-black to purple-brown |

> **The gills turn from white to purple-black, the upper face of the ring has star-shaped grooves, and the base has no volva. These three are the identity card of the king stropharia.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;A single spore does not produce a mushroom. A spore has only one nucleus, so the primary mycelium grows and then dies out; only when two different mycelia meet and mate do they form thick mycelial cords that take over the substrate and push out contaminants. So scatter spore powder generously, not one spore at a time.

| Stage | Period and temperature | Key point |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Spawn run | 30-60 days, 5-35℃ (best 26℃) | White mycelial cords spread through the whole substrate |
| Casing | After the spawn run | 2-4 cm of soil or leaves |
| Primordia, growth | 30-60 days after casing (12-20℃), then 7-14 days (12-25℃) | Grain-sized knobs form under the casing, swell, and the caps open |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Per 100 g: about 25 kcal and 2.40 g of protein (38.3% of calories; 25.75-34.17% on a dry-matter basis). Potassium is 3.58 g/100 g on a dry-matter basis, the highest value reported for an edible mushroom. Eleven nutrients meet 10% of the RDA (phosphorus, iron, zinc, manganese, copper, calcium, magnesium, selenium and others), and the umami is very strong.

## 3. Bed Materials and Treatment
> **Other mushrooms need a substrate to be made and sterilized, but this one only needs its bed laid on the ground. In a disaster this difference decides success or failure.**

| Material | Characteristics |
|:---|:---|
| Hardwood chips | Most recommended. Keeps producing for years |
| Rice straw, wheat straw, corn stalks | High suitability. Straw fruits quickly but the bed is short-lived; corn stalks are rich in nutrients and give high yield |
| Rice hulls, fallen leaves, sawdust, spent substrate | Medium. Rice hulls (aeration), leaves, and sawdust (too fine blocks air) are mixed with wood chips; spent substrate left over from growing shiitake or wood ear is used as it is. Coffee grounds and bean pods are added in small amounts as a nitrogen source |

| Wood chips | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Species | Best: maple, willow, poplar, elm (soft and quick to decompose, so the mycelium spreads well). Possible: oak, alder, cherry (slower to fruit but long-lasting). Forbidden: conifers (resin and terpenes suppress the mycelium), walnut and Manchurian walnut (allelopathic compounds kill the mycelium) |
| Size, freshness | Fingernail to thumb size (too fine blocks air; too large and the mycelium cannot bridge the gaps). Cut within 1 year (chips piled up for long already host other fungi) |

| Treatment | Method | Time, suppression |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Water soak | Soak chips or straw in water for 24-48 hours, lift out, and drain for 6-12 hours (until no water drips when squeezed; moisture content 65-80%, best 75%). Oxygen is cut off and aerobic contaminants weaken | 24-48 hours, low. Most common for outdoor chip beds |
| Lime soak | 1-1.5 kg of slaked lime (or 0.5-1 kg of quicklime) per 100 L of water, pH 12-13. Soak cut straw or chips for 12-24 hours, drain, and do not rinse (the remaining alkali keeps suppressing contaminants). It destroys mold spores but the mushroom mycelium tolerates it fairly well | 12-24 hours, medium. For straw-based beds or summer |
| Straw fermentation | Wet the straw and pile it 1-1.5 m high; thermophilic microbes generate heat and reach 60-70℃. Do not ferment too long | 1-2 weeks, medium. When there is plenty of straw and time |
| Boiling water, steam | Boil straw for 1 hour, or steam it for 2-4 hours in a 200 L drum (20-30 cm of water at the bottom, material on a rack) | 1-4 hours, high. Uses a lot of fuel. Indoor beds, securing the first bed |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Partial shade (100-500lux) suits the bed. The best spot is a well-drained flat or gentle slope in the shade cast by vegetables, under trees, or north of a wall, in a permanent bed that is not tilled, between orchard trees, or beside a path, where you pass every day and can water it along with the vegetables. Avoid places in direct sun all day, complete darkness, low ground where water collects, and the middle of a field that is tilled every year.

## 4. Obtaining Spawn
> **Mushrooms have no seeds, so the spawn must be carried on. Once this mushroom is established, you can dig up spawn with a spade.**

| Method | Details |
|:---|:---|
| Bed division (easiest) | With a spade, cut out a block about 20 cm square from a part where thick white mycelial cords have spread and there are no signs of contamination, and break it into fist-sized pieces (breaking it too fine damages the mycelium). Bury the pieces 30-40 cm apart between the chip layers of a new bed and water thoroughly. One bed makes 3-5 new beds. Fill the hole with new chips and it soon recovers |
| Tissue culture (most reliable) | Split a fresh mushroom lengthwise by hand, not with a knife (a knife drags surface contaminants inside), put pieces of the inner flesh between boiled and cooled straw or wet chips, and incubate in a dark place at 22-26℃. White mycelium spreads in 3-5 days |
| Spore print | Place a large mature specimen, cap fully open and gills stained purple-black, gill side down on aluminum foil (paper holds moisture and contaminants) and cover it; spore powder is left behind. Dry the foil in the shade for one more day and seal it to keep 5-10 years or more (never store in liquid) |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The order of priority is bed division, tissue culture, a straw substrate made from a spore print (when you have lost your beds), and commercial spawn (secure it in advance when you can get it). To make the straw substrate, boil straw for 1 hour or more, let it cool, scatter spore powder generously, and incubate in a dark place at 22-26℃ for 2-4 weeks; it has succeeded when white mycelial cords cover the straw. Seed longevity D is based on commercial spawn (6 months-1 year); an established bed lasts for years.

## 5. Inoculation, Spawn Run, Fruiting, and Harvest
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;A spring bed (after the last frost) is harvested from early summer of that year; an autumn bed (up to 2-3 weeks before the first frost) overwinters and is harvested from the next spring. In temperate zones, work between April and October, and avoid the midsummer heat and the depth of winter, when temperatures above 30℃ or freezing make establishment difficult.

| Building the bed | Details |
|:---|:---|
| Order | Clear weeds and large debris to expose bare ground (the bed must touch bare ground directly); lay 3-5 cm of wet chips or straw; scatter spawn broken up fine and evenly; 3-5 cm of chips; spawn; a last layer of 5-10 cm of chips; 2-3 cm of dry leaves, straw, or compost; water thoroughly |
| Specifications | Total thickness 15-20 cm; spawn 0.5-0.8 kg per ㎡; bed width 1-1.2 m (wider and the middle cannot be reached); 40-60 cm paths between beds. If spawn is short, reduce the area, not the layers |
| Casing | Once the mycelium has spread through the whole substrate (30-60 days after building), cover it with 2-4 cm of a not-too-fine mix of field soil, leaf mold, and sand (or dry leaves and compost) and water thoroughly. A necessary condition for keeping moisture and inducing primordia |

| Spawn-run management | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Temperature, moisture | Mycelium 5-35℃ (best 26℃). Dies above 40℃ or when the substrate dries. Moisture content 65-80% (best 75%). It does not die below freezing in winter; cover with 5-10 cm of straw or leaves |
| Water | Thoroughly right after building, until the inside is wet. During the spawn run, water when the surface dries (1-2 times a week; in summer once every 3-4 days at dusk). In drought, cover thickly with straw or leaves |
| Healthy growth | Thick white threads or cords weave the chips together, with a faint mushroom smell close to the smell of soil. Half or more of the substrate in a month (a problem of moisture or material if it has not spread after two months). A fine, frost-like growth, a sour or ammonia smell, or green, orange, or black means contamination. Gray inky caps appearing briefly early on are fine |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The fruiting signals are casing (the strongest signal), a temperature drop (from 26℃ for the spawn run to 12-20℃ for fruiting, which the season supplies), a rise in moisture (soaking the substrate and raising the air humidity to 85-95%), weak light of 100-500lux, and rain. Mushrooms come up in clusters within a few days after rain, so outdoors there is almost nothing to do. Fruiting bodies grow at 12-25℃ (best 12-20℃; thick and sturdy at 16℃ or below, thin and quick to open at 24℃ or above), and forming them is difficult below 4℃. Air humidity is 85-95% at the start of fruiting and 75-80% in the growth stage, and the caps crack if it gets too dry. They come up most in early summer; in midsummer above 25℃ they decrease or stop, so increase shading and watering; they come up heavily again in autumn with the best quality, so add 5-10 cm of fresh chips.

| Harvest | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Timing | Just before the cap opens fully from the stem (cap 5-10 cm across; the button stage with the ring still attached or just detached). Firmest and tastiest. It is late once the cap flattens and the gills darken to purple-black (texture, taste, and storability all worsen) |
| Method | Hold the base of the stem, twist, and pull out the whole mushroom down to the base |
| Cycle | First harvest about 90 days after building the bed (30-60 days after casing). Each flush lasts 7-14 days, with 12-25 days between flushes and 3-5 flushes per season |
| Bed life, yield | Covering with 5-10 cm of fresh chips in autumn or early spring keeps the bed going for years. Yield is a national average of 6.47 kg per ㎡ in Chinese field cultivation (2.59-5.22 kg depending on the substrate mix), or 1,618 kcal per ㎡ |

## 6. Contamination, Pests, and Problem Diagnosis
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The most common cause of failure in disaster mushroom growing is not pests but losing the substrate to a competing mold (green mold). Green mold has already spread before the blue-green color appears, that is, before spores form, so judge it by speed and texture.

| Item | King stropharia mycelium | Green mold |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Color | White, slightly yellowish | White at first, soon blue-green |
| Texture | Thick, thread-like strands, somewhat coarse | Fine like powder, cottony |
| Speed, smell | 3-8 mm a day. Forest-soil smell, mushroom aroma | 10-30 mm a day (3-5 times faster). Musty mold smell |

| Situation | Response |
|:---|:---|
| Spot smaller than a palm | Dig it out generously, including the surrounding soil, and sprinkle wood ash there to make it alkaline |
| Spread to several places, or all blue-green | Give up that section and keep only the parts the mycelium won, moving them elsewhere. If all of it is blue-green, discard everything (burn it, or bury it deep far from the growing site) |

> **Do not return contaminated substrate to the compost. Green mold spores survive in the compost pile and come back into the substrate the next year.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This mushroom is grown without fully sterilizing the substrate, so the answer to contamination is not sterility but letting the mushroom win the competition. Add plenty of spawn, 5-10% of the substrate weight; raise the pH to 7.5-8.5 with slaked lime or wood ash (green mold likes weak acidity); do not add too much nitrogen; do not pack the substrate too hard; wash your hands with soap and heat knives and spades in a fire. The pests are fungus gnats (they eat the mycelium and carry green mold spores: sticky traps and water-dish traps), slugs (a ring of ash or sawdust, picked off by hand at night), and rats and birds (cover with netting). Outdoors they cannot be stopped completely, but the flushes are large enough that some losses still leave a harvest.

| Symptom | Cause and action |
|:---|:---|
| Mycelium does not spread, or spreads and stops | Substrate too dry, or too wet and short of oxygen, low temperature, exhausted nutrients, or dead spawn. Water to 65-70% moisture, check the soil temperature, and lay new substrate on top |
| Small fruiting bodies with only the stem growing long | Too little light, too much carbon dioxide. Remove the cover to give light and ventilation |
| Stem softens and falls over | Too wet, bacterial soft rot. Stop watering, ventilate, and remove the diseased ones |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The greatest advantage of this mushroom is that it does not need a field of its own. Put it between corn or sorghum plants (the stalks become the next substrate after harvest), under fruit trees, under squash or cucumber vines, or north of a fence or wall, wherever crops cannot grow. Substrate after fruiting is high-quality organic matter whose woody material the mycelium has broken down, so work it into the field in autumn, or mix lumps with living mycelium into new substrate as spawn. On the same spot, green mold and pest density rise from the second or third year, so in the third year remove all the old substrate to the field, move the bed, and let the original spot rest for 1-2 years.

## 7. Mistaking Poisonous Mushrooms and Safety Checks
> **This section bears directly on life. Checking whether it is safe to eat matters more than growing it.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Even on a cultivated bed, wild mushrooms can come up mixed in.

| Part | King stropharia |
|:---|:---|
| Cap, gills | Hemispherical at first, then flattening. 5-15 cm across (large ones 30 cm). Reddish brown to wine-colored, fading when dry. Gills turn from gray-white to purple-brown to purple-black as they grow |
| Stem | Thick and firm. White to pale yellow, 8-15 cm long. Has a ring, the remains of the veil that covered the cap when young, torn and left on the stem |
| Base, flesh | No sac (volva); white mycelial cords like roots are attached. Flesh is white and does not turn blue when cut or bruised |

| Item | King stropharia | Amanitas (deadly) |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Spore color | Purple-brown to purple-black | White |
| Gill color | Dark purple-brown as it grows | White to the end |
| Stem base | No sac | A membranous sac (volva) |
| Cap color | Reddish brown to wine | White, yellow, green, gray, and others |
| Where it grows | Straw or wood by-product substrate | Ground near the roots of living trees |

> **If the spores are white, never eat it (turn the cap upside down on white paper, cover it with a cup, and check after a few hours). Deadly mushrooms such as the destroying angel (Amanita virosa) and the fool's mushroom (Amanita verna) have white spores, gills that stay white to the end, and a sac at the base of the stem. If even one of these three applies, do not eat it.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The sac must be checked by digging. If you look only at the part above ground, the sac is buried in the soil and cannot be seen. Pholiota species have many coarse scales on the cap and come up in clusters, and the species are hard to tell apart. Some species in the same genus Stropharia contain hallucinogenic compounds, so do not eat any whose flesh turns blue when cut. Do not confuse it either with yellow coral mushrooms or Entoloma species, which have strong gastrointestinal toxicity.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The first time you eat it, take a spore print and check the color (discard it if white), dig up the base of the stem to confirm there is no sac, cook it thoroughly (never raw), and have one person eat a small amount (one piece) and wait 24 hours. If nothing goes wrong, increase the amount, but do not let several people eat it at the same time, and keep one specimen aside (to use for identification in case of poisoning). Stomach pain within 30 minutes to 2 hours of eating is usually gastric irritation, but vomiting and diarrhea beginning 6-24 hours later is the typical course of amatoxin poisoning, which destroys the liver. The later it starts, the more serious it is, and even when symptoms briefly subside and the person seems to have recovered, the liver is still being destroyed.

## 8. Storage, Cooking, and Annual Schedule
> **Storability E is a raw-basis grade. Drying uses almost no fuel or equipment, so in a disaster the storability problem of mushrooms is, in practice, solved by drying alone.**

| Storage | Details |
|:---|:---|
| Sun drying | Slice the caps 4-6 mm thick and spread them on a reed or bamboo screen. 2-3 days in fine weather. Easiest, and vitamin D increases |
| Shade and fireside | Wind-dry in the shade under the eaves for 4-7 days (color and aroma are kept well); string whole mushrooms and hang them under the eaves for 5-10 days; on a shelf above the hearth at 40-50℃ for a day (for the rainy season; take care not to burn them) |
| Keeping, others | 1-2 years in a cool, dark place; 2 years or more as powder. Soy-sauce pickles (blanch, then soy sauce and vinegar, 3-6 months), lactic fermentation in brine (3-6 months) |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Slicing before drying is better than drying whole (thick stems do not dry inside and grow mold). When dried mushrooms start to crumble, turn them into powder instead of throwing them away. Mushroom powder is compact, adds umami to soup, and can be mixed into wheat or buckwheat flour dough (sujebi dough soup, kalguksu noodles) to add protein and micronutrients.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Do not eat it raw. Grilling takes 5-10 minutes (the thick stem suits grilling), soup or stew 10-15 minutes (umami in the broth), and boiling 10 minutes (blanch and dress as a seasoned vegetable dish), and the thick, firm stem gives a meat-like texture. Use mushrooms not as a source of calories but as a supplement of protein and micronutrients (amino acids that grains do not supply, vitamin D, selenium, and copper).

| Month | Work |
|:---|:---|
| Jan-May | Gather wood chips and rice straw and check the spawn. In March, soak the straw and pre-ferment it; in April, make the first inoculation (check that the soil temperature is 12℃ or higher, then lay the substrate and add spawn); in May, keep up moisture and check for contamination |
| June-Oct | June: first flush (harvest before the rainy season and dry at once). July: in the hot season protect the mycelium with shade and watering. August: add the second substrate (use some spent substrate as inoculum). September-October: second flush (the largest harvest) and mass drying |
| Nov-Dec | Remove spent substrate to the field and cover the beds with straw and leaves to prepare for winter. Check the stored spawn and spore prints |

> **Never forget: do not eat it if the spores are white; dig up the base of the stem to check there is no sac; judge green mold by speed and texture; store by drying; and return spent substrate to the field.**
