## 5. Inoculation Method

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Inoculating wine cap (king stropharia) is a matter of building up layers. Because substrate and spawn are laid down in alternating layers, it is sometimes called a mushroom lasagna. The principle is simple: spawn is scattered through several layers so that the mycelium spreads upward and downward at the same time, and in this way it claims the substrate ahead of contaminant fungi.

### 5-1. Timing

| Item | Standard | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Spring bed | After the last frost has passed | Harvest begins in early summer of the same year |
| Autumn bed | Up to 2-3 weeks before the first frost | The bed overwinters and harvest begins the following spring |
| Temperate-zone guideline | Between April and October | Leave enough time for the mycelium to grow |
| Times to avoid | Midsummer heat waves, midwinter | Establishment is difficult above 30°C or when the bed freezes |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;An autumn bed is especially advantageous. If it is built before the frost, the mycelium spreads slowly through the winter and has already taken over the substrate by the time temperatures rise the following spring. This is where the S grade for Cold hardiness in the evaluation table, and the record that an autumn-sowing, winter-harvest cropping system works, come from.

### 5-2. Building the Bed

| Step | Details | Thickness |
|---|---|:---:|
| 1 | Level the ground and clear away weeds and large debris to expose bare soil | - |
| 2 | Lay down wet wood chips or straw | 3-5cm |
| 3 | Break the spawn into small pieces and scatter it evenly | - |
| 4 | Lay wood chips again | 3-5cm |
| 5 | Scatter spawn again | - |
| 6 | Lay a final layer of wood chips | 5-10cm |
| 7 | Cover with dry leaves, straw, or compost | 2-3cm |
| 8 | Water thoroughly | - |

| Item | Standard |
|---|---|
| Total thickness | 15-20cm |
| Amount of spawn | 0.5-0.8kg per m² |
| Bed width | 1-1.2m. Anything wider and you cannot tend the middle |
| Bed length | No limit |
| Bed spacing | Leave aisles of 40-60cm |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Total thickness matters. A bed thinner than 15cm dries out quickly and freezes in winter, while one thicker than 25cm lets no air reach its core, so it turns anaerobic before the mycelium has finished spreading. 15-20cm is the compromise.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;It is equally important to lay the bed in direct contact with bare ground. If plastic sheeting or boards go underneath, the mycelium cannot reach the moisture and microorganisms of the soil. Wine cap grows best at the boundary between soil and woody layer, and it draws on the mineral nutrients in the soil.

### 5-3. When Spawn Is Scarce

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;If spawn is short, reduce the area rather than the number of layers. Below 0.5kg per m², establishment slows down, and in the meantime ink caps take the ground first. It is better to build 1.5m² properly with 1kg of spawn than 2m² poorly. And once even a single bed is properly established, it can be lifted the following spring and spread to three times the area.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Spot inoculation is also possible. After spreading a layer of wood chips, fist-sized lumps of spawn are buried in a checkerboard pattern at 30-40cm intervals. Establishment is slower than with scattering, but it is useful when spawn is limited. Lumps of mycelium moved from a divided bed are planted in the same way.

### 5-4. Casing Layer

| Item | Standard |
|---|---|
| Timing | After the mycelium has spread through the whole substrate. 30-60 days after building the bed |
| Material | A mix of garden soil, leaf mold, and sand; or dry leaves and compost |
| Thickness | 2-4cm |
| Moisture | Soak thoroughly after casing |
| Purpose | Retaining moisture and inducing primordia formation |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Casing is a requirement for this mushroom to fruit. The mycelium feeds on the woody layer, and the fruiting bodies form at the boundary between the casing layer and the woody layer. Without casing, it is common for the mycelium to form a white film on the substrate surface and produce no mushrooms. For outdoor beds, a covering of dry fallen leaves and compost is enough; for indoor beds, soil is prepared separately.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Soil for casing should not be too fine. If only fine soil is spread, it sets into a hard crust when watered and young mushrooms cannot break through. Mix in sand or coarse leaf mold to keep it loose and crumbly.

## 6. Mycelium Culture Management

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;After the bed is built, the next 30-60 days are the culture period. The goal in this period is to let the white mycelial cords take over the whole substrate. All a person has to do is keep it from drying out, avoid trampling it, and pull weeds.

### 6-1. Temperature and Moisture

| Item | Range | Notes |
|---|:---:|---|
| Mycelial growth temperature | 5-35°C | An exceptionally wide range |
| Optimal temperature | 26°C | Growth is fastest around this temperature |
| Substrate moisture content | 65-80%, optimum 75% | Enough that water just seeps out between the fingers when squeezed |
| Danger conditions | 40°C or above, dried-out substrate | The mycelium dies |
| Winter | It does not die even below freezing | Cover with 5-10cm of straw or leaves |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;A mycelial growth range of 5-35°C is among the widest of any cultivated mushroom. It means there is almost no need to struggle to hold a particular temperature, and in a situation without electricity, that translates directly into success rate. It is the exact opposite of enoki mushroom, which demands precise cooling.

### 6-2. Water Management

| Period | Management |
|---|---|
| Right after building | Soak thoroughly. Water must reach deep into the substrate |
| During culture | Water when the surface dries. Usually 1-2 times a week |
| Summer | Once every 3-4 days. Water around dusk |
| Drought | Cover thickly with straw or leaves to prevent evaporation |
| If next to a vegetable plot | Simply water it together with the vegetables |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;When watering, do not just wet the surface; water slowly and for a long time so that it soaks into the substrate. Spraying hard makes the water run down between the wood chips, while the layer that actually holds the mycelium stays dry. Water in several passes with a watering can, or lay straw over the bed and water on top of it, and the water soaks in evenly.

### 6-3. How to Tell Whether the Mycelium Is Growing Properly

| What to observe | Normal | Abnormal |
|---|---|---|
| Mycelium appearance | Thick white threads or cords bind the wood chips together | If it looks like fine frost, it may be a different fungus |
| Smell | A faint mushroom scent close to the smell of soil | A sour or ammonia smell is abnormal |
| Wood chips | Lifted by hand, they come up in a clump tangled in mycelium | If they scatter as loose pieces, the mycelium has not yet spread |
| Rate of progress | More than half the substrate in a month | If it has not spread after two months, the problem is moisture or materials |
| Color | Stays white | Green, orange, or black means contamination |
| Surface | A few small gray mushrooms appearing briefly are fine | If ink caps keep appearing, the substrate is not yet mature enough |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;It is common for ink caps to appear in the early stage of a new bed. This is not a failure. Ink caps are pioneer fungi that quickly consume the easy nutrients and then disappear, and once they have passed, wine cap takes hold. Pull them out as you see them, but do not give up on the bed itself. If, however, only ink caps keep appearing after two or three months, the spawn has failed to establish.

## 7. Inducing Fruiting

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Left comfortable, mycelium simply keeps spreading. Producing mushrooms is, for the mycelium, a response to a crisis signal. In the case of wine cap, that signal is a drop in temperature, casing, and moisture. Unlike shiitake, there is no need to soak or strike the substrate.

### 7-1. Stimuli That Suit This Mushroom

| Stimulus | Method | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Casing | Cover with 2-4cm after the mycelium has spread | The strongest signal |
| Temperature drop | From 26°C for culture down to 12-20°C for fruiting | The seasons do this on their own |
| Rising moisture | Soak the substrate thoroughly and raise air humidity to 85-95% | Primordia form |
| Light | Weak light of 100-500lux | Acts as a signal |
| Rainfall | A few days after rain, mushrooms appear in flushes | The best stimulus nature gives |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In outdoor cultivation, a person almost never has to provide these stimuli separately. When summer ends, temperatures fall, and autumn rains come, the mushrooms appear on their own. The same is true in spring. All a person has to do is keep the substrate from drying out. This is the fruiting method that takes the least labor, and it is also the real substance behind the SS grade for Infrastructure needs.

### 7-2. Fruiting Conditions

| Item | Condition | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Fruiting body growth temperature | 12-25°C | Range of 13°C |
| Optimal fruiting temperature | 12-20°C | At 16°C or below, mushrooms are thick and sturdy; at 24°C or above, they are slender and open up quickly |
| Lower limit | Below 4°C | Fruiting body formation becomes difficult |
| Air humidity | 85-95% at the start of fruiting, 75-80% during growth | Below 85%, the caps crack |
| Substrate moisture content | 65-80% | |
| Light intensity | 100-500lux | Partial shade is enough |
| Ventilation | Not a problem outdoors | Indoors, carbon dioxide must be removed |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The shape of the mushrooms changes with the fruiting temperature. Those that appear in cool weather have thick caps and stout stems, so they weigh more and keep better. Those that appear in hot weather grow fast and open up fast, so they are light and spoil easily. This is why spring and autumn harvests are better than summer harvests.

### 7-3. Fruiting by Season

| Season | Fruiting status | Management |
|---|---|---|
| Spring | Starts once temperatures exceed 12°C after the thaw | Remove the straw laid over the bed for winter to let in light |
| Early summer | The heaviest fruiting | Concentrate on water management |
| Midsummer | Declines or stops above 25°C | Add more shade and water often |
| Autumn | Heavy fruiting again. The best quality | Top up with 5-10cm of fresh wood chips |
| Early winter | Continues as long as it is 4°C or above | Cover with straw, but leave openings where mushrooms will emerge |
| Midwinter | Stops. The mycelium stays alive | Cover with 5-10cm of straw or leaves to prevent frost damage |

## 8. Harvest and the Re-fruiting Cycle

### 8-1. Judging When to Harvest

| Criterion | Details |
|---|---|
| Optimal | Just before the cap opens fully from the stem. While the ring is still attached or has just come off |
| Size | Cap diameter 5-10cm |
| Texture | At this stage it is firmest and tastes best |
| Late signs | Once the cap flattens and the gills darken to purple-black, it is late |
| Time of day | Early morning |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Specimens that are too large lose texture and flavor and also keep poorly. Past 15cm in diameter, the flesh becomes loose and watery. Picking diligently while they are small gives a larger total harvest and better quality.

> **The button stage, which is the best time to harvest, is also when the mushroom looks most like a young deadly amanita. Be sure to read Section 12, and pick only what grew on a bed you built yourself.**

### 8-2. How to Pick

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Grasp the base of the stem and pull it out with a twisting motion. It is better to pull than to cut, because a leftover stump rots and attracts bacteria and fungus gnats. If the spot where it came out is left hollow, fill it with wood chips.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;And always pull the whole mushroom, base included. As Section 12 shows, the presence or absence of a volva (the sac at the base of the stem) can be confirmed only at the base, and that check can decide between life and death. It is a motion you need to make a habit.

### 8-3. Fruiting Cycle

| Item | Details |
|---|---|
| First harvest | Around 90 days after building the bed. 30-60 days after casing |
| Duration of one flush | 7-14 days |
| Interval between flushes | 12-25 days |
| Flushes per season | 3-5 |
| Bed lifespan | With management, from several seasons up to several years |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The rhythm of this mushroom is to fruit once, rest for 2-3 weeks, and fruit again. All there is to do during the rest is keep the bed from drying out. There is no need to go out of your way to give any stimulus.

### 8-4. Renewing the Bed

| Timing | Task | Thickness |
|---|---|:---:|
| Autumn or early spring | Cover the bed with fresh wood chips | 5-10cm |
| At the same time | Repair the edges where they have collapsed | - |
| Every 2-3 years | Lift out part of the bed and move it to a new location | - |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;If fresh wood chips are laid over the bed every year, the bed can effectively continue indefinitely. The lower layers break down completely into leaf mold, and the mycelium climbs up into the new layer above. Since the leaf mold that accumulates underneath can go straight onto the field as fertilizer, you are in effect building soil while you harvest mushrooms.

### 8-5. Yield

| Condition | Yield |
|---|---|
| China open-field national average | 6.47kg per m² |
| Variation by substrate formulation | 2.59-5.22kg per m². Up to a 101.5% difference |
| Difference between substrate types | About 3.9 times |
| Conversion to calories | 1,618kcal per m². 0.62× the self-sufficiency line |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The wide variation in yield is worth remembering. Even for the same mushroom, the difference between substrates is nearly fourfold. A substrate centered on wood chips with straw and a nitrogen source mixed in at suitable proportions works best, while a substrate made of straw alone fruits quickly but gives a smaller total. Testing several formulations on a small scale and settling on the combination that suits your own land and materials makes a large difference in harvest a few years later.

> **1,618kcal per m² is an exceptional figure for a mushroom. The calculation shows that a bed of 30m², less than ten pyeong (about 33 m²), could supply a substantial part of one person's daily calories.**
