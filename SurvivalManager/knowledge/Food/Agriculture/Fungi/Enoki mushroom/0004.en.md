## 5. Inoculation Method

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Inoculation is where enoki cultivation fails most often. The substrate has been sterilized clean and left empty, and it is common for green mold to move into that empty space before the mushroom's own mycelium does. Inoculation has a single goal: make sure the mushroom's mycelium claims the space first.

### 5-1. Timing and temperature for inoculation

| Item | Standard | Reason |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Substrate temperature | **Cooled to 25°C or below** | Above that, the spawn dies |
| Cooling time | One night inside the drum, or 13-14 hours at 15°C | The substrate must cool through to its center |
| Speed | As soon as it has cooled | Leave it longer and airborne spores settle on it |
| Time of day | **Early morning, or right after rain** | Airborne spores and dust are at their lowest then |
| Season | September to November | The starting point of the winter cropping cycle |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The advice to inoculate right after rain is especially useful without a clean room. Rain washes spores and dust out of the air, sharply cutting the number that fall and settle. The worst time, by contrast, is midday on a clear, windy day.

### 5-2. Inoculating without a clean room

| Item | Method |
|:---|:---|
| Location | The smallest draft-free room available — deep in a storeroom, a corner of a shed |
| Pre-treatment | Spray water on the floor and walls to settle dust. Wait 30 minutes |
| A makeshift clean box | Lay a large box or jar on its side, wipe the inside with boiling water, and work with only your hands inside it |
| Hands | Wash with soap, and wipe with strong liquor or vinegar if you can |
| Tools | Heat the spoon and knife over a flame, then let them cool |
| Clothing | Slightly damp clothing raises less dust than dry clothing |
| Breathing | Do not talk or breathe over the substrate while working. Cover your mouth with cloth |
| Motion | Open the container, add the spawn, and close it again within 10 seconds |

> **Without a clean room, the key is not perfect sterility but speed and spawn volume. You cannot stop a few contaminants from getting in, so make the mushroom's own mycelium overwhelmingly abundant so it claims the ground first.**

### 5-3. Amount of spawn

| Item | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Standard | 2-3% of substrate weight |
| Without a clean room | **Raise it to 5-10% of substrate weight** |
| Placement | Half into the center hole, half on the surface |
| Covering | Lightly flatten the surface once it is added |
| Sealing | Plug the opening with cotton or cloth and tie it with string. Do not seal it airtight with plastic |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;It matters that the opening is not sealed completely. Mycelium uses oxygen, so it cannot grow if it cannot breathe. At the same time, contaminant spores must be kept out, so cotton or several layers of cloth — letting air through while filtering out dust — is the right answer. Raw cotton, or an old towel folded into four layers, works well.

### 5-4. The 24 hours after inoculation

| Time | What to look for |
|:---|:---|
| 12 hours | Still no visible change |
| 24-48 hours | **A faint, cottony white haze** begins to appear around the spawn |
| 3-5 days | White mycelium spreads outward from the spawn by 1-2cm |
| 7 days | The top of the substrate is covered in white |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;These first few days decide everything. If white mycelium is visibly spreading within 3 days, it is almost a success. If nothing has changed after 5 days, the spawn was dead, or the substrate was still too hot when it was inoculated. And if any color other than white appears, move that bottle away from the others at once.

## 6. Managing Mycelium Incubation

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Incubation runs about 22 days after inoculation. The goal is to let white mycelium take over the whole substrate. The grower's job is simply to hold the temperature, keep it dark, and check every day to pull out any contaminated bottle.

### 6-1. Temperature and humidity

| Item | Range | Note |
|:---|:---:|:---|
| Mycelium growth temperature | 23-25°C | Fastest growth |
| Workable range | 5-30°C | Slow at low temperatures, dangerous at high ones |
| **Absolute ceiling** | **34°C** | **Even slightly over this and the mycelium dies** |
| Air humidity | 65-75% | Too humid and contaminants take hold on the surface |
| Light | Dark | Light is not needed |
| Duration | About 22 days | 18-28 days depending on substrate amount and temperature |

> **34°C is an absolute line. Because the mycelium itself gives off metabolic heat, the inside of the substrate can pass 34°C even when the surrounding air is only 30°C. Stacking bottles tightly makes the center especially hot.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This metabolic-heat problem is worst partway through incubation, when the mycelium is growing most vigorously. There are three responses: stack the bottles with finger-width gaps between them; lower the surrounding temperature by 2-3°C starting around day 10 of incubation; and avoid incubating at the height of summer.

### 6-2. Daily checks

| Observation | Normal | Abnormal |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Color | White to very pale cream | Blue-green, black, orange, or pink means contamination |
| Texture | A fine, dense, cottony white layer | If it looks powdery and drifts, it is a contaminant's spores |
| Smell | A faint mushroom scent, like forest soil | A sour, musty, or ammonia smell is abnormal |
| Progress | Advancing 2-4mm downward each day | If it stalls, suspect a temperature or moisture problem |
| Inside wall of the container | Mycelium clings to the glass, leaving a white line | Heavy condensation means excess moisture |
| Substrate surface | Evenly white | Covered on only one side means the spawn was clumped there |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Pull out a contaminated bottle the moment you find it. Hesitate, and the spores spread and take the neighboring bottles with them. Open the removed bottle far away from the incubation area, then either burn its contents or bury them deep. Boil the bottle before reusing it.

### 6-3. Judging when incubation is complete

| Criterion | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Color | The whole substrate is evenly white |
| Bottom | **Mycelium must reach the bottom.** If only the top is white, it is not finished |
| Firmness | The substrate holds together as a single mass even when the container is turned upside down |
| Extra time | **Leave it 3-5 more days** once fully covered. This is called after-ripening, and it raises the yield |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Skipping after-ripening lowers the yield. Even when it looks fully covered on the outside, parts inside the substrate still have less mycelium than they should, so a few extra days give a better result.

### 6-4. Scratching

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Once incubation ends, the aged mycelium layer on the substrate surface is scraped off. This clears away the hardened mycelial skin that formed on the surface during incubation, along with the aged cells piled on top of it.

| Item | Method |
|:---|:---|
| Tool | A spoon, a wooden spatula, or a small hook dipped in boiling water and cooled |
| Depth | Only 2-5mm from the surface |
| Coverage | Evenly across the whole surface |
| Purpose | To bring on pinning evenly, all at once |
| Caution | Scraping too deep badly damages the mycelium and delays pinning |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Scratching makes the mushrooms come up across the whole surface at once, so the harvest finishes in a single pass. Skip it, and they emerge over several days, scattered and uneven in size. If you need to harvest and dry everything together, it is worth doing.

## 7. Inducing Fruiting

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Left comfortable, mycelium just keeps spreading. Forming mushrooms is, to the mycelium, a signal of crisis. For enoki, that signal is almost entirely temperature. Take mycelium that has been growing in the warmth and chill it suddenly, and it puts out mushrooms.

### 7-1. The temperature staircase

| Stage | Temperature | Duration | Humidity | CO2 | What it produces |
|:---|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---|
| Incubation | 23-25°C | 22 days | 65-75% | - | Full colonization |
| **Pinning** | **14-15°C** | 8-10 days | 95-98% | 1,800-2,000ppm | Millet-sized primordia |
| **Growth restraint** | **5-7°C** | 5-15 days | 75-96% | 3,000-4,000ppm | Thick, short stems |
| **Growth** | **4-9°C** | Until harvest | 75-85% | 6,000ppm or more | Stems lengthen |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Building this staircase is essentially the whole of enoki cultivation. Commercial growers do it with refrigeration; without electricity, you do it with the season and the site.

| Step | How to get it without electricity |
|:---|:---|
| Incubation, 23-25°C | Indoors in September-October, or one side of a room with a hearth |
| Pinning, 14-15°C | A November root cellar or north-facing shed, or October night temperatures |
| Restraint, 5-7°C | Under the eaves or in a north-facing shed in December. Night-time temperatures |
| Growth, 4-9°C | A north-facing shed, the mouth of a root cellar, or beside an ice pit in December-February |

> **Mushrooms will still come even if the staircase is not hit precisely. What matters is direction. After incubation, the substrate must move somewhere colder. Leave it at the same temperature and the mycelium only ages — no mushrooms form.**

### 7-2. What to do during pinning

| Item | Method |
|:---|:---|
| Moving | Move the scratched, fully incubated bottles to a spot at 14-15°C |
| Humidity | 95% or higher. Drape a wet cloth over the bottles or sprinkle water on the floor |
| Light | Bring in weak light — bright enough to read a newspaper by |
| Waiting | 8-10 days. Dense, millet-sized white beads form on the surface |
| Sign of failure | If the surface dries out and hardens, humidity is insufficient |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The 95% humidity needed during pinning is a very high figure. Without electricity, sealing plus water is how you get it. Put the bottles together in a large box or jar, place wet sand or a dish of water on its floor, and leave only a few small holes in the lid — the humidity inside rises quickly. With no holes at all, CO2 builds up too far and condensation drips onto the mushrooms, bringing on bacterial soft rot.

### 7-3. Growth Restraint — a Stage Unique to Enoki

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;No other mushroom has this stage. Once primordia have formed, the temperature drops further, to 5-7°C, for 5 to 15 days. Over this stretch, instead of lengthening, the stems grow thick and firm.

| Effect | Detail |
|:---|:---|
| Thicker stems | Fewer stems for the same weight, and each one is thicker |
| Firmer | Denser tissue gives a better texture and better storability |
| Even growth | Lengths come out uniform rather than ragged |
| If skipped | Stems come out thin and drawn-out, weak, and spoil fast |

> **In a disaster situation, the restraint stage is not optional. Thick, firm mushrooms dry easily and do not crumble once dried. Thin, drawn-out mushrooms turn to threads when dried, leaving nothing worth eating.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The easiest way to build the restraint stage without electricity is a winter night. A north-facing shed in December-January runs around 7°C by day and near 0°C by night. As long as the day-night average falls within 5-7°C, that is enough. But if it stays below freezing for long stretches the mushrooms freeze and turn soft, so cover them with straw or matting to keep them above zero.

### 7-4. Shaping the Mushroom with CO2

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This is a quirk unique to enoki. High CO2 keeps the cap small and the stem long. Low CO2 lets the cap grow larger while the stem stays short.

| Goal | Ventilation | Result |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Long stem, small cap (market shape) | Nearly sealed. Wrap paper around the bottle's neck and stand it up | 6,000ppm or more. A bean-sprout-like shape |
| Thick stem, large cap (wild shape) | Ventilate freely | The cap opens out and the stem thickens |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In a disaster situation, the latter is better. The same substrate yields more weight, the capped form has a higher nutrient density, and it holds its shape better when dried. There is no reason to copy the shape sold in markets.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Since CO2 concentration cannot be measured, judge it by feel. If a person can stand inside for a while without feeling stuffy, it is about right.

## 8. Harvest and the Recropping Cycle

### 8-1. Judging harvest time

| Criterion | Detail |
|:---|:---|
| Optimal | The cap is still closed and the stem has lengthened enough |
| Late sign | **The cap opens and white powder scatters from between the gills.** It has started shedding spores |
| If left too long | Flavor drops, texture turns soft, and storability worsens sharply |
| Total duration | 50-70 days from inoculation to harvest |
| Time of day | Early morning |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;A mushroom that has started shedding spores is past its best for eating, but it still has a use. This is exactly the stage best suited to collecting spores, as in Section 4. If a batch comes up late with the caps already open, do not discard it — take a spore print from it instead.

### 8-2. How to pick

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Grip the cluster by its base and twist gently as you pull it free. Yanking it out tears the substrate, and contaminants enter through that wound. Cut away the substrate-covered part of the base with a knife.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Do not wash harvested mushrooms. Contact with water sharply cuts their storability. Wipe off substrate crumbs with a dry cloth instead.

### 8-3. Recropping

| Item | Details |
|:---|:---|
| Rest period | 10-15 days after harvest |
| Care during rest | Scrape the surface to remove leftover stubs, keep humidity up, and hold it at 13-15°C |
| 2nd-flush yield | 50-70% of the 1st flush |
| 3rd-flush yield | 20-30% of the 1st flush — barely worth the effort |
| Total flushes | **A maximum of 2-3** |
| Interval between flushes | 30-60 days |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This is what the evaluation table's Perenniality D reflects. Yield falls to half by the 2nd flush and to 25% or less by the 3rd. Harvesting through the 2nd flush and then replacing the substrate gives the best return on effort.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In a disaster situation, the calculation shifts slightly. If getting fresh sawdust and boiling it again costs a lot of fuel and labor, pushing on to a 3rd flush despite the lower yield can be worthwhile. When the substrate turns black and smells sour, that is the limit.

### 8-4. Yield

| Condition | Yield |
|:---|:---|
| 1000ml bottle, 600-750g substrate | 150-250g for the 1st flush |
| Including the 2nd flush | 250-350g per bottle |
| With 10% eggshell added | 1.21× |
| With 20% herbal-medicine residue added | 1.10× |
| Biological efficiency | 30-50% of the substrate's dry weight |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;One bottle yields around 300g across two flushes. 300g fresh is 63kcal and 7.4g of protein. As a calorie source it barely counts, but it counts for protein and B vitamins. Running twenty bottles as one batch is enough to give a handful of greens or soup ingredients every day for two months in the depth of winter.

> **Planning around enoki's yield in calories leads to disappointment. Instead, its value should be measured this way: in winter, when nothing green is available at all, it is the one fresh food that supplies vitamin B1, B2, and lysine.**

## 9. Contamination and Pests

### 9-1. Green Mold Is the Number-One Cause

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The most common reason mushroom growing fails in a disaster situation is not pests or disease, but losing the substrate to a competing mold. By far the most common of these is Trichoderma — green mold, in everyday terms. Because enoki uses a sterilized substrate, this risk is far greater than it is for wine cap mushrooms: a sterilized substrate is also empty ground for contaminants.

| Category | Enoki mycelium | Green mold |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Color | White, very pale cream | Starts white, soon turns **blue-green** |
| Texture | A fine, dense, cottony layer | Fine and powdery, crumbly |
| Spreading speed | 2-4mm a day | 10-30mm a day, **5-10 times faster** |
| Smell | Forest soil, a mushroom scent | Musty, sometimes a faint coconut-like smell |
| Border | Spreads evenly along the substrate | Spreads outward from round spots |

> **Green mold has already spread before its color even becomes visible.**
> The blue-green stage is when it has already begun sporulating — that is, it has already started reproducing. By that point, assume a significant part of the substrate is already lost. Tell it apart early by its spreading speed and texture, not by color.

### 9-2. Other Contaminants

| Contaminant | Appearance | Cause |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Black mold (Aspergillus) | Clumps of black powder | Insufficient sterilization, especially with a lot of rice bran |
| Red mold (Fusarium) | Pink, cottony growth | Substrate too wet |
| White cotton mold (Mucor) | Tall white hairs shoot up fast | Substrate too wet and poorly ventilated |
| Bacteria (Bacillus) | Sticky substrate with a sour smell | Insufficient sterilization plus excess moisture |
| Yellow bacterial ooze | Yellow slime on the mushroom stem | Excess humidity with dripping condensation |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Bacterial contamination barely changes color, so it is hard to spot except by smell. If the substrate smells sour or like rotten dough, it is bacterial — a sign that sterilization ran too short.

### 9-3. What to Do When You Find Contamination, and How to Prevent It

| Situation | Response |
|:---|:---|
| **A small spot on one bottle's surface** | Remove only that bottle at once. Move it **without opening it** |
| **It has spread to several bottles** | Move the whole incubation batch. Clear the original spot, wash it with water, and dry it |
| **The whole substrate has turned blue-green** | Discard the entire batch. Burn it, or bury it far from the growing area |
| **The substrate smells sour** | That is bacterial. Discard it and lengthen sterilization time for the next batch |

> **Do not put contaminated substrate back into compost.**
> Green mold spores survive in a compost pile and come right back into next year's substrate. Always burn it, or bury it deep, well away from the growing area.

| Prevention | Principle |
|:---|:---|
| **Sterilize thoroughly** | 4-6 hours at 100°C, unpressurized. Skimp on this and you always pay for it |
| **Use plenty of spawn** | 5-10% of substrate weight. Whichever side claims the ground first wins |
| **Never exceed 20% rice bran** | Too much nutrient, and competing molds explode first |
| **Stack bottles with gaps between them** | Airflow lets metabolic heat escape and keeps mold from spreading |
| **Inoculate right after rain** | Airborne spore fall is lowest then |
| **Change the incubation site every year** | Reusing the same spot lets spores build up |

### 9-4. Pests

| Pest | Damage | Response |
|:---|:---|:---|
| **Fungus gnats (sciarids)** | Eat through the mycelium, and their larvae infest the fruiting bodies. They also carry green mold spores | Block them physically by plugging the opening with cloth. Once they appear, use a water-dish trap or sticky traps |
| **Mites** | Mycelium turns brown and stops growing | Keep conditions dry, and isolate any affected batch |
| **Slugs** | Chew on young fruiting bodies | Ring the area with wood ash or a band of sawdust |
| **Rats** | Carry off mushrooms and tear up the substrate | Put bottles on shelves and cover them with netting |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Growing this mushroom in winter is a major advantage for pest control. Fungus gnats and mites almost stop being active once the temperature drops below 10°C. Running enoki as a winter-only crop cuts the pest problem by more than half on its own. Push it into summer instead, and pests and contamination arrive together.
