## 3. Substrate Materials and Preparation

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Enoki mushroom is grown by packing a substrate of sawdust mixed with rice bran into a container and sterilizing it. It is not a mushroom where you can simply lay material on the ground the way you do with wine cap — sterilization is a precondition. This fact is what produces the evaluation table's Infrastructure needs E, and it's also the first problem to solve when there's no power.

### 3-1. Enoki Mushroom's Own Advantage - It Can Use Conifers

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Most cultivated mushrooms cannot grow on the sawdust of conifers in the pine and Korean pine family. Resin and terpene compounds inhibit the mycelium. Shiitake, lion's mane mushroom, and wine cap all treat conifers as a forbidden wood. Enoki mushroom's standard substrate material, however, is Douglas-fir sawdust.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This difference is decisive depending on the region. A large share of South Korea's forest plantations are pine, Korean pine, and larch, and in mountain areas with few broadleaf trees, both shiitake and lion's mane mushroom struggle to find substrate material. In such places, enoki mushroom becomes almost the only choice.

| Wood species | Suitability | Notes |
|:---|:--:|:---|
| Douglas-fir, pine, Korean pine | High | Standard substrate material; material other mushrooms cannot use |
| Poplar, willow | High | Soft and breaks down quickly |
| Hackberry, mulberry, persimmon | High | Species it fruits on in the wild |
| Oak, elm | Medium | Hard, so mycelium spreads slowly |
| Walnut, Manchurian walnut | Forbidden | Allelopathic compounds kill the mycelium |
| Hinoki cypress, Oriental arborvitae, Chinese juniper | Forbidden | Antimicrobial compounds are very strong |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;When using conifer sawdust, it is better to stack it outdoors and let it weather in the rain for six months to a year. The resin washes out and a little decomposition proceeds, so the mycelium spreads much better. Freshly milled sawdust has a strong resin smell, so it isn't used right away.

### 3-2. Substrate Mix

| Material | Ratio | Role |
|:---|:---:|:---|
| Sawdust (Douglas-fir or broadleaf) | 60% | Main carbon source |
| Spent substrate | 20% | Recycles substrate left over after the first harvest |
| Rice bran | 20% | Source of nitrogen and vitamins |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;If spent substrate isn't available, use 80% sawdust to 20% rice bran. The rice-bran ratio matters. Above 20%, there are too many nutrients and green mold takes over first; below 10%, yield drops noticeably.

| Additive | Amount | Effect |
|:---|:---:|:---|
| Eggshell (crushed to about 5mm) | 10% of sawdust weight | Yield 121% |
| Spent herbal-medicine dregs (left over from brewing traditional medicine) | 20% of sawdust weight | Yield 110% |
| Wheat bran | Replaces rice bran | When rice bran is unavailable |
| Ground corn cob | Replaces part of the sawdust | Improves aeration |
| Beet pulp | Replaces part of the rice bran | Used in commercial mixes |

> **Mixing in eggshell at 10% of the sawdust weight raises yield by 21%. Collecting shells left over from eating, drying them thoroughly, and grinding them costs nothing and is the single most reliably effective step you can take.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The reason eggshell works is calcium. Calcium carbonate buffers the substrate's acidity, keeping the pH from dropping sharply even as the mycelium releases acidic metabolic byproducts. Roasted, ground shellfish or oyster shells, charcoal powder, and a small amount of wood ash serve the same role. Wood ash, though, is strongly alkaline, so it should not exceed 1% of the sawdust weight.

### 3-3. Moisture Control

| Item | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Target moisture content | 65-70% |
| Judging method | Squeeze a handful — moisture should show between your fingers, but no drops should fall |
| Too dry | Mycelium doesn't spread and the substrate crumbles |
| Too wet | Water pools at the bottom, creating anaerobic conditions where bacteria spread |
| pH | Grows anywhere from 4.5-8.6; a very wide range |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The fact that enoki mushroom tolerates a pH range of 4.5-8.6 helps in a disaster situation. Even mixing by feel, with no pH meter or test strips, it's hard to fall outside this range. It's a mushroom you barely need to work at getting the acidity right for.

### 3-4. Containers - When There's No Bottle

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Commercial cultivation packs 600-750g of substrate into a 1,000ml plastic bottle. In a disaster, that bottle isn't there. What matters is not the object called a bottle, but the following four functions: holding the substrate upright, keeping contaminants out after sterilization, letting air through, and leaving room above for the mushroom to grow.

| Container | How to use | Caution |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Glass jars (jam jars, pickling jars) | Hold up best under sterilization. Cover the mouth with cloth and tie it with string | Cracks if cooled too fast; cool slowly |
| Bamboo tubes | Cut leaving one node intact at one end. Can be boiled whole | Splits easily, so it can't be reused many times |
| Earthenware pots, crocks | Heat-resistant, hold humidity well | Heavy, and sterilization takes longer |
| Plastic bags | Use only heat-resistant ones. Plug the opening with cotton or cloth | Only withstand up to 100 degrees at normal pressure |
| Cans, tin containers | Easy to obtain | Discard once they rust |
| Wooden box covered with cloth | Becomes a flat-bed cultivation form | Highest risk of contamination |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There are three tricks to filling it. Fill only 75% of the container, leaving space above. Tamp the surface lightly flat, but don't press hard. And bore a 1cm-diameter hole down to the bottom in the center. With this hole, spawn reaches deep into the substrate and mycelium spreads outward from the center, which shortens incubation by several days and lowers the failure rate.

### 3-5. Sterilizing Without Electricity

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The standard is 121°C under pressure for 60-90 minutes. Without a pressure sterilizer, choose from the following.

#### 3-5-1. Drum-Barrel Atmospheric Steam Sterilization

| Item | Method |
|:---|:---|
| Equipment | 200L drum barrel, a stand, a lid, firewood |
| Water | Fill 20-30cm at the bottom |
| Placement | Set the stand in and stand the containers on it so they don't touch the water |
| Covering | Put the lid on and seal the gaps with wet cloth or mud |
| Time | 4-6 hours at 100°C, counted from when steam starts rising |
| Cooling | Put out the fire and let it cool overnight with the lid on |
| Fuel | 30-50kg of firewood |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This is the most reliable power-free method. However, it uses a lot of fuel. Filling the drum barrel completely and processing everything at once each time you build a fire is far more fuel-efficient. The firewood needed to sterilize ten bottles isn't much different from what's needed to sterilize a hundred.

#### 3-5-2. Boiling in Water

| Item | Method |
|:---|:---|
| Method | Pack the sawdust-and-rice-bran substrate into a sack and boil it in water for 2-3 hours |
| Follow-up | Lift it out, press to squeeze out water, adjust to 65-70%, and pack into containers |
| Limitation | Not full sterilization; heat-resistant bacterial spores survive |
| Compensation | Inoculate quickly once cooled, and use plenty of spawn |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This is an alternative for when there's no drum barrel. The sterilization level is lower, but putting in plenty of spawn so it claims the space first raises the success rate.

#### 3-5-3. Heated Rice-Straw Substrate Method

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This method is for when there's no sawdust at all, or when spawn has to be raised fresh from spores. In theory, scattering spores on a substrate lets mycelium spread, but in reality, airborne green mold and bacteria colonize the substrate hundreds of times faster than mushroom spores do. The richer the substrate's nutrients, the more vulnerable it is. So instead of an overly nutrient-rich artificial substrate, a primitive substrate that relies on nature's own defenses is used.

| Stage | Method |
|:---|:---|
| Boiling | Boil rice straw, wheat straw, or bean pods thoroughly in water for at least 1 hour |
| Wringing | Lift it out, wring out the water, and let it cool until it can be handled |
| Scattering | Scatter the stored spore powder generously and evenly, without holding back |
| Covering | Cover with clean cloth to block wind and falling spores |
| Incubation | 2-4 weeks in a dark place at 20-25°C. Success is when white mycelium covers the straw |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Straw, being low in nutrients, is also less attractive to green mold. The mycelium grows slowly, but in exchange it isn't crowded out in competition. The mycelium mass obtained this way is transferred to a sawdust substrate and grown into proper spawn.

### 3-6. Comparing Sterilization Methods

| Method | Fuel | Time | Sterilization level | Recommended situation |
|:---|:--:|:--:|:--:|:---|
| Pressure, 121 degrees | Power | 60-90 min | Complete | When power is available |
| Drum-barrel steam, 100°C | High | 4-6 hrs | High | The power-free standard; process a large batch at once |
| Boiling in water | Medium | 2-3 hrs | Medium | When there's no drum barrel |
| Heated rice-straw substrate | Low | 1 hr | Medium | When raising spawn from spores |

### 3-7. Securing Low Temperature - Cooling Without Power

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In growing enoki mushroom, the biggest problem after sterilization is low temperature. The growth stage needs 4-9°C, and the inhibition stage before it needs 5-7°C. Without a cooling unit, a person cannot manufacture this temperature. It has to be borrowed.

| Means | Temperature | How to secure it | Limitation |
|:---|:---:|:---|:---|
| **Winter itself** | Natural | November to March; a north-facing shed or under eaves | Only possible in season |
| Earthen cave, cave | 10-15°C | Dig 2m or more into a hillside | Doesn't go below 10 degrees even in summer |
| Cellar | 12-18°C | Use an existing structure | Same |
| Beside a well, a spring | 10-14°C | Use the air by the cold water's edge | Humidity is excessively high |
| Ice pit | 0-5°C | Fill with ice in midwinter and cover with straw and rice husks | Requires a lot of labor |
| North-facing slope pit | 8-14°C | Dig a pit in the shade on the north side and cover with straw | Fluctuates widely |
| Hanging wet cloth | 3-5°C below ambient | Lowers temperature through evaporative cooling | No effect on humid days |

> **Without power, enoki mushroom is a winter crop. Trying to grow it year-round will fail. Making the substrate in October, incubating in November, going through pinning in December, and harvesting in the depths of winter is this mushroom's natural cropping pattern.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In summer, an earthen cave cannot produce 4°C. At that time, you don't grow enoki mushroom — you grow other mushrooms that fruit in summer. Wine cap and wood ear mushroom fill that spot. This is why several mushroom species are covered together. One species cannot cover all four seasons, but staggering several species can.

## 4. Obtaining Spawn

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Mushrooms have no seeds. If the seed suppliers close, there's nowhere to buy spawn, and the method of collecting seeds and storing them in a jar doesn't work either. On the evaluation table, enoki mushroom's Seed saving is C and Seed longevity is D. That means commercial spawn loses vigor within 6 months to a year, and keeping a pure strain going requires aseptic technique. But fungi have three paths that plants don't: keeping spores in reserve, taking tissue from a living mushroom and growing it, and splitting up an already-grown substrate to transfer it.

### 4-1. Spore Collection and Spore Prints

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Spores are the longest-lasting form of preservation, and an insurance policy you must make early in a disaster.

| Stage | Method |
|:---|:---|
| Choosing a specimen | A large, healthy specimen with a fully opened cap |
| Cutting the stem | Cut the stem off close, right where it meets the cap |
| Base | Set it gills-down on **aluminum foil** |
| Covering | Cover with a bowl or cup to block drafts |
| Waiting | 24 hours in a cool place |
| Collecting | Lift the cap away and the spore powder remains in the shape of the cap |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Enoki mushroom's spores are white to a very pale yellow. Caught on white paper, they're invisible. **You must catch them on dark-colored paper, foil, or a glass plate.** This differs from wine cap, which produces purplish-brown spores, or button mushroom, which produces brown spores, and it also makes a decisive difference in the safety check in Section 12.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Aluminum foil is better than paper. Paper absorbs moisture, and contaminants hide between its fibers. Foil doesn't absorb moisture, and its smooth surface preserves spores as they are. If there's no foil, a glass plate is the next choice.

### 4-2. Preserving Spores - 10 Years Without Electricity

| Step | Method |
|:---:|:---|
| 1 | Catch spores on foil |
| 2 | Leave the spore-printed foil for one more day in a ventilated **shaded spot** to drive off even trace moisture |
| 3 | Fold the foil in half to protect the spore side inward, and put it in a zip bag |
| 4 | Add in **thoroughly roasted salt or a piece of charcoal** to trap moisture |
| 5 | Gather the zip bags into a glass jar and **seal it with beeswax** |
| 6 | Keep it in an **earthen cave or cellar** where the temperature stays constant year-round. It must stay dark |

| Preservation form | Lifespan | Notes |
|:---|:---:|:---|
| Spore print (dried on foil) | **5-10+ years** | Moisture is fully excluded, halting cellular activity |
| Liquid spawn (suspended in sterile water) | 3-6 months | Without refrigeration it germinates early or bacteria multiply |
| Ready-to-use spawn (sawdust or grain) | 6 months-1 year | Being living mycelium, it keeps consuming nutrients |
| Colonized substrate chunk | 1-2 months | Left too long, it ages and loses vigor |

> **In a disaster situation, do not store spores as a liquid. They must be kept as a dried spore print.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Ultraviolet light destroys spore DNA, so it must be kept somewhere deep where light doesn't reach. The difference in lifespan between spores and plant seeds comes from their structure.

| Comparison | Tomato seed | Mushroom spore |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Average lifespan (room temperature) | About 2-4 years | About 5-10+ years |
| Vulnerability | Temperature swings, depletion of its own nutrients | Mold contamination from moisture |
| Cell structure | Complex embryo and nutrient tissue | A single cell with a thick protective wall |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;A tomato seed holds an embryo and nutrients inside, so over time it ages and dies as it consumes its own nutrients. A mushroom spore is only a single cell wrapped in a thick cell wall, with no nutrients to consume, so given the right conditions it lasts far longer. In academic literature, reviving spores decades old is not even rare.

> **The evaluation table's Seed longevity D is based on commercial spawn. Switch to spore prints, and that D effectively loses its meaning. In the first year of a disaster, make 20-30 spore prints from the largest, healthiest specimens and store them in an earthen cave. Even if you lose all your spawn, you can start again five or ten years later.**

### 4-3. Tissue Culture - the Most Reliable Method

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;If you have a fresh, living mushroom in hand, there's almost no reason to use spores. The white flesh inside, where the cap meets the stem, is already a mass of secondary mycelium whose mating-type fusion is complete. Take a piece of it and put it into the substrate, and it skips germination and mating altogether, spreading outward as mycelium right away.

| Step | Method |
|:---|:---|
| Selecting | A fresh specimen whose cap is just about to open. Don't use one that's old or insect-eaten |
| Disinfecting | Heat the knife over a flame and let it cool, or dip it in boiling water and take it out |
| Splitting | **Split the mushroom lengthwise by hand.** Cutting with a knife drags contaminants from the surface into the interior |
| Extracting | Take a rice-grain-sized piece of the white flesh inside where cap meets stem |
| Inoculating | Tuck it into a boiled-and-cooled sawdust substrate or between rice straw |
| Incubating | A dark place at 20-25°C. In 3-5 days, white mycelium spreads around the piece |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Splitting matters because the inside of a mushroom is close to sterile. All sorts of spores sit on the outer skin and gills, but the flesh inside is clean. Splitting by hand exposes that clean surface fresh, and a piece taken from it becomes pure mycelium without any disinfection. Enoki mushroom has a thin stem, so it isn't easy to take a piece of flesh from it — it's better to use the thicker part at the base of a cluster. Wild specimens have a thicker stem and are far easier to handle.

### 4-4. Multiplying Spawn by Splitting Substrate

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Break up a substrate that mycelium has spread through well and rub it into a new substrate, and it becomes spawn. It's a primitive but reliable method.

| Step | Method |
|:---|:---|
| Target | A bottle where white mycelium covers the whole substrate with no sign of contamination |
| Timing | Right after incubation finishes. A substrate left too long has mycelium that's aged and lost vigor |
| Preparation | Boil the new substrate and let it cool |
| Transferring | Scoop the mycelium-covered substrate with a spoon or by hand and mix it into the surface and interior of the new substrate |
| Ratio | 5-10% of the new substrate's weight |
| Caution | One substrate can be multiplied into 5-10. But repeating this method past four or five generations lowers vigor, so periodically go back to tissue culture or spores to raise it fresh |

### 4-5. Priority Order for Securing Spawn

| Rank | Method | Condition |
|:--:|:---|:---|
| 1 | Commercial spawn | Secure it ahead of time whenever available. Since Prevalence is S, this is the easiest option in normal times |
| 2 | Tissue culture | When you have a fresh mushroom in hand; the most reliable |
| 3 | Splitting substrate | When you already have a well-grown substrate; the fastest |
| 4 | From a spore print to rice-straw substrate | When everything else is lost but you have stored spores |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;What to do in normal times is clear. Grow it once while spawn is available, then make 20-30 spore prints from that mushroom and bury them in an earthen cave. The spawn itself dies within a year, but spores last 10 years. This one piece of work becomes a ten-year insurance policy.
