## 10. Diagnosing Growth Problems

| Symptom | Cause | Action |
|:---|:---|:---|
| No mycelium visible even 5 days after inoculation | Inoculated while the substrate was hot; the spawn died | Cool the substrate to 25°C or below and inoculate again |
| Mycelium spreads, then stops | Substrate exceeded 34°C, or lacked moisture | Space the bottles apart to vent heat and lower the surrounding temperature |
| Mycelium has covered the substrate, but no mushrooms come | Temperature was not lowered. Scratching was skipped | Move it to 14-15°C and scratch the surface |
| Primordia formed, then dried up and died | Insufficient humidity | Drape a wet cloth over it and sprinkle water on the floor to raise humidity to 95% |
| Only the stems come out thin and long | Restraint stage was skipped; too much CO2 | Hold it at 5-7°C for 5-15 days and increase ventilation |
| Caps grow too large, stems too short | Too little CO2, temperature too high | Reduce ventilation and move it somewhere colder |
| Mushrooms turn soft with yellow slime | Bacterial soft rot. Condensation dripped onto them | Shake condensation off the cover and ventilate. Remove any affected ones |
| Caps turn brown | Temperature too high. Harvest time has passed | Move it somewhere colder and harvest on time |
| Base turns brown and rots | Water pooled in the substrate | Do not pour water directly on it. Raise humidity only through the air |
| Mushrooms grow on only one side | Spawn was clumped on one side; light came from only one side | Next time, scatter spawn evenly and rotate the bottle |
| The 2nd flush yields almost nothing | Substrate's nutrients are exhausted | Normal. Replace the substrate |
| Substrate turns black and sour | Bacterial growth. Substrate has reached the end of its life | Discard it and put it toward spent-substrate use |

## 11. Recycling Spent Substrate

### 11-1. Spent substrate is not something to throw away

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Once the mushrooms are done, the substrate is high-quality organic matter that the mycelium has already broken the wood down into. It is far more decomposed than raw sawdust and can go straight onto a field. The evaluation table's Fertilizer Demand D points to 60kg of organic matter input a year, and what matters is that this 60kg is not used once and gone — it circulates.

| Use | Method |
|:---|:---|
| **Mixed into the next batch of substrate** | Mix in up to 20% of the new substrate. This is already part of the standard formula |
| **Base fertilizer** | Spread on the field and tilled in, in autumn. A large source of organic matter and potassium |
| **Mulch** | Laid 5cm thick between crops to block weeds and moisture loss |
| **Substrate for another mushroom** | Can be mixed into wine cap mushroom's wood-chip substrate |
| **Compost accelerator** | Mixed into a compost pile, it speeds decomposition |
| **Earthworm bedding** | Earthworms feed on it well, and it yields worm castings |
| **Fuel** | Once fully dried, it burns, though weakly |

```
Softwood/hardwood sawdust + rice bran → substrate → mushroom harvest → spent substrate
                                                ↓                ↓
                                    20% of next substrate   field organic matter
```

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Mixing 20% spent substrate into the next batch cuts the sawdust you need to find by 20%. It is the only direct way to ease the burden behind Perenniality D and Fertilizer Demand D. However, never mix in contaminated substrate. Green mold spores go straight into the new substrate along with it.

### 11-2. Yearly management of the incubation site

| Year | Management |
|:--:|:---|
| Year 1 | Fine to keep using the same site |
| Year 2 | Empty the site between batches, wash it with water, and dry it in the sun |
| Year 3 | Safer to move to a different site. Contaminant spores have built up |
| Emergency measure | Coat the walls and floor with lime-sulfur solution or strong wood-ash lye |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Indoor growing lets contaminant spores build up fast. Once a batch is finished, leave the empty space idle for a few days, and open the door to let sunlight in if you can. Ultraviolet light considerably reduces falling spores. It costs no electricity and no chemicals.

## 12. Mistaking Poisonous Mushrooms and Safety Checks

> **This section is a matter of life and death. Confirming that it is safe to eat matters more than growing it.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Of the six species covered in this document, enoki carries the greatest risk of being confused with a poisonous mushroom in the wild. There are three reasons. First, other mushrooms sprout in the same clusters on the same rotting hardwood stumps where wild enoki grows. Second, the cultivated and wild forms differ so much that someone who has only seen the cultivated form cannot recognize the wild one. Third, and most important, enoki's spores are white.

### 12-1. The rule "white spores mean do not eat" cannot be used here

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The wine cap mushroom document has a simple, powerful line of defense: take a spore print by laying the cap gill-down on white paper, and if the powder is white, do not eat it. Because destroying angels and death caps — deadly poisonous species — produce white spores while wine cap mushrooms produce dark grayish-brown ones, this single check filters out the most dangerous species. The button mushroom produces brown spores, so the same rule works for it too.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Enoki is different. **Enoki's spores are white to very pale yellow.** The same color as Amanita mushrooms. So spore color cannot be used to rule out Amanita here.

> **For enoki, spore color is not a tool for filtering out Amanita. It is used the other way around: if the spores are rusty brown or dark grayish-brown, that is used to rule out enoki.**

| Mushroom | Spore color | What this color means |
|:---|:---|:---|
| **Enoki mushroom** | **White to pale yellow** | The baseline |
| Destroying angel, death cap | White | **Not distinguished by spore color.** Other traits must be checked |
| Gymnopilus junonius | **Rusty brown** | Not enoki |
| Cortinarius species | **Rusty brown** | Not enoki |
| Hypholoma fasciculare | **Dark grayish-brown to blackish-brown** | Not enoki |
| Pholiota species | Brown | Not enoki |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Taking a spore print also needs care. Since enoki's spores are white, they will not show up on white paper. Collect them on black paper, foil, or glass instead. And always use both a white half and a black half of paper side by side. That way you can judge both white and dark spores in a single check.

### 12-2. Five Identifying Traits of Enoki — Check All Five

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Since a single line of defense based on spore color cannot be used, switch to checking several traits together. All five of the following must hold for it to count as enoki. If even one is off, do not eat it.

| No. | Trait | Enoki's standard |
|:--:|:---|:---|
| **1** | **Growing substrate** | **A rotting hardwood stump, a dead standing tree, a fallen trunk.** One growing from the ground is not enoki |
| **2** | **Slime on the cap** | **Sticky and slippery to the touch when wet.** This weakens once dry, so check right after rain |
| **3** | **Dark brown hair at the base of the stem** | **The base is dark brown to blackish-brown and covered in short, velvety hair** |
| **4** | **Clustering habit** | **Several grow fused together at the base in a dense cluster.** One growing alone is a different mushroom |
| **5** | **No ring and no sac at all** | **No ring, no veil remnants, no cobweb-like trace on the stem, and no sac when the base is dug up** |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Add these secondary traits on top.

| Secondary trait | Enoki's standard |
|:---|:---|
| Season | **Late autumn to early spring.** One found in high summer is likely not enoki |
| Gill color | White to pale cream. **Does not turn brown or rusty even when mature** |
| Cap color | Yellow-brown to orange-tinted reddish-brown, darker at the center and paler at the edge |
| Taste | **Not bitter.** If a light touch to the tip of the tongue tastes bitter, spit it out and do not eat it |
| Flesh color | White. Does not turn yellow when cut or bruised |

> **If even one of the five main traits is off, do not eat it. One growing from the ground in particular is not enoki, no matter how similar it looks.**

### 12-3. Gymnopilus junonius — the Trickiest Look-Alike

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Gymnopilus junonius is the mushroom most easily confused with enoki. It grows in the same season on the same hardwood stumps, in the same large clusters, and its cap runs from yellow-brown to orange, so from a distance it is barely distinguishable. In Japan it is called the "laughing mushroom," because eating it brings on hallucinations, agitation, and mental confusion.

| Feature | **Enoki** | **Gymnopilus junonius (poisonous)** |
|:---|:---|:---|
| **Spore color** | **White to pale yellow** | **Rusty brown. The decisive difference** |
| **Gill color** | **White to pale cream throughout** | **Starts yellow → turns rusty brown to reddish-brown with age** |
| **Ring** | **None** | **Present.** A fibrous ring remains on the upper stem |
| **Cap surface** | **Slimy when wet. Sticky** | **Dry, with fine fibrous scales.** Not sticky |
| **Stem base** | **Dark brown velvety hair** | Same yellow-brown as the stem. No hair, and **the base is swollen, club-shaped** |
| Cap diameter | 2-8cm | **6-15cm. Much larger** |
| Stem thickness | 0.3-0.8cm | 1-3cm. Thick and firm |
| Taste | Not bitter | **Very bitter** |
| Growing substrate | Rotting hardwood stumps | Both hardwood and softwood stumps |
| Season | Late autumn-early spring | Summer to autumn |

> **Three things alone tell them apart. Do the gills turn rusty brown? Is there a ring on the stem? Is the cap dry with fibrous scales instead of sticky? If any one of these is true, it is Gymnopilus junonius.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Poisoning symptoms begin 30 minutes to 3 hours after eating it: dizziness, severe agitation and laughter, visual and auditory hallucinations, muscle rigidity, vomiting, and abdominal pain. Recovery usually comes within a day or two, but eating a large amount worsens the confusion and dehydration becomes dangerous. Without medical support, there is nothing to do beyond giving plenty of water and keeping the person warm and lying down while you watch over them.

### 12-4. Destroying Angel and Death Cap — Not Filtered Out by Spore Color

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;These are more lethal still. A single specimen's worth of amatoxin can kill a person. And their spores are white, the same color as enoki's.

| Feature | **Enoki** | **Destroying angel / death cap (deadly)** |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Spore color | White to pale yellow | **White. Not distinguishable** |
| Gill color | White to pale cream | **White. Not distinguishable** |
| **Growing substrate** | **Rotting hardwood stumps, dead standing trees** | **Soil near the roots of a living tree. The decisive difference** |
| **Growth habit** | **Dense clusters** | **Alone, or a few scattered apart** |
| **Sac at the base** | **None** | **Present.** A membranous sac wraps the base |
| **Ring** | **None** | **Present.** A white membranous ring remains on the upper stem |
| Cap color | Yellow-brown to orange-tinted reddish-brown | **White to pale greenish-yellow** |
| Cap surface | Slimy when wet | Smooth and dry |
| Stem-base hair | Dark brown velvety hair | None. White and smooth |
| Size | Cap 2-8cm | Cap 6-15cm, stem 9-20cm. Much larger |

> **What separates enoki from Amanita mushrooms is not spore color but four things: did it grow from wood or from the ground; is it clustered or solitary; is there a sac at the base; is there a ring on the stem.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;**Checking for a sac must be done by digging.** Looking only at the part above ground, the sac can be hidden in soil and invisible. Make it a habit to dig out the base along with its soil to check. Enoki grows from wood, so there is no soil to dig in the first place — pull it out and the base that was attached to the wood is right there, exposed. Needing to dig through soil at all is already a sign that it is not enoki.

### 12-5. Cortinarius Species — the Ones with Delayed Symptoms Are More Dangerous

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The genus Cortinarius includes species carrying a toxin called orellanine. Cortinarius orellanus and Cortinarius rubellus are among them. This poison destroys the kidneys, and worse, its symptoms do not appear until 2 to 21 days after eating it. In between, nothing seems wrong, so the person may not even know they have been poisoned.

| Feature | **Enoki** | **Cortinarius species (kidney-destroying)** |
|:---|:---|:---|
| **Spore color** | **White to pale yellow** | **Rusty brown** |
| **Cortina (cobweb veil)** | **None** | **A cobweb-like membrane between the cap edge and stem when young.** It leaves a rusty-brown band on the stem as it matures |
| Gill color | White to pale cream | Starts pale → turns rusty brown |
| **Growing substrate** | **Rotting hardwood stumps** | **Soil.** Mycorrhizal, living in partnership with tree roots |
| Growth habit | Dense clusters | Scattered apart |
| Cap surface | Slimy | Fibrous or scaly |
| Stem base | Dark brown velvety hair | No hair |

> **If there is a rusty-brown band on the stem, or a cobweb-like veil at the cap edge of a young specimen, it is a Cortinarius species. Never eat it. The most frightening thing about this poison is feeling fine and thinking you are safe. Even symptoms that appear late should still be linked back to the mushroom.**

### 12-6. Hypholoma fasciculare — Identical Substrate and Habit

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Hypholoma fasciculare is more common than the three species above, and it grows in the same place and the same shape as enoki more than any of them. It forms dense clusters on rotting hardwood and softwood stumps alike, and its cap is a yellowish tan. Growing substrate and clustering habit alone will not tell them apart.

| Feature | **Enoki** | **Hypholoma fasciculare (poisonous)** |
|:---|:---|:---|
| **Spore color** | **White to pale yellow** | **Dark grayish-brown to blackish-brown** |
| **Gill color** | **White to pale cream throughout** | **Starts sulfur-yellow → greenish-yellow → olive-brown → dark grayish-brown** |
| **Taste** | **Not bitter** | **Very bitter. You can tell the moment it touches your tongue** |
| Cap color | Yellow-brown to orange-tinted reddish-brown | Sulfur-yellow to yellow-brown, reddish-brown at the center |
| Cap surface | Slimy when wet | Smooth and dry |
| Stem base | Dark brown velvety hair | No hair. The whole stem is yellow to yellow-brown |
| Growing substrate | Rotting hardwood stumps | Same |
| Growth habit | Dense clusters | Same |

> **Hypholoma fasciculare is told apart by gill color and taste. If the gills are yellowish or olive-tinted, it is not enoki, and if it tastes bitter on the tongue, it is not enoki.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Tasting itself needs caution. Touch only a tiny piece to the tip of your tongue, taste it, and spit it out at once without swallowing. And never use this method on Amanita mushrooms. Amanita tastes good, so taste will not filter it out, and amatoxin damages the liver even in a tiny amount. Tasting is the last supporting check to use, only after a specimen has already passed the spore-color and substrate checks.

### 12-7. Other Look-Alikes

| Mushroom | How to tell it apart |
|:---|:---|
| **Hypholoma sublateritium** | Edible. Cap is brick-red, spores are dark grayish-brown. Hard to distinguish from Hypholoma fasciculare, so if unsure, do not eat it |
| **Pholiota species** | Cap has many coarse scales and some slime. Spores are brown. Mildly toxic, but the species are hard to tell apart |
| **Entoloma species** | Spores are pink. Grow from the ground and cause severe gastrointestinal poisoning |
| **Coprinus species** | The cap dissolves black over time. Causes poisoning if eaten with alcohol |
| **Coprinellus micaceus** | Grows in clusters on stumps too, but the cap is egg-shaped and dissolves quickly |
| **Panaeolus species** | Grow on manure piles and dung. Some species contain hallucinogenic compounds |

### 12-8. Cultivated Is Safest

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Reading this far makes it clear. Foraging and eating wild enoki is work for someone with substantial experience telling mushrooms apart. That is even more true in a disaster situation with no medical support.

> **In a disaster situation, the safest way to eat enoki is not to find it in the wild, but to grow it. If it grew from a bottle you inoculated yourself, the identification problem never arises in the first place.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Even so, cultivated enoki is not absolutely safe either. Airborne spores can settle on the substrate surface and let another mushroom come up alongside it. In particular, substrate that was insufficiently sterilized commonly produces Coprinus species. If a bottle produces mushrooms of differing shapes mixed together, discard all of the ones that look different.

### 12-9. Principles for Eating It the First Time

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Without medical support, the verification process itself is your defense.

| Order | What to do |
|:--:|:---|
| 1 | **Confirm it grew from wood.** Discard anything that grew from the ground |
| 2 | **Confirm it is clustered.** Discard a solitary one |
| 3 | **Check the stem for a ring or a cobweb trace.** Discard it if there is one |
| 4 | **Check the base for a sac.** Discard it if there is one |
| 5 | **Confirm the stem base is dark brown and velvety.** Discard it if not |
| 6 | **Take a spore print and check its color.** Discard it if rusty brown or dark grayish-brown |
| 7 | **Always cook it thoroughly.** Do not eat it raw |
| 8 | At first, **only one person eats a small amount (a single piece)** and waits 24 hours |
| 9 | If nothing is wrong, increase the amount. Do not have several people eat it at the same time |
| 10 | **Set aside** one specimen from what was eaten. It can be used to check in case of poisoning |

> **Poisoning that shows symptoms more than 6 hours later is the most dangerous kind.**
> Stomach pain within 30 minutes to 2 hours of eating is usually just gastric irritation, but vomiting and diarrhea that begin 6 to 24 hours later is the classic course of liver-destroying amatoxin poisoning. The later it begins, the more serious it is. There is often a period where the symptoms briefly ease and the person seems to be recovering, when in fact the liver is being destroyed. And Cortinarius species' orellanine does not show up as kidney symptoms until days to three weeks later, so even a problem that appears late should still be connected back to the mushroom.
