## 13. Storage Without Refrigeration

### 13-1. Fresh, It Doesn't Last Three Days

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;On the evaluation table, the button mushroom's storability is E, based on the raw product. Without power, at room temperature, that's 3-5 days. It's 90% water, and enzymes on the cap surface brown quickly on contact with air. In particular, **if it touches water, it turns brown within a day.**

| What not to do | Reason |
|:---|:---|
| **Store it washed** | It absorbs water and browns and softens right away |
| Seal in plastic | Condensation forms and it rots |
| Pile them up | The bottom ones get bruised from the weight and spoil starting there |
| Leave in the sun a long time (for eating fresh) | It browns. But for drying, this is used to the opposite advantage |

> **But drying changes the grade completely.**
> The exclusion ruling and the storability grade are both based on the unprocessed raw product. Drying uses almost no fuel or equipment, so in a disaster, the button mushroom's storability problem is essentially solved by drying alone. One of the two disqualifications behind its exclusion is resolved with nothing more than a knife and a drying rack.

### 13-2. Drying - Vitamin D Increases

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Drying the button mushroom has an advantage other mushrooms don't. When the mushroom's ergosterol is exposed to UV light, it converts to vitamin D2, so **the process of sun-drying is itself a process of nutritional fortification.** In a disaster where time spent indoors stretches on and oily fish is cut off, this is a large value that doesn't show up in any accounting.

| Method | Condition | Duration | Result |
|:---|:---|:--:|:---|
| **Sun-drying** | Slice 4-6mm thick and spread on a mat **with the gills facing up** | 1-2 clear days | Easiest, and **vitamin D increases substantially** |
| Shade air-drying | Under well-ventilated eaves | 3-5 days | Color changes less, but vitamin D doesn't increase |
| Stringing and hanging | Split in half, thread on a string, hang under the eaves | 4-7 days | Takes up less space |
| Drying by the fire | On a rack above the hearth, 40-50°C | Half a day | Usable during the monsoon season. Take care not to scorch it |

> **Vitamin D only increases if you place it with the gills facing up.** The gills are the part with the most ergosterol, and UV light has to hit that surface directly for the reaction to happen. Laying the cap face-down still dries it, but gives almost no fortification effect.

| Stage | Judgment |
|:---|:---|
| Fully dry | **It snaps with a crack when broken by hand.** If it bends, it's still not dry enough |
| Storage | After cooling completely, **in a sealed container with charcoal or roasted salt** |
| Duration | **A year or more** in a cool, dark place. Ground into powder, two years or more |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Slicing before drying matters. The button mushroom's cap is thick and its stem is fat, so drying it whole leaves the inside undried and lets mold grow. Always slice it 4-6mm thick before drying. And because browning progresses quickly once it's sliced, **spreading it out to dry the moment you slice it** keeps the color from changing as much.

### 13-3. Other Storage Methods

| Method | How to make it | Duration |
|:---|:---|:--:|
| **Mushroom powder** | Grind after full drying. Sieve and keep only the fine part | 2 years or more |
| **Salt-cured** | Blanch, drain, and layer with salt at 20% of the weight | 6 months-1 year |
| **Jangajji (soy-sauce pickle)** | Blanch and steep in soy sauce and vinegar | 3-6 months |
| **Fermented storage** | Submerge in 3% brine for lactic fermentation | 3-6 months |
| **Oil-preserved** | Blanch, squeeze out the water, and submerge in oil | 1-3 months |
| **Short-term pit storage** | Unwashed, wrapped in paper or dry cloth, in a 12-15°C pit | 5-7 days |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Blanching before drying or pickling is especially advantageous for the button mushroom. Because heat destroys the browning enzyme, blanching in boiling water for 2-3 minutes before drying keeps the color much brighter. Blanching with a little salt or vinegar added is even better. In exchange for a little fuel, the quality improves noticeably.

> **Mushroom powder is especially valuable in a disaster.**
> It takes up little space, adds umami to broths, and can be mixed into grain flour to add protein and vitamin D. Once dried pieces start crumbling, grinding them into powder is better than throwing them out.

## 14. Cooking and Processing

### 14-1. It's Better Cooked

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Among the six species this document covers, the button mushroom is the only one that can be eaten raw. The Cooking & Processing grade of A on the evaluation table reflects that. In a disaster, though, it's better cooked. There are three reasons. Without cooking, the nutrients inside the cell walls aren't absorbed. Raw mushrooms contain a small amount of agaritine, and reducing it with heat is the safer choice. And for wild specimens, even after passing the identification checks in Section 12, they must always be cooked.

| Method | Fuel | Time | Notes |
|:---|:--:|:--:|:---|
| Grilling | Little | 5-8 min | Can be set right on the embers. Open-capped ones are especially good grilled |
| Stir-frying | Little | 5-8 min | A little oil greatly improves the flavor |
| Soup/stew | Medium | Under 10 min | Adds umami to the broth |
| Blanching | Little | 2-3 min | For seasoned namul or as a first step before pickling |
| Raw | None | - | Cultivated ones only. Sliced thin and dressed with salt and oil |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Large specimens with an open cap have thick flesh and a meat-like chewiness. The fact that it's an ingredient that gives chewing satisfaction when protein is scarce is reflected in the evaluation table's Taste grade of S. Set whole on the embers with just a sprinkle of salt, it becomes a side dish for a meal on its own.

### 14-2. How to Use the Dried Kind

| Step | Method |
|:--:|:---|
| 1 | Soak in lukewarm water for 20-30 minutes to rehydrate |
| 2 | **Don't throw out the soaking water.** Umami and water-soluble nutrients have dissolved into it |
| 3 | Avoiding the sand settled at the bottom, use only the water on top as broth |
| 4 | Slice the rehydrated mushrooms and stir-fry them or add them to soup |
| 5 | Add the powder directly to broth or dough without rehydrating it |

### 14-3. Combinations That Make Up for Grain Shortages

| Combination | Effect |
|:---|:---|
| **Mushroom + grain porridge** | Mushroom protein makes up for grain's shortfall in lysine |
| **Mushroom powder + wheat or buckwheat flour** | Mixed into the dough for sujebi (hand-torn noodle soup) or kalguksu (knife-cut noodles) to add protein and vitamin D |
| **Mushroom + oil** | Since vitamin D is fat-soluble, eating it with oil improves absorption |
| **Mushroom + namul (seasoned greens)** | Their micronutrient makeups differ from each other |
| **Mushroom + potato or sweet potato** | Fill calories with the tuber crop and supply protein with the mushroom |

> **Mushrooms are properly used not as a calorie source but as a supplement for protein and micronutrients.**
> For the button mushroom, that point is especially clear. It's 15kcal and 3.12g of protein per 100g. Its protein share of calories, 83.2%, is the highest of the six species in this document. Filling calories with grain or tuber crops and adding protein and vitamin D with the button mushroom is the correct way to use it.

## 15. Annual Work Calendar

| Month | Task |
|:--:|:---|
| January | Gather rice straw and manure. Roast and crush shells and eggshells ahead of time. Repair frames and the growing house |
| **February** | **Start the spring cycle's first fermentation.** Pre-piling and main piling. Turn every 2-3 days |
| **March** | **Second fermentation and inoculation.** Pasteurize at 60°C for 6 hours. Check for ammonia, then inoculate spawn. Culture the mycelium |
| **April** | **Casing and fruiting.** 16-18°C. Open the vents to clear carbon dioxide. First harvest at month's end |
| **May** | **Peak harvest of the spring cycle.** 2-3 flushes. Sun-dry immediately. **Mushroom flies surge this month, so check the insect screens** |
| June | Wrap up the spring cycle. Work spent compost into the field. Wash the bed and growing house |
| July | Too hot to grow. Secure rice straw and manure. If you have a pit, keep it running |
| **August** | **Start the autumn cycle's first fermentation.** The hot season makes it heat up well |
| **September** | **Second fermentation and inoculation.** Culture the mycelium |
| **October** | **Casing and fruiting.** First harvest |
| **November** | **Peak harvest of the autumn cycle.** The best-quality period. Dry in bulk |
| December | Clear out spent compost. Check spore prints. Check the condition of dried mushrooms |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The key to this schedule is that **it staggers the fermentation season against the fruiting season.** Compost ferments well when it's hot, and mushrooms fruit well when it's cool. So fermentation happens in late winter and late summer, and harvest comes in spring and autumn. Midsummer and midwinter are rest seasons, and that gap is filled by wine cap and wood ear mushroom in summer, and enoki mushroom in winter.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;If you have a pit, the calculation changes. A pit stays at 12-18°C year-round, so it can run all year. But fermentation still has to happen outside, so the arrangement becomes fermenting the compost outdoors and bringing it into the pit afterward. In midwinter, fermentation doesn't go well outside, so build the pile bigger and cover it thickly with straw to hold in the heat.

### Never Forget

| Item | Details |
|:--:|:---|
| 1 | **Dig up the base of the stem and check there's no sac.** If there's a sac, it's an Amanita, and a single specimen can kill a person |
| 2 | **Cut one button open and look at the gill color.** Pale pink means button mushroom; pure white all the way means don't eat it |
| 3 | **If the spores are white, don't eat it.** The button mushroom's spores are brown to chocolate brown |
| 4 | **If the cut surface turns yellow and smells of medicine, don't eat it** |
| 5 | **Don't inoculate compost that still smells of ammonia.** The spawn will simply die |
| 6 | **Always steam the casing soil before use.** Dry bubble disease and mushroom flies come in through the soil |
| 7 | **Compost fermentation is itself sterilization.** Being the only mushroom that sterilizes its substrate without burning firewood is this mushroom's single biggest strength |
| 8 | **When sun-drying, place it with the gills facing up.** Vitamin D increases |
| 9 | **Drying makes the storability problem disappear.** Don't be misled by the raw-product E grade |
| 10 | **Because it doesn't photosynthesize, it's a food source that holds steady in a disaster with reduced sunlight.** The SS in Sunlight doesn't mean it uses light well — it means it's outside that axis entirely |
| 11 | **Even a failure leaves compost behind.** A safety net no other mushroom has |
