## 5. Inoculation Method

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Inoculating the button mushroom is simpler than for other mushrooms. Mix spawn into the compost, and you are done. No sterile room, no quick hands needed. That is because compost that has finished conditioning is already a selective substrate, tilted in the button mushroom mycelium's favor.

> **This is the most underrated advantage of growing button mushrooms. Enoki and lion's mane mushroom have to be inoculated within 10 seconds under near-sterile conditions, but the button mushroom can have spawn mixed into its compost with bare hands.**

### 5-1. Inoculation Conditions

| Item | Standard | Reason |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Compost temperature | **Cooled to 25°C or below** | Any hotter and the spawn dies |
| Ammonia | **There must be no smell at all** | If any remains, the spawn dies |
| Compost moisture | 68-72% | Should show water between the fingers when squeezed |
| Compost pH | pH 6.8-7.2 | Neutral |
| Inoculation rate | **1.5kg spawn per 125kg of compost per 3.3m² (1 pyeong)** | Field practice recommends 2.7-3.6kg |
| Without a sterile room | **Lean toward the upper end, 3.6kg** | Whoever claims the territory first wins |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Using a generous amount of spawn is the surest way to make up for the lack of sterile technique. 1.5kg per pyeong is the floor, and 3.6kg is the upper end recommended in the field. Without electricity or a sterile room, choose the upper end. Skimp to save on spawn, and green mold claims the territory first, costing you the whole batch.

### 5-2. Inoculation Procedure

| Step | Method |
|:--:|:---|
| 1 | Put your hand in to check the compost has cooled to 25°C or below |
| 2 | Bring your nose close to check there is no ammonia smell |
| 3 | Crumble the spawn block by hand into rice-grain-sized pieces |
| 4 | Scatter it evenly over the compost surface |
| 5 | Using your hands or a rake, **mix it evenly into the top 10-15cm layer of compost** |
| 6 | Level the surface evenly and press it down lightly |
| 7 | **Cover with paper, a damp cloth, or straw sacking** to hold in moisture |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The trick is to mix spawn only into the top layer, not the whole batch of compost. Mycelium spreads downward from the top, and mushrooms form near the top. Scattering spawn all the way to the bottom just wastes it.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The covering material needs to hold in moisture while still letting air through. Sealing it completely with plastic sheeting lets condensation drip and bacteria spread. Paper, damp straw sacking, matting, or a thin layer of straw all work well.

## 6. Mycelium Culture Management

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The 2-3 weeks after inoculation is the culturing period. The goal is to let white mycelium take over the whole batch of compost. During this time, the grower's job is mostly just controlling temperature and keeping it from drying out.

### 6-1. Temperature - Metabolic Heat Is the Biggest Danger

| Period | Room temperature | Management |
|:---|:---:|:---|
| Days 1-5 after inoculation | 25-27°C | Encourages mycelium to take hold. Keep it a little warm |
| After days 6-7 | **Lower to 20°C** | The mycelium's own metabolic heat makes the inside of the compost hotter |
| Danger period | **5-10°C below the ideal temperature** | Prevents the mycelium from being burned out by its own heat |
| Compost temperature ceiling | **30°C** | Above this the mycelium weakens, and it dies at 34 degrees |
| Duration | 2-3 weeks | Varies with the amount of compost and temperature |

> **Overheating is the most common failure in button mushroom culturing. Vigorously growing mycelium generates its own heat, so even at a room temperature of 25°C, the inside of the compost can top 30°C. Get in the habit of pushing your hand deep into the compost to check how warm it is.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There are three responses. Starting one week into culturing, lower the room temperature to 20°C. Keep the compost no thicker than 30cm. And gradually increase ventilation. If the inside feels hot, rake the surface lightly to work air in.

### 6-2. Humidity and Ventilation

| Item | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Compost moisture | Maintain 68-72% |
| Air humidity | 85-90% |
| Watering | **Avoid it as much as possible** during culturing. If the covering dries out, spray only the covering |
| Ventilation | Briefly, once or twice a day. A little buildup of carbon dioxide is good for the mycelium |
| Light | Not needed. Darkness is fine |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Never water the compost directly during culturing. Mycelium that gets waterlogged cannot breathe and dies. If water is needed, spray it only onto the paper or sacking covering, delivering it as vapor. Compost moisture is set once, when conditioning finishes, and during culturing the job is simply to maintain it.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Ventilation is kept light during mycelium culturing. Button mushroom mycelium actually likes some carbon dioxide — the exact opposite of the fruiting-body stage. Keep things closed up during culturing, and increase ventilation only after casing.

### 6-3. How to Tell Culturing Is Going Well

| Observation | Normal | Abnormal |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Color | White mycelium spreads, wrapping the straw | Blue-green, black, or orange means contamination |
| Texture | Fine white threads spread evenly over the straw surface | If it puffs up like powder, those are contaminant spores |
| Smell | A mild mushroomy, earthy-moldy smell | **If ammonia smells again, conditioning was insufficient** |
| Progress | 5-7cm downward per week | If it stalls, the problem is temperature or ammonia |
| Compost cohesion | Lifted by hand, it comes up as a clump bound together by mycelium | If it falls apart into loose bits, the mycelium has not spread yet |
| Temperature | 25-30°C inside the compost | Above 30°C, cool it immediately |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Sometimes the ammonia smell returns during culturing. This happens when conditioning did not fully clear the ammonia, or when a clump of nitrogen source was left bunched up inside the compost. When this happens, turn the compost lightly to work in air and increase ventilation. If it is severe, it is better to just give up on that section.

### 6-4. Casing

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Once mycelium has spread through the whole batch of compost, apply the casing. In growing button mushrooms, no mushrooms form without this step.

| Item | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Timing | After mycelium has reached all the way to the bottom of the compost. 2-3 weeks after inoculation |
| Verdict | The compost is evenly white on the surface and inside, and lifting it brings up a bound clump |
| Material | Leaf mold and sand mixed with lime, to pH 7.5 or above |
| Thickness | 2-3cm |
| Texture | Loose and crumbly. Fine soil alone packs hard, and mushrooms cannot push through it |
| Watering | Soak it thoroughly right after casing. Slowly, in several rounds |
| Follow-up management | Temperature 18-22°C, humidity 95% |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;After casing, mycelium climbs up into the soil. Within 5-7 days, white mycelium starts to show on the casing surface. At this point, lightly raking the soil to break up the mycelium (called ruffling) evens out fruiting. If a thick white mycelial film covers the surface, mushrooms actually fail to form, so the trick is to rake before that film forms.

## 7. Inducing Fruiting

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Left comfortable, mycelium just keeps spreading. Fruiting is a stress signal for the mycelium. For the button mushroom, that signal has three parts: casing, a drop in temperature, and venting out carbon dioxide.

### 7-1. Fruiting Conditions

| Item | Culturing stage | **Fruiting stage** | Notes |
|:---|:---:|:---:|:---|
| Temperature | 23-25°C | **16-18°C** | A narrow range. Below 12°C, primordia do not form |
| Air humidity | 85-90% | **85-95%** | Especially high in early fruiting |
| **Carbon dioxide** | High is fine | **Must be lowered** | **Ventilation is decisive** |
| Light | Not needed | Not needed | Forms even in total darkness |
| Casing | - | 2-3cm | Without it, no mushrooms form |

> **The key to inducing fruiting in the button mushroom is ventilation. After casing and lowering the temperature to 16-18°C, opening the vents that had stayed closed until then to clear out carbon dioxide is what makes mushrooms form. Mycelium likes carbon dioxide and fruiting bodies dislike it, so this switch is itself the signal.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;With a medium-to-warm-tolerant cultivar, the range widens to 13-20°C. If you cannot control temperature precisely without electricity, choosing this kind of cultivar works in your favor.

### 7-2. How to Reach 16-18°C Without Electricity

| Means | Temperature | How to secure it | Limits |
|:---|:---:|:---|:---|
| **Cave** | 12-18°C | Dig 2m or more into a hillside | Usable year-round, so it is the best option |
| **Spring crop** | Natural | Late March to May | Turns hot by June |
| **Fall crop** | Natural | September to November | Turns cold by December |
| Basement | 12-18°C | Use an existing structure | Humidity needs to be maintained separately |
| Semi-underground pit | 14-20°C | Dig 1m down and cover with earth and straw | Not enough in summer |
| North-facing earth-walled shed | Varies by season | Thick earth walls dampen temperature swings | Out of range in summer and winter |

> **Without electricity, the button mushroom is either a spring-and-fall crop or a cave crop. It is not grown in the height of summer or winter.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Running two crops a year, spring and fall, is the standard. For the spring crop, compost is made in February, inoculated in March, and harvested from late April. For the fall crop, compost is made in August, inoculated in September, and harvested from late October. Composting itself actually benefits from warmer weather, so the arrangement becomes: ferment in summer or late winter, and time fruiting for the cool season.

### 7-3. Water Management

| Period | Management |
|:---|:---|
| Right after casing | Thoroughly. Let it soak in slowly, in several rounds |
| During pinning | **Cut back on water or stop it.** Too much water now turns the primordia mushy |
| Once mushrooms reach bean size | Resume lightly. Just enough that the casing does not dry out |
| During development | A little between harvests |
| 2-3 days before harvest | **Stop watering.** Storability improves |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;How you water matters. Spraying hard packs the casing down hard and crushes young mushrooms. Use a watering can to mist it like fog, or flick water with a broad-leafed branch to scatter it. And instead of giving a lot at once, split it into several rounds.

### 7-4. Confirming Pinning

| Timing | What you see |
|:---|:---|
| 5-7 days after casing | White mycelium shows on the casing surface |
| 10-15 days after casing | **White pins the size of millet to adzuki beans** form beneath the casing |
| 5-7 days later | The pins swell to the size of beans to chestnuts |
| Harvest | The closed-cap button stage |

## 8. Harvest and Flush Cycles

### 8-1. Judging Harvest Timing

| Criterion | Details |
|:---|:---|
| **Optimal** | **The button stage, cap rounded and closed, before the veil tears** |
| Size | Cap diameter 2-4cm |
| Sign of lateness | The white veil between cap and stem tears, and the gills become visible |
| If picked late | The cap opens, the gills turn brown, and texture suffers |
| Time of day | Early morning |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There is no reason to discard ones picked late. With the cap open, it is called a portobello, with thick, meat-like flesh. It suits grilling and has a stronger umami flavor. But storability drops and it starts shedding spores, so it is best eaten that same day. And a specimen with a fully open cap and dark brown gills is the best choice for the spore collection described in Section 4.

> **The white button stage, the ideal time to harvest, is also when it most closely resembles the young stage of the deadly Amanita. Be sure to read Section 12, and pick only what grew on the bed you made yourself.**

### 8-2. Picking Technique

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Wrap your fingers around the cap and twist gently as you pull it out. Yanking straight up tears out the casing and snaps the roots of neighboring young mushrooms. A twisting pull does the least damage to what is around it.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Do not leave the spot where you picked as is. Clear away the leftover base and any clumped soil by hand, and fill it back in with fresh casing. A leftover base rots and draws in bacteria and mushroom flies.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;And when picking, **always pull out the whole thing, base and all**. As Section 12 shows, whether a volva is present can only be checked at the base, and that check separates life from death. The habit of cutting off only the part above the soil is dangerous.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;**Do not wash** harvested mushrooms. The button mushroom strongly dislikes water. Washed, it soaks up moisture and turns brown within a day. Brush off soil with a dry cloth or a soft brush.

### 8-3. Flush Cycle

| Item | Details |
|:---|:---|
| First harvest | 40-60 days after filling the bed with compost. 10-15 days after casing |
| Duration of one flush | 3-5 days. Comes up all at once, in a cluster |
| Interval between flushes | 7-14 days |
| Second-cycle yield | 50-70% of the first cycle |
| Third-cycle yield | 20-30% of the first cycle |
| Total harvest cycles | **Up to 3** |
| Bed lifespan | 2-3 cycles, about 2-3 months |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Button mushrooms come up in waves. They rise all at once, in a cluster, over three or four days, rest for 1-2 weeks, and then come up again. During the rest, the job is to keep the casing from drying out and to fill in the spots you picked from. Because so many come up at once, you can gather and dry them together in a single batch, which is a plus for storage.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This table is what Perenniality C on the evaluation table refers to. After two or three cycles, the compost's nutrients are spent and the mycelium ages. At that point, clear out the compost and put in fresh. Since composting takes 2-3 weeks, **the trick is to start fermenting the next cycle's compost ahead of time.** If you are turning the next pile outside while harvesting the current bed, you can keep going without a break.

### 8-4. Yield

| Condition | Yield |
|:---|:---|
| Compost per m² | 50-60kg |
| Total yield per m² | 10-20kg (sum of 3 cycles) |
| Biological efficiency | 20-35% of the compost's dry weight |
| Energy equivalent | 1,500-3,000kcal per m² (sum of 3 cycles) |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Over 3 cycles, m² produces 10-20kg. At 15kcal per 100g, that is 1,500 to 3,000kcal in energy — just over one person's daily needs. This is not a value to plan calories around.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Protein changes the picture. 15kg means 470g of protein. A person needs 50g to 60g of protein a day, so that is close to ten days' worth. And building the bed in 2 or 3 tiers doubles or triples the output from the same floor area.

> **When assessing button mushroom yield, calculate by bed area, not floor area. Build the shelving in 3 tiers, and 1m² of floor gives you 3m² of bed. In a tight space like a cave or basement, this structure makes a big difference.**

## 9. Contamination, Pests, and Diseases

### 9-1. Green Mold and Competing Fungi

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The most common reason mushroom growing fails in a disaster is not pests or disease — it is losing the substrate to a competing mold. The button mushroom has an advantage over other species because composting builds a selective substrate in advance, but if fermentation is insufficient, this line of defense collapses.

| Category | Button mushroom mycelium | Green mold |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Color | White, thread-like, wrapping the compost straw | White at first, soon **blue-green** |
| Texture | Fine white threads spread evenly | Fine and powdery, crumbly |
| Spread rate | 3-5mm a day | 10-30mm a day, **3-6 times faster** |
| Smell | Earthy, mushroomy | A musty smell, sometimes coconut-like |
| Border | Even, following the compost | Radiating outward from round spots |

> **Green mold has already spread before its color even shows.**
> The blue-green color appears at the spore stage, after reproduction has already begun. By that point, you should assume a substantial part of the substrate is already lost. Early identification goes by spread rate and texture, not color.

### 9-2. Diseases Especially Common in the Button Mushroom

| Disease | Symptoms | Cause | Response |
|:---|:---|:---|:---|
| **Dry bubble disease (Mycogone)** | Mushrooms deform into crushed white masses that ooze brown liquid | **When the casing soil was not sterilized** | Remove diseased specimens and the surrounding soil, and sprinkle salt or ash |
| **Green mold disease** | Compost and casing turn blue-green | Insufficient conditioning, poor hygiene | Remove the affected section |
| **Bacterial blotch (brown-spot disease)** | Brown, sunken spots appear on the cap | When water droplets sit on the cap too long | Reduce watering and increase ventilation |
| **Bacterial soft rot** | Fruiting bodies turn mushy and foul-smelling | Excess moisture, poor drainage | Stop watering and remove diseased specimens |
| **Cobweb disease** | A white, cobweb-like mold wraps around the mushrooms | Humidity too high, insufficient ventilation | Increase ventilation and sprinkle salt |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Dry bubble disease is a disease particular to the button mushroom. It comes in through the casing soil, so skipping the casing sterilization described in Section 3 makes it almost certain to appear. It also spreads through hands and tools, so do not touch healthy spots with hands that touched diseased ones, and carry the removed soil well away.

### 9-3. Six Ways to Prevent Contamination

| Method | Principle |
|:---|:---|
| **Do primary and secondary fermentation properly** | 60°C pasteurization kills pathogens, and conditioning removes competing microbes' food supply |
| **Always steam the casing soil before use** | Dry bubble disease and mushroom flies come in through the soil |
| **Raise the casing's pH to 7.5 or above** | Green mold prefers slightly acidic conditions |
| **Use spawn generously** | Whoever claims the territory first wins |
| **Do not water hard or heavily** | Water droplets left on the cap bring bacterial disease |
| **Fill in each harvested spot right away** | A rotting base draws bacteria and mushroom flies |

### 9-4. Pests - Mushroom Flies Come First

| Pest | Damage | Response |
|:---|:---|:---|
| **Mushroom flies (sciarid flies and phorid flies)** | Larvae eat mycelium and infest fruiting bodies with maggots. Carry green mold spores and dry bubble disease | **Block them physically by covering doorways and vents with fine cloth or insect screening.** If they appear, use water-dish traps, sticky traps, or light traps |
| **Mites** | Mycelium turns brown and stops growing. Causes itchy skin in people | Steam the casing soil before use. Isolate affected sections |
| **Nematodes** | Mycelium dissolves away and the compost turns black and wet | Make sure pasteurization reaches 60°C. If they appear, discard the compost |
| **Slugs** | Eat young mushrooms | Ring the area with a band of ash or sawdust |
| **Rodents** | Carry off mushrooms and disturb the bed | Cover the bed with netting |

> **Mushroom flies are controlled with structure, not chemicals.** Without pesticides, the only reliably effective method is blocking the openings they get in through. Cover vents with two layers of fine cloth and hang a damp cloth like a curtain over doorways, and that blocks most of them. Numbers rise sharply from May, so take special care with the spring crop.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Hygiene is half of pest control. Piling spent compost next to the growing house turns it into a breeding ground for mushroom flies. Move spent compost far away right away, working it into a field or covering it over.
