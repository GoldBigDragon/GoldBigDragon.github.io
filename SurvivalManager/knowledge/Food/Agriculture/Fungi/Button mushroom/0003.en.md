## 3. Substrate Materials and Preparation

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The button mushroom's substrate is entirely different in character from the other five species. It is not sawdust packed into bottles and boiled; it is **fermented compost**, made by stacking rice straw and manure so the pile heats itself.

> **Other mushrooms sterilize their substrate by burning firewood; the button mushroom sterilizes its substrate with heat that microbes produce. People do not light a fire — they only turn the pile. It is the only sterilization method that needs neither electricity nor fuel.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Composting does three things. First, the temperature inside the pile reaches 60-70°C, killing pathogens, pest eggs, and weed seeds. Second, thermophilic microbes eat up the easy sugars that competing fungi would otherwise use, so what remains is a selective substrate of hard-to-break-down organic matter that only button mushroom mycelium can digest. Third, the straw softens and nitrogen is fixed into microbial protein, converted into a form the button mushroom can eat.

### 3-1. Compost Materials

| Category | Standard mix | Disaster-scenario substitute |
|:---|:---|:---|
| **Carbon source** | Rice straw, wheat straw, barley straw 80% | Wild grasses, dried grass, corn stalks, reeds, rice hulls, old paper |
| **Nitrogen source** | Chicken manure, urea, ammonium sulfate, rice bran | **Horse manure**, cattle manure, pig manure, aged urine, bean pods, oil cake |
| **Minerals** | Gypsum (calcium sulfate), zeolite | **Roasted, ground shells**, eggshell powder, wood ash, slaked lime |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The traditional nitrogen source for button mushroom compost was horse manure. It already contains undigested straw, so it gives a good carbon-to-nitrogen ratio. If you keep livestock, horse or cattle manure is easier to handle than chicken manure, and human urine, aged a few days and poured over straw, also works as a nitrogen source.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Gypsum (calcium sulfate) is not used as a nutrient source. The calcium keeps straw fibers from matting together, which stops the compost from clumping, buffers acidity, and supplies sulfur. Roasting and grinding shells, oyster shells, or eggshells serves the same purpose. Sulfur is not supplied that way, though, so a little wood ash is mixed in as well.

| Standard | Value | Notes |
|:---|:---:|:---|
| C/N ratio | Starts at **28-32 to 1**, finishes at 16-18 to 1 | Straw alone runs above 70 to 1, so nitrogen must be added |
| Chicken manure per 100kg rice straw | 10-15kg | 30-50kg if using horse manure |
| Rice bran per 100kg rice straw | 5kg | |
| Urea per 100kg rice straw | 400-500g | 10-15L if substituting urine |
| Gypsum (calcium sulfate) per 100kg rice straw | 3-5kg | Can be replaced with ground shells |

### 3-2. Primary Fermentation - Outdoor Pile, 2-3 Weeks

| Step | Method | Duration | Temperature |
|:---|:---|:---:|:---:|
| **Pre-wetting** | Soak the rice straw thoroughly with water. Add **at least 70% of the total water** at this point to soften the straw | 1-2 days | - |
| **Main stacking** | Layer chicken manure, rice bran, urea, and gypsum with the straw. Shape the cross-section like a **turtle shell** | 1 day | Initial heating |
| **Turning** | Turn **5-8 times**, every 2-3 days. Swap the outside and inside | 2-3 weeks | 55-70 degrees inside the pile |

| Item | Standard | Reason |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Pile size | Height 1.5-1.8m, width 1.5-2m, no limit on length | Too low and heat does not build up; too high and the inside rots from lack of oxygen |
| Cross-section shape | A turtle shell, slightly rounded on top | Rain runs off the outside instead of soaking in |
| Target temperature | 55-70 degrees inside the pile | Should be too hot to hold your hand in |
| Moisture | A squeezed handful should release a few drops of water between the fingers | Too dry and it will not heat up |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Fermentation is an aerobic process that consumes oxygen. Once the oxygen inside the pile is used up, heating stops and the pile slides into anaerobic decay. Turning brings in fresh oxygen while moving the outer parts that never got hot into the center, so everything cooks evenly. It is not simple mixing — you **lift the outer straw and place it in the middle of the new pile**.

| Fermentation signal | Normal | Abnormal |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Temperature | Above 55°C within 2-3 days of stacking, then holds | If it does not rise, nitrogen or moisture is lacking |
| Smell | A strong smell of ammonia | A sewage or hydrogen-sulfide smell means anaerobic decay |
| Color and straw | Yellow-brown to dark brown. Breaks easily when rubbed | Black and slimy means too much water |
| White powder | Actinomycete spores puff up like white smoke when you turn the pile | A good sign |

> **The smell of ammonia is normal during primary fermentation — it comes from nitrogen being transferred to the microbes. The problem is if it is still there after secondary fermentation ends.**

### 3-3. Secondary Fermentation (Post-Fermentation) - Indoors, 5-7 Days

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Compost that has finished primary fermentation is moved into the bed and fermented once more. This stage is, in effect, pasteurization.

| Step | Method | Temperature | Purpose |
|:---|:---|:---:|:---|
| **Filling the bed** | Quickly fill the bed frame with compost at **50-60kg per m²** | - | Work fast so the compost does not lose its own heat |
| **Pasteurization** | Raise the temperature inside the growing house to 60°C and hold for **6 hours** | 60°C | **Kills pests, diseases, and competing microbes** |
| **Conditioning** | Compost at 50-55°C for 2-3 days, then 48-50°C for 1-2 days, finishing at 45 degrees | 45-55°C | **Activates actinomycetes, removes ammonia** |
| **Completion check** | The ammonia smell is completely gone and the compost surface is covered with **dense white actinomycetes** | - | The right time to inoculate with spawn |

#### 3-3-1. How to Reach 60 Degrees for Pasteurization Without Electricity

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Commercial growers raise the growing house to 60 degrees with boiler steam. Without electricity or fuel, the answer is to use the compost's own heat.

| Method | Description | Notes |
|:---|:---|:---|
| **Using its own heat** | Move the compost into the bed hot, while primary fermentation is still going strong, and cover the bed with straw sacking or matting to trap the heat | The most basic method. The inside of the compost is already above 60°C |
| **Thick pile, small room** | Fill the bed 25-30cm deep. In a small room or a cave, the compost's own heat warms the space | A thin layer or a large space lets it cool |
| **Using an ondol floor** | If you have an ondol floor-heating structure, light the firebox to warm the floor | The most reliable option, but it uses fuel |
| **Choosing a sunny day** | A clear day in May-June or September-October. Covering with plastic sheeting adds solar heat | Pick the season |

> **If you cannot hold 60°C for 6 hours for pasteurization, switch to 55°C for 12 hours, or 50°C for 24 hours. Temperature and time can be traded off against each other — at a lower temperature, just hold it longer.**

#### 3-3-2. If Ammonia Remains, the Spawn Dies

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This is the single most important goal of conditioning. Button mushroom mycelium is highly sensitive to ammonia — if more than 0.1% remains in the compost, the inoculated spawn simply dies.

| Check | Verdict |
|:---|:---|
| **Smell** | There should be no sharp ammonia smell at all |
| **White actinomycetes** | Done when a fine white layer evenly covers the compost surface and interior |
| **Color and straw condition** | Dark brown to chocolate. Breaks with a light pull of the hand |
| **Moisture and pH** | 68-72%, pH 6.8-7.2, neutral |

> **If any ammonia smell remains, never inoculate.** Increase ventilation and ferment for a few more days. Failing to follow this one rule is the most common cause of failure in button mushroom growing.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Ventilation is the key to removing ammonia. During conditioning, ventilate briefly and often, 10 to 15 minutes at a time. Leave it open too long and the compost cools and fermentation stops; never open it at all and the ammonia never clears.

### 3-4. The Growing House and Bed - Structures Built Without Electricity

| Item | Standard | Disaster-scenario alternative |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Growing house | An enclosed space that can hold 16-25°C | **A cave, basement, north-facing shed, semi-underground pit, or earth-walled shed** |
| Bed | Shelf frames 1-1.2m wide, holding compost 20-30cm deep, in 2-4 tiers | Built from logs, planks, and bamboo |
| Floor and vents | A draining floor, with vents top and bottom | A gravel floor. Natural ventilation by the chimney effect |
| Light | **Not needed** | Grows even in total darkness |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;A cave is the growing space that best suits the button mushroom. It holds 12 to 18 degrees year-round, stays humid, has no light, and barely varies in temperature — meeting the requirements almost exactly as they are. Just dig 2m or more into a hillside, support the ceiling with wood, and cover it with earth.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Leave one vent below and one above. Warm inside air escapes through the top while cool air enters from below, giving natural convective ventilation with no fan needed. Because the button mushroom dislikes carbon dioxide while its fruiting bodies are growing, this structure matters.

### 3-5. Casing Materials

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Casing is the soil layer applied on top of the compost, and it is a step that cannot be skipped in growing button mushrooms. Mycelium feeds on the compost, but fruiting bodies form at the boundary between the casing and compost layers, so without casing the mycelium just forms a white film on the surface and produces no mushrooms.

| Item | Standard | Disaster-scenario substitute |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Material | Peat moss and decomposed granite soil mixed with soil 1 to 1 | **Leaf mold + sand + lime**, or paddy soil + rice hulls + ground shells |
| pH and thickness | **pH 7.5 or above**, 2-3cm | Mix in wood ash or slaked lime to raise pH |
| Texture | **Must be loose and crumbly** | Fine soil alone packs hard, and young mushrooms cannot push through it |
| Sterilization | Steamed for several hours above 60°C before use | Steam it in a cauldron, or spread it on a griddle and roast it |

> **Raising the casing's pH to 7.5 or above matters. Button mushroom mycelium tolerates neutral to slightly alkaline conditions, while green mold prefers slightly acidic ones. Making the casing alkaline tips the competition in the mycelium's favor. Without lime, dissolve wood ash in water, let it sit a day, and use the water off the top — or mix the ash straight into the soil.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Sterilizing the casing soil is easy to skip, but it matters. Soil carries mushroom fly eggs, mites, and dry bubble disease spores, so skipping it brings disease right back into compost you worked hard to sterilize. Add a little water to the soil in a cauldron and steam it for an hour, or spread it thin and roast it on a griddle. It does not need to be fully sterile — reducing pests and pathogens is enough.

## 4. Obtaining Spawn

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Mushrooms have no seeds. If the seed shop closes, there is nowhere to buy spawn, and the old trick of saving seed in a jar does not apply either. On the evaluation table, the button mushroom scores C for seed saving and D for seed longevity, meaning sterile culturing is required and commercial spawn loses vigor within six months to a year — and on top of that, raising a strain from spores is trickier for the button mushroom than for other mushrooms. But fungi have a path plants do not.

### 4-1. Why the Button Mushroom's Spores Are Special

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In an ordinary mushroom, a single spore germinates into primary mycelium carrying only half the genes, and two primary mycelia of different mating types must meet and fuse into secondary mycelium before a mushroom can form. Primary mycelium that never fuses just keeps spreading, loses vigor, and dies out.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The button mushroom is different. Each basidium carries not four spores but only **two**, and those spores often already hold two nuclei of different mating types. In other words, a single spore is born already equivalent to secondary mycelium, so a mushroom can grow from just one spore.

| Item | Ordinary mushroom | Button mushroom |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Spores per basidium | 4 | **2** |
| Spore nuclei | Single nucleus (half the genes) | **Usually two nuclei (full genes)** |
| Condition to become a mushroom | Two spores of different mating types must meet | **Possible from a single spore** |
| Diversity and germination | High diversity, relatively high germination rate | **Almost no diversity, low germination rate** |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Working from a single spore looks convenient, but it comes at a cost. With no genetic diversity, vigor declines over successive generations, and because germination is low, sowing spores often fails.

> **That is why, when shaking button mushroom spores onto a sterile medium, you do not carefully drop in a single spore — given the low germination rate, you shake in a generous cloud of spore powder all at once.**

### 4-2. Collecting Spores and Making a Spore Print

| Step | Method |
|:---|:---|
| Choosing a specimen | A large one with **a fully open cap and gills stained dark brown** |
| Cutting the stem | Cut the stem off close, right where it meets the cap |
| Base and cover | Set it gills-down on **aluminum foil** and cover it with a bowl |
| Waiting and collecting | Leave in a cool place for 24 hours. Lift the cap away and **brown to chocolate-brown** spore powder remains |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Button mushroom spores are brown to chocolate brown. That color is information for preserving spawn, but it is also **the key marker for confirming safety**. As Section 12 shows, the deadly Amanita species have white spores, so the single act of taking a spore print filters out the most dangerous misidentification.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Aluminum foil is better than paper. Paper absorbs moisture, and contaminants hide between its fibers.

### 4-3. Preserving Spores - 10 Years Without Electricity

| Step | Method |
|:--:|:---|
| 1 | Lay out foil and leave the mushroom cap face-down on it for a full day to collect spores |
| 2 | Leave the foil in an airy **shaded spot** for one more day to drive off even trace moisture |
| 3 | Fold in half, protecting the spore side on the inside, and place in a zip-top bag |
| 4 | Add **well-roasted salt or a piece of charcoal** to trap moisture |
| 5 | Gather the bags in a glass jar, **seal it with beeswax**, and store it in a **cave or basement** where temperature stays steady year-round and no light gets in |

| Storage form | Lifespan | Notes |
|:---|:---:|:---|
| Spore print (dried on foil) | **5-10 years or more** | With moisture excluded, cell activity stops |
| Liquid spawn (suspended in sterile water) | 3-6 months | Without refrigeration, it germinates early or bacteria multiply |
| Ready-to-inoculate spawn | 6 months-1 year | Living mycelium, so it keeps consuming nutrients |

> **In a disaster, do not store spores in liquid form. Keep them wrapped in foil instead.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;UV light destroys spore DNA, so it needs to be somewhere deep where no light reaches. The difference in lifespan between spores and plant seeds comes from their structure.

| Comparison | Tomato seed | Mushroom spore |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Average lifespan (room temperature) | About 2-4 years | About 5-10 years or more |
| Vulnerability | Temperature swings, depletion of its own nutrient reserves | Mold contamination from moisture |
| Cell structure | A complex embryo and nutrient tissue | A single cell with a thick protective wall |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;A seed carries an embryo and its own nutrients, so over time it consumes them and ages to death, but a mushroom spore is only a single cell wrapped in a thick wall, with no reserves to consume.

> **The seed longevity D on the evaluation table is based on commercial spawn. Store spores wrapped in foil, and the grade becomes, in effect, SS. In the first year of a disaster, take spore prints on foil from your largest, healthiest specimens, make 20-30 of them, and store them in a cave. Even if you lose all your spawn, you can start over five or ten years later.**

### 4-4. Tissue Culture - The Most Reliable Method for the Button Mushroom

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Because spore germination is low in the button mushroom, tissue culture matters especially. The white flesh inside, where the cap meets the stem, is already a lump of secondary mycelium with mating-type fusion complete, so mycelium spreads outward in every direction immediately, with no need to go through spore germination at all.

| Step | Method |
|:---|:---|
| Selecting | **A fresh button whose cap is just about to open.** Do not use old or worm-eaten ones |
| Disinfecting | Heat the knife over a flame and let it cool, or dip it in boiling water and remove it |
| Splitting | **Split it lengthwise by hand.** Cutting with a knife drags contaminants from the surface into the interior |
| Collecting and inoculating | Take a rice-grain-sized piece of the white inner flesh where cap meets stem, and tuck it into boiled, cooled rice straw or compost |
| Culturing | A dark spot at 23-25°C. Within 3-5 days, white mycelium spreads around the piece |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Splitting by hand matters because the inside of a mushroom is nearly sterile. All sorts of spores sit on the outer skin and gills, but the flesh inside is clean. Splitting it by hand exposes that clean surface fresh, so the piece you take is pure mycelium even without disinfecting it.

### 4-5. The Boiled Rice-Straw Method - Raising Spawn from Spores or Tissue

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In theory, shaking spores onto a sterile medium lets mycelium spread, but in practice, airborne green mold or bacteria colonize the medium hundreds of times faster than mushroom spores can. The richer a medium's nutrients, the more vulnerable it is to contamination, so without electricity, a primitive medium that relies on nature's own defenses works better.

| Step | Method |
|:---|:---|
| Boiling and wringing | Sterilize rice straw, wheat straw, or bean pods by **boiling for at least 1 hour**, then wring out the water and let cool |
| Inoculating | Sprinkle spore powder evenly and generously, or tuck a tissue-culture piece between the straws |
| Covering | **Cover with a clean cloth** to keep out drafts and stray spores |
| Culturing | 2-4 weeks in a dark spot at 23-25°C. Success is white mycelium covering the straw |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Straw, being low in nutrients, is also less attractive to green mold, so even slow-growing mycelium is not pushed out of the competition. The mass of mycelium obtained this way is then transferred to grain or fermented compost and grown into spawn.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The button mushroom has an advantage here. Because its main substrate is fermented compost, the same compost can be scooped out to expand spawn, and since it is already a pasteurized, selective substrate, it is less vulnerable to contamination. Scoop a handful of compost into a small jar, add a tissue-culture piece, and that jar becomes a spawn incubator.

### 4-6. Bed Transfer - The Easiest Way to Carry Spawn Forward

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Seed saving scores C on the evaluation table, but in practice, spawn can be carried forward with no sterile technique at all, simply by transferring compost the mycelium has already colonized.

| Step | Method |
|:---|:---|
| Timing | Right after mycelium has spread through the whole batch of compost, before casing |
| Source | Scoop out a fist-sized chunk where the white mycelium is dense and shows no trace of contamination |
| Transferring | Bury the chunks in freshly conditioned compost at 20-30cm intervals |
| Ratio | 3-5% of the new compost's weight |
| Limit | **Vigor declines past four or five generations.** Periodically go back to tissue culture |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Declining vigor over successive generations is a real weakness of the button mushroom. After five or six transfers, yield drops noticeably, so pick the best specimen and start fresh with tissue culture, or use a spore print you have saved.

### 4-7. Priority Order for Obtaining Spawn

| Priority | Method | Condition |
|:--:|:---|:---|
| 1 | Commercial spawn | With prevalence S, this is easiest in normal times. Secure it in advance |
| 2 | Tissue culture | When you have a fresh mushroom. The most reliable method for the button mushroom |
| 3 | Bed transfer | When you have compost the mycelium has already colonized. The fastest method |
| 4 | Spore print | When everything else is lost. Use generously, since germination is low |
