## 10. Diagnosing Growth Problems

| Symptom | Cause | Action |
|:---|:---|:---|
| No mycelium visible even a week after inoculation | **Ammonia remained in the compost**, or the compost was too hot when inoculated | Condition it longer, or turn it again to add air. Always check for ammonia smell |
| Mycelium stops spreading | Compost temperature above 30°C, insufficient moisture | Lower the room temperature to 20°C and rake the compost surface to release heat |
| Mycelium is fine but no mushrooms form | No casing applied, insufficient ventilation, temperature too high | Apply casing, open the vents to clear carbon dioxide, then lower to 16-18°C |
| A thick white film covers the casing surface | Mycelium is too vigorous. Excess humidity and carbon dioxide | Lightly rake to break up the mycelium and increase ventilation |
| Primordia form, then dry out and die | Insufficient humidity, casing has dried out | Mist with water to raise humidity to 85-95% |
| Primordia form, then turn mushy and die | Watered too much, too hard | Reduce watering and ventilate |
| Long stem, small cap | **Excess carbon dioxide.** Insufficient ventilation | Open the vents and exchange the air |
| Cap opens flat right away and is thin | Temperature 22°C or above | Move to a cooler spot |
| Cap surface cracks roughly | Air is too dry | Spray water on the floor and walls to raise humidity |
| Brown sunken spots on the cap | Bacterial blotch. Water droplets left on the cap | Reduce watering and ventilate to dry the cap |
| Mushrooms deform into crushed white masses | **Dry bubble disease.** Comes in through the casing soil | Remove diseased specimens and the surrounding soil, and be sure to steam the casing soil next cycle |
| Maggots in the mushroom stem | Mushroom flies | Block vents and doorways. Remove affected specimens |
| Cap discolors yellow | Overripe, excess temperature | Harvest on time |
| Almost no yield in the second flush | Compost nutrients exhausted | Normal. Replace the compost |

## 11. Recycling Spent Substrate

### 11-1. Spent Compost Is Not Waste

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Compost left after the mushrooms are done is organic matter that has already been fermented twice and further broken down once by mycelium. Among the six species this document covers, **the button mushroom is the one whose spent substrate makes the best fertilizer.** Spent sawdust substrate still has woody material in it, so working it into a field robs it of nitrogen, but spent button mushroom compost already has a low carbon-to-nitrogen ratio and can be applied as is.

| Use | Method |
|:---|:---|
| **Base fertilizer** | Spread over the field and till it in. Contains nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, and calcium in balanced amounts |
| **Mulch** | Lay 5cm thick between crops to block weeds and moisture evaporation |
| **Casing material** | Sieve it and mix it into the next cycle's casing. Always steam it again before use |
| **Substrate for other mushrooms** | Mixed into wine cap's wood-chip substrate, it serves as a nitrogen source |
| **Seedbed soil** | Mixed with sand, it becomes potting soil for raising seedlings |
| **Earthworm bedding** | Earthworms' favorite substrate. Yields worm castings |
| **Acidic soil amendment** | Contains gypsum and lime, which raise the pH of acidic soil |

```
Rice straw + manure + shells → fermented compost → mushroom harvest → spent compost → field fertilizer
                                                                      └ earthworms → worm castings
```

> **Growing button mushrooms is, in effect, the same process as making compost. Harvesting mushrooms is just something that happens once along the way. Even if you get no mushrooms, the compost remains. This is a safety net no other mushroom has.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This perspective matters in a disaster. With other mushrooms, a failure means the sawdust and firewood were spent for nothing. With the button mushroom, even a failure leaves compost to put on the field. Because the inputs never disappear entirely, it's the lowest-risk mushroom farming there is.

### 11-2. Cautions When Reusing Spent Compost

| Situation | Judgment |
|:---|:---|
| Compost that finished harvest normally | Apply it to the field as is |
| **Compost contaminated with green mold** | **Do not apply it to the field.** Burn it, or bury it deep far away from the growing area |
| **Compost and casing that had dry bubble disease** | Same as above. Spores survive in the soil for several years |
| Compost infested with mushroom flies | Pile it again to let it heat up and kill the eggs before using it |

> **Do not put contaminated substrate back into compost.** Green mold and dry bubble disease spores survive in the compost pile and come right back into next year's substrate.

### 11-3. Year-to-Year Management of the Growing Space

| Year | Management |
|:--:|:---|
| Every cycle | Empty the bed frame, wash it with water, and dry it in the sun |
| 1 year | Fine to keep using the same growing house |
| 2-3 years | Coat the walls and floor with lime-sulfur solution or strong wood-ash lye |
| If problems build up | Leave the growing house empty for a season and open the doors to let in sunlight and wind |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Because the frame is wood, pathogens seep into it. Wash it with water when a cycle ends, and if possible, douse it with hot water or scorch the surface with fire. If you're using bamboo or planks, it's better to replace them every few years.

## 12. Poisonous Look-Alikes and Safety Checks

> **This section is a matter of life and death. Confirming that a mushroom is safe to eat matters more than growing it.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Among the six species this document covers, the button mushroom is **the one with the most reported actual deaths from being mistaken for a deadly poisonous mushroom.** It happens again and again, in South Korea and abroad. The reason is that three conditions overlap.

| Overlapping condition | Details |
|:---|:---|
| **It grows out of soil** | The button mushroom pushes up through the casing soil, and Amanita species also grow from the ground |
| **It's picked at the white button stage** | The right time to harvest is when it's a closed white egg shape, and young Amanita specimens are also a white egg shape |
| **It has a ring** | Both the button mushroom and Amanita species have a white membranous ring on the stem |

> **The one and only thing that separates the button mushroom from the fool's mushroom is the volva at the base. If you don't check for it, sooner or later you will eat the wrong one.**

### 12-1. Identifying Traits of the Button Mushroom

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Even in a cultivated bed, wild spores can get mixed into the casing soil, and other mushrooms can come up alongside it. **Growing on your bed is not, by itself, proof of safety.** Check each of the traits below one by one.

| Part | Button mushroom |
|:---|:---|
| Cap | 3-10cm across. Opens from hemispherical to flat. White, cream, or brown |
| Cap surface | Smooth or finely fibrous. No slime layer |
| **Gills** | **Pale pink at first → brown as it grows → deepen to blackish brown** |
| **Spore color** | **Brown to chocolate brown.** Confirmed at once with a spore print on white paper |
| Stem | Short and thick. 3-7cm long, 1-2cm thick |
| **Ring on the stem** | **Present.** The white membrane that covered the cap when young tears and remains on the stem |
| **Base of the stem** | **No volva (no sac at the base).** The base simply sits buried in the compost |
| Flesh | White. When cut, it slowly changes **from pale pink to brown** |
| Smell | Mushroomy. No unpleasant odor |
| Growing substrate | The boundary between fermented compost and casing soil |

> **To check the gills, you have to cut one open.** At the button stage, the gills are hidden inside the cap and can't be seen. If the gills on the cut surface are pale pink, it's a button mushroom; if they're pure white all the way, suspect an Amanita. This one action decides life and death.

### 12-2. Fool's Mushroom and Destroying Angel - the Volva Is the Dividing Line

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Fool's mushroom (Amanita verna) and the destroying angel (Amanita virosa) are deadly poisonous mushrooms containing amatoxins. A single specimen's worth is enough to kill a person. There is no antidote, and without medical support, death is nearly certain.

| Feature | **Button mushroom** | **Fool's mushroom / destroying angel (deadly)** |
|:---|:---|:---|
| **Base of stem** | **No volva** | **A membranous sac (volva) wraps the base** |
| **Gill color** | **Pale pink → brown → blackish brown** | **Pure white from start to finish** |
| **Spore color** | **Brown to chocolate brown** | **White** |
| Cap color | White, cream, brown | Pure white. The destroying angel is slightly yellowish at the center |
| Stem | Short and thick. 3-7cm | **Long and slender. 9-20cm** |
| Stem surface | Smooth | **The destroying angel is rough with white fibrous scales** |
| Ring | Present | Present. **Not distinguishable by this** |
| Flesh color change | Pink to brown when cut | Stays white, unchanged |
| Smell | Mushroomy | Older specimens smell foul |
| Growing substrate | Fermented compost and casing | **Soil near the roots of living trees** |
| Growth habit | In clusters on the bed | Scattered singly or a few at a time in forests |

> **Make it a habit to dig up the base along with the soil around it to check.**
> Checking for a volva must be done by digging. If you only look at the part above ground, the sac stays buried and invisible. Because the button mushroom is an easy mushroom to just cut off above the soil, this habit matters especially here. This is exactly why Section 8 says to always pull it out whole, base and all.

> **If you lay the cap gill-side down on white paper, cover it with a cup, leave it for a few hours, and the powder that falls is white, never eat it.**
> The button mushroom's spores are brown to chocolate brown. If the spores are white, if the gills stay white all the way, or if there's a sac at the base of the stem — if even one of those three applies, do not eat it.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;You need to know how amatoxin poisoning progresses. For 6-24 hours after eating it, there are no symptoms at all. Then severe vomiting, diarrhea, and abdominal pain begin and continue for about a day. After that comes a period of two to three days when symptoms ease and the person seems to be recovering — and it's during exactly that period that the liver is being destroyed. By the time jaundice, bleeding, and a decline in consciousness appear, it's already hard to do anything.

> **The most dangerous poisoning is the kind whose symptoms take more than 6 hours to appear.** Stomach pain within 30 minutes to 2 hours of eating is usually just gastric irritation, but vomiting and diarrhea that start 6-24 hours later are the classic course of amatoxin poisoning. The later it starts, the more serious it is.

### 12-3. A Poisonous Species in the Same Genus - the One Whose Wound Turns Yellow

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There are also species in the same genus as the button mushroom, Agaricus, that must not be eaten — the group that discolors yellow. In South Korea, this group is known as the Agaricus xanthodermus complex (yellow-stainer).

| Feature | **Button mushroom** | **Yellow-stainer group (Agaricus xanthodermus, gastrointestinal toxin)** |
|:---|:---|:---|
| **Wound discoloration** | **Pale pink to brown** when cut | **Turns bright yellow when cut or rubbed,** especially at the base |
| **Smell** | Mushroomy | **A foul smell like ink, carbolic acid, or medicine.** Gets stronger when cooked |
| Spore color | Brown to chocolate brown | Brown. Not distinguishable by this |
| Gill color | Pale pink → brown | White → pink → brown. Similar |
| Growing substrate | Fermented compost and casing | Grassy areas, gardens, roadside ground |

> **If the base turns yellow when rubbed and smells like medicine, do not eat it.** It won't kill you, but it brings severe vomiting and diarrhea. Without medical support, dehydration alone is dangerous.

### 12-4. Other Look-Alikes

| Mushroom | Distinguishing point |
|:---|:---|
| **Field mushroom (wild, edible)** | A wild relative of the button mushroom, and edible. Grows in grassy areas. Still needs to be distinguished from Amanita species, so follow the same checking procedure |
| **Amanita virgineoides** | Has white spine-like projections on the cap and a volva at the base |
| **Destroying angel button (egg stage)** | **A white egg shape, the closest look-alike to a button mushroom.** Cut open, it has a mushroom shape inside, with a gel layer between the outer skin and the interior |
| **Puffballs** | Cut open, the inside is evenly white and cottony. No gills. Young ones are edible, but if a mushroom shape is visible inside, it's an Amanita |
| **Inky caps** | The cap dissolves into black liquid over time. Causes poisoning if eaten with alcohol |

> **If you find a white, egg-shaped mushroom, always cut it open lengthwise.** If the inside is evenly white and cottony, it's a puffball; if a folded mushroom shape is inside, it's an Amanita button. An Amanita button has a translucent gel layer just under the outer skin, which feels slippery to the touch.

### 12-5. Rules for the First Time You Eat It

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Without medical support, the checking procedure itself is your line of defense.

| Step | Details |
|:--:|:---|
| 1 | **Dig up the base along with the soil and check there's no sac.** If there's a sac, discard it |
| 2 | **Cut one open lengthwise and look at the gill color.** Pale pink means button mushroom; pure white all the way means discard it |
| 3 | **Watch whether the cut surface turns yellow.** If it turns yellow and smells of medicine, discard it |
| 4 | **Take a spore print and check the color.** If white, discard it |
| 5 | **Always cook it thoroughly.** It's a mushroom that can be eaten raw, but cook wild specimens |
| 6 | The first time, have **only one person eat a small amount (one piece)** and wait 24 hours |
| 7 | If nothing is wrong, increase the amount. Do not have several people eat it at the same time |
| 8 | **Set aside** one specimen of what was eaten. It can be used for identification if poisoning occurs |

> **In a disaster, the safest way to eat button mushrooms is not to forage them from the wild but to grow them. If it grew on a bed where you made the compost and added the spawn yourself, identification problems almost never arise. Even so, keep the habit of digging up the base to check.**
