### 1-3. What the SS in Sunlight Means

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The Sunlight item on the evaluation table originally asks how much yield remains under 40% shade. The SS attached to the button mushroom does not mean it won that competition; it means it was never on that axis to begin with. Fungi have no chlorophyll. The carbon the button mushroom uses is cellulose already locked up in rice straw, and how bright the sky is has no effect on that stockpile. The button mushroom does not require light at all. It forms fruiting bodies normally even in total darkness, making it the cultivated mushroom least dependent on light.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This difference becomes decisive in a situation like a major volcanic eruption or nuclear winter, where sunlight drops for years at a stretch. Once aerosols spread through the stratosphere, grain crops, tuber crops (tubers/storage roots), and vegetables collapse together. Yet even then, straw, fallen leaves, and livestock manure are still there, and mushrooms are the only route that turns them into protein people can eat.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;And one more thing applies to the button mushroom specifically. When sunlight drops, temperature drops too. Its fruiting-body formation temperature is 16-18°C, and mycelium culturing is 23-25°C. It is easier to reach 16°C in a world that has turned cool than to manufacture it indoors in midsummer. Temperature range D and cold hardiness E are narrow, weak values, but the temperature band that narrow window points to is, itself, the cool side.

> **Mushrooms are the only food source whose yield does not drop even in a world without sunlight, and the button mushroom is the only species among them that does it without a single stick of firewood.**

### 1-4. How to Read the Strengths

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Four criteria carry an SS: protein density, sunlight, tillage & digging energy, and yield stability. Five carry an S: speed to harvest, continuous-cropping tolerance, taste, prevalence in South Korea, and mushroom by-product suitability.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The SS in protein density is special. At 15kcal and 3.12g protein, the protein share of calories is 83.2%. That is eight times the 10% requirement line, and the highest value among the six species this document covers. The button mushroom is, in effect, a food made of almost nothing but protein and water. As a calorie source it is nothing, but as a protein supplement it is extremely efficient.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The S in mushroom by-product suitability is the most practical strength. Five categories are met: fiber (rice straw, wheat straw, barley straw, corn stalks, dried grass, rice hulls), slaughter by-products and livestock manure (horse, cattle, chicken, and pig manure), charcoal and ash (wood ash), bone and shell (shells and eggshells replacing gypsum), and processed pulp (waste paper, old paper). This stands in contrast to the other mushrooms in this document, which are tied to hardwood sawdust alone. In a disaster, this adaptability matters directly. Even with no sawdust to be had, no oak forest nearby, and the sawmill shut down, button mushroom farming still works as long as there is straw in the rice paddy and manure in the barn.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The S in speed to harvest also stands out. Filling the bed with compost to first harvest takes 40-60 days. Even adding the 2-3 weeks of compost fermentation beforehand, the whole process stays under two and a half months. Compared with shiitake, which needs 12-18 months, the difference is large. You can start the year a disaster hits and still eat that same year.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;And there is the biggest strength of all, one the evaluation table's numbers do not capture: composting itself is sterilization. Section 3 covers this in detail, but this one fact fundamentally sets the button mushroom apart from the other five species. The grade D in infrastructure needs means it requires a growing house, bed frames, and a composting yard, and all three of those can be built from wood, earth, and straw. Not one of them needs electricity.

### 1-5. How to Read the Weaknesses

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Three criteria carry an E: cold hardiness, storability, and non-food value. Four carry a D: fertilizer demand, infrastructure needs, temperature range, and seed longevity. Four carry a C: calories per area, perenniality, pest-control self-reliance, and seed saving.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Temperature range D and cold hardiness E should be read together. The fruiting-body formation temperature is a narrow 16-18°C, and fruiting stops once you leave that window. Below 12°C, primordia do not form; above 22°C, the cap opens immediately and quality collapses. Without electricity, the response is season and ground. Spring and fall come closest to 16-18°C, and caves and basements hold 12-18°C year-round. Growing the button mushroom without electricity means growing it as a spring-and-fall crop, or growing it in a cave.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Fertilizer demand D needs to be read precisely for what it is. Not one cent of chemical fertilizer is used. Instead, rice straw and manure must be gathered in bulk. It takes 50-60kg of compost per m², and making that compost takes roughly twice that much fresh rice straw. Without a rice paddy nearby, this condition is hard to meet. The button mushroom is not a crop tied to land; it is a crop tied to the rice paddy and the barn.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Perenniality C means that after two or three flushes, the compost must be made fresh again. That is short next to shiitake's 4-5 years on a log, or the several years a wine cap bed stays productive. But spent compost makes excellent field fertilizer as is, so nothing is wasted. Section 11 covers this.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Seed saving C and seed longevity D are real obstacles. Button mushroom spawn is made through sterile culturing, and commercial spawn loses vigor within six months to a year. What's more, raising a strain from spores is harder for the button mushroom than for other mushrooms. This species produces only two spores per basidium, and those spores often already carry two nuclei, so mycelium grown from a single spore can fruit right away — but at the cost of almost no genetic diversity. Germination rate is also low. The response is the tissue culture and bed transfer covered in Section 4.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Non-food value E means it meets none of the 8 criteria. No use for the button mushroom beyond eating has been confirmed.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;And there is the heaviest weakness of all, one the evaluation table does not record: the risk of misidentification. The button mushroom grows out of soil, has the classic mushroom shape with a cap, stem, gills, and ring, and the ideal harvest stage is the closed white button. These three traits overlap exactly with the young stage of the deadly fool's mushroom and destroying angel. Section 12 must be read without fail.

## 2. What Kind of Fungus Is This Mushroom?

### 2-1. Name and Taxonomy

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The button mushroom belongs to phylum Basidiomycota, class Agaricomycetes, order Agaricales, family Agaricaceae, genus Agaricus. In the scientific name Agaricus bisporus, the species epithet bisporus means "two spores." Most basidiomycetes carry four spores per basidium, but this species carries only two. This small difference is what creates the difficulty in obtaining spawn covered in Section 4.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The Korean name yangsongi means "songi from the West," and it has nothing to do with the pine mushroom songi (Tricholomamatsutake). English speakers call it the button mushroom or white mushroom, and the large form with an open cap, portobello. All three are the same species, differing only in harvest stage and cultivar.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Ecologically, it is a saprotroph that lives in piles of organic matter that have already broken down substantially. It cannot decompose wood directly. Unlike shiitake, which tears apart the lignin in a log, or wine cap, which breaks down wood chips, the button mushroom feeds on compost that other microbes have already decomposed. In nature, it grows around manure piles, at the edges of pastures, and where fallen leaves have rotted into a thick layer.

> **This ecology explains everything about growing the button mushroom. This mushroom is not handed a sterilized substrate; it eats a substrate other microbes have already "cooked" for it. That is why composting is 70% of cultivation.**

### 2-2. Appearance

| Part | Feature |
|:---|:---|
| Cap | 3-10cm across. Starts hemispherical, then flattens out |
| Cap color | White, cream, or brown. Varies by cultivar |
| Cap surface | Smooth or with a fine fibrous pattern. No slime layer |
| **Gills** | **Pale pink at first → brown as it grows → deepens to blackish brown** |
| Gill attachment | Free from the stem (free gills) |
| **Spore color** | **Brown to chocolate brown** |
| Stem | Short and thick. 3-7cm long, 1-2cm thick |
| **Ring** | **Present.** The white membrane that covers the cap when young tears and remains on the stem as a ring |
| **Volva** | **Absent.** The base simply sits buried in the compost |
| Flesh | White. When cut, slowly changes from **pale pink to brown** |
| Smell | Mushroomy. No unpleasant odor |
| Fruiting substrate | The boundary between fermented compost and the casing layer |

> **The gills change from pale pink to brown, the spores are brown, the stem has a ring, the base has no volva, and the cut flesh changes from pink to brown. These five traits are what define the button mushroom.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The change in gill color especially needs to be remembered. At the young button stage, the gills are hidden inside the cap and cannot be seen. To check, you have to split one open. Pale pink means button mushroom; white could mean Amanita. This one action separates life from death.

### 2-3. Life Cycle - From Spore to Mushroom

| Stage | What happens |
|:---|:---|
| Spore | Only **two** per basidium. Usually already carries two nuclei |
| Germination | Germination rate is low. Harder to raise from spores than other mushrooms |
| Mycelium | Mycelium grown from a two-nucleus spore can fruit right away |
| Colonizing the compost | Mycelium spreads through the whole batch of fermented compost like white threads. 2-3 weeks |
| **Casing** | **A layer of soil is applied. Without it, no mushrooms form** |
| Pinning | Millet-sized pins form at the boundary between the casing layer and the compost layer. 10-15 days after casing |
| Fruiting body | The white pin swells into a button, and left longer, the cap opens |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Carrying only two spores per basidium is what makes the button mushroom unusual. In most mushrooms, a spore is a single-nucleus cell with only half the genes, so two spores of different mating types must meet and form secondary mycelium before a mushroom can form. Most button mushroom spores already carry two nuclei, so a mushroom can grow from a single spore. That looks convenient, but it has a cost. With no genetic diversity generated, vigor declines over successive generations, and the spore germination rate itself is low.

> **Even so, spores are not something to skimp on when sowing them. The very fact that the germination rate is low is the reason to shake in a generous amount.**

| Stage | Duration | Temperature | What to look for |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;15kcal is the lowest energy value in this document. Filling a full day's 2,100kcal with button mushrooms alone would mean eating 14kg, so the calculation itself is meaningless. The button mushroom is not a calorie source.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;What it has instead is overwhelming protein efficiency. At 3.12g protein per 100g, it ranks high among mushrooms, and because the calorie count is so low, the protein share of calories comes out to 83%. In practical terms, a plate of button mushrooms supplies protein without filling the stomach. Using grain or potatoes to fill out calories and adding button mushrooms for protein is the correct way to use it.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Vitamin D is especially valuable in a disaster. Extended time indoors and a break in oily fish supply bring on vitamin D deficiency. A mushroom's ergosterol converts to vitamin D2 when it gets UV light, so **leaving harvested button mushrooms cap-side up in the sun for a few hours substantially raises their vitamin D content.** The drying process is, at the same time, a nutrient-boosting process. Section 13 returns to this.
