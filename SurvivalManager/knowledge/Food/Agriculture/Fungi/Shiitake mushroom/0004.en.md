## 5. Inoculation Methods

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Inoculation is the stage of shiitake farming where people intervene the most, and also the stage where failure is decided. The result of the next year and a half hangs on this one day. The principle is simple: make the shiitake mycelium occupy the log before the contaminants do. To do that, plenty of spawn must go in, in many places, quickly, and the places where it went in must be closed up.

### 5-1. Timing and Weather

| Item | Criterion | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Timing | Late March to early April (in South Korea) | Log temperature suits mycelial establishment, and contaminant activity is still slow |
| Air temperature | 10-20°C in the daytime | At 5°C or below, establishment is slow, and above 25°C contaminants get ahead |
| Weather | A cloudy day without wind | On clear, windy days many spores are blown about in the air |
| Time of day | Early morning or dusk | Avoids drying and direct sunlight |
| Place | Shade, indoors if possible | If the inoculated surface dries, the establishment rate drops |

### 5-2. Drilling

| Item | Criterion |
|---|---|
| Hole diameter | 1.2-1.3cm |
| Hole depth | 2.4cm |
| Vertical spacing | 3-5cm |
| Horizontal spacing | 10-15cm |
| Number of rows | About 7 rows around the log, in a zigzag arrangement |
| Total number of holes | 25-35 for a log 12cm in diameter and 1m long |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The reason for drilling in a zigzag is that mycelium spreads three to four times faster lengthwise inside the wood than along the direction of the growth rings. If the holes are drilled neatly in a single line, the mycelium runs down along that line like a band, and the space between the lines stays empty for a long time. If they are drilled staggered, the whole log is covered more evenly and more quickly.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The more holes, the better. With many inoculation points, the mycelium spreads from many places at once, and there is less room for contaminants to squeeze in. If spawn is short and the number of holes must be cut, it is better to reduce the number of logs rather than the number of holes, and put enough into the remaining logs. Five properly inoculated logs give better results than ten sloppily inoculated ones.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Without an electric drill, holes are made with a woodworking hand drill, or with a chisel and hammer. To keep the depth uniform, wrap tape around the drill bit as a marker, or attach a piece of wood to the chisel to limit the depth. Do not leave holes drilled for a long time. Finish the inoculation on the day they are drilled.

### 5-3. Filling with Spawn and Sealing

| Step | Method |
|---|---|
| Filling | Pack the spawn into the hole tightly, with no gaps. If it is loose, air circulates, the spawn dries out, and contaminants get in |
| Tamping | Press it down firmly with a finger or a wooden stick |
| Sealing | Drive in a wooden plug, fit a Styrofoam plug, or melt wax and cover the hole |
| Wax mix | Melt paraffin and pine resin at a ratio of 6 to 4. It adheres better and cracks less than pure paraffin |
| Treating cut ends | Apply wax to both cut ends of the log as well to prevent moisture evaporation |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Sealing has two purposes: keeping moisture from escaping and keeping contaminants out. If there is no wax, it can be replaced by kneading clay and daubing it on, or by carving and driving in a wooden plug and covering it with wet moss. It is not perfect, but far better than doing nothing.

### 5-4. Inoculating with Split Spawn Logs

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This is the method used when there is no commercial spawn. An old log in which the mycelium has spread well is split with an axe to make wedges 3-4cm long and 1-1.5cm thick. These wedges are hammered into the holes drilled in new logs and closed with plugs or wax. A wedge dies if it dries, so it must be driven in immediately after splitting, and kept covered with a wet cloth during the work.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;When choosing wedges, use the parts densely filled with white mycelium. Parts where the wood is soft and crumbles easily by hand may already have used up their nutrients or been invaded by other fungi. If the split face gives off the characteristic mushroom aroma, that is a good sign; if it smells of mold or sourness, that log is not used.

### 5-5. Inoculating Sawdust Substrate

| Item | Criterion |
|---|---|
| Substrate temperature | After cooling to 25°C or below | If put in while hot, the spawn dies |
| Inoculation rate | 50-80g of spawn per 1kg of substrate | The more, the more resistant to contaminants |
| Method | Place on top of the bag or fill the center hole | |
| Tool disinfection | Spoons or tongs are heated in a flame and cooled before use | |
| Work environment | A small, windless space; wet the floor and walls with a wet cloth to settle the dust | |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The knack when there is no sterile room is to reduce the dust in the air. Thirty minutes before the work, water is sprinkled on the floor and walls to settle the dust, the door is closed, and only one person goes in. Turn your head so that your exhaled breath does not go toward the substrate, and do not talk. Observing even this much cuts the contamination rate to less than half.

## 6. Managing Mycelial Incubation

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;When inoculation is finished, 12-18 months of incubation begins. The goal of this period is for the mycelium to take over the inside of the log completely and accumulate nutrients. There is not much for a person to do, but what must not be done is clear: do not let the logs dry out, do not leave them in direct sunlight, and do not stand them up early.

### 6-1. Temperature and Humidity

| Period | Temperature | Log moisture content | Management |
|---|:---:|:---:|---|
| 3-4 days right after inoculation | 20°C | 38-42% | Induce establishment. Do not touch |
| After establishment | 22-25°C | 35-45% | The stretch where mycelial extension is fastest |
| Summer | Keep at 30°C or below | Do not let it dry | Above 35°C the mycelium weakens |
| Winter | Natural temperature | Watch for condensation | It does not die even if frozen. It grows again the following year |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Shiitake mycelium does not die even below freezing. Logs can overwinter outdoors as they are, and grow again when temperatures rise in spring of the following year. The cold hardiness S in the evaluation table refers to this fact. In a disaster, where heating is impossible, this is a great advantage. Conversely, it is weak against high temperatures. If a pile of logs receives direct sunlight in summer and the inside exceeds 35°C, the mycelium is damaged. The reason shading is needed lies not in cold but in heat.

### 6-2. Laying Down and Stacking

| Step | Timing | Method |
|---|---|---|
| Temporary laying down | From right after inoculation until May | Stack the logs horizontally and tightly on supports to waist height. Cover the top with straw or branches |
| Main laying down | From May until autumn | Stack loosely in a crib (log-cabin) pattern or a pillow-log pattern so that air can pass |
| Turning over | Once every 2-3 months | Swap the positions of logs top and bottom, inside and outside, so that the mycelium spreads evenly |
| Standing up | October to December of the year after inoculation, or the spring after that | Stand the logs at a slant, crossed against one another |

> **In the first year, always keep the logs laid down. If they are stood up early, the logs dry out quickly and the mycelium dies of drying before it reaches the core.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;While the logs are laid down, shade is important. Under a broadleaf forest is best; if there is none, make about 70% shade with shade netting, reed screens or straw thatch mats. Rain is fine. It actually helps keep the logs from drying out. However, if they are left in direct contact with the ground, contaminants and termites from the soil get in, so always prop them on supports and lift them 20cm or more off the ground.

### 6-3. Water Management

| Period | Management |
|---|---|
| Spring and autumn | Once every 2 weeks, if it does not rain, soak thoroughly |
| Summer | Once every 7-10 days. Avoid midday and water at dusk |
| Winter | None is given. If it freezes solid, the bark bursts |
| Criterion | If the fine cracks on the cut face open wide, the log lacks water |

### 6-4. How to Tell Whether the Mycelium Is Growing Properly

| Observation | Normal | Abnormal |
|---|---|---|
| Around the inoculation hole | White mycelium shows through the gaps of the plug | Green, black or orange means contamination |
| Cut face of the log | 6-10 months after inoculation, white mycelium appears on the cut face | If nothing white is seen after 1 year, establishment has failed |
| Weight | The log gradually gets lighter | If the weight stays the same, the mycelium is not feeding |
| Sound | A dull sound when struck | A clear ringing sound means it is still hard |
| Bark | After a year and a half from inoculation, it is covered with a brown film | With no browning, it is not yet mature |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Browning is the sign of maturity in shiitake log cultivation. The log surface, especially where the bark has come off and at the cut faces, is covered with a dark brown film. This is the mycelium producing pigment on surfaces exposed to air to form a protective barrier, and at the same time it means that nutrient accumulation is finished. If fruiting is induced before browning has progressed enough, only a few mushrooms appear and then it stops, and the life of the log is shortened as well.

## 7. Inducing Fruiting

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;As long as it is comfortable, mycelium simply keeps spreading. Producing mushrooms is, for the mycelium, a crisis signal. The fruiting body forms when the mycelium judges that the nutrients here are about to run out and that it must make spores and spread elsewhere. That is why the principle of inducing fruiting is the same in every case: give the mycelium a shock.

### 7-1. Three Kinds of Stimulus

| Stimulus | Method | Principle |
|---|---|---|
| Soaking | Fully submerge the logs in cold water for 12-24 hours | Rapid water uptake and a temperature drop occur at the same time |
| Striking | Knock down the standing logs or hit the cut face with a hammer | Physical shock breaks the mycelial network and induces primordia formation |
| Temperature difference | Create an 8-10°C difference, 20°C by day and 12°C by night | Mimics the signal of a seasonal change |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;What works best on shiitake is soaking. If the logs are submerged whole in a water tub or a stream, left for a day, then taken out and stood up, primordia form within a week. The colder the water, the greater the effect. Soaking in lukewarm water in summer not only gives a weak response but also invites bacterial soft rot. Use water at 15°C or below, such as stream water or well water.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;If there is no water for soaking, striking is substituted. The standing logs are pushed over all at once and stood up again a day later. Hitting the cut face three or four times with a wooden mallet is also effective. However, if the bark is struck hard enough to peel off, contaminants get in at that spot.

### 7-2. Fruiting Conditions

| Item | Condition | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Fruiting temperature | 10-25°C, optimum 15-18°C | Depending on the strain: low-temperature 5-15°C, medium-temperature 10-20°C, high-temperature 15-25°C |
| Primordia formation temperature | 15-25°C, optimum 20-22°C | |
| Air humidity | 85-95% at the start of fruiting, 70-90% in the growth period | Too much humidity gives watery mushrooms |
| Light | Indirect light is needed. Bright enough to read a newspaper | In total darkness, primordia do not form |
| Ventilation | So that carbon dioxide does not build up | If trapped, the stipes just grow long and the caps get small |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;That shiitake requires light differs from many other mushrooms. It does not photosynthesize; it uses light as a signal. In complete darkness, primordia do not form or become deformed. That said, direct sunlight is not acceptable either. The logs dry out and the caps crack. The right brightness is about that of forest shade, where a hand held up casts a faint shadow.

### 7-3. Standing and Arranging

| Item | Method |
|---|---|
| Angle | Stand at a slant of 70-80˚ to the ground |
| Arrangement | Lean the logs in a staggered way on a crossbar, or lean them against one another in a tripod shape |
| Spacing | 5-10cm between logs |
| Direction | If the rows of logs run north-south, sunlight reaches them evenly |
| Ground | Spread fallen leaves or straw so that soil does not splash up |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;If the logs are stood too upright, the water runs down and only the lower part gets wet; if they lie too flat, mushrooms appear only on the upper face. 70-80˚ is the compromise. After standing them up, turning them upside down once every 3-4 weeks lets mushrooms come evenly from the whole log.

## 8. Harvest and the Re-fruiting Cycle

### 8-1. Judging When to Harvest

| Criterion | Details |
|---|---|
| Degree of cap opening | When 60-80% open. When fully open, the flesh gets thin and spores are released |
| Cap margin | Still curled inward |
| Gills | White, not yet tinged with brown |
| Time of day | Early morning. If picked during the day, the high moisture lowers storability |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;If the mushrooms are to be dried for storage, they can be picked a little later when they have opened further, but once the gills on the underside of the cap begin to turn brown, it is too late. A mushroom that has already released its spores has loose flesh and loses its aroma.

### 8-2. How to Pick

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Hold the stipe, twist it left and right, and detach it with a slight pull. If it is pulled straight up, the surface of the log is torn off with it, and contaminants enter through that wound. If the base is left on the log, scrape the remaining piece off cleanly with the tip of a knife. A remaining base rots and attracts bacteria and flies. Spread the picked mushrooms in the shade at once and start drying them the same day.

### 8-3. Grades

| Grade | Features | Conditions |
|---|---|---|
| Hwago (flower shiitake) | Top grade, with the cap surface cracked white like a tortoise shell | When grown slowly in the cool season in dry wind |
| Donggo (winter shiitake) | High grade, with a round, thick cap and little cracking | Normal growth in the cool season |
| Hyanggo (fragrant shiitake) | Ordinary, with a thin, widely spread cap | Hot season or excess-moisture conditions |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The conditions that produce hwago are low temperature and dry air. While the mushroom grows slowly, the cap surface cracks and the white flesh shows. In a disaster, grade should be seen not as a matter of marketability but as a matter of storability. Thick-fleshed donggo and hwago, when dried, hold more nutrition for their volume and keep longer.

### 8-4. Resting and Re-fruiting

| Step | Period | Details |
|---|:---:|---|
| Harvest | 7-14 days | All the mushrooms from one fruiting are picked |
| Resting | 10-12 days or more | Lay the logs in the shade or leave them standing, giving no stimulus |
| Recovery | 30-60 days | The mycelium gathers nutrients again. The more you rush, the smaller the next yield |
| Re-stimulation | Soaking or striking | Stimulate again after recovery is complete |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;If out of greed you keep soaking without rest, the logs are exhausted quickly. Logs meant to last 4-5 years end up thrown away after 2 years. The lifespan of a shiitake log is like a piggy bank with a fixed total amount of fruiting: the faster you take it out, the sooner it is empty. In a disaster, it is far more advantageous to stretch it out for a long time.

### 8-5. Yield by Year

| Year | Yield trend | Management |
|---|---|---|
| Year 1 (year after inoculation) | Low. Just trial fruiting | Do not overdo it |
| Years 2-3 | The most abundant | 1-2 fruitings each in spring and autumn |
| Year 4 | Begins to decline | Inoculate new logs |
| Year 5 | The last. The logs become soft | Spent logs are used as spawn material or compost |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The total output of one log, for a log 12cm in diameter and 1m long, is about 1.2-2kg of fresh shiitake over 4-5 years. For one person to eat it steadily as a side dish, 30-50 logs must be kept in rotation, and to do that, 8-10 logs must be newly inoculated each year so that generations overlap. This overlapping is the core design of shiitake farming.

> **Shiitake supply does not break as long as a few new logs are added every year so that generations overlap. If all the logs are inoculated in one year, supply stops all at once 4 years later.**
