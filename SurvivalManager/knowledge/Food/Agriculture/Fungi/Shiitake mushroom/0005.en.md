## 9. Contamination, Pests and Diseases

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The first cause of failure in mushroom farming in a disaster is neither disease nor pests but competing molds. Among them, green mold, more precisely molds of the genus Trichoderma, is overwhelming. When shiitake mycelium extends 2-3mm a day, Trichoderma extends three to four times that. In the race over the substrate, if shiitake loses, there is no way to turn it back.

### 9-1. How to Recognize Green Mold

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The biggest trap is that Trichoderma also starts out as white fluff. For the first few days right after inoculation, it cannot be told from shiitake mycelium by eye. Telling them apart becomes possible from 3-5 days later.

| What to observe | Shiitake mycelium | Trichoderma |
|---|---|---|
| Color change | Stays white | Changes from white through a yellowish tint to bright green and blue-green |
| Growth rate | 2-3mm a day | 7-10mm a day. Noticeably fast |
| Edge | Fluff spreads in a neat, even line | Spreads like a fan's ribs, forming concentric ring patterns |
| Texture | Cotton or frost-like | Cotton at first, later clumps of powder |
| Substrate surface | No change | Sometimes watery, transparent bands form |
| Smell | The characteristic shiitake aroma | A sweet, coconut-like smell or a damp, musty smell |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;By the moment green is visible, hundreds of millions of spores have already been produced. So the judgment must be made before the color appears, from the growth rate and the shape of the edge. If, on the third day after inoculation, one particular hole is spreading unusually fast, that is not shiitake.

### 9-2. Telling Other Contaminants Apart

| Contaminant | Color and shape | Conditions | Risk |
|---|---|---|:---:|
| Trichoderma (green mold) | Bright green, blue-green. Spreads fast | High temperature, excess moisture, acidity | Very high |
| Penicillium | Blue-green, but the powder is finer and the edge has a white band | Low temperature, damp | High |
| Neurospora (red bread mold) | Vivid orange. Spreads explosively | Hot and humid | Very high |
| Mucor, Rhizopus | Gray fluff dotted with black spots | Excessive substrate moisture content | Medium |
| Aspergillus | Black or yellow powder | Hot and dry | High |
| Bacterial slime | Not a color but sticky mucus and a sour stench | Excess moisture, poor aeration | Medium |

> **If orange Neurospora is found, never open the bag. Its spores are extremely light, and once they are released the whole incubation room is lost. Burn it bag and all, or bury it deep in the ground.**

### 9-3. What to Do When Contamination Occurs

| Situation | Action |
|---|---|
| Contaminated sawdust bag | Do not open it; carry it out of the incubation room in the bag. Incinerate it or bury it 50cm or deeper |
| Contaminated log inoculation hole | Pull out the plug, cut out the contaminated part with a knife, apply lime water, put in fresh spawn, and seal it again |
| Wide contamination of the log surface | Coat it with lime powder and keep it 1m or more away from other logs. It sometimes recovers |
| Green all the way into the log | It will not recover. Pull it out of the pile and burn it |
| Work tools | Dip in boiling water or heat in a flame |
| Worker's clothing | Do not touch healthy substrate in clothes that touched contaminated substrate |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;If contaminated substrate is simply thrown onto the compost heap, that heap becomes a spore factory. At the very least, boil it for 30 minutes to kill the organisms before recycling it as compost. If it can be burned, burning is the most certain.

### 9-4. Prevention Is Everything

| Preventive measure | Details |
|---|---|
| Log drying | Keep the moisture content at 38-42%. A wet log is certain to be contaminated |
| Inoculation rate | Plenty, in many places. If the mycelium establishes first, contaminants cannot get in |
| Inoculation timing | Early spring. Once temperatures rise, Trichoderma gains a much greater advantage |
| Incubation temperature | Keep it 1-2°C lower than the mycelium's optimum. Trichoderma grows faster at high temperature |
| pH | Raise the substrate pH to 6.5-7 with lime. Trichoderma likes acidity |
| Aeration | Lift logs 20cm or more off the ground and stack them so that air circulates |
| Cleaning | Do not keep spent logs, spent substrate or mushroom bases near the growing site |

### 9-5. Pests

| Pest | Damage | Chemical-free response |
|---|---|---|
| Fungus gnats | Larvae eat the mycelium and fruiting bodies | Insect screens, boards smeared with tree resin or jocheong (Korean grain syrup) in place of sticky traps, immediate removal of spent substrate |
| Mites | Eat the mycelium and carry contaminants | Keep humidity steady; isolate affected logs |
| Springtails | Gnaw the mycelial surface | Clear fallen leaves from the ground and scatter ash |
| Termites, longhorn beetles | Gnaw the log itself and shorten its life | Do not let logs touch the ground |
| Slugs | Gnaw the fruiting bodies | Catch them by hand at night. Ring the logs with a band of ash or sawdust to block access |
| Storage pests | Cigarette beetles and Indian meal moths get into dried mushrooms | Complete drying, airtight storage, and roasted salt or charcoal enclosed |

## 10. Diagnosing Growth Problems

| Symptom | Cause | Action |
|---|---|---|
| No mycelium visible even after 1 year of inoculation | Log excessively dry, weak spawn vigor, use of conifer wood | Soak the logs to raise the moisture content and re-inoculate |
| Mycelium has spread but no mushrooms form | Browning incomplete, insufficient stimulus, insufficient light | Wait for browning, and combine soaking and striking. Secure indirect light |
| Long stipes and small caps | Carbon dioxide buildup, insufficient light | Ventilate and increase brightness |
| Caps cracking severely | Insufficient air humidity, dry wind | Mist or add shading. Light cracking in the cool season is actually a sign of a premium product |
| Mushrooms soft with a sour smell | Excess moisture, poor aeration, bacterial soft rot | Reduce watering and remove diseased specimens immediately |
| Mushrooms very small and numerous | Log nutrients exhausted, excessive consecutive fruiting | Give a long rest and prepare new logs |
| Log soft and crumbles by hand | End of life or invasion by other wood-rot fungi | Discard it or recycle it as compost |
| Brown spots on the cap surface | Brown blotch disease, water droplet marks | Do not spray water directly, and increase ventilation |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;For mushrooms, the concepts of deficiency and excess work differently from field crops. The nutrients in the substrate are already fixed at the time of inoculation and cannot be added later. So the cause of growth problems should mostly be sought not in nutrients but in four environmental factors: water, air, temperature and light.

## 11. Site Layout and Spent-Substrate Cycling

### 11-1. Do Not Keep Using the Same Spot

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The continuous-cropping tolerance A in the evaluation table is a value that comes with the condition of moving the layout site when logs are replaced. In the spot where logs have stood for 4-5 years, spores, organic matter and dead mycelium that fell in the meantime accumulate, and Trichoderma and fungus gnats settle on top of them. If new logs are stood in the same spot, the contamination rate rises noticeably. A spot that has been used once is rested for 2-3 years, and in the meantime fallen leaves are raked up or vegetables are planted.

### 11-2. Criteria for Choosing a Growing Site

| Item | Good conditions | Conditions to avoid |
|---|---|---|
| Shade | 70% shade under a broadleaf forest | Total darkness, direct sunlight |
| Wind | A place with gentle airflow | A place hit hard by valley winds, or a place with no wind at all |
| Water | Near a stream or well | A place where water has to be hauled |
| Terrain | A gentle north- or east-facing slope | A steep south-facing slope, low ground where water collects |
| Accessibility | On a path passed every day | A remote spot where the fruiting time is easily missed |

### 11-3. Uses for Spent Logs and Spent Substrate

| Use | Method |
|---|---|
| Spawn material | Split the parts where mycelium is still alive and use them to inoculate new logs |
| Compost | In crushed spent logs the lignin has already been broken down, so they are close to leaf mold. Added to the field, they improve water retention |
| Substrate for other mushrooms | Wine cap (king stropharia) feeds on spent shiitake logs and spent substrate as they are |
| Mulch | Spread over vegetable ridges, it suppresses weeds and holds moisture |
| Fuel | Completely dry spent logs are used as kindling. However, their heat content is lower than that of green wood |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Mushroom growing does not compete with field farming but actually helps it. This is because a flow forms in which wood is brought from the mountain and turned into mushrooms, and the leftover residue is sent to the field. Keeping the shiitake growing site close to the vegetable garden shortens this cycle.

## 12. Poisonous Look-Alikes and Safety Checks

### 12-1. The Toxicity of Shiitake Itself

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Shiitake is safe to eat. There are, however, two cautions.

| Item | Details |
|---|---|
| Eating raw | Eating a lot without cooking can sometimes cause a whip-shaped red skin rash (shiitake dermatitis). The cause is a lentinan-family compound, and it disappears with heating |
| Digestion | Raw, the fibers are tough and hard to digest. Always eat it cooked |
| Spoiled | If it turns soft and smells sour or of ammonia, throw it away |

### 12-2. Mushrooms Confused with Shiitake in the Wild

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In a disaster, people end up picking and eating mushrooms that grow in the mountains. The one that poisons the most people who think they know shiitake is the Japanese jack-o'-lantern mushroom.

| Feature | Shiitake | Japanese jack-o'-lantern (highly toxic) |
|---|---|---|
| Occurrence | On dead broadleaf wood such as oak, usually scattered | On dead beech and hornbeam wood, with several overlapping in clusters |
| Cap | Brown to dark brown, sometimes with a tortoise-shell pattern | Purplish brown to dark brown, glossy, no pattern |
| Gills | White, notched at the stipe | White, but running down the stipe (decurrent gills) |
| Stipe base | When cut, the inside is a uniform white | When cut, has black stains or spots |
| Luminescence | None | Gills glow blue-white in the dark |
| Aroma | The characteristic strong shiitake aroma | Almost no aroma |
| Toxicity | None | Illudin S. Severe vomiting and diarrhea; death if eaten in large amounts |

> **If you split the stipe base lengthwise and see black stains, it is the Japanese jack-o'-lantern. This one check is the most reliable.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Checking for luminescence is also valid. If you pick a cap, put it in a completely dark room, and look at it after about 10 minutes, once your eyes have adapted to the dark, the gills of the Japanese jack-o'-lantern glow a faint blue-white. The fresher the specimen, the clearer it is. Shiitake does not glow in any condition.

### 12-3. Three Ironclad Rules for Wild Foraging

| Rule | Details |
|---|---|
| Take a spore print | Lay the cap face down half on white paper and half on black paper. Shiitake has white spores |
| Dig up the base too | If the stipe base is left behind when picking, decisive markers such as the volva or black stains are missed |
| One kind at a time, a little at a time | Eat a mushroom for the first time as one kind only, in a one-bite portion, and wait 24 hours |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The safest answer is not to forage in the wild. If you eat only mushrooms grown on logs with a clear inoculation history, misidentification accidents cannot arise at the source. This is one of the reasons for recommending shiitake log cultivation. A mushroom that grew where I planted it is a mushroom I know.

## 13. Storage Without Refrigeration

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In the evaluation table, shiitake's storability is SS. How a food that cannot last even a few days when raw earned SS is that the value was assigned on the premise of drying. Compared with many other mushrooms that are judged unstockpilable with a storability of E, this difference is clear. Shiitake is easy to dry, keeps for 3 years or more when dried, and actually tastes better when dried.

> **Almost the whole storage problem of mushrooms is solved by drying alone. Instead of struggling to extend the storage period of fresh mushrooms, the right answer is to start drying on the day they are picked.**

### 13-1. The Principle of Drying

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Fresh shiitake is 88-90% water. In this state the water activity exceeds 0.98, and bacteria and molds multiply freely. If the moisture is lowered to 12% or below, the water activity falls below 0.6, and below that level microorganisms stop being active. Refrigeration merely slows microorganisms, but drying stops their activity altogether. That is why drying is overwhelmingly advantageous in an environment without electricity.

### 13-2. Sun Drying

| Step | Method |
|---|---|
| Cleaning | Only brush off the soil; do not wash. Contact with water lengthens the drying time and the aroma is lost |
| Slicing | Thick ones are sliced to 5-7mm. Small ones are dried whole |
| Placement | Place with the gills facing up. Ultraviolet light must reach the gills directly for vitamin D to form |
| Spreading | On trays or mats, without overlapping. Air must be able to pass underneath |
| Schedule | 2-3 clear days. Bring them in at sunset to avoid night dew |
| Finishing | Leave them for the last day in the shade in a breeze to dry them through to the center |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Complete dryness is judged by hand. If, when bent in half, it does not flex but snaps with a crisp 'tak' sound, it is done. If it bends even slightly or is leathery, moisture still remains and mold will grow during storage.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;What sun-dried shiitake has over machine-dried shiitake is vitamin D. The ergosterol in shiitake turns into vitamin D2 when exposed to ultraviolet light. The conversion occurs even if the mushrooms are left in the sun for only half a day with the gills facing up, and exposing them to ultraviolet light for about two hours before drying gives an even greater effect. In a disaster where life indoors drags on and fresh food is cut off, this is a real gain that heads off one deficiency.

### 13-3. Keeping

| Item | Method |
|---|---|
| Cooling | Pack only after it has cooled completely. If sealed while warm, condensation forms inside |
| Container | Glass jar, earthenware pot, thick plastic. Paper bags do not block moisture |
| Desiccant | Thoroughly roasted salt, pieces of charcoal, or quicklime wrapped in cloth are put in together |
| Place | Cool, dark, dry. An earth cellar or basement is ideal |
| Re-drying | Before and after the monsoon season (jangma, late June to July in South Korea), take them out once each time and dry them again |
| Period | 3 years or more at room temperature if completely dried and sealed |

### 13-4. Other Storage Methods

| Method | Period | Notes |
|---|:---:|---|
| Drying | 3 years or more | The best |
| Powder | 2 years or more | Pound after complete drying. Goes straight into porridge and soup. The aroma fades faster |
| Salting | 6 months to 1 year | Blanch, then layer with salt at 20-30% of the mushroom weight. Low priority if salt is scarce |
| Smoke drying | 2 years or more | Can be dried even on rainy days and is less prone to insects. Takes fuel |
| Fresh, kept in shade | 3-5 days | Wrapped in paper in a cool place. In a plastic bag it turns soft in a day |

## 14. Cooking and Processing

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Shiitake is on the low side of fuel use. Fresh, it cooks in 5-10 minutes, and dried, it is soaked first, so the heating time is short. In a disaster fuel is as precious as food, so this is worth counting.

| Step | Method |
|---|---|
| Soaking | 20-30 minutes in cold water. If in a hurry, 15 minutes in lukewarm water |
| Soaking water | Do not throw it away. Guanylic acid and minerals are dissolved in it, and it becomes the base of a broth |
| Heating | 5-15 minutes. Boiling for long lets the aroma escape |
| Stipe | Tough and hard to eat as it is. Tear it finely and boil it for a long time, or dry and pound it into powder for use |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Umami comes out most when the mushrooms are soaked slowly in cold water. Soaking quickly in hot water saves time but produces less guanylic acid. If you get into the habit of leaving them in water the night before, you save fuel and keep the flavor.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Making it into powder broadens its uses. A spoonful added to porridge, soup, batter or namul (seasoned vegetable side dishes) adds protein and umami together. No soaking time is needed either. However, powder loses its aroma quickly, so it is better to pound only as much as is needed each time.

## 15. Annual Work Calendar

| Month (South Korea) | Work | Notes |
|:---:|---|---|
| January | Finish felling logs, check logs that are drying | Felling in midwinter is also possible |
| February | Cut logs to length (1-1.2m), prepare for inoculation, check spawn | Get wax, plugs and drill bits ready in advance |
| March | Begin drilling and inoculation, temporary laying down | The end of the month is the right time |
| April | Finish inoculation, first check for contamination | Checking on day 3-5 after inoculation is important |
| May | Main laying down, install shading | Prepare for summer heat |
| June | Start watering, harvest logs fruiting in spring | Comes from logs in their second year or older |
| July | Reinforce shading against heat, water at 7-10 day intervals | Do not let it exceed 35°C |
| August | Turn logs over, check for fungus gnats | The most dangerous month |
| September | Prepare for autumn fruiting, soaking stimulus | Start when the water temperature drops |
| October | Autumn harvest, drying work, spore print collection | Take spores from the best specimens |
| November | The best time for felling logs, standing logs, splitting spawn logs | Right after the leaves have fallen is best |
| December | Prepare for winter, re-dry dried stock, tidy up spent logs | Logs can overwinter outdoors |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;As the table shows, shiitake work is concentrated in spring inoculation and in autumn harvesting and drying. In summer and winter it is little more than inspection. That this seasonal distribution does not overlap much with the busy periods of field farming makes shiitake easy to grow alongside other crops. A natural rhythm takes shape: plant potatoes and inoculate shiitake in spring, dig potatoes and dry shiitake in autumn.

> **The schedule of shiitake farming is not 1 year but 5. A log inoculated this year reaches the table the year after next, and by the time that log is spent, another log planted this year takes its place.**
