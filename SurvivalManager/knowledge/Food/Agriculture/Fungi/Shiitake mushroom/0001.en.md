## Introduction
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The shiitake (Lentinula edodes) received an overall grade of A and 49.2 points in the evaluation (rank 24 of 183 valid species). It is a white-rot fungus that lives by decomposing dead oak, so it asks for no tilling, no fertilizer, and no field-scale watering, only wood; even with no land to turn and no manure to spread, food comes out of the mountain as long as there are oaks. It does not photosynthesize, so yield stays almost the same even when sunlight is halved (sunlight SS). Its weak points are that it is slow (1 to 1.5 years from inoculation to the first mushroom) and that it has almost no calories (22 kcal per 100 g raw). The shiitake is not a staple but a side dish that supplies protein, B vitamins, and umami (supplementary food SS, short-term B). In return, a log inoculated once keeps producing in spring and autumn for 4-5 years, so it is the pillar of long-term self-reliance: plant it in the first year of a disaster and harvest from the second year. This document is written on the premise that there is no electricity, no pressure sterilizer, and no sterile room.

## 2. Mushroom Overview
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;It belongs to the genus Lentinula of the family Omphalotaceae, order Agaricales, class Agaricomycetes, phylum Basidiomycota, and is a white-rot fungus that decomposes the lignin and cellulose of dead broadleaf trees.

| Feature | Details |
|:---|:---|
| Cap | 5-20 cm across. Hemispherical, flattening with age. Brown to dark brown, the surface sometimes cracked like a turtle shell |
| Gills, stem | Gills white to pale brown, crowded, and notched at the stem. Stem 3-10 cm long and 1-2 cm thick, with tough fibers |
| Ring, spores | Remnants of the partial veil stay like fine down on the upper stem or the edge of the cap. Spores are white (directly tied to safety checks) |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;A single spore does not produce a mushroom. The primary mycelium from a germinated spore has only half the genes, so it cannot make a mushroom and dies out; only the secondary mycelium, formed when two primary mycelia of different mating types meet and fuse, takes over the whole log and bears mushrooms. So scatter spore powder generously.

| Stage | Time and temperature | Key point |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Inoculation, attachment | Late March-early April, 15-20℃ | 3-4 days for the spawn to attach to the log |
| Spawn run | 12-18 months after attachment, 22-25℃ | The whole cut face is covered with white mycelium; a brown film (browning) over the surface in the late stage is a sign of maturity |
| Fruiting, rest | 7-14 days after stimulation (10-25℃), 10-12 days after harvest | Primordia form and caps grow. During rest the mycelium gathers nutrients again |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Per 100 g, raw shiitake has 22 kcal and 2.74 g of protein (49.8% of calories); dried, it has about 272 kcal and about 7.6 g of protein. Vitamins B1 and B2 are about twice those of vegetables, and ergosterol turns into vitamin D2 under ultraviolet light. It also contains lentinan (a beta-glucan polysaccharide).

## 3. Logs and Substrate
> **Without electricity, shiitake means log cultivation. The one fact that the sterilization step disappears entirely outweighs all the other inconveniences.**

| Log | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Species | Best: sawtooth oak, oriental white oak, konara oak, Mongolian oak (high lignin and thick sapwood give the mycelium plenty to eat). Possible: chestnut, hornbeam, alder, cherry (yield 20-30% lower). Forbidden: conifers (resin and terpenes suppress the mycelium), lacquer tree and walnut (allelopathic compounds kill the mycelium) |
| Size | 9-15 cm across (thinner dries quickly; thicker and the mycelium cannot reach the core), 1-1.2 m long (the limit for a person to lift and stand), 15-25 years old (thick sapwood and small heartwood) |
| Felling, drying | Fell after the leaves fall and before buds open (November-February) and dry for 1-4 months to a moisture content of 38-42%. Inoculating an under-dried log always brings green mold |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;When sterilization is possible, a sawdust substrate can also be used (oak sawdust 70-80% + rice bran 20-30% + 1% calcium carbonate or shell powder, moisture 63-65%), and when sawdust is short, rice straw, wheat straw, corn stalks, bean pods, and rice hulls can be crushed to replace 20-30% of it. Sterilization without electricity uses a 200 L drum (20-30 cm of water at the bottom, substrate bags piled loosely on a rack, lid on, wrapped in a wet straw sack): heat for 4-6 hours after the steam fills it so the core reaches 95-100℃ (good when a skewer pushed into the core is too hot to hold), then let it cool overnight with the lid on (opening it while hot draws in spores with the air). Straw and rice hulls are boiled for 1-2 hours, or straw cut to 5 cm or shorter is soaked for 12-24 hours in limewater of pH 12-13, drained, and adjusted to a moisture content of 65-70% (not rinsed; the remaining alkali suppresses contaminants while shiitake mycelium tolerates it fairly well). If there is no fuel either, pile straw 1-1.5 m high and ferment it to 60-70℃, then leave it for 2-3 weeks.

## 4. Obtaining Spawn
> **The most important asset in shiitake farming is not the mushrooms but a log full of mycelium. That one log makes the next five years.**

| Method | Details |
|:---|:---|
| Splitting spawn logs (most reliable) | Split a log with well-spread mycelium with an axe into finger-thick wedges (taking the parts where white mycelium is dense) and hammer them into the inoculation holes of new logs. Split spawn logs die if they dry, so use them at once. Crushing mycelium-filled wood like sawdust and mixing it into new boiled and cooled rice straw or sawdust multiplies the amount |
| Tissue culture | Split a fresh mushroom by hand (the inside is close to sterile), put pieces of the inner flesh between boiled and cooled straw or sawdust or into a log's inoculation hole, and incubate in a dark place at 22-25℃. White down spreads in 3-5 days |
| Spore print | Turn the cap of the largest, healthiest, fully open, worm-free specimen upside down on aluminum foil and cover it with a bowl to block the wind; white spore powder is left behind. Dry the foil in the shade for one more day and seal it (never store in liquid). In the first year make 20-30 sheets and save them in a cave |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Scatter spore powder generously on boiled and cooled rice straw and incubate in a dark place at 20-25℃ for 2-4 weeks to make spawn (success when white mycelium covers the straw). Finished spawn (sawdust or spawn logs) lasts only about 1 year (seed longevity C). Seed saving S is thanks to splitting spawn logs.

## 5. Inoculation, Spawn Run, Fruiting, and Harvest
| Inoculation | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Timing | Late March-early April, daytime 10-20℃ (attachment is slow at 5℃ or below, and contaminants take the lead above 25℃). Early morning or dusk on a cloudy, windless day, in shade |
| Drilling | Hole diameter 1.2-1.3 cm, depth 2.4 cm, vertical spacing 3-5 cm, horizontal spacing 10-15 cm, about 7 rows around the log in a zigzag (25-35 holes for a log 12 cm across and 1 m long). Without an electric drill, use a hand drill or a chisel and hammer |
| Filling, sealing | Pack the spawn into the holes tightly and press it down (if loose, air circulates, the spawn dries, and contaminants get in), and seal with a wooden plug or melted wax (paraffin and pine resin 6 : 4). Also coat both cut ends with wax to prevent water loss |
| Sawdust substrate | After cooling to 25℃ or below (spawn dies if the substrate is hot), put 50-80 g of spawn per kg of substrate (the more, the stronger against contaminants) on top or in a center hole. Heat tools in a fire and let them cool before use, and in a small windless space wet the floor and walls with a damp cloth to settle the dust |

| Spawn run | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Temperature | For 3-4 days after inoculation, 20℃ for attachment (do not disturb); afterward 22-25℃. In summer keep it at 30℃ or below (the mycelium weakens above 35℃). It does not die even when frozen in winter |
| Laying down | From right after inoculation to May, stack the logs horizontally and densely on supports to waist height and cover with straw or branches. From May to autumn, stack loosely in a log-cabin pattern so air circulates, and turn them over, top to bottom and inside to outside, every 2-3 months. In the first year always keep them lying down (if stood early, they dry out and die before the mycelium reaches the core) |
| Water | Spring and autumn: soak thoroughly once every 2 weeks if it does not rain; summer: once every 7-10 days at dusk; winter: none (the bark bursts if frozen). Wide cracks in the cut face mean a lack of water |
| Checks | White mycelium shows around the inoculation holes (green, black, or orange means contamination); white mycelium appears on the cut face after 6-10 months (failed attachment if still none after 1 year); the surface is covered with a brown film after 1.5 years (browning, a sign of maturity) |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In October-December of the year after inoculation, or the following spring, stand the logs slanted and crossed at 70-80˚ to the ground (leaning on a crossbar or tripod, 5-10 cm apart, with leaves or straw on the ground). The fruiting stimuli are soaking (12-24 hours in cold water, the most effective), striking (knock the logs down or hit the cut face with a hammer; a substitute when there is no water), and a temperature difference (20℃ by day, 12℃ at night). Fruiting temperature is 10-25℃ (best 15-18℃; by variety, low-temperature 5-15℃, medium-temperature 10-20℃, high-temperature 15-25℃) and air humidity is 85-95% at the start of fruiting and 70-90% in the growth stage (too wet gives watery mushrooms); indirect light bright enough to read a newspaper and ventilation are needed (in complete darkness primordia do not form, and if carbon dioxide builds up, the stems grow long and the caps stay small).

| Harvest | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Timing, method | When the cap is 60-80% open, its edge is still rolled inward, and the gills are white, in the early morning (picking by day, when they hold more water, lowers storability). If they will be dried, they may open a little more, but it is late once the gills begin to turn brown. Hold the stem, twist, and pull lightly to detach |
| Grades | Hwago (the top grade, the cap surface cracked white like a turtle shell, when it grows slowly in cold, dry wind), donggo (a good grade, round and thick), hyanggo (ordinary, thin and widely open, in hot or too-wet conditions) |
| Re-flush | Harvest over 7-14 days, rest 10-12 days or more (no stimulation), recover 30-60 days (the more you rush, the lower the next yield), then stimulate again by soaking or striking. Soaking continuously without rest wears the log out quickly |
| Yield by year | Year 1 (the year after inoculation) is small, years 2-3 give the most (1-2 flushes each in spring and autumn), year 4 begins to decline so inoculate new logs, and in year 5 the log softens and becomes a spent log. One log (12 cm across, 1 m long) gives 1.2-2 kg of fresh shiitake over 4-5 years |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Add a few new logs every year so the generations overlap and production does not break. If you inoculate everything in one year, everything stops together 4 years later.

## 6. Contamination, Pests, and Problem Diagnosis
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The first cause of failure in mushroom farming is neither disease nor pests but a competing mold. Trichoderma (green mold) also starts as white down, and by the time green appears hundreds of millions of spores have already formed, so tell it by speed and form.

| What to observe | Shiitake mycelium | Trichoderma |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Color | Stays white | White, through yellowish, to bright green and blue-green |
| Speed | 2-3 mm a day | 7-10 mm a day |
| Edge | Down spreads neatly and evenly | Spreads like fan ribs, forming concentric rings |
| Smell | The shiitake's own aroma | A sweet coconut-like smell or a damp musty smell |

> **If you find orange Neurospora (red bread mold), never open the bag. Its spores are extremely light, and once released you lose the whole culture room. Burn the bag as it is or bury it deep in the ground.**

| Situation | Response |
|:---|:---|
| Contaminated sawdust bag | Do not open it; carry it out of the culture room as it is and burn it or bury it 50 cm or deeper |
| Contaminated inoculation hole on a log | Pull the plug, cut out the contaminated part, coat with limewater, put in new spawn, and seal again |
| Wide contamination on the log surface | Coat with lime powder and keep it 1 m or more away from other logs (sometimes it recovers) |
| Green through the log | No recovery. Remove it from the pile and burn it |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Dip tools in boiling water or heat them in a fire, and do not throw contaminated substrate onto the compost heap (it becomes a spore factory). Prevention is log moisture content of 38-42% (a wet log is always contaminated), generous inoculation, early-spring inoculation, a culture temperature 1-2℃ below the mycelium's optimum (Trichoderma grows faster in heat), a substrate pH of 6.5-7 raised with lime (Trichoderma likes acid), logs piled 20 cm or more above the ground so air circulates, and spent logs, spent substrate, and mushroom stumps kept away from the growing site. The pests are fungus gnats (screens, boards smeared with tree sap or syrup, remove spent substrate at once), mites (keep humidity steady, isolate fruiting logs), slugs (pick by hand at night, ring of ash or sawdust), and termites and longhorn beetles (do not let logs touch the ground); dried mushrooms attract cigarette beetles and Indian meal moths, so dry them completely, seal them, and add roasted salt or charcoal.

| Symptom | Cause and action |
|:---|:---|
| No mycelium visible after 1 year of inoculation | Log too dry, weak spawn vigor, or conifer used. Soak to raise moisture and re-inoculate |
| Mycelium has spread but no mushrooms | Browning incomplete, too little stimulation, or too little light. Wait for browning, combine soaking and striking, and secure indirect light |
| Mushrooms soften with a sour smell | Too wet, poor ventilation, bacterial soft rot. Reduce watering and remove diseased ones at once |
| Cap cracks badly | Air humidity too low. Mist or increase shading (light cracking in cold weather is actually a mark of quality) |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;A good growing site has 70% shade under a broadleaf forest, a gentle breeze, a stream or well nearby, a gentle north or east slope, and a spot you pass every day. Split the living parts of spent logs to inoculate new logs; crushed ones go to compost (the lignin is already broken down, so it is close to leaf mold and improves the field's water retention) or vegetable-bed mulch, or serve as substrate for king stropharia (which eats spent shiitake logs as they are).

## 7. Mistaking Poisonous Mushrooms and Safety Checks
> **Shiitake itself is safe, but in a disaster people will pick mushrooms in the mountains. You need to know the highly poisonous mushroom that is mistaken for shiitake in the wild.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Eating a lot of uncooked shiitake can cause a whip-like red rash (shiitake dermatitis; a lentinan-type compound, destroyed by heating), and raw it is also hard to digest because the fibers are tough, so always cook it. Throw away any that is soft with a sour or ammonia smell.

| Item | Shiitake | Jack-o'-lantern mushroom (Omphalotus, highly poisonous) |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Occurrence | Dead oak and other broadleaf trees, usually scattered | On dead beech and hornbeam, several overlapping in a cluster |
| Cap | Brown to dark brown, sometimes with a turtle-shell pattern | Purple-brown to dark brown, glossy, with no pattern |
| Gills | White, notched at the stem | White but running down along the stem |
| Stem base | Uniformly white inside when cut | Black stains or spots when cut |
| Luminescence, smell | No glow. The shiitake's strong aroma | Gills glow blue-white in the dark. Almost no smell |
| Toxicity | None | Illudin S. Severe vomiting and diarrhea; death in large amounts |

> **If you split the stem base lengthwise and see black stains, it is a jack-o'-lantern mushroom. This one check is the most reliable.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There are three iron rules for wild gathering. Take a spore print (turn the cap upside down across white and black paper, half on each; shiitake has white spores), dig up the whole base (leaving the base behind loses decisive marks such as a sac or black stains), and for any mushroom you eat for the first time, eat only one kind, one bite's worth, and wait 24 hours. The safest answer is not to gather in the wild.

## 8. Storage, Cooking, and Annual Schedule
> **The storability problem of mushrooms is almost entirely solved by drying alone. Instead of trying to extend raw storage, start drying on the very day you pick.**

| Storage | Details |
|:---|:---|
| Sun drying | Shake off only the soil and do not wash (washing lengthens drying and loses aroma). Slice thick ones to 5-7 mm and dry small ones whole, spread on a tray or screen without overlapping, gills up (ultraviolet light must reach the gills directly for vitamin D to form), for 2-3 days of fine weather. Bring them in at sunset to avoid night dew, and leave them in shade and wind for the last day |
| Keeping | Cool completely, then put them in glass jars, crocks, or thick plastic (paper bags do not keep out moisture), together with thoroughly roasted salt, charcoal pieces, or quicklime wrapped in cloth. Keep in a cool, dark, dry place, and take them out to dry again before and after the rainy season. 3 years or more at room temperature |
| Others | Powder (2 years or more; the aroma fades faster), salting (blanched, salt 20-30%, 6 months-1 year), smoke drying (2 years or more; works on rainy days but uses fuel), raw in shade (wrapped in paper, 3-5 days; in plastic it turns soft in a day) |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Soak dried shiitake in cold water for 20-30 minutes (15 minutes in lukewarm water if in a hurry), and do not throw away the soaking water, which holds guanylic acid and minerals and serves as the base of a broth. Heat for 5-15 minutes; boiling long drives off the aroma. Tough stems are torn fine and boiled long, or dried and ground into powder.

| Month | Work |
|:---|:---|
| Jan-Feb | Finish felling logs and check the drying. In February cut logs to length (1-1.2 m) and prepare for inoculation (wax, plugs, drill bits, check the spawn) |
| Mar-Apr | Late March is the right time: drilling, inoculation, temporary laying down. In April finish inoculating, with a first contamination check on days 3-5 |
| May-Aug | In May lay the logs down for the main stage and set up shade. In June start watering and harvest the spring flush from logs in their second year or later. In July strengthen shade and water every 7-10 days (do not let it exceed 35℃). In August turn the logs and check for fungus gnats (the most dangerous month) |
| Sep-Oct | In September, start soaking stimulation once the water temperature drops. In October, autumn harvest and drying, and take spore prints from the best specimens |
| Nov-Dec | November is the best time to fell logs (just after leaf fall), stand them, and split spawn logs. In December prepare for winter, re-dry stored dried mushrooms, and tidy spent logs (logs can overwinter in the open) |

> **The schedule of shiitake farming is not one year but five. A log inoculated this year reaches the table the year after next, and as that log runs out, another log planted this year takes its place.**
