## Introduction

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Shiitake (Lentinula edodes) is a white-rot fungus that lives by breaking down dead oak wood. It does not attack living trees; it grows by feeding on the lignin and cellulose of trees that have already died and fallen. This one habit explains almost everything about the character of shiitake. It does not need the soil to be tilled, it does not consume fertilizer, and it does not demand water at the scale of a field. What it demands is wood. In a disaster this is a decisive difference: even with no ground to turn over and no manure to spread, if there are oaks on the mountain, food can be produced.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The biggest reason shiitake holds a special place on a disaster-preparedness food list lies elsewhere. Mushrooms do not photosynthesize. Even if volcanic ash or soot spreads through the atmosphere and cuts sunlight in half, under the very conditions in which rice, wheat and potatoes collapse together, shiitake yields stay almost unchanged. What shiitake feeds on is not sunlight but the carbon already stored in the wood. The SS that shiitake receives for the sunlight criterion in the evaluation table does not mean that it uses light better than other crops; it means that it lies outside the axis of light altogether. In scenarios where solar radiation falls for a long period, such as a volcanic winter or a nuclear winter, mushrooms are effectively the only food source whose output holds up.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The weaknesses are just as clear. First, it is slow. It takes from 1 year to a year and a half from driving spawn into a log to seeing the first mushroom. It is not a crop you plant now to eat now, when you are hungry. Second, it has almost no calories. 100g of fresh shiitake is only 22kcal. Shiitake alone can never fill a day's calorie needs. Shiitake is not a staple but a side dish, in the role of supplying protein, B vitamins and umami. That the Supplementary grade is SS while the Short-term grade is B in the evaluation table points to exactly this situation.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In return, once it is established, it lasts. A log inoculated once keeps producing in spring and autumn for 4-5 years. There is no seed to collect every year, no field to plow, no manure to spread. So shiitake is a crop that you set out in the first year of a disaster and begin harvesting from the second year; in other words, a pillar of long-term self-reliance. This document is written on the premise that there is no electricity, no pressure sterilizer and no sterile room at all. It puts particular weight on how to choose logs, how to carry on the spawn yourself, what to do so that green mold does not take the substrate away, and how to dry the mushrooms so that they can be eaten over several years.
