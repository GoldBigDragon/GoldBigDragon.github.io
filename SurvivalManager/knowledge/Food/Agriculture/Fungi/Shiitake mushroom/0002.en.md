## 1. Disaster-Preparedness Evaluation

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Shiitake's Overall grade is A, with a score of 49.2. Broken down by scenario on the same scale, its Short-term capacity to withstand the early phase of a disaster is B (47.4), its Long-term self-reliance over many years is S (51.0), and its Supplementary value as a side-dish resource beyond staple foods is SS (64.8). This arrangement, with Long-term higher than Short-term and Supplementary far above both, sums up the character of shiitake. It is not a crop that fills the stomach right away, but one that supplies side dishes and protein over a number of years.

### 1-3. What SS for Sunlight Means

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The sunlight criterion in the evaluation table originally asks how much relative yield remains under 40% shade. Potatoes keep 80%, leafy vegetables more than that, and rice and maize fall much further. The SS given to shiitake does not mean that it came first in this contest. Shiitake is not on this axis to begin with. Fungi have no chlorophyll and do not photosynthesize. The carbon that shiitake eats is the lignin and cellulose that the oak has already fixed over several decades. Whether or not the sky grows dark, if the wood is already there, shiitake grows.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This difference is decisive in scenarios such as a volcanic winter or a nuclear winter, in which solar radiation declines over several years. When aerosols spread through the stratosphere, every crop collapses at the same time. At that point the fields are empty, but the dead trees on the mountain are still there, and the only means of turning that wood into food is the mushroom. The first reason for putting shiitake on a disaster-preparedness list lies not in calories or yield but in this irreplaceability.

> **Mushrooms are the only food source whose yield does not shrink even in a world where sunlight has vanished. That is the first reason to plant shiitake.**

### 1-4. How to Read the Strengths

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The criteria that received S or higher are protein density, perenniality, sunlight, cold hardiness, storability, tillage and digging energy, yield stability, taste, prevalence in South Korea, and seed saving. The picture this combination paints is clear. Shiitake needs no field and no fertilizer, is independent of the weather, yields the same amount year after year, can carry on its own spawn, and keeps for years once dried.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The protein density SS is a value worth special attention. With 22kcal of energy and 2.74g of protein, the protein share of calories is 49.8%. The minimum a person needs is 10%, so this is five times that. Of course, at only 22kcal per 100g, shiitake cannot fill the stomach. But a combination in which potatoes or sweet potatoes supply the calories and shiitake adds the protein is very effective when meat and beans are cut off. Rehydrating 10g of dried shiitake yields close to 100g of fresh weight, and it contains 2.7g of protein.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;It also matters that perenniality S and seed saving S appear together. A single inoculation of one log yields for 4-5 years. And in the meantime, if you split a well-colonized log and rub it onto new logs, you can carry the line on without a spawn shop. Shiitake is one of the few cultivated mushrooms whose farming continues even after commercial spawn is cut off.

### 1-5. How to Read the Weaknesses

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There are two Es: speed to harvest and non-food value. With 12-18 months from inoculating a log to the first mushroom, even if you start on the day the disaster hits, you will eat nothing from it that year. There is only one response: plant before the disaster comes, or get through the first year of the disaster on other crops while at the same time filling up logs. Shiitake is an investment not for today's rice but for next year's side dishes.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The calories per area grade of C must be read alongside these. This value was originally calculated as E, then rejected because of an input unit error and imputed as C, the median for fungi. Either way, the conclusion is the same. It is better not to make any plan that relies on shiitake for calories. Since 100g of fresh shiitake is 22kcal, filling 2,100kcal a day with shiitake alone would require eating 9.5kg. That is physically impossible.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The fertilizer demand grade of C is of a somewhat different nature. That no chemical fertilizer is needed at all is a strength, but in exchange oak trees must keep being cut. Logs at 135kg per m² have to be replaced every 4-5 years, which comes to about 30kg a year. If there is no oak forest near the house, shiitake farming does not work. In this respect shiitake is a crop tied not to the land but to the forest.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The seed longevity grade of C also deserves attention. Commercial spawn stays viable for about 1 year. Buying spawn all at once and using it over several years does not work. The means of solving this weakness are spore print preservation and tissue culture, covered in Section 4. Spores dried on aluminum foil last 5-10 years, so even if the spawn dies, farming can start again as long as the spores are alive.

## 2. What Kind of Fungus Is This?

### 2-1. Taxonomy and Ecology

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Shiitake belongs to the phylum Basidiomycota, class Agaricomycetes, order Agaricales, family Omphalotaceae, genus Lentinula. It is worth remembering that the same family contains the Japanese jack-o'-lantern mushroom (Omphalotus japonicus). It is highly toxic and is the typical mushroom confused with shiitake in the wild. Section 12 covers how to tell them apart separately.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Ecologically, shiitake is a white-rot fungus. It breaks down the lignin of the wood and feeds while bleaching the timber white. This differs from brown-rot fungi, which eat only the cellulose, leave the lignin behind, and crumble the wood into brown pieces. White-rot fungi are among the few organisms able to break lignin, the polymer hardest to decompose in nature. This is why shiitake prefers hard broadleaf trees such as oak, and also why it cannot grow on conifers such as pine. The resin and terpenes of conifers act as poisons to the mycelium.

### 2-2. Appearance

| Part | Features |
|---|---|
| Cap | Spreads from hemispherical to flat. 5-20cm across, brown to dark brown, and the surface sometimes develops a pattern cracked like a tortoise shell |
| Gills | White to pale brown. Densely arranged and notched where they meet the stipe |
| Stipe | 3-10cm long, 1-2cm thick. The fibers are tough, so it does not break easily even when dried |
| Ring | Remnants of the inner veil that joined the cap and stipe when young remain like cottony fluff on the upper stipe or the cap margin |
| Spores | White. A spore print leaves a white powder |
| Mycelium | White. Spreads through logs or sawdust like threads |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;That the spores are white bears directly on safety checks. If you cut off a cap and leave it face down on black paper and white powder settles, it belongs to the shiitake line; if brown or purplish-black powder comes out, it is a different mushroom. Spore print color is the most basic and hardest-to-fake marker for sorting mushrooms.

### 2-3. Life Cycle - From Spore to Mushroom

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The life cycle of a mushroom differs from that of a plant. Unlike a plant, in which one seed becomes one tree, a single mushroom spore cannot become a mushroom on its own. Without knowing this, you cannot understand why spore collection in Section 4 is done the way it is.

| Stage | What happens |
|---|---|
| Spore | Released from the gills of a mature cap. A single-nucleus cell carrying only half of the genes |
| Primary mycelium | The spore germinates and extends in a thread shape. It can grow, but with only half the genes it cannot form mushrooms and eventually weakens and dies |
| Secondary mycelium | Two primary mycelia of different mating types meet and fuse. Only in this state, with the genes made whole, can it bear mushrooms |
| Mycelial colonization | The secondary mycelium takes over the whole log or substrate and accumulates nutrients |
| Primordia formation | When stimulated by a temperature drop or physical shock, the mycelium clumps together to form primordia like grains of millet |
| Fruiting body | The primordia grow into the mushrooms we eat. When mature, it scatters spores again |

> **A single spore will not produce a mushroom. When sowing spores, do not handle them one grain at a time with care; shake in plenty of powder so that they meet and pair up inside the substrate.**

### 2-4. Growth Stages and Duration

| Stage | Timing (in South Korea) | Temperature | What to look for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Log felling and drying | November to February | Natural temperature | Fell after the leaves have dropped and the sap has gone down. Dry for 1-4 months to a moisture content of 38-42% |
| Inoculation | Late March to early April | 15-20°C | Before the log temperature rises, when contaminant activity is low |
| Establishment | 3-4 days after inoculation | 20°C | The period when the spawn attaches to the log |
| Mycelial colonization | 12-18 months after establishment | 22-25°C | The process in which the whole cross-section of the log is covered with white mycelium |
| Browning | Late in colonization | Natural temperature | The log surface is covered with a brown film. A sign of maturity |
| Fruiting | 7-14 days after stimulation | 10-25°C | Primordia form and the caps grow |
| Resting | 10-12 days after harvest | Natural temperature | The mycelium gathers nutrients again |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;What stands out in the table is the 12-18 months that mycelial colonization takes. In this period there is almost nothing for a person to do beyond keeping the logs from drying out and avoiding direct sunlight. The labor of shiitake farming is concentrated in the first month and in the harvest season, and the time in between is waiting. In a disaster, this trait actually becomes an advantage. In seasons when you are busy with other work, shiitake matures on its own.

| Nutrient | Fresh | Dried | Notes |
|---|:---:|:---:|---|
| Energy | 22kcal | about 272kcal | Drying concentrates the calories relative to volume |
| Protein | 2.74g | about 7.6g | Very high, at 49.8% relative to energy |
| Carbohydrate | about 5g | about 11.4g | A large part is indigestible polysaccharides |
| Fat | around 0.3g | about 0.9g | Almost none |
| Sugars | 0.43g | - | Almost no sweetness |
| Potassium | High | about 180mg | Involved in sodium excretion |
| Vitamins B1 and B2 | Abundant | about 2 times that of vegetables | Needed for energy metabolism |
| Ergosterol | Contained | - | Converts to vitamin D2 when exposed to ultraviolet light |
| Lentinan | Contained | Contained | A beta-glucan-type polysaccharide |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The ergosterol entry has special meaning in a disaster. When life indoors drags on and fresh food is cut off, vitamin D deficiency sets in. Even if you leave shiitake in the sun for only half a day with the gills facing up, the ergosterol converts to vitamin D2. It is almost the only case in which sun-dried shiitake is nutritionally superior to shiitake dried in a dryer. Section 13 covers this method in detail.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Umami cannot be ignored either. When shiitake is dried, the cells break and guanylic acid increases sharply. When salt and soy sauce become scarce, the water in which dried shiitake has been soaked provides the base of a broth. It does not count in calories, but for a person who has to eat the same porridge every day, this is real value.
