## 3. Substrate Materials and Preparation

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There are broadly two ways to grow shiitake: log cultivation, which uses whole logs as they are, and sawdust cultivation, in which sawdust is mixed with a nutrient source and packed into bags. In normal times commercial farms lean toward the faster-turnover sawdust cultivation, but when the electricity is cut, the judgment flips to the exact opposite. Sawdust bags must be sterilized, and sterilization takes fuel. Logs need no sterilization. The inside of a log is already close to sterile, and the wood's own defensive compounds and low moisture hold contaminants back.

> **Without electricity, shiitake means log cultivation. This one reason alone, that the whole sterilization step disappears, outweighs every other inconvenience.**

### 3-1. Choosing Logs

| Item | Criterion | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Best species | Sawtooth oak, Oriental white oak, konara oak, Mongolian oak | High lignin content and thick sapwood give the mycelium plenty to eat |
| Acceptable species | Chestnut, hornbeam, alder, cherry | Yields are 20-30% lower than from oak |
| Forbidden species | Pine, Korean pine, fir and other conifers | Resin and terpenes suppress mycelial growth |
| Forbidden species | Lacquer tree, walnut | Allelopathic compounds kill the mycelium |
| Diameter | 9-15cm | Too thin and it dries out quickly; too thick and the mycelium cannot reach the core |
| Length | 1-1.2m | The limit a person can lift and stand up |
| Tree age | 15-25 years | The sapwood is thick and the heartwood is still small |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The felling time determines the yield. The best time is early to mid-November in South Korea, when the leaves have all fallen and the sap has gone down to the roots. Trees felled then retain the most stored nutrients in the trunk, and their yield is clearly higher than that of trees felled in spring. After felling, leave them in the shade with the branches trimmed to dry for 1-4 months.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The standard for judging dryness is a moisture content of 38-42%. Without a moisture meter, judge by three signs. First, 3-5 fine cracks radiate across the cut face. If there are no cracks at all, the log is still wet, and if more than ten cracks have split wide open, it is too dry. Second, the log has reached 85-90% of its weight right after felling. Third, when you press the bark with a fingernail, no moisture seeps out.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;If you inoculate a log that is not dry enough, green mold is sure to come. When the log holds a lot of water, the tree's own cells are still alive and resist the invasion of shiitake mycelium, and in that gap contaminants take hold first. Conversely, if it is too dry, there is no water for the mycelium to spread, and establishment does not happen at all. Half of the failures in shiitake log cultivation come from this drying judgment.

### 3-2. Sawdust Substrate - The Choice When Sterilization Is Possible

| Material | Proportion | Role |
|---|:---:|---|
| Oak sawdust | 70-80% | Main carbon source |
| Rice bran | 20-30% | Source of nitrogen and vitamin B1 |
| Wheat bran | Can replace rice bran | Nitrogen source |
| Calcium carbonate or ground shell powder | 1% | pH adjustment, calcium supply |
| Moisture | 63-65% | When squeezed in the hand, one or two drops of water form between the fingers |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Sawdust works best with a mix of particle sizes. With fine powder alone, air cannot pass and the mycelium suffocates, and with coarse planer shavings alone, the density is low and nutrients are lacking. Sift it and mix particles of 2-3mm and finer ones half and half. Sawdust piled outdoors and aged for 3-6 months is better than fresh sawdust, because the resin escapes from the wood and the tissue softens.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In a disaster, if sawdust runs short, rice straw, wheat straw, corn stalks, bean pods and rice husks can be crushed finely and substituted for up to 20-30%. However, shiitake depends heavily on woody material, so once the substitution rate exceeds 40%, yields fall noticeably. Substitute materials must be cut to 5cm or less so that the substrate packs evenly.

### 3-3. Four Ways to Sterilize Substrate Without Electricity

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;A pressure sterilizer runs at 121°C for 90 minutes and kills even bacterial spores. It needs both electricity and a pressure vessel. The methods available without them are not complete sterilization but low-temperature pasteurization, which reduces the number of competing organisms enough for shiitake mycelium to establish first. It is essential to understand first that the goal is different.

#### 3-3-1. Steam Sterilization in a Drum

| Item | Method |
|---|---|
| Equipment | 200L steel drum, lid, stand (bricks or a grate), firewood |
| Water | 20-30cm at the bottom of the drum |
| Loading | Pile substrate bags or sacks loosely on the stand. Leave gaps for steam to circulate |
| Sealing | Cover with the lid and wrap with wet gamani (straw sacks) or plastic sheeting so steam does not leak |
| Heating | 4-6 hours from the time the water boils and steam fills the drum |
| Judging | A wooden skewer pushed into the core of the substrate is too hot to hold in the hand |
| Cooling | Let it cool overnight with the lid on. If opened while hot, spores are sucked in with the air |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This method brings the core of the substrate to 95-100°C. Fungal conidia and most bacteria die, but heat-resistant bacterial spores survive. Even so, it is sufficient in practice. If the shiitake mycelium covers the surface within three days, there is no room left for contaminants to enter after that.

#### 3-3-2. Boiling-Water Soak

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This is the simplest method, used for rice straw and other finely cut straw-type materials. Boil water in a pot, add the material, and boil it thoroughly for 1 hour or more. Lift it out, squeeze out the water, cool it to a moisture content of 65-70% and a temperature of 30°C or below, and then inoculate. It uses a lot of fuel, but its strength is that the only equipment is a single pot. Because recontamination can occur while squeezing after boiling, the squeezing tools and the floor where it cools should be washed with boiling water beforehand.

#### 3-3-3. Lime Soak - A Method That Needs No Fuel at All

| Item | Method |
|---|---|
| Mix | 1-1.5kg of slaked lime, or 0.5-1kg of quicklime, per 100L of water |
| pH | 12-13. A strong alkali that feels slippery to the touch |
| Soaking | Submerge straw cut to 5cm or less completely, for 12-24 hours |
| Draining | Lift it out, drain for 6-12 hours, and adjust the moisture content to 65-70% |
| Inoculation | Do not rinse with water. The alkali remaining in the substrate keeps suppressing contaminants |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Strong alkali destroys the cell membranes of mold spores, while shiitake mycelium tolerates it relatively well. Above all, no fire is used at all. In a disaster, when fuel is most precious, this is worth a great deal. If there is no lime, you can substitute lye made by stirring wood ash into water, leaving it for a day, and taking only the clear liquid on top, but the pH is lower, around 11, and varies from batch to batch, so the success rate drops. Lime is a material to stock up on generously whenever it can be obtained.

#### 3-3-4. Rice Straw Fermentation

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;If straw is soaked in water and piled to a height of 1-1.5m, thermophilic microbes generate their own heat and the inside of the pile reaches 60-70°C. Turn it every 2-3 days and leave it for 2-3 weeks: the easy sugars that contaminants could eat are used up in advance, and it becomes a selective substrate that only shiitake mycelium can feed on. The idea is different, because the method removes the food first rather than sterilizing. It suits button mushroom or wine cap (king stropharia) better than shiitake, but it can also be used for shiitake as a pretreatment for making a straw-blend substrate when sawdust is short.

### 3-4. Comparison of Unpowered Sterilization Methods

| Method | Fuel | Time required | Success rate | Suitable substrate |
|---|:---:|:---:|:---:|---|
| Log cultivation (no sterilization needed) | None | None | Highest | Logs |
| Drum steam | A lot | 4-6 hours + cooling | High | Sawdust, straw |
| Boiling-water soak | A lot | 1-2 hours + cooling | Medium | Straw, rice husks |
| Lime soak | None | 12-24 hours | Medium | Straw-type materials only |
| Rice straw fermentation | None | 2-3 weeks | Medium | Straw, compost |

## 4. Securing Spawn

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Mushrooms have no seeds. That means that once the seed-and-seedling dealers close, there is nowhere to buy seed, and at the same time that the method of saving seed and keeping it in a jar does not work. But fungi have three routes that plants do not. One is to collect and keep spores; another is to tear off a piece of flesh from a living mushroom and grow it as it is; the third is to split wood through which mycelium has spread and transfer it to new wood. If you layer these three, the spawn is never cut off.

### 4-1. Collecting Spores and Making Spore Prints

| Step | Method |
|---|---|
| Choosing the specimen | Choose the largest, healthiest specimen, with the cap fully open and not eaten by insects |
| Cutting the stipe | Cut the stipe off close to where the cap and stipe meet |
| Support | Place it on aluminum foil with the gills facing down |
| Covering | Put a cup or bowl over it to block the wind |
| Leaving it | 24 hours in a cool place |
| Collecting | Lift off the cap and a white spore powder remains on the foil in the exact shape of the cap |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Aluminum foil is better than paper. Paper absorbs moisture, and contaminants hide between its fibers. Foil absorbs no moisture and its surface is smooth, so it preserves the spores as they are. If foil cannot be had, a glass plate comes next in order.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;After collecting the spores, leave the foil as it is in a breezy shade for one more day to drive off even the faintest moisture. Then fold the foil in half so the spore side is protected on the inside, and put it in a zip bag. At this time, add thoroughly roasted salt or a piece of charcoal to take up moisture. Gather several zip bags in a glass jar, seal it with beeswax, and keep it in an earth cellar or basement where the temperature is constant through all four seasons. Ultraviolet light destroys the DNA of spores, so it must be a deep place where no light enters.

| Form of storage | Lifespan | Notes |
|---|:---:|---|
| Spore print (dried on foil) | 5-10 years or more | Moisture is completely excluded, so cell activity stops |
| Liquid spawn (suspension in sterile water) | 3-6 months | Without refrigeration, spores germinate early or bacteria multiply |
| Finished inoculated spawn (sawdust, spawn logs) | About 1 year | It is living mycelium, so it keeps consuming nutrients |

> **In a disaster, never store spores as a liquid. Always keep them in the form of a dried spore print.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The difference in lifespan between spores and plant seeds comes from structure. A tomato seed holds an embryo and nutrient reserves, so over time it consumes its own nutrients, ages and dies. At room temperature, that is 2-4 years. A mushroom spore is merely a single cell wrapped in a thick cell wall, so it has no nutrients to use up. If conditions are right, 5-10 years, and in academic circles it is not rare to revive spores that are decades old.

| Comparison item | Tomato seed | Mushroom spore |
|---|---|---|
| Average lifespan (room temperature) | About 2-4 years | About 5-10 years or more |
| Vulnerability | Temperature change, exhaustion of its own nutrients | Mold contamination caused by moisture |
| Cell structure | A complex embryo and nutrient reserves | A single cell with a thick protective wall |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;That is why it is important, in the first year of a disaster, when the largest and healthiest mushrooms appear, to make spore prints in bulk, 20-30 sheets, and bank them in the earth cellar. Even if you carry the mycelium on year after year and fail because of contamination, the banked spores serve as a safeguard: 5 or 10 years later you can take them out and start over at any time.

### 4-2. Making Spawn from Spores - The Heated Rice-Straw Substrate Method

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In theory, if spores are sown on a sterile substrate, mycelium spreads. In reality, however, green mold and bacteria in the air take over the substrate hundreds of times faster than mushroom spores. The more nutritious the substrate, the more vulnerable it is. To raise the success rate without electricity, you should use a primitive substrate that relies on nature's own defenses, instead of an over-nutritious artificial one.

| Step | Method |
|---|---|
| Boiling | Boil rice straw or wheat straw thoroughly in boiling water for 1 hour or more |
| Squeezing | Lift it out, squeeze out the water, and let it cool until it can be handled |
| Sprinkling | Sprinkle the stored spore powder evenly and generously, without holding back |
| Covering | Cover with a clean cloth to block wind and falling spores |
| Incubation | 2-4 weeks in a dark place at 20-25°C. If white mycelium covers the straw, it has succeeded |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;If you economize and drop the spores one at a time, only primary mycelium grows and then dies. Mushrooms appear only when different mating types meet and become secondary mycelium, so you must shake in a great many spores at once and let them find mates among themselves inside the substrate. This is the most common mistake in spore inoculation and also the easiest one to fix.

### 4-3. Tissue Culture - The Most Reliable Method

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;If you have a live, fresh mushroom in hand, there is almost no reason to use spores. The white flesh on the inside, where the cap and stipe of the mushroom meet, is a mass of secondary mycelium in which the male-female union is already complete. If you tear off a piece and put it in the substrate, it skips germination and mating, and the mycelium immediately spreads in all directions.

| Step | Method |
|---|---|
| Choosing | A fresh specimen whose cap is just about to open. Do not use old or insect-eaten ones |
| Disinfecting | Heat the knife in a flame and let it cool, or dip it in boiling water and take it out |
| Splitting | Split the mushroom lengthwise by hand. If cut with a knife, contaminants on the surface are dragged inside |
| Taking a sample | Tear off the white flesh on the inside where the cap and stipe meet, in a piece the size of a grain of rice |
| Inoculation | Insert it among boiled and cooled rice straw or sawdust, or into the inoculation hole of a log |
| Incubation | A dark place at 22-25°C. In 3-5 days, white fuzz spreads around the piece |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Splitting matters because the inside of a mushroom is close to sterile. All kinds of spores sit on the outer skin and gills, but the inside of the flesh is clean. When you split it by hand, that clean surface is newly exposed, and a piece taken from there becomes pure mycelium even without disinfection.

### 4-4. Carrying On Spawn Through Logs

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Shiitake earned its S for seed saving thanks to this method. A log that has produced mushrooms well is itself a huge mass of spawn. If you split this log and transfer it to new logs, you can carry the line on without aseptic handling, without spore collection, and without substrate sterilization.

| Method | How | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Splitting a spawn log | Split a well-colonized log with an axe to make wedges as thick as a finger, and hammer them into the inoculation holes of new logs | The most reliable |
| Rubbing sawdust | Crush mycelium-filled wood into sawdust and mix it into newly boiled and cooled rice straw or sawdust | Good for multiplying the amount |
| Natural infection | Stand newly inoculated logs right beside a log that is fruiting | A supplementary measure |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;A split spawn log dies when it dries. It must be driven in right where it is split, and the inoculation holes sealed. It is best to work on a cloudy day or early in the morning, when there is no wind. The wedges from one log can fill 5-10 new logs, so if you sacrifice one well-producing log every year, the spawn supply continues indefinitely.

> **The most important asset in shiitake farming is not the mushrooms but the log full of mycelium. That one log makes the next 5 years.**
