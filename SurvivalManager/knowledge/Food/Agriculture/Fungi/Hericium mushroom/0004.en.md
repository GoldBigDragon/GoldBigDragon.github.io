## 5. Inoculation Method

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Inoculation is where sawdust-substrate cultivation fails most often. Sterilization leaves the substrate cleanly emptied, but it is common for green mold to move into that empty space before the mushroom's mycelium does. On top of that, since the lion's mane mushroom needs a mildly acidic substrate, the method other mushrooms use - making the substrate alkaline with wood ash or lime to suppress contaminants - cannot be used here. Inoculation has just one goal: getting the mushroom's mycelium to claim the space first.

### 5-1. Inoculating Sawdust Substrate

| Item | Standard | Reason |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Cooling | **13-14 hours** after sterilization, until substrate temperature reaches **18-20 degrees** | If it is hotter, the spawn dies |
| Speed | As soon as it has cooled | Leaving it too long lets airborne spores settle |
| Time of day | **Early morning or right after rain** | Airborne spores and dust are at their lowest |
| Inoculation rate (standard) | 2-3% of the substrate weight | |
| Inoculation rate (without a sterile room) | **5-10% of the substrate weight** | Whichever claims the space first wins |
| Placement | Half into the center hole, half on the surface | The mycelium spreads outward from the center |
| Sealing | Plug the opening with cotton or cloth and tie it with string | Air passes through while dust is filtered out |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The advice to inoculate right after rain is especially valid without a sterile room. Rain washes spores and dust out of the air, sharply reducing the number of contaminants that settle. The worst time, by contrast, is midday on a clear, windy day.

### 5-2. Tips for Inoculating Without a Sterile Room

| Item | Method |
|:---|:---|
| Location | The smallest room with no draft: the back of a storeroom, a corner of a shed |
| Preparation | Sprinkle water on the floor and walls to settle dust, and wait 30 minutes |
| Improvised sterile enclosure | Lay a large box or jar on its side, wipe the inside with boiled water, and work by reaching only your hands inside |
| Hands | Wash with soap, and if possible wipe with strong liquor or vinegar |
| Tools | Heat the spoon and knife over a flame and let them cool |
| Clothing | Slightly damp clothing raises less dust than dry clothing |
| Breathing | Do not talk or breathe out over the substrate. Cover your mouth with cloth |
| Motion | Finish opening the container, adding the spawn, and closing it within 10 seconds |

> **Without a sterile room, the key is not perfect sterility but speed and spawn quantity. You cannot stop a few contaminants from getting in, so make sure the mushroom's mycelium overwhelmingly outnumbers them and claims the space first.**

### 5-3. Log Inoculation

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Logs are not sterilized. The inside of the wood is naturally close to sterile, and the bark acts as a protective barrier. That makes inoculation far simpler.

| Step | Method |
|:---|:---|
| Timing | **March-May** or September-October, when sap starts moving |
| Drilling holes | 12mm diameter, 25-30mm deep, in a spiral pattern **15-20cm apart** |
| Adding spawn | Press sawdust spawn into the hole, or drive in a spawn dowel (a wooden-plug spawn) |
| Sealing | Seal the hole with beeswax, pine resin, wet clay, or a wooden plug |
| Face inoculation | For short segments, spread spawn on the cut face and stack another segment on top |
| Spawn amount | 20-30 holes per log (15cm diameter, 1m long) |
| Aftercare | Lay them down and stack them in the shade, covering with straw or fallen leaves to keep them from drying out |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Sealing the holes matters. Through an open hole, water gets in, causes rot, and lets contaminants settle. Beeswax is best; if none is available, melt pine resin or apply wet clay to seal it. Carving a wooden plug and driving it in also works.

### 5-4. Checking After Inoculation

| Timing | What to check |
|:---|:---|
| 24-48 hours | A **cottony white haze** starts appearing around the spawn |
| 3-5 days | White mycelium spreads out radially 1-2cm from the spawn |
| 7-10 days | The top of the substrate becomes covered in white |
| Logs | In 2-3 months, white mycelium becomes visible around the holes |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The first few days decide the outcome. If white mycelium spreads visibly within 3 days, it is nearly a success. If 5 days pass with no change at all, either the spawn was dead or the substrate was too hot when inoculated. If any color other than white appears, move that container away from the others immediately.

## 6. Managing Mycelium Incubation

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The 20-30 days after inoculation is the incubation period. The goal is for white mycelium to take over the entire substrate. What the grower needs to do is keep the temperature steady, keep it dark, and check daily to remove any contaminated ones.

### 6-1. Temperature and Humidity

| Item | Range | Notes |
|:---|:---:|:---|
| Mycelium growth temperature | 22-25°C | Fastest |
| **Lower limit** | **6°C** | Below this, growth stops |
| **Upper limit** | **35°C** | Above this, growth stops and it soon dies |
| Air humidity | 65-75% | If too humid, contaminants attach to the surface |
| Light | Dark | No light is needed during incubation |
| Duration | 20-30 days | Varies with substrate amount and temperature |

> **35°C is an absolute limit. Because the mycelium itself generates metabolic heat, the inside of the substrate can exceed 35°C even when the surrounding air is 30°C. Stacking containers tightly together makes the center especially hot.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There are three responses. Stack containers with a finger-width gap between them. From about ten days into incubation, lower the surrounding temperature by 2-3°C. And avoid incubating in midsummer.

### 6-2. Mycelium Color Change - Do Not Mistake It for Contamination

| Timing | Normal color |
|:---|:---|
| Right after inoculation-2 weeks | Pure white |
| 2-3 weeks | White with **a faint yellow tinge** |
| Maturity | Turns yellow, sometimes **pale pink** |

> **This mushroom's mycelium normally turns yellow and pink.** With other mushrooms, such a color would raise suspicion of contamination, but for the lion's mane mushroom it is a sign of maturity. Not knowing this leads people to discard a perfectly healthy substrate.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;So how do you tell it apart from contamination? Do not look at color alone - check texture and smell together with it.

| Observation | Normal maturation | Contamination |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Color | White to yellow, pale pink | Blue-green, black, deep orange |
| Texture | A tangled layer of mycelial threads. Springy to the touch | **Flies off like powder** |
| Boundary | Spreads evenly through the whole substrate | **Radiating outward from a round spot** |
| Smell | A faint mushroom aroma, like forest soil | A musty smell, a sour smell |
| Speed | 3-5mm a day | 10-30mm a day |

### 6-3. Judging When Incubation Is Complete

| Criterion | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Color | The entire substrate is evenly covered, from white to pale yellow |
| Bottom | **The mycelium must reach all the way to the bottom.** If only the top is covered, it is not finished |
| Firmness | The substrate holds together as one mass even if the container is turned upside down |
| Additional time | **Leave it for another 5-7 days** after it is fully covered. This is called after-ripening, and it raises the yield |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Skipping the after-ripening reduces the yield. Even when it looks fully covered on the outside, parts of the substrate where the mycelium has not fully penetrated remain, so leaving it a few more days gives a better result.

## 7. Inducing Fruiting

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;When comfortable, mycelium just keeps extending. Forming a mushroom is a crisis signal to the mycelium. For the lion's mane mushroom, there are three such signals: **wounding the substrate surface**, a drop in temperature, and a rise in humidity.

### 7-1. Wounding the Surface Is the Most Effective Method

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There are trial results comparing fruiting-induction methods.

| Treatment | Method | Reflush rate | Quality |
|:--:|:---|:---:|:---:|
| T1, T2 | Oyster-mushroom method (scratching the mycelium, etc.) | Low | Average |
| **T5** | **Wounding the surface** | **Highest** | **Excellent** |

> **For fruiting induction, wounding the surface gives both the highest reflush rate and the best quality.** Poke a few spots on the substrate surface with a knife tip or a nail, or score it crosswise, tearing the mycelium layer. Primordia form at the wound sites.

| Item | Method |
|:---|:---|
| Tool | A knife tip, nail, or bamboo skewer dipped in boiling water and removed |
| Depth | 5-10mm from the surface |
| Number | 2-3 spots on the surface for a bottle, 3-5 spots on one face for a bag |
| Mechanism | The wound becomes a stress signal, and air and light reaching that spot causes primordia to form |
| Caution | Tearing too much splits the fruiting body into several smaller ones |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;To grow one large fruiting body, make just one or two wounds. To get several small ones, make more. In a disaster situation, one large one is better - it takes less work to trim and dry, and its spines hang down fully enough to give a good texture.

### 7-2. Fruiting Conditions

| Item | Incubation stage | **Fruiting stage** | Notes |
|:---|:---:|:---:|:---|
| Temperature | 22-25 degrees | **15-24 degrees.** Optimal is 15-20°C | Range 9°C. The lower it is, the longer and firmer the spines |
| Air humidity | 65-75% | **Fruiting-induction stage 85-95%, growth stage 80-85%** | Too low dries it out, too high makes it mushy |
| **Light** | Not needed | **Indirect light, bright enough to read a newspaper by** | Without it, the spines stay clumped in a mass |
| Ventilation | Minimal | **Ample** | If CO2 builds up, it grows deformed, like coral |
| Duration | - | Fruiting induction 3-7 days, growth 15-20 days | |

> **The lower the temperature, the better the quality.** Grown at 15-18°C, it has longer spines, firmer tissue, and less bitterness. Above 22°C, it grows faster but turns soft and quickly turns yellow. And since turning yellow is what causes the bitterness problem in section 13, harvesting in a cool season also governs storage quality.

### 7-3. How to Create 15-24 Degrees Without Electricity

| Means | Temperature | How to achieve it | Limitation |
|:---|:---:|:---|:---|
| **Spring cycle** | Natural | April to early June | Gets hot by July |
| **Fall cycle** | Natural | September to early November | Fruiting-body initiation stops by December |
| Earth cellar, cave | 12-18°C | Dig 2m or more into a hillside | Usable year-round, the best option |
| Basement | 12-18°C | Use an existing structure | Humidity and light must be adjusted separately |
| North-facing earthen-wall shed | Follows the seasons | Thick earthen walls reduce temperature swings | Falls outside the range in summer and winter |
| Outdoor log cultivation | Natural | Stand or lay logs in partial shade | Entirely at the mercy of the season |

> **Without electricity, the lion's mane mushroom is a spring-and-fall crop. In midsummer, the mycelium dies at 35°C, and in deep winter, fruiting-body initiation stops.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Outdoor log cultivation suits conditions without electricity best. Keep the logs in partial shade and just manage them so they do not dry out, and they fruit on their own in spring and fall. There is no need for a person to adjust temperature, humidity, and light - the season does that job. The only drawback is the long wait to the first harvest.

### 7-4. How to Create Humidity Without Electricity

| Method | Description |
|:---|:---|
| **A bowl of water inside a box or jar** | Put the containers together in a large box and place a water-filled dish or wet sand on the bottom |
| Covering with wet cloth | Cover the top with wet cloth, keeping it raised so it does not touch the mushrooms directly |
| Sprinkling water on the floor and walls | Raises the humidity of the whole room |
| Adjusting vent size | Make the openings small so humidity does not escape. Do not seal them completely |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;**Do not spray water directly onto the mushroom.** If water pools between the lion's mane mushroom's spines, that spot starts turning yellow and bacteria spread. Raise humidity through the air, and spray water only around it. This rule must be kept far more strictly here than with other mushrooms.

### 7-5. Managing Dryness Before Harvest

| Timing | Management |
|:---|:---|
| 3-4 days before harvest | **Stop spraying water entirely and lower humidity slightly** |
| Effect | The color stays white, the tissue firms up, and storability improves |
| Caution | Overdoing it dries and cracks the tips of the spines |

> **These 3-4 days make a major difference to storability.** One harvested while still holding moisture turns yellow within a day, and if dried, the bitterness grows even stronger. Letting it dry out for a few days before harvest is a way to head off and reduce the problem covered in section 13.
