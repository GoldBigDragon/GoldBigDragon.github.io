## 1. Disaster-Preparedness Evaluation

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The lion's mane mushroom (Hericium erinaceus) is an excluded species. It was judged that storability E combined with perenniality B means stockpiling does not work. The assumed score without the gate applied is 42.5 points, equivalent to rank 83 among the 183 valid species. The storability figure is based on the raw form, so drying would normally resolve it, but drying this mushroom brings a separate problem of bitterness and toughening. Perenniality B is the best value among the three excluded mushroom species.

### 1-3. What the SS in Sunlight Means

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The sunlight criterion on the evaluation table asks how much yield remains under 40% shade. For potatoes, around 80% remains; for rice and maize, the drop is much steeper. The SS given to the lion's mane mushroom does not mean it won that competition - it means it was never on that axis to begin with. Fungi have no chlorophyll. The carbon this mushroom uses is cellulose and lignin already locked into oak sawdust, and how bright the sky is has no effect on that stock. The light needed for cultivation is only indirect light bright enough to read a newspaper by, and even that is used only as a shape-forming signal, not as nourishment.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This difference becomes decisive in a situation like a major volcanic eruption or a nuclear winter, where sunlight drops for several years running. When aerosols spread through the stratosphere, cereals, tuber crops (tubers/storage roots), and vegetables collapse all at once. But even then, fallen trees and dry branches remain, and mushrooms are the only route that turns them into protein people can eat.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The lion's mane mushroom adds one more thing on top of this: the vitamin D included in its Micronutrients A rating. When the sky darkens, people also get less sunlight. The vitamin D the skin produces drops, and when a deficiency sets in, bones weaken and immunity falls. But a mushroom's ergosterol converts to vitamin D2 when it receives ultraviolet light, and this is one of the rare species whose composition table lists vitamin D to begin with. That mushrooms are the route that supplies people with vitamin D in a disaster where sunlight is reduced is not a coincidence - it is structural.

> **Mushrooms are the only food source whose yield does not fall even in a world without sunlight, and the lion's mane mushroom is a species that also supplies the vitamin D people would otherwise lack in that world.**

### 1-4. How to Read the Strengths

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The criteria rated SS are four: protein density, sunlight, tillage & digging energy, and yield stability. S covers four: speed to harvest, continuous-cropping tolerance, taste, and prevalence in South Korea. A covers three: micronutrients, water use, and cooking & processing.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Micronutrients A is the highest value among the three excluded species and is, in practical terms, the most important strength. Fourteen nutrients are met, and vitamin D is among them. Compared with enoki mushroom and button mushroom, which meet 13 nutrients for a B, the difference is just one - but that one being vitamin D matters a great deal. The deficiencies commonly seen in disaster situations are thiamine, riboflavin, folate, iron, and vitamin D, and this mushroom meets all five.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Perenniality B is also a value worth noting. It gives three to four harvests from the same substrate, better than enoki's two to three or button mushroom's two to three cycles. Since making a fresh substrate is the biggest labor burden in a disaster situation, getting long use out of one substrate means saving both labor and fuel.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Water use A is also valuable. It only takes enough to maintain substrate moisture, and field-scale irrigation is not needed. In a disaster compounded by drought, this is decisive. Even under conditions where rice and vegetables collapse for lack of water, enough water can be secured to keep a few bags humid.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Seed saving B is also the highest among the three excluded species. Whereas enoki and button mushroom rate C, this mushroom's mycelium subcultures relatively easily. Tissue taken by culture survives well, and switching to log cultivation lets it continue for several years.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;And there is one more major strength that the evaluation table cannot capture in numbers: the risk of misidentification is nearly nonexistent. Section 12 covers this.

### 1-5. How to Read the Weaknesses

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There are three E's: cold hardiness, storability, and non-food value. There are four D's: fertilizer demand, infrastructure needs, temperature range, and seed longevity.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Infrastructure needs D weighs the heaviest. This mushroom requires the substrate to be sterilized. The standard is 121°C under high pressure for 90 minutes, or, at normal pressure, 100°C for 4 hours or 98°C for 6 hours. Without electricity, a drum must be heated with firewood, and that fuel is the biggest constraint in a disaster situation. On top of that, bags and jars are consumed every cycle. Section 3 addresses this problem. In short, switching to log cultivation is the answer: logs are not sterilized, and once inoculated, they last for several years.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Temperature range D and cold hardiness E should be read together. Fruiting body formation occurs at 15-24°C, a range of 9°C. Mycelium grows at 22-25°C and stops below 6°C or above 35°C. Without electricity, this window has to be matched to the season: spring and fall come closest to 15-24°C. In deep winter, fruiting-body initiation stops, and in midsummer the mycelium dies, so a spring-and-fall growing pattern is this mushroom's natural form.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Fertilizer demand D needs to be read carefully for what it actually means. Not a single unit of chemical fertilizer is used. Instead, hardwood sawdust and rice bran go in at about 56kg/m² a year. Without an oak forest and a rice mill nearby, this condition is hard to meet. However, as section 7-3 shows, rice straw, soybean pods, and corn stalks can replace 20-30% of the sawdust, and trial results even show yield increasing with corn stalks.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Storability E, as noted earlier, is normally resolved by drying, but for this mushroom a condition attaches. Simply drying it makes it bitter and tough. A step of blanching before drying, or grinding it into powder, is needed, and section 13 covers this.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Mushroom by-product suitability C is also a real constraint. Only two materials work: hardwood sawdust and rice bran. This contrasts with button mushroom's S, which can use rice straw, manure, shells, and even waste paper. Conifer wood cannot be used, since its resin suppresses the mycelium.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Non-food value E means none of the 8 criteria apply. However, what this criterion evaluates is agricultural uses such as medicinal material, soil improvement, and pest control - it does not mean this mushroom has no pharmacologically active compounds. Hericenones and erinacines have been reported to promote nerve growth factor synthesis, and there is a possibility they could help with mental health under the kind of prolonged, extreme stress a disaster brings. This simply does not fall within the category the evaluation criteria cover.

## 2. What Kind of Fungus Is This?

### 2-1. Name and Taxonomy

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The lion's mane mushroom belongs to phylum Basidiomycota, class Agaricomycetes, order Russulales, family Hericiaceae, genus Hericium. What stands out is that its order differs from other common mushrooms. Enoki, button mushroom, shiitake, and wine cap all belong to order Agaricales, while this mushroom alone belongs to order Russulales. Its cap-less, stem-less, gill-less appearance comes from this difference in lineage.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Its Korean name means "roe deer's rump mushroom," for its resemblance to a white-tailed roe deer's rump. English speakers call it lion's mane for its resemblance to a lion's mane, and in Japan it is called yamabushitake, after its resemblance to the robes of a mountain ascetic monk. All three names point to the same shape: a hanging cluster of white strands.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Ecologically, it is a white-rot fungus that decomposes the dead trunks and wound sites of broadleaf trees. It grows attached to the trunks or dead wood of oak, walnut, beech, maple, and willow. It never grows from the ground - it always grows attached to wood, and is usually found high off the ground. This habit becomes the basis for the safety check in section 12.

### 2-2. Appearance - No Cap, No Stem, No Gills

| Part | Characteristics |
|:---|:---|
| Fruiting body | **Does not form a cap.** Grows as a single mass, 5-25cm |
| Shape | Egg-shaped to hemispherical at first. Later in growth, **white needle-like spines 1-5cm long hang down** |
| **Spines** | A downward-hanging bundle of needles. Spores are produced on this surface |
| Color | **Pure white when young → yellow or yellowish-brown as it matures** |
| Stem | Short and blunt, attached to the wood. Hard to regard as a separate part |
| Gills | **None.** Spines produce the spores instead of gills |
| Ring (annulus) | **None** |
| Volva | **None** |
| Flesh | Soft like a sponge, white |
| Spores | 5.5-7.5 × 5-6.5μm. Subglobose, colorless. Spore print is white |
| Mycelium | White at first → gradually turns yellow → then pink |
| Growth substrate | **Dead broadleaf wood and wound sites. Never grows from the ground** |

> **No cap, no stem, no gills - just a bundle of white needles hanging from wood. This one shape is the lion's mane mushroom's identity card, and it is, by itself, a safety check.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The color change must be remembered. It is pure white when young, turns yellow as it grows, and becomes yellowish-brown later still. **Once it starts turning yellow, it is already too late.** The taste turns bitter and the flesh toughens. The bitterness problem in section 13 is directly tied to this color change.

### 2-3. Life Cycle - From Spore to Mushroom

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;A mushroom's life cycle differs from a plant's. Unlike a plant, where a single seed becomes a single tree, a single mushroom spore cannot become a mushroom on its own.

| Stage | What Happens |
|:---|:---|
| Spore | Drops from the surface of the spines. A haploid cell carrying only half the genes |
| Primary mycelium | The spore germinates and extends into thread-like strands. Having only half the genes, it cannot form a mushroom, and its vigor weakens until it dies out |
| Secondary mycelium | Two primary mycelia of different mating types meet and fuse. Only in this state can a mushroom form |
| Substrate colonization | White mycelium covers the entire sawdust substrate. 20-30 days |
| Mycelium aging | Changes from white to yellow, then to pink. This is a normal maturation process |
| Fruiting induction | Wounding the substrate surface and lowering the temperature causes small white lumps like millet grains to form |
| Fruiting body | The lump swells, and spines hang down from its surface. 15-20 days |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The fact that a single spore does not produce a mushroom matters in practice. If you carefully drop spores in one at a time to conserve them, only primary mycelium grows before dying out. You need to shake in a generous amount of spore powder all at once, so that they meet and fuse with each other inside the substrate.

> **Do not be sparing with spores. A single one will not become a mushroom.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The mycelium turning yellow and then pink is characteristic of this mushroom. With other mushrooms, such a color would raise suspicion of contamination, but for the lion's mane mushroom it is normal maturation. Not knowing this leads people to discard a perfectly healthy substrate. However, if a sour smell or a powdery texture appears along with the color change, that is contamination.

### 2-4. Growth Stages and Time Required

| Stage | Duration | Temperature | Humidity | What to Check |
|:---|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---|
| Substrate prep & sterilization | 1 day | - | Substrate moisture 65-75% | Mix sawdust and rice bran and pack into bags or jars |
| Cooling | 13-14 hours | Substrate down to 18-20°C | - | Must cool through to the center |
| Inoculation | - | - | - | Work quickly to keep out contaminants |
| Mycelium incubation | 20-30 days | 22-25°C | 65-75% | The substrate becomes covered in white, then matures to yellow |
| Fruiting induction | 3-7 days | 15-20°C | 85-95% | **Wound the surface.** White lumps form |
| Growth | 15-20 days | 15-24°C | 80-85% | Spines hang down |
| Harvest | - | - | Manage drier conditions 3-4 days before harvest | 50-60 days from inoculation |
| Reflush | 10-15 day intervals | - | - | 3-4 cycles |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The threshold temperatures must be remembered. Mycelium stops growing below 6°C and above 35°C. And because the mycelium itself generates metabolic heat, the inside of the substrate can exceed 35°C even when the surrounding air is only 30°C. This is why incubation is avoided in midsummer.

### 2-5. The Substrate Is Acidic

| Item | Standard | Notes |
|:---|:---:|:---|
| Substrate pH | **4.0-5.5, mildly acidic** | Lower than most mushrooms |
| Reason | Nutrient breakdown and absorption work well in an acidic environment | |
| How to lower it | Adding rice bran naturally lowers it. If more is needed, use a very dilute vinegar or organic acid | |
| Caution | **Do not add wood ash or lime** | If it turns alkaline, the mycelium will not grow |

> **This is the exact opposite of other mushrooms.** Wine cap and button mushroom use wood ash or lime to make the substrate alkaline and suppress green mold. That method cannot be used for the lion's mane mushroom. Since it requires mild acidity, alkaline treatment is itself a failure. Contamination control here rests on sterilization and spawn quantity, not on alkalinity.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;At 13kcal, calories are close to the lowest value in this document. Filling a day's 2,100kcal with this mushroom alone would require eating 16kg, so the calculation itself does not hold up. The lion's mane mushroom is not a calorie source.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Instead, its value as a supplementary food is high. Protein efficiency is 79.1% of calories, it meets 14 micronutrients, and vitamin D is among them. And when cooked, it has a texture with a grain similar to crab meat or chicken, giving a satisfying chew when meat is unavailable. The Taste S on the evaluation table reflects this.
