## Introduction

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Lion's mane mushroom (Hericium erinaceus) was excluded in the disaster-preparedness evaluation. The reason is that a storability grade of E combines with a perenniality grade of B to rule out stockpiling. Its hypothetical score, if the exclusion gate were not applied, is 42.5 points, ranking 83rd among the 183 valid species. That is the highest score of the three excluded mushrooms this document covers.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Of the two reasons behind the exclusion, the storability grade of E is based on the fresh mushroom alone. Fresh, it turns soft and yellow within three to five days at room temperature. Dried, however, it keeps for over a year. The storability basis on the evaluation table even notes "sharply upgraded when dried." Drying uses almost no fuel or equipment, so half of the disqualifications behind the exclusion are solved with nothing more than a knife and a drying rack. The perenniality grade of B means the same substrate can be harvested three to four times, the best value among the three excluded mushrooms. Compared with enoki mushroom's D and button mushroom's C, this means this mushroom gets the longest use out of a single substrate.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;However, drying lion's mane mushroom has a problem other mushrooms do not. **Dried, it turns bitter and tough.** As the sponge-like flesh dries, it shrinks and its fibers tangle, and rehydrating it never brings back the original texture. Specimens harvested late, once the cap has turned yellow, are especially bitter. The general rule that drying solves storability problems still applies to this mushroom, but simply hanging it out to dry produces something hard to eat. Section 13 covers this problem and how to handle it separately, at length.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The strengths are clear. Its micronutrients grade is A, the highest among the three excluded mushrooms, and it meets 10% of the RDA for 14 nutrients, including **vitamin D**. Its protein density grade is SS, at 79.1% of calories. Water use is also graded A, needing only enough to keep the substrate moist. And above all, the fact that it is a mushroom is decisive. Because it does not photosynthesize, its yield does not fall even if volcanic ash or soot darkens the sky. The SS grade for the sunlight criterion on the evaluation table does not mean it uses light well — it means it sits outside that axis altogether. Under conditions where rice, wheat, and potatoes all collapse together, mushrooms are practically the only food source whose output holds steady.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This mushroom also has a major advantage: safety. Lion's mane mushroom has **no cap, no stem, and no gills — just a mane-like mass of hanging white spines.** No poisonous mushroom with this shape is known, and every other species in the same genus (Hericium) is also edible. Because it grows on wood rather than emerging from soil, there is no room for confusion with Amanita mushrooms either. Unlike enoki mushroom or button mushroom, which need a long, life-or-death identification discussion in Section 12, Section 12 for lion's mane mushroom instead explains why the risk of misidentification is low. In a disaster situation, this is an advantage that is hard to put a value on.
