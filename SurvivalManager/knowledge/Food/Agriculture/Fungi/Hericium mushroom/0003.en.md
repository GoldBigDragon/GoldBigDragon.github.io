## 3. Substrate Materials and Preparation

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There are two paths for the lion's mane mushroom's substrate. One mixes hardwood sawdust with rice bran, packs it into jars or bags, and sterilizes it; the other simply drives spawn straight into oak logs. The first is fast and gives a calculable yield but requires sterilizing fuel and containers. The second is slow but needs no sterilization, and once inoculated it lasts for several years.

> **Without electricity or fuel, log cultivation is the answer. What earned Infrastructure needs a D on the evaluation table was sterilizing equipment and consumed bags - and logs skip both.**

### 3-1. Comparing Cultivation Methods

| Method | Yield | Growing period | Sterilization | Suitability for disasters |
|:---|:---|:---:|:---:|:---|
| **Bottle cultivation** | 356g per 850ml bottle | 40-45 days | **Required** | When containers and fuel are available |
| **Bag cultivation** | 810g per 2,500ml bag | 50-60 days | **Required** | Favorable for higher yield, but bags are consumed |
| **Log cultivation** | 1,195.5g per 0.1m³ | 69+ days, then several years | **Not required** | **Best suited to long-term disasters** |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;It is worth noting that log cultivation gives the highest yield of all: 1,195.5g from 0.1m³, with a biological efficiency of 17.3%. What's more, once inoculated it produces year after year. The drawbacks are heavy initial labor and a long wait to the first harvest, but in a disaster that drags on for years, this method is overwhelmingly the better choice.

### 3-2. Log Cultivation - The Path That Needs No Sterilization

| Item | Standard | Reason |
|:---|:---|:---|
| **Best species** | **Oaks** | Yield 1,195.5g/0.1m³, biological efficiency 17.3% |
| Usable species | Alder, poplar, black locust | Lower than oak, but it grows |
| Others | Walnut, beech, maple, willow | Species it occurs on naturally |
| **Prohibited species** | **Conifers (pine, Korean pine, larch)** | **Resin and terpenes suppress the mycelium** |
| Felling time | After leaf fall and before new buds (November-March) | When the tree has the most nutrients stored |
| Drying | Leave in the shade for 1-2 months after felling to bring moisture to 40-45% | Freshly cut wood has too much moisture |
| Diameter | 10-20cm | If too thick, it takes the mycelium a long time to reach the core |
| Length | Short 15-20cm segments, or 1m logs | Short segments are easier to handle |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Standing short segments up and stacking them suits a disaster situation well. Cut segments 15cm in diameter and 20cm long, spread spawn on the cut faces, then stack the segments on top of one another. The mycelium links up across the cut faces and turns several segments into a single mass. A saw and an axe are the only tools you need.

### 3-3. Sawdust Substrate Formulation

| Material | Bottle cultivation | Bag cultivation |
|:---|:---:|:---:|
| Oak and other hardwood sawdust | Main material | 80% |
| Rice bran | Within the 20-30% range | 20% |
| Wheat bran | Replaces part of the rice bran | |
| Beet pulp | Replaces part of the rice bran | |
| Moisture | 65-67% | 68-75% |
| Substrate amount | 580-620g in an 850ml bottle, 750-800g in a 1,100ml bottle | 2kg per bag |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Rice bran, wheat bran, and beet pulp combined should not exceed 30% of the total. More nutrient source raises the yield, but it also raises the risk that green mold will take over first. Without sterile equipment, it is safer to keep it closer to 20%.

### 3-4. Replacing Sawdust with Agricultural By-products

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The reason mushroom by-product suitability is C on the evaluation table is that it is tied to just two materials: hardwood sawdust and rice bran. However, trial results show a certain amount of substitution is possible.

| Substitute material | Substitution rate | Result |
|:---|:---:|:---|
| Rice straw | 20% | No difference from the control group |
| Soybean pods | 20% | No significant difference in biological efficiency |
| **Corn stalks** | **20%** | **Biological efficiency and average fresh weight increased significantly** |

> **Replacing 20% of the sawdust with corn stalks actually increases the yield.** Chopping up rice straw, soybean pods, and corn stalks that can be found nearby and using them to replace 20-30% of the sawdust makes securing substrate in a disaster far easier, without yield dropping.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;When substituting, chopping the material fine matters. Putting in long pieces of straw or stalk leaves large empty pockets in the substrate that the mycelium cannot cross, and water pools there. Cut it into pieces the size of a fingernail or smaller before use.

### 3-5. How to Sterilize Without Electricity

| Method | Conditions | Fuel | Notes |
|:---|:---|:---:|:---|
| High-pressure sterilization | 121°C, 90 min | Electricity | Standard |
| Atmospheric-pressure sterilization | 100°C for 4 hours or 98°C for 6 hours | High | Standard without electricity |
| **Drum steaming** | 20-30cm of water in the bottom of a 200L drum, containers on a rack above it, steam at 100°C for 4-6 hours | 30-50kg of firewood | Large batches at once |
| Boiling in water | Substrate in a sack, boiled for 2-3 hours | Medium | Not full sterilization, but reduces the risk of contamination |
| **Log cultivation** | **Not needed** | **None** | There is no sterilization step at all |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Drum steaming is the standard method without electricity. Pour water into the bottom, set a rack so the containers do not touch the water, then cover with a lid and seal the gaps with a wet cloth or mud. Keep it going for 4-6 hours from when steam starts rising, then put out the fire and let it cool overnight with the lid still on.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;It is far more fuel-efficient to fill the drum completely and process everything at once each time you light the fire. The firewood needed to sterilize ten containers is not much different from what is needed to sterilize a hundred.

### 3-6. Containers - When There Are No Bags

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Bags and bottles being consumed every cycle is what earned Infrastructure needs a D. The alternative is a container that can be used over and over.

| Container | How to use | Caution |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Glass jars (jam jars, pickling jars) | Withstand sterilization well and last for years | Cracks if cooled too fast. Cool slowly |
| Bamboo tubes | Cut leaving one node intact. Boil whole | Splits easily, cannot be reused many times |
| Earthenware crocks and jars | Heat-resistant and hold humidity well | Heavy, and take longer to sterilize |
| Wooden box covered with cloth | Becomes a flat-bed cultivation form | Highest risk of contamination |
| Heat-resistant plastic bag | The standard method. Plug the opening with cotton or cloth | Withstands only up to 100°C at normal pressure |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;A container has four requirements: it must hold the substrate, keep out contaminants after sterilization, let air through, and leave a face for the fruiting body to emerge from. Since the lion's mane mushroom's fruiting body grows large, a container with a wide opening is favorable. If the opening is narrow, the mushroom gets squeezed and grows deformed.

### 3-7. Growing Space

| Item | Standard | Disaster-situation alternative |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Temperature | Incubation 22-25°C, fruiting 15-24°C | Indoors in spring/fall, an earth cellar, a basement, a north-facing shed |
| Humidity | Incubation 65-75%, fruiting 80-95% | Create it by placing a bowl of water inside a large box or jar |
| **Light** | **Indirect light, bright enough to read a newspaper by** | A spot away from windows. Avoid direct sunlight |
| Ventilation | Fresh air is needed | Make vents top and bottom to use natural convection |
| Space | Shelves in 2-3 tiers | Increases usable area in a small space |

> **Unlike button mushroom, the lion's mane mushroom needs a bit of light.** In complete darkness, the spines do not hang down properly and it stays a clumped mass. But direct sunlight makes it dry out and turn yellow. Indirect light bright enough to read a newspaper by is the right answer - a spot two or three steps from a window, or a shed where light comes in through a gap in the door, works well.

## 4. Securing Spawn

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Mushrooms have no seeds. If the seed supplier closes, there is nowhere to buy spawn, and the method of saving seeds in a jar does not work either. On the evaluation table, the lion's mane mushroom's seed saving is B and seed longevity is D - meaning sterile technique is required but mycelium can be subcultured, and commercial spawn loses vigor within six months to a year. Fungi have three paths that plants do not: keeping spores in storage, taking tissue from a living mushroom to grow, and moving an already-grown substrate or log.

### 4-1. Collecting Spores and Spore Prints

| Step | Method |
|:---|:---|
| Choosing a specimen | A mature specimen with spines hanging down fully. One that has started turning yellow is also fine for spore purposes |
| Removing it | Take the fruiting body off the substrate |
| Placing | Set it **spines-down on aluminum foil** |
| Covering | Cover with a bowl to block drafts |
| Leaving it | 24 hours in a cool place |
| Collecting | Lift the fruiting body away and **white** spore powder is left behind |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This mushroom's spores are white. So **catching them on white paper makes them invisible.** You must catch them on dark-colored paper, foil, or a glass plate. This is different from wine cap, which produces purple-brown spores, or button mushroom, which produces brown spores.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Aluminum foil is better than paper. Paper absorbs moisture, and contaminants hide between its fibers. Foil does not absorb moisture, and its smooth surface preserves the spores as they are. If foil is not available, a glass plate is the next choice.

### 4-2. Preserving Spores - 10 Years Without Electricity

| Step | Method |
|:--:|:---|
| 1 | Catch the spores on foil |
| 2 | Leave the foil in an airy **shaded spot** for one more day to drive off even the last trace of moisture |
| 3 | Fold in half to protect the spore side on the inside, and put it in a zip-lock bag |
| 4 | Add **well-roasted salt or a piece of charcoal** alongside to absorb moisture |
| 5 | Gather the zip-lock bags into a glass jar, **seal it with beeswax**, and keep it in an **earth cellar or basement** where the temperature stays steady all year and no light gets in |

| Storage form | Lifespan | Notes |
|:---|:---:|:---|
| Spore print (dried on foil) | **5-10+ years** | Moisture is excluded, so cell activity stops |
| Liquid spawn (suspended in sterile water) | 3-6 months | Without refrigeration, it germinates early or bacteria multiply |
| Inoculated spawn (sawdust or grain) | 6 months-1 year | Being living mycelium, it keeps consuming nutrients |
| **Inoculated logs** | **Several years** | With just upkeep, it produces year after year |

> **In a disaster situation, spores must not be stored as a liquid. They must be kept as a dried spore print.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Ultraviolet light destroys the DNA in spores, so the storage spot must be deep enough that no light reaches it. The difference in lifespan between spores and plant seeds comes from their structure.

| Point of comparison | Tomato seed | Mushroom spore |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Average lifespan (room temperature) | About 2-4 years | About 5-10+ years |
| Vulnerability | Temperature swings, depletion of its own nutrient reserves | Mold contamination from moisture |
| Cell structure | A complex embryo and nutrient tissue | A single cell with a thick protective wall |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;A seed holds an embryo and nutrients, and over time it consumes them itself and ages to death, but a mushroom spore is just a single cell wrapped in a thick cell wall, with no nutrients to consume. Given the right conditions, it holds on far longer, and cases of reviving spores decades old are not rare in the scientific literature.

> **The Seed longevity D on the evaluation table is based on commercial spawn. Once converted into spore prints, that D effectively loses its meaning. In the first year of a disaster, make 20-30 spore prints from the largest, healthiest specimens and store them in an earth cellar. Even if all the spawn is lost, you can start again five or ten years later.**

### 4-3. Tissue Culture - The Most Reliable Method

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;If you have a living, fresh mushroom in hand, there is almost no reason to use spores. The white flesh inside the fruiting body is already a mass of secondary mycelium whose mating-type fusion is complete. Take a piece of it and put it into a substrate, and the mycelium skips germination and mating and spreads outward right away.

| Step | Method |
|:---|:---|
| Selecting | A young specimen **whose spines are still short and entirely pure white.** Do not use one that has turned yellow |
| Disinfecting | Heat the knife over a flame and let it cool, or dip it in boiling water and take it out |
| Splitting | **Split it lengthwise by hand.** Cutting with a knife drags contaminants from the surface into the interior |
| Taking the sample | Take a rice-grain-sized piece of the white inner flesh from the base that was attached to the wood |
| Inoculating | Tuck it into boiled-and-cooled sawdust substrate or between rice straw |
| Incubating | 22-25°C, in the dark. In 3-5 days, white mycelium spreads out around the piece |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Splitting matters because the inside of the mushroom is close to sterile. All kinds of spores sit on the outer surface, but the flesh inside is clean. Splitting it by hand exposes that clean surface fresh, so the piece taken becomes pure mycelium even without disinfection. The lion's mane mushroom's fruiting body is soft as a sponge, so splitting it is easy, and the base has the firmest tissue, making it the best spot to take flesh from.

### 4-4. Heated Rice-Straw Substrate Method - Raising Spawn from Spores

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In theory, scattering spores on a substrate lets the mycelium spread. In reality, though, green mold and bacteria in the air take over the substrate hundreds of times faster than mushroom spores do. The richer the substrate's nutrients, the more vulnerable it is. Without electricity, instead of an overly rich artificial substrate, a primitive substrate that relies on nature's own defenses is used.

| Step | Method |
|:---|:---|
| Boiling and wringing | Boil rice straw, wheat straw, or soybean pods **in boiling water for at least 1 hour** to sterilize, then wring out the water and let cool |
| Inoculating | Scatter the stored spore powder generously and evenly, without holding back |
| Covering | **Cover with clean cloth** to block drafts and airborne spores |
| Incubating | 22-25°C, in the dark, for 2-4 weeks. Success is when white mycelium covers the straw |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Straw, being low in nutrients, is less appealing to green mold as well, so even though the mycelium grows slowly, it does not lose out in the competition. Transfer the mass of mycelium obtained this way into a sawdust substrate and grow it up into spawn.

### 4-5. Log Transplanting - The Longest-Lasting Spawn

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Splitting a log the mycelium has already spread through and rubbing it onto a new log turns it into spawn. It is primitive, but reliable, and it is the basis for the Seed saving B on the evaluation table.

| Step | Method |
|:---|:---|
| Source log | A log where white mycelium has spread all the way to the cut face, with no signs of contamination |
| Timing | Spring or fall. Avoid midsummer and deep winter |
| Splitting | Split with an axe to expose the mycelium-colonized interior |
| Transferring | Insert a piece of the interior into a hole or the cut face of a new log and seal it with a wooden plug |
| Or | Scoop out well-grown substrate with a spoon and mix it in at 5-10% of the new substrate's weight |
| Limit | Vigor declines past four or five generations. Periodically go back to tissue culture or spores |

### 4-6. Priority Order for Securing Spawn

| Priority | Method | Condition |
|:--:|:---|:---|
| 1 | Commercial spawn | Since prevalence is S, this is the easiest option in normal times. Secure it in advance |
| 2 | Tissue culture | When a fresh mushroom is available. The most reliable |
| 3 | Log/substrate transplant | When something already grown is available. The fastest |
| 4 | From spore print to rice-straw substrate | When everything is lost but stored spores remain |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;What to do in normal times is clear. Grow it once while spawn is available, then make 20-30 spore prints from that mushroom and bury them in an earth cellar. And if possible, inoculate a few logs as well. Logs become a living spawn reserve for several years.
