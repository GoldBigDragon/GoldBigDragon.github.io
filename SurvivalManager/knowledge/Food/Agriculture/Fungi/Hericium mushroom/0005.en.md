## 8. Harvest and Reflush Cycle

### 8-1. Judging Harvest Timing - Pick While White

| Criterion | Details |
|:---|:---|
| **Optimal** | **When the whole fruiting body is milky white and the spines have lengthened enough** |
| Late sign | **The white begins to turn yellow** |
| If too late | **The bitterness intensifies and the flesh toughens.** It starts shedding spores, so powder flies off |
| Spine length | 1-5cm. Long enough, but before the tips dry out |
| Care before harvest | Stop misting 3-4 days ahead |
| Time of day | Early morning |

> **Once yellow appears, it is already too late.** This mushroom's bitterness comes mainly from over-maturity. One picked while white is almost never bitter, while one that has turned yellow is bitter even fresh, and more bitter still when dried. The single biggest way to reduce the bitterness problem covered in Section 13 is not drying technique but harvesting at the right time.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There is no need to discard a specimen that has turned yellow. This stage is actually best for the spore collection described in Section 4, and blanching it to leach out the bitterness makes it edible. It should not be used for storage, though.

### 8-2. Picking Technique

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Grip the base of the fruiting body with your hand and twist it gently free. Pulling it off by force tears out chunks of substrate, and contaminants enter through that wound. Cut the stub still attached to the substrate away cleanly with a knife, since a leftover stub rots and draws in bacteria and fungus gnats.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;**Do not wash** the harvested mushroom. Its sponge-like flesh soaks up water and turns yellow and soft within a day. If debris is stuck to it, brush it off with a dry cloth or a soft brush.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Process it the same day you harvest it. Cook what you plan to eat right away, and for anything you plan to store, tear it apart and start drying it that same day. Putting it off by even a day lets the color change and the bitterness rise.

### 8-3. Reflush

| Item | Details |
|:---|:---|
| Rest period | 10-15 days after harvest |
| Care during rest | Remove leftover stubs from the surface, keep the humidity up, and hold it at 22-25°C |
| **Inducing the reflush** | **Damage the surface again.** This does the most to raise the reflush rate |
| 2nd-flush yield | 50-70% of the first flush |
| 3rd-4th flush | Gradually declines |
| Total harvests | **3-4 times.** The highest of the three excluded mushrooms covered here |
| Log cultivation | Spring and autumn, every year for several years |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This table is what the perenniality grade of B on the evaluation table refers to. Enoki mushroom manages two to three flushes (D) and button mushroom two to three cycles (C), while this mushroom yields three to four. Making a fresh substrate is the heaviest labor in a disaster situation, so getting one more harvest out of the same substrate means saving fuel and labor.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;It is important not to let the substrate dry out during the rest period. If it dries, the mycelium dies and there is no reflush. Even so, keep the humidity up through the surrounding air rather than pouring water directly onto it.

### 8-4. Yield

| Method | Yield | Period |
|:---|:---:|:---:|
| Bottle culture, 850ml | 356g | 40-45 days |
| Bag culture, 2,500ml | 810g | 50-60 days |
| **Log cultivation, 0.1m³** | **1,195.5g** | 69 days or more, then several years |
| Biological efficiency (log, oak) | 17.3% | |
| 20% corn-stalk substitution | Significant increase in fresh weight | |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;One bag yields 810g. Fresh, 810g is 105kcal and 21g of protein. It means almost nothing for calories, but it matters for protein and vitamins D and B. Running twenty bags as one batch gives you roughly a handful a day for a little over a month, in both spring and autumn.

> **Planning around lion's mane mushroom's yield in terms of calories will only disappoint you. Its real value is as a supplementary food supplying 14 micronutrients including vitamin D, and as an ingredient that gives a crab-like chew once meat has run out.**

## 9. Contamination, Pests, and Diseases

### 9-1. Green Mold Is the Number One Cause

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The most common reason mushroom cultivation fails in a disaster situation is not pests or disease, but losing the substrate to a competing mold. By far the most common culprit is Trichoderma, known as green mold. Lion's mane mushroom uses a sterilized substrate that has to stay slightly acidic, which makes this risk especially high. **Green mold thrives in slightly acidic conditions.**

| Trait | Lion's Mane Mycelium | Green Mold |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Color | White → pale yellow → pale pink (normal maturing) | White at first, soon **blue-green** |
| Texture | A tangled layer of mycelial threads. Springy to the touch | **Fine and crumbly, like powder** |
| Spreading speed | 3-5mm a day | 10-30mm a day, **3-6 times faster** |
| Smell | Forest soil, a mushroom aroma | A musty smell, sometimes coconut-like |
| Boundary | Even, following the substrate | **Radiating out from round spots** |

> **Green mold has already spread before its color even shows.**
> The blue-green appears once spores have formed - that is, after it has already begun reproducing. By that point, you should assume a significant part of the substrate is already lost. Tell it apart early by spreading speed and texture, not color.

> **And because this mushroom's own mycelium turns yellow and pink, judging by color alone will make you throw away a perfectly healthy substrate.** Always check texture, smell, and speed together.

### 9-2. Alkaline Treatment Is Not an Option

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Wine cap and button mushroom suppress green mold by making the substrate and casing soil alkaline with wood ash or lime. Lion's mane mushroom cannot use this method, because its substrate has to stay at a pH of 4.0 to 5.5. Making it alkaline does cut down green mold, but it also stops the mushroom's own mycelium from growing.

| What You Can Use Instead | Why It Works |
|:---|:---|
| **Sterilize thoroughly** | 100°C at atmospheric pressure for 4-6 hours. Skimp here and you will pay for it |
| **Use plenty of spawn** | 5-10% of the substrate's weight. Whoever occupies the space first wins |
| **Keep nutrient supplements under 30%** | Too much rice bran or wheat bran lets competing molds explode first |
| **Stack containers with gaps between them** | Air needs to move so metabolic heat can vent and mold cannot spread |
| **Inoculate right after rain** | Airborne falling spores are at their lowest |
| **Change the incubation site every year** | Spores build up if you keep using the same spot |
| **Switch to log cultivation** | **The bark and the wood itself are the defense** |

> **The fundamental way to avoid the contamination problem altogether is log cultivation.** There is no sterilization to fail because none is done, the wood's own tissue and bark block invading organisms, and the inoculated mycelium takes over the whole log over several years. If a sawdust substrate keeps falling to green mold, it is better to switch methods.

### 9-3. When You Find Contamination

| Situation | Response |
|:---|:---|
| **A small spot on one container's surface** | Remove just that container right away. Move it **without opening it** |
| **It has spread to several containers** | Move the entire incubation site. Empty the original site, wash it, and let it dry |
| **The whole substrate has gone blue-green** | Discard all of it. Burn it, or bury it far from the growing site |
| **A sour smell from the substrate** | That is bacteria. Discard it and lengthen the sterilization time next cycle |

> **Do not put contaminated substrate back into your compost.**
> Green mold spores survive in a compost pile and come right back into next year's substrate. Always burn it, or bury it deep somewhere separate from the growing site.

### 9-4. Pests

| Pest | Damage | Response |
|:---|:---|:---|
| **Fungus gnats (sciarids)** | They eat into the mycelium, and their larvae infest the fruiting body. They also carry green mold spores | Block the opening physically with a fine cloth. If they appear, use a water-dish trap or sticky paper |
| **Mites** | The mycelium turns brown and stops growing | Keep conditions dry and isolate any affected section |
| **Slugs** | They tear into young fruiting bodies. The soft spines make this mushroom especially vulnerable | Ring the area with a band of ash or sawdust. Hand-pick them at night |
| **Squirrels, rats, birds** | In log cultivation, they carry off the fruiting bodies | Cover with netting, or ring the logs with thorny branches |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Slug damage is especially severe for this mushroom. With no cap and soft flesh, it is easy prey anywhere. For outdoor log cultivation, ring the base of the logs with a band of ash, and check at night after rain to hand-pick slugs.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Without pesticides, control comes down to physical barriers and cleanliness. Cover ventilation openings with two layers of fine cloth, do not pile spent substrate next to the growing site, and clear away any dropped fruiting-body scraps as soon as they fall.

## 10. Diagnosing Growth Abnormalities

| Symptom | Cause | Action |
|:---|:---|:---|
| No mycelium visible even 5 days after inoculation | Inoculated while the substrate was still hot; the spawn died | Cool the substrate to 18-20°C and re-inoculate |
| Mycelium was spreading, then stopped | **Substrate temperature exceeded 35°C**, or lack of moisture | Space the containers apart to vent heat and lower the ambient temperature |
| Mycelium turns yellow or pink | **This is normal maturing** | Leave it alone. If the texture and smell are normal, there is no problem |
| Mycelium is fine but no mushrooms form | **The surface was not wounded**, the temperature was not lowered, or there is no light at all | Puncture the surface, move it to 15-20°C, and add weak light |
| Primordia form, then dry up and die | Insufficient humidity | Raise it to 85-95% with a water dish and a damp cloth |
| **The fruiting body branches like coral or cauliflower** | **Excess carbon dioxide** from insufficient ventilation | Increase ventilation substantially |
| **The spines stay clumped instead of hanging down** | **Insufficient light** | Add indirect light bright enough to read a newspaper by |
| The fruiting body discolors to yellow | Over-maturity, temperature above 22°C, direct sunlight | Harvest on time and move it somewhere cooler and shadier |
| Spine tips dry out brown | The air is too dry | Mist the surroundings to raise humidity |
| The fruiting body softens and weeps brown liquid | **Water landed directly on the mushroom.** Bacterial soft rot | Do not mist the mushroom itself. Ventilate and remove any diseased ones |
| The fruiting body is small and split into many pieces | Too many surface wounds were made | Next cycle, make only one or two wounds |
| Almost no yield on the second flush | The substrate's nutrients are exhausted, or it dried out during the rest period | Keep the humidity up and wound the surface again |
| The substrate turns black and sour | Bacterial growth; the substrate has reached the end of its life | Discard it and recycle it as spent substrate |

## 11. Spent Substrate Recycling

### 11-1. Spent Substrate Is Not Waste

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Once the mushrooms are done fruiting, the substrate is organic matter whose wood has already been broken down by mycelium. Because white-rot fungus has already broken down a substantial share of the lignin, it is far better for the field than raw sawdust. The evaluation table's fertilizer-demand grade of D refers to 56kg of organic matter input a year, and what matters is that this 56kg is not used once and gone - it keeps circulating.

| Use | Method |
|:---|:---|
| **Base fertilizer** | Spread over the field in autumn and till it in. A major source of organic matter and potassium |
| **Mulch** | Laid 5cm thick between crops to block weeds and moisture loss |
| **Another mushroom's substrate** | Can be mixed into wine cap's wood-chip substrate |
| **Compost accelerant** | Mixed into a compost pile, it speeds up decomposition |
| **Earthworm bedding** | Earthworms eat it readily, and you get worm castings from it |
| **Seedbed soil** | Mixed with sand, it becomes a growing medium for raising seedlings |
| **Fuel** | Fully dried, it burns with weak heat, but it does burn |

```
Hardwood sawdust + rice bran → substrate → mushroom harvest (3-4 times) → spent substrate → field organic matter
                                                                         └ earthworms → worm castings
```

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There is one caveat, though. Lion's mane mushroom's spent substrate still has a fair amount of woody material left, so its carbon-to-nitrogen ratio is high. Not as high as raw sawdust, but putting a lot of it straight into the field lets soil microbes draw nitrogen out of the soil as they break it down. There are two fixes: pile the spent substrate separately for a few more months to let it rot further before applying it, or add a nitrogen source such as diluted urine or chicken manure along with it.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Never put contaminated substrate back into the field or compost. Green mold spores will simply carry straight into next year's substrate.

### 11-2. The Wood After Log Cultivation

| Condition | Use |
|:---|:---|
| A log that has finished fruiting | It has crumbled and breaks apart by hand. Put it straight into the field or use it as mulch |
| A log that is still solid | If the mycelium is still alive, split it and use it to inoculate new logs |
| A fully decayed log | It is close to leaf mold by now. Good as potting soil or seedbed soil |

### 11-3. Managing the Growing Space Year by Year

| Year | Management |
|:--:|:---|
| Every cycle | Empty the space, wash it with water, and dry it in the sun |
| 1 year | Fine to keep using the same space |
| 2-3 years | Safer to move to a different space. Contaminant spores accumulate |
| Emergency measure | Coat the walls and floor with lime-sulfur solution or strong wood-ash lye |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Alkaline treatments are fine on walls and floors. Since it is the space and not the substrate, it is not bound by the pH restriction. After finishing a cycle, leave the space empty for a few days with the door open to let sunlight in. Ultraviolet light cuts down falling spores substantially. It is a method that needs neither electricity nor chemicals.
