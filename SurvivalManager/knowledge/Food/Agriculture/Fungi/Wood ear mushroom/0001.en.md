## Introduction
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The wood ear mushroom (Auricularia auricula-judae) received an overall grade of A and 48.5 points in the evaluation (rank 30 of 183 valid species). It is a gelatinous mushroom that grows attached to dead broadleaf branches like an ear. Its greatest trait is that it does not die from repeated drying and wetting. In drought it dries to a black crust, and when rain comes it swells back into jelly within hours, so in cultivation too the mycelium and young fruiting bodies survive even if you forget to water for several days, and it tolerates neglect. It is also unrivaled in storage: when dried its volume shrinks to 10%, it returns to nearly its original size in water, and dried wood ear lasts 3 years at room temperature. It does not photosynthesize, so yield does not fall when sunlight falls (sunlight SS). Its weak points are calories (13 kcal per 100 g raw) and a slippery, crunchy texture (taste B), so it is not a staple but supplies iron, dietary fiber, and vitamin D and adds bulk to broth. On the premise of no electricity or sterilization equipment, this document covers growing wood ear on logs and waste corrugated cardboard, carrying on your own spawn, and drying it to eat for years.

## 2. Mushroom Overview
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;It belongs to the genus Auricularia of the family Auriculariaceae, order Auriculariales, class Agaricomycetes, phylum Basidiomycota, and is a white-rot fungus that decomposes dead broadleaf trees. Its most distinctive ecological trait is drought tolerance.

| Feature | Details |
|:---|:---|
| Fruiting body | Ear- or shell-shaped. 2-10 cm across, 1-3 mm thick. Blackish brown to purple-brown, shriveling black when dry. The tissue is gelatinous, elastic, and hard to tear |
| Upper and lower surfaces | The upper surface is gray-brown covered with short hairs (hairy wood ear has clear hairs). The lower surface (hymenium) is smooth and glossy with shallow veins, and the spores come from there |
| Attachment, spores | Almost no stem; attached to the wood at one point on the side or back. Spores are white and slightly curved cylinders |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;A spore has only half the genes and a single nucleus, so one spore does not produce a mushroom. So scatter spore powder generously so that spores meet and mate within the substrate.

| Stage | Period and temperature | Key point |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Spawn run | About 30 days (sawdust), 60-90 days (log), 21-22℃ | The substrate is covered with white mycelium |
| Pinning, growth | 10-20 days each, 20-28℃ | Jelly droplets form on the surface and spread into ear shapes |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Per 100 g, raw wood ear has 13 kcal, 0.79 g of protein (24.3% of calories), and about 4 g of dietary fiber (the highest level among mushrooms); dried, it has about 271 kcal and about 10.3 g of protein. It is said to be rich in iron, but values in the literature vary widely. Ergosterol is converted by ultraviolet light, so vitamin D increases with sun drying, and because of the fiber, eating a lot suddenly can cause stomach pain and diarrhea.

## 3. Logs, Waste Cardboard, and Substrate
> **The strength of wood ear is not yield but the number of options. If one is blocked, you can fall back on another material.**

| Material | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Logs | Best: mulberry, alder, oak (the trees on which it most commonly grows in nature). Possible: willow, poplar, hackberry, black locust (soft but fruiting well; short-lived). 8-15 cm across, 1-1.2 m long or 20-30 cm short logs. Fell in November-February after the sap has gone down and dry for 1-2 months (shorter than shiitake; do not dry them hard) |
| Sawdust substrate | Hardwood sawdust (oak, poplar) 85-90% + rice bran 5-10% (add little) + 1% calcium carbonate or shell powder, moisture 65-70% (one or two drops of water form between the fingers when squeezed), 1.2-1.5 kg per bag |
| Waste cardboard | A hidden resource in a disaster. Take plain brown cardboard with little printing and no coating (leave out glossy paper and plastic-coated) and remove all tape, staples, and labels; tear it into palm-sized pieces or cut it into 5 cm strips. Pour boiling water over it for 1-2 hours, or soak it in limewater for 12 hours, then squeeze until no water drips. Layer it with spawn between the sheets, pack it into sacks or tubs, and make holes so air circulates. Mycelium spreads extremely fast along the air layers inside the corrugation |
| Straw types | Cut rice or wheat straw to 5 cm or shorter and boil it or soak it in lime (alone the yield is low, so mix half and half with sawdust). Rice hulls up to 30% and bean pods up to 20% can be mixed in, and corn stalks are crushed and boiled |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Sterilization without electricity uses a 200 L drum (20-30 cm of water at the bottom, substrate bags or sacks piled loosely on a rack, lid on, wrapped in a wet straw sack or plastic): heat for 4-6 hours after the steam fills it (good when a skewer pushed into the core is too hot to hold) and let it cool overnight with the lid on (opening it while hot draws in spores with the air). Straw and cardboard are soaked in boiling water for 1-2 hours, or cut straw or cardboard is soaked in limewater for 12-24 hours (no fuel). If there is no fuel either, pile straw 1-1.5 m high and ferment it to 60-70℃. Wood ear does not grow well on a rich substrate, so the sterilization burden is small.

## 4. Obtaining Spawn
> **The most valuable asset in wood ear farming is not the mushrooms but the logs and cardboard stacks full of mycelium. They are the seed of the next generation.**

| Method | Details |
|:---|:---|
| Mycelium transfer (easiest) | Slip one sheet of mycelium-covered cardboard into a new stack of cardboard, or crush mycelium-filled wood and mix it into new boiled and cooled sawdust or cardboard |
| Splitting spawn logs | Split a log with well-spread mycelium into finger-thick wedges (taking the parts where white mycelium is dense) and hammer them into the holes of new logs (most reliable). Split spawn logs die if they dry |
| Tissue culture | Tear a thick, elastic, fresh specimen (not dried or softened ones) by hand, not with a knife, to expose a fresh cut face, take a rice-grain-sized piece of flesh from the very center, and slip it between boiled and cooled straw, sawdust, or cardboard. White down spreads in 3-5 days in a dark place at 21-25℃ |
| Spore print | Carefully detach a fresh, thick specimen whose edge has just opened from its base on the wood and place it on aluminum foil with the smooth hymenium side down; white spore powder is left behind. Dry the foil in the shade for one more day and seal it (never store in liquid). In the first year make 20-30 sheets and save them in a cave |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Scatter spore powder generously on boiled and cooled rice straw and incubate in a dark place at 21-25℃ for 2-4 weeks to make spawn. Seed saving S is thanks to mycelium transfer and splitting spawn logs.

## 5. Inoculation, Spawn Run, Fruiting, and Harvest
| Inoculation | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Logs | Late March-early May (possible later than shiitake), daytime 15-25℃, on a cloudy, windless day. Hole diameter 1-1.2 cm, depth 1.5-2 cm, vertical spacing 5-8 cm, horizontal spacing 8-12 cm in a zigzag (30-40 holes for a log 10 cm across and 1 m long). Pack the spawn tightly, seal with a wooden plug, a Styrofoam plug, or wax (paraffin and pine resin 6 : 4), and coat both cut ends too. Without wax, smear on kneaded mud or cover the plug with wet moss |
| Sawdust, cardboard | Possible in all four seasons (only the substrate temperature needs to be right). After cooling to 25℃ or below (spawn dies if hot), mix about 50 g of spawn per kg of substrate evenly through the whole substrate (mixed inoculation works well) or put it on top; for cardboard, scatter spawn on each sheet as you stack them. Heat tools in a fire and let them cool before use, and in a small windless space sprinkle water on the floor and walls to settle the dust |

| Spawn run | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Temperature, light | For 3-5 days after inoculation, 21-22℃ (do not disturb); afterward 21-25℃, humidity 60-70%. Growth only stops at 0-3℃ without dying, and above 32℃ the mycelium weakens and contaminants take the lead. Block light during the spawn run |
| Logs | For the first month after inoculation, stack them densely on supports and cover with straw or branches. After that, stack loosely in a log-cabin pattern, swap top and bottom and inside and outside every 1-2 months, and stand them slanted and crossed 5-6 months after inoculation. Burying 20-30 cm short logs half into the ground is possible right after inoculation and suits wood ear especially well |
| Water | Only enough that the logs do not dry (a damp surface actually invites contaminants). In summer once every 7-10 days at dusk; none in winter. Wide cracks in the cut face mean a lack of water |
| Checks | White mycelium shows at the inoculation holes (green, black, or orange means contamination). Sawdust is half white on the surface in 7-10 days (a spawn vigor problem if it has not spread after 2 weeks), cardboard is covered sheet by sheet in 3-5 days, and white mycelium appears on the cut face of logs after 3-5 months (failed attachment if still none after 6 months). A sour, musty, or ammonia smell is abnormal |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The combination of a rise in humidity (from 60-70% during the spawn run to 85-95% for pinning, the strongest signal) and light (moving from a dark culture room to a place with indirect light bright enough to read a newspaper makes primordia form and deepens the color) works best for fruiting. In bag cultivation, cutting an X into the surface or the bag with a knife makes the fruiting bodies concentrate there; logs are soaked in cold water for 6-12 hours (shorter than shiitake), and a day-night temperature difference of 5-8℃ is only an aid. Fruiting temperature is 20-28℃ (slow at 15℃ or below, soft above 30℃), growth humidity is around 80%, and ventilation is needed (if trapped, the flesh gets thin). Mist water 2-3 times a day like fog or wet the floor of the growing area (safest). Water jets hitting the fruiting bodies directly, or water pooling on them, bring bacterial soft rot. Wood ear does not die right away when short of water but dries up and waits.

| Harvest | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Timing, method | When it has spread well to 5-8 cm across, the edge has opened into a wavy shape, and the flesh is at its thickest (left longer it thins and goes soft). It is late once white spore powder starts to settle on the lower surface. In the early morning (picking by day, when they hold more water, makes drying hard), hold the base and twist to detach. Since sizes come out uneven on one substrate, harvest over several days |
| Re-flush | Harvest over 10-20 days, cut the water and dry the substrate surface for 7-10 days (dry rest), then raise humidity and water again, and new primordia form after 7-14 days. The switch of drying the substrate once and then rewetting it calls up new mushrooms |
| By method | Cardboard: 30-40 days to the first harvest, 1-2 re-flushes, 2-3 months in total. Sawdust bags: 50-60 days, 2-3 re-flushes, 3-4 months. Logs: 150-180 days, every spring and autumn, 3-4 years. Buried short logs: 120-150 days, every year, 2-3 years. One log (10 cm across, 1 m long) gives 1-1.5 kg of fresh wood ear over 3-4 years |

> **It is best to run all three substrates at once with wood ear. Cardboard gives the first harvest within a month, sawdust carries you through the year, and logs prepare you for the next several years.**

## 6. Contamination, Pests, and Problem Diagnosis
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The first cause of failure in mushroom farming in a disaster is a competing mold. Trichoderma (green mold) also starts as white down, and by the time green appears hundreds of millions of spores have already formed, so tell it by speed and smell. Wood ear mycelium grows 3-4 mm a day with a faint mushroom aroma, while Trichoderma grows noticeably faster at 7-10 mm a day and smells sweet like coconut or damp and musty.

> **If you find orange Neurospora (red bread mold), never open the bag. Its spores are extremely light, and once released you lose the whole culture room. Burn the bag as it is or bury it deep in the ground.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Because wood ear uses a high-moisture substrate, Mucor and bacterial slime (sticky mucus and a sour stench) appear more often than with shiitake.

| Situation | Response |
|:---|:---|
| Contaminated cardboard stack | Pull out only the contaminated sheets and move the rest to a dry place (the layers are separate, so partial recovery is possible) |
| Contaminated inoculation hole on a log | Pull the plug, cut out the contaminated part, coat with limewater, put in new spawn, and seal again |
| Wide contamination on the log surface | Coat with lime powder and keep it 1 m or more away from other logs |
| Green through the log | No recovery. Remove it and burn it |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Do not throw contaminated substrate onto the compost heap (it becomes a spore factory). Prevention is generous inoculation (mixed inoculation), minimal rice bran (the poorer the nutrition, the less there is for contaminants to eat), moisture content of 65-70% or less (with too much water, bacteria come first), a culture temperature of 21-22℃ (above 25℃ Trichoderma gains the advantage), ventilation by not packing the substrate hard and making holes in the bag, and cleaning by keeping spent substrate and mushroom stumps away from the growing site. The pests are fungus gnats (screens, boards smeared with tree sap or syrup, remove spent substrate at once), mites (keep humidity steady, isolate fruiting substrate), slugs (they love the gelatinous flesh and eat large pieces: pick by hand at night, ring of ash or sawdust), and cigarette beetles and Indian meal moths in dried wood ear (dry completely, seal, and add roasted salt or charcoal).

| Symptom | Cause and action |
|:---|:---|
| No mycelium spreading after inoculation | Substrate temperature too high, weak spawn vigor, or low moisture. Lower the temperature to 21-22℃ and inoculate again |
| Mycelium has spread but no fruiting | Humidity or light too low. Raise humidity to 85-95% and move to a place with indirect light |
| Fruiting bodies thin and pale | Too little light, too much carbon dioxide. Raise brightness and ventilate |
| Fruiting bodies soften and stink | Too wet, poor ventilation, bacterial soft rot. Stop watering, ventilate, and remove diseased ones |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;A good growing site has 60-70% shade under a broadleaf forest and a gentle breeze (avoid places with strong valley winds or no wind), on a path you pass every day; wood ear likes a brighter place than shiitake. Split the living parts of spent logs and spent substrate to inoculate new logs or cardboard; crushed ones go to compost close to leaf mold, or serve as substrate for king stropharia (which eats spent wood ear logs as they are), and spent cardboard substrate absorbs well, so it can be used as livestock bedding and then returned to compost.

## 7. Mistaking Poisonous Mushrooms and Safety Checks
> **No highly poisonous species is known in the genus Auricularia, so it is the mushroom with the lowest risk of confusion. But if dried wood ear is soaked wrongly, it can hurt you badly.**

| Item | Wood ear | What could be confused |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Tissue | Gelatinous. Springs back when pressed and released | Exidia-type jelly fungi are also gelatinous but blacker, smaller, and tasteless |
| Shape | Ear- or shell-shaped, shallow veins | Snow fungus (white jelly fungus) clumps in a petal shape and is also edible |
| Lower surface | Smooth and glossy | If it has gills or pores, it is not wood ear |
| Dry state | Hardens like a black crust and swells in hours when soaked in water | If it does not swell in water and stays hard, it is a woody fungus; do not eat it |
| Where it grows | Branches and trunks of dead broadleaf trees | Anything growing from the ground is not wood ear |

> **A black lump that does not swell in water and stays hard is not wood ear. This one check filters out most mistakes.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;People are seriously hurt by wood ear not from picking the wrong mushroom but from soaking dried wood ear wrongly. If it sits in lukewarm water at room temperature for a long time, especially a day or more, bacteria multiply in the water. So soak it in cold water where possible for 2-3 hours (about 30 minutes in warm water) and no longer, never leave it overnight (even more so in summer), cook it right after soaking, and throw away any left over after soaking, however wasteful. You cannot judge it by smell, so judge by time.

> **The greatest danger of wood ear is not poisonous mushrooms but water it has soaked in for too long. Keeping to the time is the only defense.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Eating a lot uncooked can cause photosensitive dermatitis (the skin exposed to sunlight swells and itches; it disappears with heating), and because of the fiber, eating a lot suddenly causes stomach pain and diarrhea, so increase from a small amount. It contains a component that slows blood clotting, so do not eat much if you are at risk of bleeding. Moldy dried wood ear cannot be saved by washing, so throw it away. In wild gathering, pick only from trees (anything from the ground is not wood ear), and it is wood ear only if it swells like jelly in water.

## 8. Storage, Cooking, and Annual Schedule
> **The storability problem of mushrooms is almost entirely solved by drying alone. Wood ear evolved to withstand drying and rehydration, so its losses are the smallest.**

| Storage | Details |
|:---|:---|
| Sun drying | Shake off soil and wood bits and do not wash. Straighten the shriveled ones by hand and lay them with the hymenium up for 1-2 days of fine weather (fresh wood ear is about 90% water). Bring them in at sunset to avoid night dew, and leave them in shade and wind for the last half day |
| Keeping | Drying shrinks the volume to 10%, so storage efficiency per volume is extremely high. Completely dried and sealed, it keeps 3 years at room temperature |
| Others | Powder (2 years or more; grind after complete drying and add straight to porridge and soup), smoke drying (2 years or more; works on rainy days and attracts fewer insects) |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Soak in cold water for 2-3 hours while keeping to the time rule in Section 7, cut off the hard base that was attached to the wood, and blanch in boiling water for 1-2 minutes to remove the photosensitivity from eating it raw. Stir-fries, soups, and dressed dishes take 5-15 minutes, and boiling for 30 minutes or more makes it much softer. Taste B comes from dislike of the texture, so keeping some ground into powder widens its uses.

| Month | Work |
|:---|:---|
| Jan-Feb | Fell logs (possible in midwinter), check the drying, and re-dry stored dried mushrooms. In February cut logs to length, prepare for inoculation, and collect and prepare cardboard (remove tape and staples in advance) |
| Mar-Apr | In March, once the temperature exceeds 15℃, drill and inoculate logs and make sawdust substrate. In April finish inoculating, with a first contamination check on days 3-5, and inoculate cardboard substrate |
| May-Aug | In May lay the logs down for the main stage, set up shade, and take the first cardboard harvest (the earliest harvest). In June harvest and dry the spring flush before the rainy season, and sawdust substrate fruits. In July shade, slug control, and humidity management (do not let it exceed 30℃). In August turn the logs, check for fungus gnats, and give the substrate a dry rest (the most dangerous month) |
| Sep-Oct | Prepare for the autumn flush. Once the temperature drops, induce fruiting with soaking and higher humidity |
| Nov-Dec | November is the best time to fell logs (just after leaf fall), stand them, and split spawn logs. In December prepare for winter, tidy spent substrate, and check stored goods (logs can overwinter in the open) |

> **The rhythm of wood ear farming is the repetition of dry and wet. The switch of drying the substrate once and then rewetting it calls up new mushrooms.**
