## Introduction

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The wood ear mushroom (Auricularia auricula-judae) is a gelatinous mushroom that grows on dead hardwood branches, attached in the shape of an ear. Its name comes from Chinese characters meaning "ear of the tree," and its appearance really is like that. It does not have the cap, stalk and gills of shiitake or oyster mushrooms; instead it clings tightly to the wood like a piece of wet leather. This unusual form is the source of all of the wood ear's strengths and weaknesses.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;What makes the wood ear better than any other mushroom is that it does not die from repeated drying and wetting. In a drought, it dries up as thin as a sheet of paper and becomes a black scab stuck to the wood. When rain comes, within a few hours it swells back into jelly. This trait carries straight over into cultivation. Even if watering is forgotten for a few days, the mycelium and young fruiting bodies do not die. Unlike other mushrooms that demand a lot of attention, the wood ear tolerates neglect. In conditions such as a disaster, where people cannot be on hand all the time, this forgiving nature is a great asset.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In storage, too, the wood ear stands alone. When dried, its volume shrinks to 10%, and when soaked in water it returns to almost its original size. Other mushrooms lose their texture when dried and rehydrated, but because the wood ear is gelatinous, its chewiness actually comes back. A handful of dried wood ear makes a meal's worth, and that handful keeps for 3 years at room temperature. Where storage space is tight and there is no refrigeration, few foods offer higher storage efficiency per volume.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;And the very fact that it is a mushroom is the wood ear's greatest strength. It does not photosynthesize. Even under conditions where volcanic ash or soot darkens the sky and rice, wheat and potatoes all fail together, the wood ear's yield does not fall. What it feeds on is not sunlight but the carbon that the tree has already stored. The wood ear's SS on the Sunlight criterion in the evaluation table does not mean that it uses light well; it means that it stands outside the axis of light altogether.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Its weaknesses are calories and texture. At only 13kcal per 100g raw, it cannot be a staple. The drop of the Taste criterion to B in the evaluation table is also because quite a few people find the squishy, crunchy texture off-putting. The wood ear is not a crop that fills the stomach; its role is to supply iron, dietary fiber and vitamin D and to add body to a broth. This document assumes there is no electricity or sterilization equipment at all, and covers how to grow wood ear on logs and waste paper, keep the spawn going on one's own, and dry the harvest to eat for several years.
