## 3. Substrate Materials and Preparation

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Wood ear is one of the cultivated mushrooms that is least choosy about materials. It accepts logs, sawdust, rice straw, rice hulls, and even waste corrugated cardboard. The by-product suitability grade of B in the evaluation table refers to this breadth. When the electricity is out, this breadth translates directly into the odds of survival, because it means that mulberry can be used if there is no oak, and boxes can be used if there is no sawdust.

> **The strength of wood ear is not its yield but the number of options it offers. When one is blocked, another material can be turned to.**

### 3-1. Choosing Logs

| Item | Standard | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Best species | Mulberry, alder, oaks | These are the trees on which wood ear most commonly occurs in nature |
| Acceptable species | Willow, poplar, hackberry, black locust | Soft, but the mushrooms come up well. In exchange, the logs have a short life |
| Prohibited species | Conifers such as pine and Korean pine | Resin and terpenes suppress the mycelium |
| Diameter | 8-15cm | Trees thinner than those used for shiitake are also used |
| Length | 1-1.2m, or 20-30cm short logs | Short logs can also be stood upright and buried in the ground |
| Felling season | November to February (in South Korea) | After the sap has gone down |
| Drying | 1-2 months | Dried for a shorter time than shiitake logs |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Wood ear logs are not dried as thoroughly as shiitake logs. Whereas shiitake requires a moisture content of 38-42%, wood ear establishes well even at 40-45%. This is because wood ear mycelium tolerates wetter conditions than shiitake. However, this also means conditions that favor contaminating microbes, so it becomes more important to inoculate generously so that the mycelium gets established first.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The softer the wood, the sooner the mushrooms appear and the sooner they stop. Inoculated on willow or poplar, the logs fruit within 5-6 months but are spent in 2 years. Oak has a later first harvest in exchange for lasting 3-4 years. In a disaster, it is safer to keep a mix of the two. Soft wood carries you through this year, and hard wood prepares you for the next several years.

### 3-2. Sawdust Substrate

| Material | Proportion | Role |
|---|:---:|---|
| Broadleaf sawdust (oak, poplar) | 85-90% | Main carbon source |
| Rice bran | 5-10% | Source of nitrogen and vitamins |
| Calcium carbonate or crushed shell powder | 1% | pH adjustment |
| Moisture | 65-70% | Enough that squeezing a handful makes one or two drops of water form between the fingers |
| Substrate amount | 1.2-1.5kg per bag | |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Wood ear takes only a little rice bran. Whereas shiitake and lion's mane mushroom add 20-30%, 5-10% is enough for wood ear. This is a decisive advantage in a disaster. Rice bran is a resource that both people and livestock eat, so competition for it is fierce, and wood ear hardly asks for any. Moreover, the richer in nutrients a substrate is, the more contaminants swarm it, so having little rice bran also means resistance to contamination.

### 3-3. Waste Corrugated Cardboard Substrate - A Hidden Resource in a Disaster

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Corrugated cardboard is wood pulp that has been broken down and pressed again. From wood ear's point of view, it is wood that has already been finely broken up. When logistics are cut off, boxes pile up in surplus and there is nowhere to use them. Because they can be turned into food, cardboard substrates are especially suited to a disaster.

| Step | Method |
|---|---|
| Choosing | Use brown corrugated cardboard with little printing and no coating. Glossy paper and plastic-coated cardboard are excluded |
| Tape removal | Peel off all adhesive tape, staples, and labels |
| Cutting | Tear into palm-sized pieces or cut into strips 5cm wide |
| Soaking | Pour boiling water over it and soak for 1-2 hours, or soak in lime water for 12 hours |
| Wringing | Wring out until the water no longer drips |
| Inoculation | Tuck spawn between the pieces of cardboard and stack in layers |
| Packing | Press into sacks or tubs and punch holes so that air can pass through |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Corrugated cardboard has air layers inside its wavy structure, and the mycelium spreads very quickly along those gaps. Three days is enough for wood ear mycelium to cover one layer of cardboard. In exchange, because nutrients are scarce, the yield is about half that of a sawdust substrate and it is finished after one or two flushes. Even so, it is excellent for multiplying spawn. Growing mycelium in bulk on cardboard and then using it as spawn for logs or sawdust substrate is practically useful.

### 3-4. Straw-Based Substrates

| Material | Treatment | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Rice straw, wheat straw | Cut to 5cm or shorter and boil, or soak in lime | Yield is low on its own, so mix half and half with sawdust |
| Rice hulls | Soak in water, then boil | Good air permeability. Can replace up to 30% |
| Maize stalks | Crush and boil | Relatively rich in nutrients |
| Bean pods | Crush and boil | Serve as a nitrogen source. Use at 20% or less |

### 3-5. Four Ways to Sterilize Substrate Without Electricity

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;An autoclave is run at 121°C for 90 minutes and kills even bacterial spores. It needs both electricity and a pressure vessel. When those are not available, the aim is not complete sterilization but low-temperature pasteurization, which cuts the number of competing microbes enough for wood ear mycelium to get established first.

#### 3-5-1. Steam Sterilization in a Drum

| Item | Method |
|---|---|
| Equipment | 200L steel drum, lid, stand (bricks or a grill rack), firewood |
| Water | 20-30cm at the bottom |
| Loading | Stack the substrate bags or sacks loosely on the stand, leaving gaps for the steam to circulate |
| Sealing | Put on the lid and wrap it with wet gamani (straw sacks) or plastic sheeting |
| Heating | 4-6 hours from the time steam fills the drum |
| Check | A wooden skewer left stuck in the core of the substrate is too hot to hold when pulled out |
| Cooling | Overnight with the lid on. Opening it while hot draws in spores along with the air |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Wood ear does not grow any better even on a rich substrate, so the sterilization burden is actually lighter. A sawdust substrate with only 5% rice bran gives contaminants little to feed on, and 4 hours of steam brings the failure rate down far enough.

#### 3-5-2. Boiling-Water Soak

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This is the simplest method, used for straw and cardboard. Boil water in a pot, put in the material, and boil it for 1-2 hours. Lift it out, wring out the water, cool it to a moisture content of 65-70% and a temperature of 30°C or below, and then inoculate. Its strength is that the only equipment needed is a single pot, and its weakness is that it uses a lot of fuel. The wringing tools and the cooling surface also need to be washed beforehand with boiling water.

#### 3-5-3. Lime Soak - A Method That Needs No Fuel at All

| Item | Method |
|---|---|
| Mix | 1-1.5kg of slaked lime, or 0.5-1kg of quicklime, per 100L of water |
| pH | 12-13. A strong alkali that feels slippery on the hands |
| Soaking | Submerge the cut straw or cardboard completely for 12-24 hours |
| Draining | Lift out and drain for 6-12 hours, adjusting the moisture content to 65-70% |
| Inoculation | Do not rinse. The remaining alkali keeps suppressing contaminants |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;A lime soak suits the combination of wood ear and cardboard substrate especially well. Cardboard turns mushy when boiled and is hard to handle, but it keeps its shape in lime water. Above all, no fire is used at all. If there is no lime, wood ash can be stirred into water, left for a day, and only the clear liquid on top collected as lye to take its place, but its pH is lower and varies greatly from batch to batch, so the success rate drops.

#### 3-5-4. Rice Straw Fermentation

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;When straw is soaked in water and piled to a height of 1-1.5m, thermophilic microbes generate heat on their own and the inside of the pile reaches 60-70°C. If it is turned every 2-3 days and left for 2-3 weeks, the easy sugars that contaminants would feed on are used up in advance, and it becomes a selective substrate. This is not sterilization but a way of removing the food first. Yield on a wood ear substrate of straw alone is low, but when mixed half and half with sawdust, treating the straw portion this way saves a great deal of fuel.

### 3-6. Comparison of Unpowered Sterilization Methods

| Method | Fuel | Time required | Success rate | Suitable substrate |
|---|:---:|:---:|:---:|---|
| Log cultivation (no sterilization needed) | None | None | Highest | Whole logs |
| Steam in a drum | A lot | 4-6 hours + cooling | High | Sawdust, straw, cardboard |
| Boiling-water soak | A lot | 1-2 hours + cooling | Medium | Straw, rice hulls, cardboard |
| Lime soak | None | 12-24 hours | Medium | Straw, cardboard |
| Rice straw fermentation | None | 2-3 weeks | Medium | Straw, compost |

## 4. Securing Spawn

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Mushrooms have no seeds. When the seed-and-seedling shops close, there is nowhere to buy seed, and the method of saving seed and storing it in a jar does not work either. But fungi have three routes that plants do not. One is to collect spores and keep them, another is to tear off a piece of flesh from a living mushroom and grow it as it is, and the third is to split wood that the mycelium has spread through and move it to new wood. Wood ear responds well to all three. The S for seed saving is the result.

### 4-1. Collecting Spores and Spore Prints

| Step | Method |
|---|---|
| Choosing a specimen | Choose a fresh, thick specimen whose edges have just flattened out |
| Detaching | Carefully detach it at the base where it is attached to the wood |
| Placing | Set it on aluminum foil with the smooth hymenium side facing down |
| Covering | Place a bowl over it to block drafts |
| Leaving | 24 hours in a cool place |
| Collecting | Lift off the fruiting body and a white spore powder remains |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Aluminum foil is better than paper. Paper soaks up moisture, and contaminants hide between its fibers. Foil absorbs no moisture and has a smooth surface, so it preserves the spores as they are. If there is no foil, a glass plate is the next choice.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Wood ear is trickier to take spores from than other mushrooms. Because the fruiting body is gelatinous, it shrivels as it dries and sticks to the foil in the process. If two chopsticks are propped under the fruiting body to hold it slightly off the foil, it does not stick and only the spores fall.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;After the spores have been collected, the foil is left for one more day in a shaded, ventilated place to drive off even the slightest moisture. Then the foil is folded in half with the spore side inward for protection and put in a zip-lock bag. Thoroughly roasted salt or a piece of charcoal is added to absorb moisture. The zip-lock bags are gathered in a glass jar and sealed with beeswax, and kept in an earth cellar or basement where the temperature stays constant through all four seasons. Ultraviolet light destroys the DNA of spores, so no light must get in.

| Form of preservation | Lifespan | Notes |
|---|:---:|---|
| Spore print (dried on foil) | 5-10 years or more | Moisture is completely excluded, so cell activity stops |
| Liquid spawn (suspension in sterile water) | 3-6 months | Without refrigeration, the spores germinate early or bacteria multiply |
| Fully colonized spawn (sawdust or spawn logs) | About 1 year | Being living mycelium, it keeps using up nutrients |

> **In a disaster, spores must not be stored as a liquid. They must always be kept as dried spore prints.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The difference in lifespan between spores and plant seeds comes from structure. A tomato seed holds an embryo and nutrient reserves, so over time it consumes its own nutrients and ages to death. At room temperature, that takes 2-4 years. A mushroom spore is merely a single cell wrapped in a thick cell wall, so it has no nutrients to burn. As long as conditions are right, it lasts 5-10 years, and in academic circles it is not rare to hear of spores several decades old being revived.

| Comparison item | Tomato seed | Mushroom spore |
|---|---|---|
| Average lifespan (room temperature) | About 2-4 years | About 5-10 years or more |
| Weak point | Temperature changes, depletion of its own nutrients | Mold contamination caused by moisture |
| Cell structure | A complex embryo and nutrient reserves | A single cell with a thick protective wall |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;It is important that, when the largest and healthiest fruiting bodies appear in the first year of the disaster, 20-30 spore prints are made in bulk and stored in the earth cellar. Even if carrying the mycelium on year after year ends in failure through contamination, they serve as a safeguard that can be taken out 5 or 10 years later to start again at any time.

### 4-2. Making Spawn from Spores - The Heat-Treated Rice Straw Substrate Method

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In theory, sprinkling spores on a sterile substrate makes the mycelium spread. In reality, however, green mold and bacteria in the air take over the substrate hundreds of times faster than mushroom spores. The more nutritious the substrate, the more vulnerable it is. Where there is no electricity, a primitive substrate that makes use of nature's own defenses is used instead of an overly nutritious artificial one.

| Step | Method |
|---|---|
| Boiling | Boil rice straw or wheat straw thoroughly in boiling water for at least 1 hour |
| Wringing | Lift out, wring out the water, and let it cool until it can be touched by hand |
| Sprinkling | Sprinkle the stored spore powder evenly and generously, without holding back |
| Covering | Cover with a clean cloth to block drafts and falling spores |
| Incubation | 2-4 weeks in a dark place at 21-25°C. If white mycelium covers the straw, it has succeeded |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;If spores are dropped sparingly one at a time, only primary mycelium grows and then dies. Mushrooms appear only when different mating types meet and become secondary mycelium, so countless spores must be shaken in all at once and left to find partners among themselves inside the substrate. The same method works well for wood ear on cardboard. Simply sprinkle the spore powder between pieces of boiled and cooled cardboard and stack them.

### 4-3. Tissue Culture - The Most Reliable Method

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;If a living, fresh mushroom is in hand, there is almost no reason to use spores. The inner flesh of a mushroom is already a mass of secondary mycelium in which the male-female union is complete. If a piece of it is torn off and put into the substrate, the germination and mating steps are skipped and the mycelium spreads right away.

| Step | Method |
|---|---|
| Choosing | A thick, elastic, fresh specimen. Dried or softened ones are not used |
| Disinfecting | Heat the knife in a flame and let it cool, or dip it in boiling water and take it out |
| Splitting open | Tear the fruiting body by hand to expose a fresh cross-section through its thickness |
| Taking a sample | Pinch off a piece of flesh from the very center of the cross-section, about the size of a grain of rice |
| Inoculation | Tuck it between boiled and cooled rice straw, sawdust, or cardboard |
| Incubation | A dark place at 21-25°C. Within 3-5 days white down spreads around the piece |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Tearing is what matters. All kinds of spores sit on the surface of a mushroom, but the inside is close to sterile. When it is torn by hand, that clean surface is newly exposed, and a piece taken from there becomes pure mycelium without any separate disinfection. Wood ear tissue is tough and does not tear easily, so hold both ends with dry hands and pull slowly.

### 4-4. Passing Spawn On Through Logs

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;It is thanks to this method that wood ear earned an S for seed saving. A log that has fruited well is itself a huge mass of spawn.

| Approach | Method | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Splitting spawn logs | Split a mycelium-colonized log into finger-thick wedges and drive them into holes in new logs | The most reliable |
| Mixing into sawdust | Crush mycelium-colonized wood and mix it into fresh boiled and cooled sawdust or cardboard | Good for increasing the quantity |
| Transferring substrate pieces | Tuck one sheet of mycelium-colonized cardboard into a new pile of cardboard | The easiest |
| Natural infection | Stand new logs right next to logs that are fruiting | A supplementary measure |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;A split spawn log dies if it dries out. The wedges must be driven in right where they were split, and the inoculation holes sealed. It is best to work on an overcast day or early in the morning when there is no wind. The wedges from one log can fill 5-10 new logs, so sacrificing one well-fruiting log each year keeps the supply of spawn from being cut off.

> **The most valuable asset in growing wood ear is not the mushrooms but the logs and cardboard piles full of mycelium. They are the seed of the next generation.**
