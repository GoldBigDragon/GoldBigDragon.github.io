## 1. Disaster-Preparedness Evaluation

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The overall grade of wood ear mushroom is A, with a score of 48.5. Broken down by scenario, short-term capability is B (46.8), long-term self-reliance is A (50.2), and supplementary value is SS (62.9). It sits in almost the same position as shiitake, with one difference. Wood ear needs only 150-180 days from log inoculation to the first harvest, and 50-60 days on a sawdust substrate, which is much faster than shiitake. Its niche is that it can serve as a long-term mainstay while still showing results within the first year.

### 1-3. What the Sunlight Grade SS Means

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The sunlight criterion in the evaluation table originally asks how much yield is left under 40% shade. The SS given to wood ear does not mean it won that contest; it means it never entered the contest in the first place. Fungi have no chlorophyll. The carbon wood ear uses is the cellulose and lignin that broadleaf trees fixed over decades, and the brightness of the sky has no effect on that stock.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This difference becomes decisive when sunlight declines over several years, as in a major volcanic eruption or a nuclear winter. When aerosols spread across the stratosphere, grains, tuber crops (tubers/storage roots), and vegetables collapse at the same time. But the dead trees in the mountains remain where they are, and mushrooms are the only route that turns that wood into protein people can eat. The first reason to put wood ear on a disaster-preparedness list is not calories but this irreplaceability.

> **Mushrooms are the only food source whose yield does not fall even in a world where sunlight has disappeared. Of all of them, wood ear is the one that takes the least work.**

### 1-4. How to Read the Strengths

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The criteria at S or above are protein density, perenniality, sunlight, storability, tillage and digging energy, yield stability, prevalence, and seed saving. Mushroom by-product suitability, at B, comes on top of these. The picture this combination paints is of a crop that is not choosy about materials, is not shaken by the weather, carries on by itself from generation to generation, and keeps for years once dried.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The by-product suitability grade of B is the second-highest value among the six mushroom species, after button mushroom, and it carries great practical significance. Wood ear grows not only on oak logs but also on sawdust, rice straw, rice hulls, and even waste corrugated cardboard. Corrugated cardboard is compressed wood pulp, so to wood ear it is already finely broken-up wood. In a disaster, paper and boxes are resources that are abundant but have no obvious use. Few mushrooms can turn them into food.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The storability grade of SS is also meaningful in practice. Wood ear is a mushroom that evolved to withstand drying and rehydration, so its tissue is not damaged as it dries. Shiitake gains a stronger aroma when dried but its texture changes, whereas wood ear returns to almost its original state when soaked. Add the fact that its volume shrinks to 10%, and it is extremely efficient as stockpiled food.

### 1-5. How to Read the Weaknesses

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Non-food value is the only E, and the real weaknesses are scattered across the D, B, and C grades.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The taste grade of B is the value worth noticing. The smell and aroma of wood ear are recorded as not off-putting, but its texture is recorded as off-putting. The gelatinous flesh, soft yet crunchy, divides people into those who like it and those who dislike it. When you have to eat the same thing every day in a disaster, this difference is bigger than you might expect. The answer lies in cooking. Chopped fine and mixed with other ingredients, the texture is less noticeable, and simmered for a long time it becomes much softer. Section 14 covers this.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The calories-per-area grade of C is the same story as with shiitake. At 13kcal per 100g raw, it is low even among mushrooms. To cover 2,100kcal a day with wood ear alone, you would have to eat 16kg. Wood ear is not planned as a source of calories. The S in protein density is the result of calories being so low that the ratio comes out high, so the absolute amount should be kept in mind too: 100g of wood ear holds just 0.79g of protein.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The speed-to-harvest grade of D is based on log cultivation. On a sawdust substrate the crop comes in 50-60 days, so a dual strategy is possible: when time is short, start with sawdust or cardboard, and set up logs separately as the long-term mainstay. Wood ear is a rare mushroom that accepts both of these routes well.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The seed-longevity grade of C means that the vigor of commercial spawn lasts 1 year. As with shiitake, this weakness is resolved through the spore-print preservation and tissue culture described in Section 4. Spores kept dry last 5-10 years.

## 2. What Kind of Fungus Is This?

### 2-1. Taxonomy and Ecology

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Wood ear belongs to the phylum Basidiomycota, class Agaricomycetes, order Auriculariales, family Auriculariaceae, genus Auricularia. It is a different branch from the order Agaricales, to which shiitake and oyster mushroom belong, and so its appearance is fundamentally different too. It has neither gills nor pore tubes, and the hymenium (spore-bearing layer) lies smoothly across the whole underside.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Ecologically it is a white-rot fungus. It decomposes the branches and trunks of dead broadleaf trees. It rarely attacks living trees and usually attaches to branches that have already dried and died or to felled logs. In nature it is common on oak, mulberry, alder, willow, poplar, and hackberry. It does not grow on conifers.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The most unusual ecological trait of wood ear is its tolerance of drying. Even when the fruiting body dries completely and stiffens like a sheet of paper, its cells do not die; they merely go dormant. When rain falls and it takes up water, it returns to its original volume within a few hours, resumes growing, and sheds spores again. A single individual may dry out and get wet many times over and live for several months. Thanks to this trait, wood ear is very forgiving in water management.

### 2-2. Appearance

| Organ | Characteristics |
|---|---|
| Fruiting body | Ear- or shell-shaped. Diameter 2-10cm, thickness 1-3mm |
| Color | Blackish brown to purplish brown. Shrivels and turns black when dry |
| Upper surface (dorsal side) | Covered with short hairs and grayish brown. Hairy wood ear (teolmogi) has conspicuous hairs |
| Underside (hymenium) | Smooth and glossy, with a shallow vein pattern. Spores come from here |
| Tissue | Gelatinous. Elastic and does not tear easily |
| How it attaches | Almost no stalk; attaches to the wood at a single point on its side or back |
| Spores | White, cylindrical, slightly curved |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The fact that the spores are white is worth remembering. If the cap is placed face down on black paper, a white powder is left behind. With wood ear, however, the hymenium covers the entire underside, so to take a spore print the whole fruiting body has to be laid face down.

### 2-3. Life Cycle - From Spore to Mushroom

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The life cycle of a mushroom is fundamentally different from that of a plant. Unlike a plant, in which a single seed becomes a whole tree, a single mushroom spore cannot become a mushroom on its own.

| Stage | What happens |
|---|---|
| Spore | Drops from the hymenium. A single-nucleus cell carrying only half the genes |
| Primary mycelium | The spore germinates and extends in a thread shape. With only half the genes, it cannot make a mushroom and eventually dies |
| Secondary mycelium | Two primary mycelia of different mating types meet and fuse. Only in this state can it form a mushroom |
| Mycelial colonization | The secondary mycelium takes over the entire log or substrate and stores up nutrients |
| Primordia formation | When humidity rises and light comes in, jelly droplets the size of millet grains form on the substrate surface |
| Fruiting body | The droplets spread and grow into an ear shape. When mature, it sheds spores from its underside |

> **A single spore will not produce a mushroom. When scattering spores, do not ration them one grain at a time; shake in plenty of the powder so that the spores meet and pair up inside the substrate.**

### 2-4. Growth Stages and Duration

| Stage | Duration | Temperature | What to look for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Substrate preparation and sterilization | 1-2 days | - | Moisture content 60-70% |
| Inoculation | Same day | Substrate 25°C or below | Add after it has cooled |
| Mycelial incubation | About 30 days (sawdust) / 60-90 days (logs) | 21-22°C | The substrate becomes covered with white mycelium |
| Primordia induction | 10-20 days | 20-28°C | Jelly droplets form on the surface |
| Growth | 10-20 days | 20-28°C | Spreads into an ear shape |
| Harvest | - | - | When the edges flatten out and thickness is at its maximum |
| Rest and re-fruiting | 10-15 days | - | Water is reduced, then raised again |

| Vitamin D | present | increases with sun-drying | Ergosterol is converted by ultraviolet light |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The iron content must be read with care. The values cited, from several tens up to the 100mg range per 100g of dried product, differ greatly between sources and are often overestimated in samples mixed with soil. Even so, the fact remains that wood ear is among the iron-rich plant-based foods. It helps prevent anemia when meat is no longer available. In practice, however, nobody eats 100g of dried wood ear at a sitting, so it is more appropriate to think about eating small amounts steadily than to worry about excessive intake.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The high fiber content cuts both ways. It has the advantage of aiding bowel movement, but eating a lot suddenly causes abdominal pain and diarrhea. When the diet changes abruptly in a disaster, such a reaction can easily leave people confused about the cause. Start with 5-10g a day on a dried basis, and increase once the body is accustomed to it.
