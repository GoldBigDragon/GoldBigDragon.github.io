## 9. Contamination, Pests and Diseases

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In a disaster, the number one cause of failure in mushroom farming is neither disease nor insects but competing molds. Among them, green mold, more precisely molds of the genus Trichoderma, is overwhelmingly the main culprit. While wood ear mycelium extends 3-4mm a day, Trichoderma extends two to three times as far. If you lose the race for the substrate, there is no way to reverse it.

### 9-1. How to Recognize Green Mold

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The biggest trap is that Trichoderma also starts out as white fluff. For the first few days after inoculation it cannot be told apart from wood ear mycelium by eye. It becomes possible to tell them apart only from 3-5 days later.

| Observation | Wood ear mycelium | Trichoderma |
|---|---|---|
| Color change | Stays white throughout | Changes from white through yellowish to bright green, then blue-green |
| Growth rate | 3-4mm a day | 7-10mm a day. Noticeably fast |
| Margin | The fluff spreads in a neat, even front | Spreads like the ribs of a fan and forms concentric rings |
| Texture | Cottony or frost-like | Cottony at first, then clumps of caked powder |
| Substrate surface | No change | A moist, transparent band sometimes forms |
| Smell | A faint mushroom aroma | A sweetish coconut-like smell, or a damp, musty smell |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;By the moment green appears, hundreds of millions of spores have already been produced. The judgment has to be made before the color shows, from the speed of growth and the shape of the margin. If, on the third day after inoculation, one spot alone is spreading unusually fast, that is not wood ear.

### 9-2. Telling Other Contaminants Apart

| Contaminant | Color and appearance | Conditions | Risk |
|---|---|---|:---:|
| Trichoderma (green mold) | Bright green, blue-green. Spreads fast | High temperature, excess moisture, acidity | Very high |
| Penicillium | Blue-green, but the powder is finer and there is a white band at the margin | Low temperature, damp | High |
| Neurospora (red bread mold) | Vivid orange. Spreads explosively | Hot and humid | Very high |
| Mucor, Rhizopus | Gray fluff studded with black dots | Excess moisture content in the substrate | Moderate |
| Aspergillus | Black or yellow powder | Hot and dry | High |
| Bacterial slime | Sticky slime and a sour stench | Excess moisture, poor aeration | Moderate |

> **If you find orange Neurospora, never open the bag. The spores are extremely light, and once they are released you lose the entire incubation room. Burn the bag as it is, or bury it deep in the ground.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Because wood ear uses a substrate with a high moisture content, Mucor and bacterial slime appear more often than with shiitake. If the substrate surface turns sticky and smells sour, it holds too much water. In that case, adding more holes to the bag to let air through and reducing watering sometimes brings recovery. Unlike mold contamination, a bacterial problem can be reversed if it is caught early.

### 9-3. What to Do When Contamination Occurs

| Situation | Action |
|---|---|
| Contaminated sawdust bag | Do not open it; carry the whole bag out of the incubation room. Incinerate it or bury it at least 50cm deep |
| Contaminated cardboard stack | Pull out only the contaminated sheets and move the rest to a dry place. Cardboard is divided into separate layers, so partial salvage is possible |
| Contaminated inoculation hole on a log | Pull out the plug, cut away the contaminated part, apply lime water, put in new spawn, and seal again |
| Wide contamination of a log's surface | Coat it with lime powder and keep it at least 1m away from other logs |
| Green inside the log | It will not recover. Take it out and burn it |
| Work tools | Dip in boiling water or heat in a flame |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;If contaminated substrate is simply thrown onto the compost heap, that spot becomes a spore factory. At the very least, boil it in water for 30 minutes to kill the contaminants before sending it to compost. If you can burn it, burning is the surest way.

### 9-4. Prevention Is Everything

| Prevention | Details |
|---|---|
| Amount of spawn | Plenty, at many points. Mixed inoculation works well for wood ear |
| Minimizing rice bran | The less nutritious the substrate, the less there is for contaminants to eat as well |
| Moisture content | Do not let it exceed 65-70%. With too much water, bacteria arrive first |
| Incubation temperature | 21-22°C. Above 25°C Trichoderma gains the advantage |
| pH | Raise the substrate pH to 6.5-7 with lime. Trichoderma likes acidity |
| Aeration | Do not pack the substrate down tightly, and make holes in the bag |
| Cleaning | Do not leave spent substrate and mushroom bases near the growing site |

### 9-5. Pests

| Pest | Damage | Pesticide-free response |
|---|---|---|
| Mushroom flies | Larvae eat the mycelium and fruiting bodies | Insect screens, sticky boards coated with tree resin or jocheong (grain syrup), immediate removal of spent substrate |
| Mites | Eat the mycelium and carry contaminants | Keep humidity constant; isolate infested substrate |
| Springtails | Nibble the surface of the mycelium | Rake away fallen leaves from the ground and scatter ash |
| Slugs | Especially fond of gelatinous tissue and gnaw heavily | Catch by hand at night. Block their approach with a band of ash or sawdust |
| Storage pests | Cigarette beetles and Indian meal moths infest dried wood ear | Complete drying, airtight sealing, include roasted salt or charcoal |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Slugs are especially troublesome in wood ear cultivation. The gelatinous tissue is soft and watery, so it is eaten far more readily than other mushrooms. Scattering wood ash in a band 5cm wide around the growing area keeps them out fairly well. After rain it must be scattered again.

## 10. Diagnosing Growth Problems

| Symptom | Cause | Action |
|---|---|---|
| Mycelium does not spread after inoculation | Substrate too hot, low spawn vigor, insufficient moisture content | Lower the temperature to 21-22°C and inoculate again |
| Mycelium has spread but no fruiting | Insufficient humidity, insufficient light | Raise humidity to 85-95% and move to a place with indirect light |
| Fruiting bodies thin and pale | Insufficient light, excess carbon dioxide | Increase the brightness and ventilate |
| Fruiting bodies tough and layered | Repeated drying and wetting | Keep humidity even during the growing period |
| Edges of fruiting bodies curl inward | Direct sunlight, dry wind | Provide shade and mist |
| Fruiting bodies soft with a foul smell | Excess moisture, poor aeration, bacterial soft rot | Stop watering and ventilate. Remove diseased ones immediately |
| Substrate surface hardens | Excessive drying, aging of the mycelium | Scrape off the surface, water it, and stimulate again |
| Substrate mushy and sour | Bacterial growth caused by excess moisture content | Add more holes and stop watering. Discard if severe |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The concepts of deficiency and excess work differently for mushrooms than for field crops. The nutrients in the substrate are fixed at the moment of inoculation and cannot be supplemented later. So the causes of growth problems should be sought mostly not in nutrients but in four environmental factors: water, air, temperature, and light.

## 11. Site Layout and Recycling of Spent Substrate

### 11-1. Do Not Keep Using the Same Spot

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The A for continuous-cropping tolerance in the evaluation table is a value that carries the condition of moving the placement site when logs are replaced. In a spot where logs have stood for some years, fallen spores, organic matter, and dead mycelium pile up, and Trichoderma and mushroom flies settle on top of it. If new logs are stood in the same spot, the contamination rate rises noticeably. Rest a spot that has been used once for 2-3 years.

### 11-2. Criteria for Choosing a Growing Site

| Item | Good conditions | Conditions to avoid |
|---|---|---|
| Shade | 60-70% shade under a broadleaf forest | Total darkness, direct sunlight |
| Wind | A place with a gentle airflow | A place hit hard by valley winds, a place with no wind |
| Water | Close to a stream or well | A place where water must be carried in |
| Terrain | A gentle north- or east-facing slope | A steep south-facing slope, low ground where water pools |
| Accessibility | A spot along a route you pass every day | A remote spot where the fruiting period is easy to miss |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Wood ear prefers brighter places than shiitake. If shiitake requires 70% shade, about 60% is right for wood ear. With too little light, the color comes out pale and thin. Even within the same forest, it is better to give wood ear a somewhat more open spot.

### 11-3. Uses for Spent Logs and Spent Substrate

| Use | Method |
|---|---|
| Spawn material | Split off the parts where the mycelium is still alive and use them to inoculate new logs or cardboard |
| Compost | Crushed spent logs have already had their lignin broken down and are close to leaf mold |
| Substrate for other mushrooms | Wine cap (king stropharia) eats spent wood ear logs and spent substrate as they are |
| Mulch | Spread over vegetable ridges, it blocks weeds and holds moisture |
| Livestock bedding | Spent cardboard substrate absorbs well, so it can be used as bedding and then sent to compost |

## 12. Confusion with Poisonous Mushrooms and Safety Checks

### 12-1. The Mushroom with the Lowest Risk of Confusion

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The genus Auricularia has no known deadly species. The form itself, a gelatinous ear shape attached to wood, is entirely different from ordinary mushrooms with a cap, stem, and gills, so there is almost no room for confusion with deadly poisonous mushrooms such as the Amanita group. If you have no choice but to forage in the wild, wood ear is among the safest options.

| Aspect | Wood ear | Things that could be confused with it |
|---|---|---|
| Tissue | Gelatinous. Springs back after being pressed and released | Jommogi-type jelly fungi (lesser wood ears) are also gelatinous, but darker, smaller, and tasteless |
| Shape | Ear or shell shape, with a shallow vein pattern | White wood ear (snow fungus) forms a petal-like cluster and is also edible |
| Underside | Smooth and glossy | If it has gills or pores, it is not wood ear |
| Dried state | Hardens like a black scab, then swells within a few hours when soaked in water | If it does not swell when soaked and stays hard, it is a woody mushroom, so do not eat it |
| Where it grows | Branches and trunks of dead broadleaf trees | Anything growing from the ground is not wood ear |

> **A black lump that does not swell when soaked in water and stays hard is not wood ear. This one check filters out most misidentifications.**

### 12-2. Storage and Soaking

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In the cases where people are actually seriously harmed by wood ear, the cause is not picking a poisonous mushroom by mistake but soaking dried wood ear the wrong way. This is a risk unique to wood ear that appears in no other mushroom guide, so you must be aware of it.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;If dried wood ear is soaked in lukewarm water and left at room temperature for a long time, especially for a day or more, bacteria multiply in the water. The toxin produced by one particular bacterium among them is extremely heat-resistant, so boiling does not destroy it; it attacks the liver and kidneys and has a high fatality rate. Deaths have been reported on several occasions in China. The problem is that wood ear spoiled in this way has almost no strange smell or taste. It looks perfectly fine.

| Rule | Details |
|---|---|
| Soaking time | 2-3 hours in cold water, about 30 minutes in warm water. Do not leave it longer than that |
| Temperature | Soak in cold water whenever possible. Lukewarm water is best for bacteria |
| Leaving overnight | Never do it. In summer it is even more strictly forbidden |
| After soaking | Cook immediately. Do not leave it sitting for several hours after soaking |
| Leftovers | Throw away whatever is left over after soaking, however wasteful it seems |
| Judging by smell | Does not work. Judge by time |

> **The greatest danger of wood ear is not poisonous mushrooms but water in which it has been soaked too long. Keeping to the times is the only defense.**

### 12-3. Other Precautions

| Item | Details |
|---|---|
| Eating raw | Eating a lot without cooking can sometimes cause photosensitive dermatitis. Sun-exposed areas become swollen and itchy. Heating eliminates it |
| Digestion | It is rich in dietary fiber, so eating a lot suddenly causes abdominal pain and diarrhea. Increase from a small amount |
| Anticoagulant effect | Wood ear contains a compound that slows blood clotting. If there is a risk of bleeding, do not eat much |
| Moldy wood ear | If dried wood ear develops mold, washing is useless. Throw it away |

### 12-4. Three Iron Rules of Wild Foraging

| Rule | Details |
|---|---|
| Pick only from trees | Anything growing from the ground is not wood ear |
| Test it in water | It must swell like jelly to be wood ear |
| One kind at a time, a little at a time | For a mushroom you are eating for the first time, eat only one kind, one bite's worth, and wait 24 hours |

## 13. Storage Without Refrigeration

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In the evaluation table, wood ear's storability is SS. Fresh wood ear is a food that does not last even a few days; it received SS because the grade was assigned on the premise of drying. Compared with the many other mushrooms rated E for storability and judged unsuitable for stockpiling, the difference is striking. Wood ear is easy to dry, keeps for 3 years or more when dried, and when soaked after drying returns almost to its original state.

> **Almost the entire problem of mushroom storage is solved by drying alone. Wood ear, among all of them, is a mushroom that has evolved to withstand drying and rehydration, so its losses are the smallest.**

### 13-1. How Drying Works

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Fresh wood ear is around 90% water. In this state the water activity exceeds 0.98, and bacteria and molds multiply freely. If the moisture is lowered to 12% or below, the water activity falls below 0.6, and below that level microorganisms stop being active. Refrigeration only slows them down, but drying halts their activity. This is why drying is overwhelmingly advantageous in an environment without electricity.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Wood ear loses especially little in drying. When dried, its volume shrinks to 10%, when soaked in water it returns to almost its original volume, and its texture does not change much. This differs from other mushrooms, whose tissue collapses when dried and turns limp even after soaking.

### 13-2. Sun Drying

| Step | Method |
|---|---|
| Preparation | Shake off soil and bits of wood. Do not wash |
| Flattening | Straighten any curled pieces by hand and place them with the spore-bearing surface facing up |
| Laying out | On wicker trays or reed mats, without overlapping. Air must be able to pass underneath |
| Schedule | 1-2 days of clear weather. Bring it in at sunset to avoid the night dew |
| Finishing | Leave it for the last half day in shaded, breezy air to dry it through |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There are two reasons for placing the spore-bearing surface up. Ultraviolet light reaches it directly, converting ergosterol to vitamin D2, and as the mushroom shrivels it curls downward, so it does not stick to the tray. Because wood ear is thin, it dries much faster than shiitake. On a clear, windy day it is done in a day.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Whether drying is complete is judged by hand. If it is hard when pressed with a finger and seems about to crumble, it is done. If it is even slightly soft or bends, moisture remains and mold will form during storage.

### 13-3. Storage

| Item | Method |
|---|---|
| Cooling | Pack it only after it has cooled completely. Sealing it while warm causes condensation inside |
| Container | Glass jar, earthenware jar, thick plastic. Paper bags do not keep out moisture |
| Desiccant | Wrap thoroughly roasted salt, pieces of charcoal, or quicklime in cloth and put them in together |
| Place | A cool, dark, dry place. An earth cellar or basement is ideal |
| Re-drying | Take it out and dry it again once before and once after the monsoon season (jangma, late June to July in South Korea) |
| Duration | 3 years or more at room temperature if completely dried and sealed |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Wood ear's storage efficiency relative to volume is extremely high. 1kg of fresh wood ear dries down to about 100g, and when that absorbs water again it returns to nearly 800g. Putting 2kg of dried wood ear in a single jar is equivalent to 20kg of fresh. Few foods are as good as this for stocking in a cramped earth cellar.

### 13-4. Other Storage Methods

| Method | Duration | Notes |
|---|:---:|---|
| Drying | 3 years or more | The best |
| Powder | 2 years or more | Grind after complete drying. Add directly to porridge and soups |
| Smoke drying | 2 years or more | Can be dried even on rainy days, and fewer insects get in |
| Fresh, kept in the shade | 3-5 days | Wrap in paper and keep in a cool place. Put in plastic, it turns soft within a day |

## 14. Cooking and Processing

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Wood ear uses little fuel. Because it is soaked before use, the heating time is short, and it keeps its shape even when boiled for a long time. In a disaster, fuel is as precious as food, so this is worth factoring in.

| Step | Method |
|---|---|
| Soaking | 2-3 hours in cold water. Always follow the time rules in Section 12 |
| Removing the base | Cut off the hard base that was attached to the wood |
| Blanching | 1-2 minutes in boiling water. Removes the photosensitivity that comes from eating it raw |
| Cooking | Stir-fries, soups, dressed side dishes. 5-15 minutes is enough |
| Long boiling | Boiling for 30 minutes or more makes it much softer |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The B for taste in the evaluation table comes from aversion to the texture. Cooking largely solves this problem. Cut into fine strips and mixed with other ingredients, the crunchy texture is not conspicuous. Long boiling releases the gelatin, thickening the broth and softening the wood ear itself. Added to porridge, it increases the quantity while the texture is barely noticeable.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Keeping it as a powder widens its uses. Grind completely dried wood ear in a mortar and add a spoonful at a time to porridge, soup, or dough, and dietary fiber and iron are added. There is no need to soak it, and the texture problem disappears. It is also the most realistic way to feed it to someone who dislikes wood ear.

## 15. Annual Work Calendar

| Month (South Korea) | Work | Notes |
|:---:|---|---|
| January | Felling logs, checking logs that are drying, re-drying dried goods | Felling in midwinter is possible |
| February | Cutting logs to length, preparing for inoculation, collecting and preparing cardboard | Remove tape and staples in advance |
| March | Drilling and inoculating logs, making sawdust substrate | Start when the temperature exceeds 15°C |
| April | Finishing inoculation, first contamination check, inoculating cardboard substrate | The check on days 3-5 after inoculation is important |
| May | Main laying, installing shade, first cardboard harvest | The earliest harvest comes in |
| June | Harvesting and drying the spring flush, fruiting of sawdust substrate | Dry it before the monsoon |
| July | Shading against high temperatures, slug control, humidity management | Do not let it exceed 30°C |
| August | Turning logs, checking for mushroom flies, dry rest of the substrate | The most dangerous month |
| September | Preparing the autumn flush, soaking and raising humidity | Start when temperatures fall |
| October | Autumn harvest, drying work, taking spore prints | Take spores from the best specimen |
| November | Best time to fell logs, standing the logs up, splitting spawn logs | Right after the leaves fall is best |
| December | Preparing for winter, tidying up spent substrate, checking stored goods | Logs can overwinter outdoors |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Wood ear work is concentrated in spring inoculation and summer-autumn harvesting and drying. It is not very different from shiitake, but if cardboard and sawdust substrates are run together, some mushrooms effectively come out little by little throughout all four seasons. Treating logs as the pillar that provides for several years, and running cardboard and sawdust briefly whenever they are needed, is the most stable approach in a disaster.

> **The rhythm of wood ear farming is the repetition of drying and wetting. The switch of drying the substrate once and then wetting it again is what calls forth new mushrooms.**
