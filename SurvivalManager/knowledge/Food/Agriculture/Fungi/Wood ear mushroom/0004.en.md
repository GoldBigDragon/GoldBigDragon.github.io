## 5. How to Inoculate

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Inoculation is the one day that decides whether wood ear cultivation succeeds or fails. There is only one principle: get the wood ear mycelium to occupy the substrate before contaminants do. To do that, put in plenty of spawn, at many points, and quickly, and then seal each spot.

### 5-1. Timing and Weather

| Item | Guideline | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Log inoculation season | Late March to early May in South Korea | It can be done later in the season than for shiitake |
| Sawdust and cardboard inoculation | Possible in all four seasons | Only the substrate temperature has to be right |
| Air temperature | 15-25°C in the daytime | Wood ear prefers warmer conditions than shiitake |
| Weather | Overcast days without wind | On clear, windy days there are many spores in the air |
| Time of day | Early morning or around sunset | Avoids drying and direct sunlight |

### 5-2. Drilling the Logs

| Item | Guideline |
|---|---|
| Hole diameter | 1-1.2cm |
| Hole depth | 1.5-2cm |
| Lengthwise spacing | 5-8cm |
| Crosswise spacing | 8-12cm |
| Pattern | Zigzag |
| Total number of holes | 30-40 for a log 10cm in diameter and 1m long |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;For wood ear, holes are drilled shallower and closer together than for shiitake. Because wood ear fruiting bodies emerge close to the log surface, it is advantageous for the mycelium to spread evenly in the layer just beneath the bark. If the holes are drilled deep, the mycelium heads toward the heartwood and fewer mushrooms appear on the surface.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The zigzag pattern is used because mycelium spreads much faster lengthwise. If the holes are drilled in neat, straight rows, the mycelium runs down along each row like a band and the spaces between rows stay empty for a long time. When the holes are staggered, the whole log is covered more evenly.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The more holes, the better. With many inoculation points, the mycelium spreads from many places at once, leaving contaminants less room to squeeze in. If spawn runs short, reduce the number of logs rather than the number of holes. Five properly inoculated logs are better than ten sloppily inoculated ones.

### 5-3. Filling with Spawn and Sealing

| Step | Method |
|---|---|
| Filling | Pack the holes completely full of spawn, leaving no gaps |
| Tamping | Press it down firmly with a finger or a wooden stick |
| Sealing | A wooden plug, a Styrofoam plug, or wax |
| Wax mix | Melt paraffin and pine resin at a ratio of 6 to 4 and use it |
| Cut ends | Also apply it to both cut ends of the log to prevent moisture loss |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Without wax, spread kneaded mud over the holes, or drive in a wooden plug and cover it with wet moss. It is not perfect, but it is far better than doing nothing. Because wood ear likes wetter conditions than shiitake, it can sometimes recover even when the seal is a little loose.

### 5-4. Inoculating with Wedges Split from a Colonized Log

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This method is used when commercial spawn is not available. Split an old log that the mycelium has colonized with an axe to make wedges about 3cm long and about 1cm thick, hammer them into the holes of a new log, and seal. Wedges die if they dry out, so drive them in as soon as they are split, and keep them covered with a wet cloth while you work.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Choose wedges from the parts densely packed with white mycelium. Parts where the wood has gone soft and crumbles easily in the hand are places where the nutrients are already used up or other microbes have moved in. If the split face smells moldy or sour, do not use that log.

### 5-5. Inoculating Sawdust and Cardboard Substrates

| Item | Guideline |
|---|---|
| Substrate temperature | After cooling to 25°C or below. Spawn added while the substrate is hot will die |
| Amount of spawn | About 50g of spawn per 1kg of substrate |
| Sawdust method | Mix it evenly through the whole substrate, or place it on the top surface |
| Cardboard method | Scatter spawn over each sheet of cardboard and stack the sheets in layers |
| Tool sterilization | Heat a spoon or tongs over a flame and let them cool before use |
| Environment | A small, windless space. Spray water on the floor and walls to settle the dust |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;When there is no sterile room, the key is to reduce the dust in the air. 30 minutes before work, spray water on the floor and walls, close the door, and let only one person in. Turn your head so that your breath does not go toward the substrate, and do not talk. Observing even this much cuts the contamination rate to less than half.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Mixed inoculation, in which spawn is blended through the whole substrate, works well for wood ear. Whereas shiitake and lion's mane start from a single point and spread from there, wood ear is much faster when it starts from many points at once, which gives contaminants that much less opportunity.

## 6. Managing Mycelium Incubation

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Once inoculation is finished, incubation begins. It takes about 30 days for a sawdust substrate and 60-90 days for logs. The goal in this period is for the mycelium to take over the whole substrate and accumulate nutrients. All a person has to do is hold the temperature, keep out light, and watch for contamination.

### 6-1. Temperature and Humidity

| Stage | Temperature | Humidity | Management |
|---|:---:|:---:|---|
| First 3-5 days after inoculation | 21-22°C | 60-70% | Helps the mycelium establish itself. Leave it untouched |
| Mid-incubation | 21-25°C | 60-70% | Mycelial growth is fastest |
| Late incubation | 21-22°C | 60-70% | The whole substrate becomes covered in white |
| Growth halts | 0-3°C | - | It does not die; it simply stops |
| Danger temperature | 32°C or above | - | The mycelium weakens and contaminants get ahead |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Keep light out during incubation. Wood ear needs light at the fruiting stage, but during incubation darkness is better. If the substrate is left in a bright place, the mycelium hurries to form fruiting bodies before it has covered the whole substrate; it then runs short of nutrients and produces only a few small, thin mushrooms before it stops.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Wood ear mycelium does not die even below freezing. Logs can overwinter outdoors, and the mycelium grows again the following year when temperatures rise. The A for cold hardiness in the evaluation table reflects this fact. In a disaster where heating is impossible, this is a great advantage. On the other hand, it tolerates heat a little better than shiitake, but above 32°C it too is at risk.

### 6-2. Log Incubation - Laying and Standing

| Step | Timing | Method |
|---|---|---|
| Temporary laying | From right after inoculation for 1 month | Stack tightly on supporting timbers and cover with straw or branches |
| Main laying | From 1 month later | Stack loosely in a crisscross log-cabin pattern so that air can pass through |
| Turning | Once every 1-2 months | Swap the top and bottom, and inside and outside positions |
| Standing up | 5-6 months after inoculation | Stand them at a slant, leaning in alternating directions |
| Burying short logs | Possible right after inoculation | Bury 20-30cm short logs about halfway in the ground |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Burying short logs is a method that suits wood ear especially well. If you inoculate short pieces of wood and set them halfway into shaded ground, moisture keeps rising from the soil and the burden of watering falls sharply. It can be done even where you lack the strength to lift logs upright or a frame to hold them. However, contaminants in the soil enter through the surface that touches the ground, so before burying it is a good idea to lightly scorch the lower part over a fire or coat it with lime.

### 6-3. Water Management

| Period | Management |
|---|---|
| During incubation | Only enough to keep the log from drying out. A damp surface actually invites contaminants |
| Spring and autumn | Once every 2 weeks, soak thoroughly if it has not rained |
| Summer | Once every 7-10 days. Water around sunset |
| Winter | Do not water |
| How to judge | If the fine cracks in the cut ends of the log open wide, water is lacking |

### 6-4. How to Check That the Mycelium Is Growing Properly

| Observation | Normal | Abnormal |
|---|---|---|
| Around the inoculation holes | White mycelium shows through the gaps around the plugs | Green, black, or orange means contamination |
| Sawdust substrate | Half the surface is white 7-10 days after inoculation | If it has not spread after 2 weeks, the problem is the spawn's vigor |
| Cardboard | One sheet is covered in 3-5 days | This is the fastest |
| Cut ends of logs | White mycelium appears after 3-5 months | If there is none even after 6 months, it failed to establish |
| Smell | A faint mushroom aroma | A sour smell, a moldy smell, or an ammonia smell is abnormal |
| Weight | The log gradually gets lighter | If it stays the same, the mycelium has not been feeding |

## 7. Inducing Fruiting

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;When conditions are comfortable, mycelium just keeps extending. Forming mushrooms is a crisis signal for the mycelium. Fruiting bodies form when it judges that the nutrients here are about to run out and that it must make spores and spread. For wood ear, the signals are humidity and light. These two act far more strongly than temperature difference or striking.

### 7-1. Stimuli That Suit Wood Ear

| Stimulus | Method | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Raising humidity | Raise the humidity from 60-70% during incubation to 85-95% for pinning (primordia formation) | The strongest signal |
| Light exposure | Move the substrate from a dark incubation room to a place with indirect light | Induces primordia formation and deepens the color |
| Scoring | Cut cross-shaped marks with a knife on the surface of the sawdust substrate or on the bag | Fruiting bodies emerge concentrated at those spots |
| Soaking | Soak the log in cold water for 6-12 hours | Shorter than for shiitake |
| Temperature difference | Make the difference between day and night 5-8°C | Supporting role only |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;What works best on wood ear is the combination of humidity and light. When a substrate that has finished incubation is moved to a humid place and exposed to indirect light, translucent jelly-like droplets form on the surface within 3-7 days. These are the primordia. Unlike shiitake, wood ear does not necessarily need a strong shock such as soaking or striking.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Scoring is important in bag cultivation. If the whole bag is stripped off, the moisture leaves all at once and the surface dries out. If you leave the bag on and make cross-shaped or straight marks 3-5cm long in 5-8 places with a knife, the fruiting bodies emerge only from those spots, which gives good shape and makes management easy.

### 7-2. Fruiting Conditions

| Item | Condition | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Fruiting temperature | 20-28°C | Slow at 15°C or below, and above 30°C the fruiting bodies turn soft |
| Pinning humidity | 85-95% | The most important condition |
| Growth humidity | About 80% | Kept slightly lower than at pinning |
| Light | Indirect light, bright enough to read a newspaper | In the dark, the color becomes pale and the fruiting bodies thin |
| Ventilation | Enough that carbon dioxide does not build up | If carbon dioxide is trapped, they become thinner |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Wood ear needs light not for photosynthesis but because it uses light as a signal. Wood ear grown in the dark comes out pale brown, thin, and mushy, while wood ear that has had enough light grows dark blackish-brown, thick, and springy. The difference in thickness translates directly into a difference in how much it stores and in its texture. Direct sunlight, however, is not acceptable: as the mushroom dries, its edges curl inward.

### 7-3. How to Water

| Method | Details |
|---|---|
| Misting | Spray 2-3 times a day as a fine mist. If a stream of water hits the fruiting bodies directly, they turn soft |
| Wetting the floor | Spray water on the floor of the growing area to raise the humidity of the air. This is the safest method |
| Covering | Cover with a wet cloth or gamani (woven straw sack) to hold the humidity |
| Caution | Water pooling on the fruiting bodies brings bacterial soft rot. It must be combined with ventilation |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Wood ear does not die at once when water is short; it dries up and waits. This trait makes its care forgiving, but if it is neglected on that account, thin, tough mushrooms result. Repeated drying and wetting creates layers and makes it tough. Where possible, keeping the humidity even throughout the growing period is better for quality.

## 8. Harvest and the Re-fruiting Cycle

### 8-1. Judging When to Harvest

| Criterion | Details |
|---|---|
| Size | When it has fully spread to 5-8cm in diameter |
| Edge | When the edge, which had been curled inward, flattens out and becomes wavy |
| Thickness | When it is thickest. Left longer, it thins and softens |
| Spores | Once white powder starts to settle on the underside, it is late |
| Time of day | Early morning. If picked during the day, it holds a lot of water and is hard to dry |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Missing the best harvest time by a little costs wood ear little. Unlike shiitake, its quality does not collapse within a single day; it holds for several days. Once it begins to release spores, however, the tissue thins and softens. When the underside looks hazy with white powder, pick them all on the spot.

### 8-2. Picking Technique

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Hold the base and detach the mushroom by twisting it left and right. If you pull straight up, the substrate surface is torn away with it, and contaminants enter through that wound. If any base remains, scrape it off with the tip of a knife. A leftover base rots and attracts bacteria and flies. Spread the picked wood ear out in the shade right away and begin drying it the same day.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The mushrooms come up uneven in size on a single substrate. If you pick only the big ones and leave the small ones, the small ones keep growing, so do not try to pick everything at once; pick in portions over several days. This is the knack of harvesting wood ear.

### 8-3. Rest and Re-fruiting

| Stage | Period | Details |
|---|:---:|---|
| Harvest | 10-20 days | Pick one flush in portions over several days |
| Dry rest | 7-10 days | Stop watering and let the substrate surface dry |
| Re-stimulation | - | Raise the humidity again and water |
| Re-fruiting | 7-14 days | New primordia form |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The knack for getting wood ear to fruit again lies in letting the substrate dry out once. If, after harvest, you stop watering and let the surface dry for about a week, the mycelium senses a crisis and forms primordia again. If you keep it constantly damp, bacteria spread and the substrate spoils. This repeated cycle of drying and wetting is the rhythm of wood ear cultivation.

### 8-4. Cycle and Yield by Method

| Method | Time to first harvest | Number of re-fruitings | Total duration |
|---|:---:|:---:|:---:|
| Cardboard | 30-40 days | 1-2 times | 2-3 months |
| Sawdust bag | 50-60 days | 2-3 times | 3-4 months |
| Log | 150-180 days | Every spring and autumn | 3-4 years |
| Buried short log | 120-150 days | Every year | 2-3 years |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The total output of one log 10cm in diameter and 1m long is about 1-1.5kg of fresh wood ear over 3-4 years. In dried form, that is 100-150g. For one person to eat it steadily, you need to keep 20-30 logs running and inoculate 6-8 new logs every year so that the generations overlap.

> **It is best to run all three substrates at the same time with wood ear. Use cardboard to see a first harvest within a month, sawdust to get through that year, and logs to prepare for the next several years.**
