## Introduction

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Water spinach (Ipomoea aquatica) is a perennial herbaceous vine of the morning glory family (Convolvulaceae), in the sweet potato genus Ipomoea. It belongs to the same genus as the familiar sweet potato, but while the sweet potato evolved to pile up starch in its roots, water spinach is a lineage that branched off toward floating on the water and stretching out stems and leaves. True to its Korean name, gongsimchae ("hollow-heart vegetable"), its stem is hollow inside, and that empty space explains almost everything about the character of this crop. It is native to the swamps, paddy banks and canals of Southeast Asia, and it is called rau muống in Vietnam, phak bung in Thailand, kangkong in the Philippines and ong choy in southern China; everywhere it has established itself as a vegetable of lean times.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;By the yardstick of disaster preparedness, the place water spinach occupies is very unusual. Most crops die when they are submerged, because their roots cannot breathe. When the monsoon season (jangma, late June to July in South Korea) drags on and a field stays under water for several days, potatoes rot, soybean leaves turn yellow, and napa cabbage roots go soft. Fields and paddies a flood has passed over, low-lying ground where the water level rises and falls, and land where a blocked drainage ditch has left water standing are all, from a farming point of view, abandoned land. Water spinach grows in exactly that abandoned land. And it does more than merely put up with it: a flooded stand yields more than a dry field.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;For that reason, this crop must not be weighed on the same scale as other leafy vegetables. In the evaluation table, water spinach's Water use is E. That means it demands more than 1,500L of water per m² per year, and in a drought year this one row alone makes cultivation impossible. In the opposite situation, however, where water is in surplus but the ground is flooded and nothing can be planted, this E becomes a qualification instead. Water spinach is a crop for floods, not for droughts, and without this distinction the value of the crop cannot be understood.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Its speed is exceptional too. From sowing to the first harvest takes 30 days. The SS for Speed to harvest in the evaluation table is among the fastest of all the crops this document covers. When something has to be planted in a hurry after a disaster begins, few crops put food on the table within a month. On top of that, new shoots rise again from the spot where the plant was cut. When the stem is cut so that 5-10cm remains above the base, the axillary buds on the remaining nodes wake up, and 1-2 weeks later the shoots are long enough to harvest again. Harvesting four or more times in a single summer is normal.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The weaknesses are just as clear, and all of them come from temperature and time. The E in Cold hardiness is the fate of a tropical crop. Growth stops below 10°C, and a single frost wipes out everything above ground. In South Korea, water spinach cannot get through the winter in an open field. It is perennial in its native range, but in South Korea it lives out its life as an annual, so to carry it into the next year, seed must be saved or a few stems must be kept alive indoors all winter. The E in Storability is another face of the same weakness. The tender stems are more than 90% water and turn soft in two or three days at room temperature. This is not a crop to harvest and stockpile but one to cut on the day, in the amount to be eaten that day.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This crop also carries one more kind of risk that other vegetables do not. Because it grows in water, it takes into its body whatever the water holds. Water spinach grown in water with a factory upstream, or in water into which waste has flowed, accumulates cadmium and lead in its leaves and stems. In water mixed with human and livestock feces, the encysted larvae (metacercariae) of flukes cling to the surface of the stems. Neither of these can be detected by eye, and neither can be told apart by taste. This conditional character, a fine crop if the water is good and a dangerous one if the water is bad, is dealt with separately in Section 14.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In summary, water spinach is neither a crop to which a whole field can be entrusted nor one that will fill a storehouse. It is a crop that turns a spot where water has pooled and nothing else can be planted into a vegetable garden, and one that keeps something green coming from that spot all summer long. That a small patch of swamp becomes a small vegetable garden is the one and entirely sufficient reason to plant this crop.
