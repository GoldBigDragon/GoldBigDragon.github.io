## 2. What Kind of Plant Is This?

### 2-1. Taxonomy and Origin

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Water spinach is a perennial herbaceous vine of the morning glory family (Convolvulaceae), in the sweet potato genus (Ipomoea). Its scientific name is Ipomoea aquatica, and the species epithet aquatica means "of the water." The same genus includes the sweet potato (Ipomoea batatas) and the morning glory (Ipomoea nil). Set the three plants side by side and their lineage is plain at a glance. The leaves are all heart-shaped or arrowhead-shaped, the flowers are all trumpet-shaped, and the stems all creep as they extend. The difference is that the sweet potato diverged toward storing starch in its underground roots, while water spinach diverged toward floating on the water and extending its stems far out.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This lineage has three practical meanings. First, as with the sweet potato, a stem that is cut and stuck in the ground takes root. The ability to produce adventitious roots at every node is a trait shared by the whole genus, and half of water spinach propagation rests on it. Second, it shares some pests and diseases with the sweet potato. If crops of the morning glory family are planted in succession on the same spot, pathogens build up. This is what the "2-year rotation break" attached to the A for Continuous-cropping tolerance in the evaluation table is about. Third, as with the morning glory, it flowers under shortening day length. In South Korea, when days begin to shorten after late August, water spinach sends up flower stalks, and for someone harvesting the leaves this is the signal that the harvest season is ending.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Its native range is the swamps and river margins of the tropical and subtropical regions stretching from India through Southeast Asia to southern China. Wild water spinach still floats there today, covering the surface of canals and reservoirs. This origin determines almost all of its growing conditions. First, as a tropical plant it knows no frost. That is why its Cold hardiness is E. Second, as a plant that lives on water, its body is built on the premise that its roots are submerged. Third, flowing or standing water always carries nutrients, so it never needed to drive its roots deep into the soil to dig out nourishment. That is why the roots are shallow and mostly fine rootlets, and why, when it is grown in a field, it wilts at once if it dries out even a little.

### 2-2. Appearance

| Organ | Characteristics |
|---|---|
| Leaf | Long heart-shaped to arrowhead-shaped. Glossy green to dark green, 5-15cm long, arranged alternately. Depending on the variety, leaves divide into a broad-leaved type and a narrow, elongated type |
| Stem | Hollow inside. A cylinder 0.5-1.5cm in diameter with long spaces between the nodes. Floats on water and creeps across it for several m |
| Root | Spreads shallowly, mostly fine rootlets. Adventitious roots form at every node that touches soil or water |
| Flower | Trumpet-shaped, 3-5cm across, white or pale pink with a purplish tint inside. Blooms as day length shortens |
| Fruit | A capsule about 1cm in diameter. When ripe it dries to brown and holds 2-4 seeds inside |
| Seed | Angular grains 4-6mm in diameter, grayish brown to black, with a hard coat |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The fact that the stem is hollow is the starting point for every trait of this crop, so it deserves a separate explanation. Most plants die in waterlogged ground because their roots cannot get oxygen. When water fills the empty spaces between soil particles, the air is pushed out, and the oxygen dissolved in the water is far too little for roots to use. When root cells cannot breathe, the work of drawing up nutrients stops too, and if that state lasts several days, the roots rot. Crops in a field flooded during the monsoon season (jangma, late June to July in South Korea) wilt and die not because there is too much water but because they suffocate.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Water spinach solved this problem through structure. The empty space inside the stem, together with the sponge-like aerating tissue that runs through the leaf stalks down to the roots, carries air from above the water directly to the roots underwater. In human terms, it is the equivalent of a diver's breathing tube. That is why the roots of water spinach can breathe even while submerged. The same structure also provides buoyancy, so the stems do not sink but keep extending while floating on the surface.

> **The reason water spinach does not die when submerged is not that it tolerates water well, but that it sends air down to the roots through the hollow space in its stem.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The ability to root at every node also has great practical significance. When any node of a stem creeping across the water touches water or wet soil, it roots right there at once and behaves like a new, independent plant. In the wild, this is how it covers the water surface in no time. In cultivation, this trait becomes the means of propagation known as cuttings, and a second path for carrying on into the next year even without seeds.

### 2-3. Growth Stages

| Stage | Timing | Key physiology | Important nutrients |
|---|---|---|---|
| Germination and seedling stage | 7 days from sowing | The hard seed coat absorbs water and splits, and the cotyledons unfold | Phosphorus, a small amount of nitrogen |
| Vegetative growth stage | Days 7-25 | Stems and leaves increase rapidly. The internodes lengthen | Nitrogen, water |
| First harvest stage | Days 25-35 | Stems reach 30-40cm | Balance of nitrogen and potassium |
| Regrowth stage | 7-14 days after harvest | Axillary buds on the remaining nodes wake up and new shoots rise | Nitrogen, water |
| Flowering stage | From late August in South Korea | As days shorten, flower stalks rise and stems harden | Phosphorus, potassium |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;What deserves attention in this table is the intervals between stages. From germination to the first harvest takes one month, and after that the cycle repeats at intervals of 1-2 weeks. Compared with other leafy vegetables, which count a single growing season as two to three months, even the unit of time is different. The value of this speed in the early days of a disaster is just as described above.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Understanding the physiology of the regrowth stage makes the knack of harvesting fall into place on its own. At the tip of a plant's stem there is a growing point, and this growing point sends hormones downward to suppress the axillary buds on the lower nodes so that they cannot wake up. This is called apical dominance. When the stem tip is cut off, this suppression disappears and the axillary buds on the remaining nodes wake up all at once. That is why the more water spinach is cut, the more branches it grows, and the added branches become the next harvest. The instruction to leave 5-10cm above the base means keeping at least two nodes whose buds can wake up.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The meaning of the flowering stage is the exact opposite. Once flowers begin to open, the plant redirects its bodily resources toward seeds. The stem fibers harden, the leaves shrink, and the flavor declines. So if the aim is to eat leaves and stems, flowering is an accident to be prevented, and if the aim is to save seed, it is a signal to wait for. To achieve both aims in one field, the plants have to be divided into separate stands, and that is the subject of Section 13.

### 2-4. Nutrition per 100g

| Nutrient | Content | Notes |
|---|:---:|---|
| Energy | 26kcal | Raw basis in the Korean Food Composition Table. Some sources give 19-20kcal |
| Water | 90% or more | Higher in the stems than in the leaves. The direct cause of the E for Storability |
| Protein | 1.67-2.5g | More concentrated in the leaves. At 25.7% of energy, the density is high |
| Carbohydrate | about 3.2g | A large part is dietary fiber |
| Dietary fiber | 1.9-2.1g | Increases further as the stem fibers harden |
| Sugars | 0.66g | Almost no sweetness |
| Potassium | 489mg | High among leafy vegetables |
| Vitamin A (beta-carotene) | 411 μg | The deeper the green, the more there is |
| Vitamin C | 24.6mg | A large part is lost on heating |
| Iron | 2.4mg | High among leafy vegetables |
| Calcium | 87mg | Less than kale, but not a small amount |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;It is this table that produced the SS for Micronutrients in the evaluation table. The iron value of 2.4mg in particular deserves attention. Plant iron is non-heme iron rather than heme iron, so its absorption rate is inherently low, but absorption rises considerably when vitamin C is present with it. Water spinach packs iron and vitamin C into a single leaf, so this combination comes about by itself. There is, however, one caution, given in the next paragraph.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Vitamin C is weak against heat. Water spinach is a crop that must always be eaten cooked, for reasons covered in Section 14, and cooking destroys a large part of the vitamin C. This loss cannot be avoided, but it can be reduced. Stir-frying quickly over short, high heat leaves far more than boiling for a long time, and if the blanching water is not thrown away but used as a broth, the share that dissolved into the water is recovered. Beta-carotene, iron and potassium withstand heat, so they remain almost intact after cooking.

## 3. Growing Conditions

### 3-1. Temperature

| Category | Limit | Optimum | Notes |
|---|:---:|:---:|---|
| Germination temperature | 15-35°C | 20-30°C | Sprouts in 1-2 days after soaking overnight |
| Growth temperature | 15-35°C | 25-32°C | The C for Temperature range in the evaluation table comes from an optimum range of 10°C |
| Upper temperature limit | 35°C or above | - | Grows even above 35°C as long as water is sufficient |
| Lower temperature limit | 10°C | - | Growth stops below this |
| Lethal temperature | Around 0°C | - | A single frost wipes out the above-ground parts |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The temperature character of water spinach is heavily lopsided. Upward, there is almost no limit. Even in midsummer weather above 35°C, it grows all the more vigorously as long as water is plentiful. This is the exact opposite of other leafy vegetables, whose leaves turn tough and bitter and which bolt once it exceeds just 25°C. The very period in which lettuce, napa cabbage and spinach collapse in summer is the heyday of water spinach.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Downward, it is extremely weak. Growth stops at 10°C, the leaves begin to be damaged at around 5°C above zero, and once a frost falls, that is the end on that very day. Because of this trait, the open-field growing season for water spinach in South Korea is fixed from mid-May, when frost has completely ended, to early October, when the first frost arrives. In central South Korea it is slightly shorter than this, and in southern South Korea slightly longer.

> **The season in which leafy vegetables all collapse in summer is the best time for water spinach. Water spinach is not a substitute for spring and autumn vegetables but a crop that fills the gap left by summer vegetables.**

### 3-2. Light

| Category | Standard | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Required sunshine | 6 hours or more a day | Stems thicken and leaves grow thicker |
| Minimum sunshine | 4 hours a day | It grows, but the internodes lengthen and leaves become thin |
| At 40% shade | Relative yield 86% | A value common to leafy vegetables and the basis for the B grade in the evaluation table |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Being relatively tolerant of shade is good for making use of odd corners of space. Keeping 86% of yield even in 40% shade means that a spot that does not get sun all day can still be used. In the case of water spinach, however, making full use of this strength requires that temperature support it as well. Shaded spots are usually cool, and coolness is this crop's weakness. A south-facing spot that gets sun for only half the day, or the bright shade between buildings, is better than the shade of a large tree.

### 3-3. Water and Soil

| Item | Standard | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Water depth for flooded cultivation | 15-20cm | Always keep this depth. Yield drops sharply if the water dries up |
| Soil moisture for field cultivation | 80-90% of maximum water-holding capacity | The topsoil should not dry out |
| Annual water requirement | 1,500L or more per m² | On a flooded basis, including leakage and evaporation |
| Soil texture | Loam or sandy loam rich in organic matter | Also adapts to sandy soil and clay |
| Organic matter content | 3% or more | Accumulates because decomposition is slow under flooded conditions |
| Soil depth | 15-20cm | Roots are shallow, so deep tillage is unnecessary |
| pH | 6.0-7.0 | Weakly acidic to neutral |
| Salt tolerance | Weak to moderate | Strains from brackish areas tolerate it to some extent |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The fact that shallow soil depth is acceptable may seem at odds with the C for Tillage & digging energy in the evaluation table. The evaluation table was calculated on the basis of full-surface tillage to a depth of 15cm, and that value includes the work of leveling and firming the bed for flooded cultivation. If only field cultivation is actually done, scraping and leveling the topsoil is enough. In flooded cultivation, however, if the bed is not level the water depth varies from place to place, and the shallow parts dry out while in the deep parts the shoots are submerged and rot. If the work is done with a shovel and no machinery, this leveling becomes the hardest job.

## 4. Varieties

### 4-1. Two Broad Types

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Water spinach varieties go by many names, but by character they divide into two types. One is the aquatic type, adapted to growing in water, and the other is the terrestrial type, which also grows well in fields. This distinction matters more than any other trait.

| Type | Stem | Leaf | Suitable environment | Seed |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Aquatic type (cheonggyeong, green-stem line) | Thick and largely hollow, so very buoyant | Broad, close to heart-shaped | Standing water, rice paddies, waterside, hydroponics | Many lines rarely set seed, so they depend on cuttings |
| Terrestrial type (baekgol, white-stem line) | Slender and firm, standing upright | Narrow and elongated, close to arrowhead-shaped | Fields, raised beds, pots | Seed ripens well |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Obtaining seed without knowing this difference leads to trouble. If one brings in water spinach to use flooded ground and what one holds is the terrestrial type, the yield will not live up to expectations. Conversely, if one obtains the aquatic type to plant in a field, one ends up tied down by the chore of watering. What is distributed as seed is mostly the terrestrial type, while the aquatic type is often carried on through cuttings.

### 4-2. Main Varieties

| Variety | Characteristics | Yield | Suitable environment | Texture | Main uses |
|---|---|:---:|---|---|---|
| Cheonggyeong Daeyeop water spinach | Large, broad leaves and thick stems. Grows best in standing water | High | Wetland, standing water | Crisp | Stir-fry, soup |
| Jungyeop Cheonggollyuyeop | Medium-sized leaves. Adapted to both field and water | Medium | Both field and wetland | Crisp | Various |
| Baekgol water spinach | Stems have a whitish tint. Strong in field cultivation | Medium | Field | Crisp | Various |
| Jeokgyeong water spinach | Stems have a reddish tint. Contains anthocyanin | Medium | Both field and wetland | Crisp | Stir-fry, geotjeori (freshly dressed salad) |

### 4-3. What to Choose in Each Situation

| Situation | Suitable type | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Flooded ground, rice paddies, land that does not drain | Aquatic types such as Cheonggyeong Daeyeop | Yield is highest under submerged conditions |
| An ordinary vegetable plot, land where water must be carried and applied | Terrestrial types such as Baekgol and Jungyeop | Water demand is relatively low, and the plants stand upright and are easy to manage |
| When seed must be saved every year to continue | Terrestrial type | Flowering and seed set go well, so seed saving is reliable |
| When continuing through overwintered cuttings | Either works | The ability to root from nodes is shared |
| Rooftop, balcony, or a spot without soil | Aquatic type | Matches the method of floating on water |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The B for Prevalence in South Korea in the evaluation table, meaning that the crop is grown in only three or four provinces, also has to be kept in mind here. Outside the main production areas it is not easy to obtain seed, and all the more so when logistics are cut off. The realistic course is to secure plenty of seed while it can be had, and once the crop has been brought in, carrying it on by oneself along the two paths of cuttings and seed saving is this crop's way of being self-reliant.
