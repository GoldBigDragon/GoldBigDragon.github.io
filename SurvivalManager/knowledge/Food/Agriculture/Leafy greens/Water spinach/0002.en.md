## 1. Disaster-Preparedness Evaluation

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Water spinach's Overall grade is C, with a score of 41.9 points. Broken down by scenario, its Short-term ability to get through the early days of a disaster is C (41.7), its Long-term capacity for self-reliance over many years is C (42.1), and its Supplementary value, which counts it as a side-dish resource apart from the staple food, is SS (59.5). That Short-term and Long-term sit at almost the same level while Supplementary alone rises nearly 18 points above them: this one fact sums up the character of water spinach as a crop. It is a crop that stays in the side-dish role whether conditions get better or worse, and it simply produces that side dish on land that no one else can work.

### 1-3. How to Read the E for Water use

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The most striking entry in this table is the E for Water use. The figure of more than 1,500L per m² per year comes to three or four times that of other leafy vegetables. Set it beside kale, which receives an A at 400mm per cropping season, that is, 400L per m², and the difference is plain. Because the evaluation scale is set against situations in which water is scarce, water spinach has no choice but to receive the lowest grade on this criterion.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;However, there is something that must be read together with this figure. The 1,500L value is based on flooded cultivation, that is, growing the crop with water held at a depth of 15-20cm, as in a rice paddy. This water includes not only the amount the crop releases through transpiration but also the amount that seeps into the ground and evaporates from the water surface. In other words, most of this water was not used up by water spinach; it was water that was already standing there.

> **The E for water spinach's Water use means that this crop uses a lot of water, but it also means that on land where water already stands, that water does not have to be newly supplied.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;So the practical meaning of this grade is conditional. If water has to be drawn from a well and poured onto a field, water spinach is one of the worst choices: with the same water, kale or Swiss chard can be grown over three times the area. Conversely, where the monsoon season (jangma, late June to July in South Korea) has dragged on and blocked the drainage ditches, or where low ground near a stream is always damp, or where there is no way to drain water from a rice paddy, or where a wastewater tank or rainwater storage tank has water to spare, that water has no use except for water spinach. This crop's place is not on land short of water but on land that cannot get rid of its water.

### 1-4. The Structure Behind the Supplementary SS

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The 18-point gap between the Supplementary score of 59.5 and the Overall score of 41.9 comes from the SS grades for Protein density and Micronutrients. But these two SS grades were not awarded because water spinach is rich in protein and vitamins. The protein in 100g is 1.67g, which is on the small side in absolute terms. The ratio is large because the denominator, energy, is so small at 26kcal. A P:E of 25.7% is two and a half times the requirement line of 10%, but to turn that ratio into an actual protein supply, one would have to eat more than 8kg of water spinach a day. That is an amount the human stomach cannot handle.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The SS for Micronutrients has the same structure. Calculated with a cap of 2.5kg, 18 of the 21 nutrients exceed 10% of the daily recommended amount. This means the nutrient density per unit of energy is extremely high, and that is precisely the definition of a side-dish crop. The leaves are rich in beta-carotene (which becomes vitamin A), vitamin C, iron, potassium and calcium. Iron in particular, at 2.4mg per 100g, is high among leafy vegetables, and the vitamin C in the same leaves makes up, to some extent, for the low absorption rate of plant-based iron. This helps slow the onset of anemia on a diet from which meat has disappeared.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;So the purpose of planting this crop has to be clear from the start. Calories are built by potatoes, sweet potatoes and grains. Protein is added by beans. Water spinach is the green on top of all that. The C for Calories per area, that is, a figure of 0.50× the self-sufficiency line, means that even assuming a substantial yield of 5kg per m², the calories fall just short of half. A plan to fill one's stomach with water spinach does not hold up.

### 1-5. How to Read the Strengths

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Three criteria carry SS: Protein density, Micronutrients and Speed to harvest. S went to Fertilizer demand and Seed saving, and A went to Perenniality, Infrastructure needs, Cooking & processing, Continuous-cropping tolerance, Yield stability and Seed longevity.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The most practical strength is the SS for Speed to harvest. The figure of 30 days from sowing to the first harvest is at the very top among all the crops this document covers. Compared with potatoes at 90-120 days, maize at around 100 days and kale at 60-70 days, the gap is large. At the moment a disaster strikes suddenly, this difference is decisive. Grains and tuber crops (tubers/storage roots) have to be planted and then waited on for three months, and water spinach is the answer to the question of what to supply as something green to eat in those three months. Starting from cuttings is faster still, since taking root needs only 5-7 days.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The A for Perenniality adds time on top of this. Water spinach is not a crop that is pulled up once and finished. When it is cut leaving the base, side shoots come out of the remaining nodes. This is the phenomenon in which the bud nearest below the cut is released from apical dominance and wakes up, and thanks to this trait a plant set out once is harvested four or more times until frost comes. This practically softens the lowest grade, the E for Storability. The fact that a harvest can be kept only three days is offset by the fact that only as much as is needed can be cut on the day. The field itself is the refrigerator.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The S for Fertilizer demand is also worth noting. A combined 14g of nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium per m² is low for a leafy vegetable. Compared with the 24g that kale requires, it is about 6 tenths (60%) of that level. In a situation where chemical fertilizer has been cut off and one must rely on compost and diluted urine, this difference is a direct gain. However, the fact that Yield retention without synthetic inputs is C, at 40.8%, has to be noted separately. A crop that eats little is not the same as a crop that can go without eating.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The S for Seed saving is the criterion that underpins this crop's self-reliance. Because it is mainly self-pollinating, varieties do not mix easily, and even when seed cannot be saved, a second path is open: taking cuttings, that is, cutting stems and sticking them in the ground. When seed packets cannot be bought, having two means of propagation is a large safeguard. Section 13 covers these two paths.

### 1-6. How to Read the Weaknesses

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Four criteria carry E: Water use, Cold hardiness, Storability and Non-food value. C went to Calories per area, Temperature range, Pest & disease pressure, Pest-control self-reliance, Yield retention without synthetic inputs, and Tillage & digging energy.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The E for Cold hardiness and the C for Temperature range are two sides of the same coin. The growth range, 20-30°C, is only 10°C wide, growth stops below 10°C, and a single frost wipes out the above-ground parts. Compared with kale's temperature range of 25°C, this is narrow. In South Korea, the period during which water spinach can stand in an open field is effectively mid-May to early October, five months at most. For the remaining seven months this crop is not in the field. It cannot serve as a winter green, and the gap in the winter diet has to be filled by other crops.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There is one more practical burden that follows from this: it has to be started anew every year. In its native range it is perennial, so the roots survive the winter and come up again the next year, but this is not the case in South Korea. Unless seed is saved or cuttings are kept alive indoors all winter, there will be nothing the next year. The fact that Seed longevity is A, three years sealed at room temperature, lightens this burden to some extent.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;As noted earlier, the E for Storability is largely neutralized by cutting only what is needed from the field. However, at the point just before frost, when the remaining plants have to be dealt with all at once, this weakness shows in full. The techniques of blanching and drying them or salting them are needed then, and Section 15 covers them.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The C for Pest & disease pressure and the C for Pest-control self-reliance look burdensome if only the numbers are considered, but actual growing experience is different. The estimated yield loss of 50.3% is an estimate drawn from an order-level anchor, and water spinach itself is known as a crop with very few pests and diseases. This is because a flooded environment excludes a large part of soil-borne pathogens and ground-dwelling pests. Section 9 deals with this difference in detail.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The qualifier "off-putting texture" attached to the B for Taste is also worth pointing out. Because the stems of water spinach are hollow inside, they have a distinctive feel when chewed, crisp yet tough, and the more fully grown the stem, the more its fibers harden and toughen. This trait is controlled by harvest timing. Cut when the stems are 30cm to 40cm long, before the flower stalk rises, they are tender. If the right time is missed, the same plant becomes an entirely different ingredient.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The E for Non-food value means that it did not qualify under any of the eight criteria. It yields neither fiber nor oil nor medicinal material, and leftover stems merely go back as fertilizer or livestock feed. It does have a trait of growing in water and absorbing nitrogen and phosphorus, so it is also used to slow the eutrophication of standing water, but this use is not among the evaluation criteria.
