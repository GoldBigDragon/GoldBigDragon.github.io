## 9. Pests and Diseases - Protecting Without Pesticides

### 9-1. The Gap Between the Evaluation Table's C and Reality

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In the evaluation table, water spinach's Pest & disease pressure is C, with an estimated yield loss of 50.3%. Going by the number alone, that would mean losing half the crop, but this value was not obtained by observing water spinach itself; it is an estimate drawn from an anchor at the level of the order Solanales. The same order contains crops with severe pest and disease problems, such as potato, tomato and chili pepper, and their value was applied to water spinach as well.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In actual cultivation, water spinach is counted among the crops with very few pests and diseases. There are three reasons. First, flooded conditions exclude most soil-dwelling pests and soil-borne fungi. They cannot live in waterlogged soil. Second, the growth period is very short at 30 days, so pests lack the time to complete a generation and multiply. Third, because the crop is cut and regrown, pests on the leaves are removed with every harvest.

> **Pest and disease control for water spinach is achieved not with chemicals but with water and time. Waterlogged ground, a short growth period and frequent harvests are the substance of the control.**

### 9-2. Main Diseases

| Disease | Symptoms | When it comes | Response without chemicals |
|---|---|---|---|
| Downy mildew | Grayish-white mold forms on the underside of leaves and angular yellow spots appear on the upper side | Cool, humid weather; when plants are crowded | Tidy the stems to secure ventilation. Pick off diseased leaves and carry them out of the field. Water the root zone and do not spray water on the leaves |
| White rust | White pustules like pus form on the underside of leaves and the upper side turns yellow | Monsoon season (jangma, late June to July in South Korea) | Remove diseased leaves. If severe, cut down that plant |
| Root rot | Roots turn brown and soft and the whole plant wilts | In field cultivation when water pools; in flooded cultivation when the water goes foul | In a field, fix the drainage. In a flooded plot, change the water |
| Damping-off | The base of young sprouts constricts and the sprouts fall over | Seedling stage; when seed was sown thickly | Do not sow thickly. Avoid fertilizer that is not fully rotted. Hot-water treatment of seed helps |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In water spinach, fungal diseases are mostly a ventilation problem. When the stems spread in every direction and tangle, the space beneath them is always damp and the air does not move. For fungal spores to germinate, moisture has to stay on the leaf surface for several hours or more, and when air moves through, that time is shortened and infection does not occur. Spreading the stems out evenly is itself pest and disease control.

### 9-3. Main Pests

| Pest | Damage | Response without chemicals |
|---|---|---|
| Aphids | Cling to new shoots and the underside of leaves and suck sap. Leaves curl and sooty mold follows | Wash them off with a strong stream of water. Spray soapy water. Take care not to harm ladybugs and lacewings |
| Spider mites | Suck sap from the underside of leaves, turning the leaves pale and spinning webs | They multiply in dry conditions, so spray water often to raise humidity. They rarely occur in flooded cultivation |
| Tobacco cutworm (Spodoptera litura) larvae | Chew leaves and make holes | Pick them off by hand at dusk. Remove egg masses together with the leaf |
| Snails and slugs | Chew young leaves and shoots | Common near water. Pick them off by hand at night. Scatter wood ash around the plants |
| Leaf beetles | Make many small holes in leaves | If the damage is not serious, they can be left alone |

### 9-4. Pest-Control Materials Made Without Chemicals

| Material | How to make it | What it is used for | Principle |
|---|---|---|---|
| Soapy water | Dissolve 20g of unscented soap in 1L of water | Aphids, spider mites | The soap dissolves the oily film on the insect's body and blocks its breathing pores. To keep it from remaining on the leaves, wash with water the next day |
| Wood ash | Sift it and scatter it on the leaves and the ground | Snails, crawling pests | The ash draws moisture out of the insect's body. Potassium is supplied as well |
| Garlic infusion | Grind 100g of garlic, steep it in 1L of water and strain | Pest repellent | The smell of sulfur compounds drives insects away. It does not kill them; it keeps them from settling |
| Tobacco infusion | Steep tobacco leaves in water | Aphids | Nicotine acts as an insecticide. It is also poisonous to people, so do not use it just before harvest |
| Wood vinegar | Cool the smoke from charcoal burning, collect the liquid and dilute it 500-1,000 times | Repellent, suppressing fungus | The acid and phenol components act |
| Bordeaux mixture | Made by dissolving copper sulfate and quicklime in water | Preventing fungal diseases | The copper stops spore germination. If the materials are hard to get, rely on other methods |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Care is needed, however, when these materials are used in flooded cultivation. What is sprayed into held water does not flow away but builds up on the spot. If soapy water is sprayed often, soap accumulates in the water and harms the roots, and where fish are being raised together, the fish die first. In flooded cultivation it is better to give priority to physical methods, picking pests off by hand and removing diseased leaves.

### 9-5. Prevention Before Planting

| Method | Details |
|---|---|
| Do not plant densely | Keep 10-15cm between plants. If crowded, moisture builds up and fungus comes |
| Crop rotation | Do not plant crops of the morning glory family in succession on the same spot. Rest it for 2 years |
| Take diseased material out of the field | If diseased leaves and plants are thrown onto the field, the spores remain. Burn them or bury them far away |
| Watering in the early morning | Lets the leaves dry out during the day and shortens the time fungal spores have to germinate |
| Harvest often | Harvest is itself control. Leaves with insects on them are removed with it |

## 10. Nutrient Deficiency and Excess

### 10-1. Reading Deficiency Symptoms

| Symptom | Where it appears first | Cause | Why | Remedy with natural materials |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Whole leaf turns yellow and the plant stays small | Starting from the older leaves at the bottom | Nitrogen deficiency | Nitrogen moves around inside the plant. When it is short, it is pulled out of older leaves and sent to new leaves | Diluted urine, liquid fertilizer from soaked grass, well-rotted compost |
| Leaf edges scorch brown | Starting from the lower leaves | Potassium deficiency | Potassium is also highly mobile, so older leaves are sacrificed first | Scatter a small amount of wood ash |
| Leaves dark with a purplish cast and growth slow | Overall | Phosphorus deficiency | It can also appear when phosphorus uptake falls in cold temperatures | Bone meal, wood ash |
| New leaves yellow with only the veins staying green | Starting from the new leaves at the top | Iron deficiency | Iron does not move well inside the plant, so new leaves starve first. When the pH is high, the iron cannot be used even if it is in the soil | Lower the pH. Use less wood ash |
| Tips of new shoots dry out and twist | Growing point | Calcium deficiency | Calcium travels with the flow of water and does not reach new shoots, which transpire little | Powder of burned and crushed eggshells or clam shells |
| Leaves small, stems thin and nodes short | Overall | Water shortage | The cells cannot fill with water and so cannot expand | Increase the water |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The most useful clue in reading symptoms is "where it appears first." If the older leaves are damaged first, a nutrient that moves easily inside the plant (nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, magnesium) is lacking; if the new leaves are damaged first, a nutrient that does not move easily (iron, calcium, boron) is lacking. Knowing just this one principle finishes half of the diagnosis.

### 10-2. Excess Symptoms

| Symptom | Cause | Why | Remedy |
|---|---|---|---|
| Only the leaves are lush, and the stems are soft and snap easily | Excess nitrogen | The cell walls stretch thin and the tissue weakens | Stop nitrogen and flush it out with plenty of water |
| Leaves dark bluish-green, and insects gather | Excess nitrogen | The sap becomes rich in amino acids, which aphids prefer | Same remedy |
| Leaf edges dry out and roots turn brown | Fertilizer concentration too high | The salt concentration of the soil rises and water is drawn out of the roots | Apply plenty of water to flush it out. In flooded plots, change the water |
| Leaves yellow and growth stops, soil hardens white | Alkalinization | Too much wood ash raises the pH so that iron and manganese cannot be used | Stop the wood ash and add organic matter |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Overuse of wood ash is a common mistake in disaster situations. Wood ash is easy to get and rich in potassium, so people reach for it often, but it is strongly alkaline. In flooded cultivation it dissolves and spreads in the water, so its effect is faster than in a field, and that makes it all the easier to overdo. The standard is not to exceed a handful per m².

### 10-3. Nitrate, a Different Kind of Excess

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Leafy vegetables given a lot of nitrogen cannot convert all the nitrogen they absorb into protein and store it in the leaves in the form of nitrate. Water spinach is no exception, and the faster a leafy vegetable grows, the stronger this tendency. Nitrate itself has low toxicity, but when bacteria in the mouth and gut convert it to nitrite, it interferes with the blood's transport of oxygen. This is not a big problem for adults, but it is dangerous for nursing infants.

| How to reduce it | Principle |
|---|---|
| Stop nitrogen fertilizer from 2 weeks before harvest | Gives the plant time to use up the accumulated nitrate itself |
| Harvest in the afternoon of a sunny day | Nitrate is converted to protein while the plant receives light, so it declines during the day |
| Discard the blanching water | Nitrate dissolves readily in water, so a large part is removed |
| Do not feed large amounts to young infants | This is the stage when they are especially vulnerable to nitrite |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;However, discarding the blanching water also discards vitamins and minerals. For water spinach given an appropriate amount of fertilizer, it is better to use the water as a broth, but if it was heavily fertilized or is going to be fed to an infant, it is safer to discard it. This choice depends on the situation.

## 11. Intercropping and Crop Rotation

### 11-1. Compatibility

| Compatibility | Crop | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| Good | Legume crops (cowpea, mung bean, Chinese milk vetch) | Part of the nitrogen fixed in the root nodules leaks out into the soil and is used by the water spinach |
| Good | Rice | They can share a paddy. The paddy banks and water furrows become water spinach spots as they are |
| Good | Water dropwort (minari), watercress | They use the same water and have similar requirements, so they share a spot |
| Fair | Lettuce, spinach | In field cultivation they cover the ground and slow soil drying. However, their water needs differ, so management pulls in different directions |
| Poor | Sweet potato, morning glory and others of the morning glory family | Being the same family, they share pathogens and pests |
| Poor | Root vegetables (radish, carrot, burdock) | Roots rot under waterlogged conditions. Their water needs are the opposite |
| Poor | Garlic, onion | Bulbs rot when there is a lot of water. They cannot be placed on the same plot |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The relationship with rice deserves separate mention. A paddy is already a place that holds water, and the conditions water spinach needs are all in place. If water spinach is put on the inner edge of the paddy bank, in the channels through which water comes and goes, or in odd corners where no rice seedlings were planted, greens come out all summer without harming the rice crop. This combination, in which rice supplies the calories and water spinach supplies the side dish, is a practice that has long continued in many parts of Asia.

### 11-2. Crop Rotation

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The Continuous-cropping tolerance in the evaluation table is A, and the basis is "morning glory family, 2-year rotation break." If it is planted on the same spot year after year, pathogens that target the morning glory family build up in the soil. In flooded cultivation, however, this burden is lighter than in a field, because many soil-borne pathogens cannot survive in waterlogged soil.

| Year | Field cultivation | Flooded cultivation |
|---|---|---|
| Year 1 | Water spinach | Water spinach |
| Year 2 | Legumes (cowpea, mung bean) | Rice or water spinach |
| Year 3 | Brassicaceae or Cucurbitaceae | Legumes after draining the water |
| Year 4 | Back to water spinach | Back to water spinach |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Switching from flooded cultivation to field cultivation for a year is also good for the soil. Soil that stays under water for a long time remains without oxygen, so organic matter piles up only partly rotted, and iron and manganese are reduced and may change into forms harmful to roots. If the water is drained and the soil is dried for a year, air enters the soil and this problem is sorted out. The long-established practice of growing a dry-field crop in a paddy for a year works on the same principle.

## 12. Harvest - The Technique of Repeated Picking

### 12-1. When to Harvest

| Round | Timing | Condition |
|---|:---:|---|
| First harvest | 25-35 days after sowing, 20-25 days after cuttings | Stems reach 30-40cm |
| From the second harvest | 7-14 days after the previous harvest | Side shoots reach 20-30cm |
| Harvest period | June to September, until frost (South Korea) | Harvested four or more times in a single summer |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The criterion for judging when to harvest is firmness rather than length. If a finger pressed on the stem sinks in easily, it is still tender; if it stays hard under pressure, the fibers have hardened. Once the length exceeds 40cm, the stem begins to toughen from the base upward, so cutting between 30 and 40cm is the point where quality and yield line up best.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;When flower buds begin to appear, the situation changes. Once the plant turns toward reproduction, the stem fibers harden quickly and the leaves shrink. If this signal appears after late August in South Korea, it is better to hurry the remaining harvest.

### 12-2. How to Cut

| Technique | Details | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Length to leave | Leave 5-10cm above the base | The axillary buds on the remaining nodes become the next shoots. If cut too close, there are no buds and it will not regrow |
| Number of nodes to leave | Leave two or three nodes | If several buds remain, several side branches grow and the next harvest is larger |
| Cutting tool | Use a sharp knife or scissors | If the stem is twisted off by hand, the remaining stem tears and disease enters there |
| Cutting angle | Cut at a slant | Water does not pool on the cut surface, so it does not rot |
| Time of cutting | Cut in the early morning | Having taken up water overnight, the stems are at their plumpest and crispest |

> **The key to harvesting water spinach is not how much is cut but how much is left. The number of nodes left determines the size of the next harvest.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There are two reasons for the advice to harvest in the early morning. One is that the plant has drawn up water all night and its cells are at their most turgid; the other is that there is no heat built up during the day, so the harvested stems wilt more slowly. Where there is no refrigeration, this difference decides about half a day of freshness. Water spinach harvested in the daytime is already limp by that evening.

### 12-3. Handling After Harvest

| Step | Details | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Moving to shade | Get out of the sun as soon as it is cut | Left in the sun it wilts within minutes |
| Washing | Rinse several times in running water. Look especially inside the stems and in the leaf axils | It is a crop grown in water, so there is a lot of soil and foreign matter on the surface. For the reason given in Section 14, this step is especially important |
| Draining | Stand the bunches upright so the water drains off | If water remains, softening starts from the spot where they were stacked |
| Trimming | Cut off tough bases and pick out damaged leaves | Damaged parts spread to sound parts |
| ÷ | Divide what will be eaten that day from what will be stored | What is to be stored must go straight on to the treatment in Section 15 |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The tough bases and damaged leaves removed in trimming are not thrown away. They become livestock feed, or, if there is none, go back onto the compost pile. Thick, sound stems, as mentioned earlier, become the next field if set aside as cuttings. Keeping this cycle unbroken is the basic structure of self-reliant cultivation.
