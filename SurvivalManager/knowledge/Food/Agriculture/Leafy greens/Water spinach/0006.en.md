## 13. Seed Saving and Overwintering - Two Ways to Carry On into the Next Year

### 13-1. Why Two Paths?

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In its native range water spinach is perennial, but in South Korea it cannot get through the winter. A single frost wipes out the above-ground parts, and the roots do not survive either. So to carry on into the next year, something must be set aside, and what can be set aside is either seed or a living stem.

| Path | What is kept | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|---|---|
| Seed saving | Seed | Easy to store and lasts up to 3 years. Takes up almost no space | Time is needed for flowering and seed ripening, so the harvest from that plant has to be given up. Fails if frost comes early |
| Overwintering cuttings | Living stems | Reliable. A faster start the next year | Indoor temperature must be kept at 10°C or above all winter. Takes work |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;It is safer to do both. Frost may come early and keep the seed from ripening, and the cuttings may die if the temperature cannot be held in winter, so each serves as insurance for the other.

### 13-2. Seed Saving

| Step | Method | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Choosing seed plants | Choose plants that are disease-free, have thick stems and show the variety's traits well | Seed resembles its parent. Good plants come only from seed taken from good ones |
| Stopping harvest | From early August, leave those plants uncut | Flowering and seed ripening take time. If they keep being cut, they cannot flower |
| Inducing flowering | Reduce water to stress them | Drought stress becomes the trigger toward reproduction. It works more reliably when combined with shortening day length |
| Seed ripening | Leave until the capsules dry to brown | Seed that is not fully ripe does not sprout |
| Harvesting | Pick the dry capsules whole and spread them to dry in the shade | Drying directly in the sun damages the seed |
| Extracting seed | Rub and crush the capsules and winnow | The husks are light and blow away in the wind |
| Drying | Dry completely in the shade. If pressing with a fingernail leaves no mark, it is completely dry | If moisture remains, mold forms during storage and germination ability falls |
| Storage | Put in an airtight container and keep in a cool, dark place | Moisture, light and heat erode germination ability |

| Storage condition | Period germination ability is retained |
|---|:---:|
| Cool, dry, dark place, airtight | 2-3 years |
| Simply left at room temperature | 1-2 years |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The S for Seed saving in the evaluation table rests on the fact that water spinach is mainly self-pollinating. Even when bees carry pollen, varieties do not mix much, so even with several varieties in one field the seed can be collected separately. Compared with the Brassicaceae crops, which have a hard time with seed saving because of cross-pollination, it is much easier.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In South Korea's climate, however, seed saving is under time pressure. Flowers open as days shorten, and it takes more than a month after the flowers fade for the seed to ripen, so if flowering begins in September the seed may not ripen before the first frost in early October. This is why the seed plants are set apart and managed from early August, and water is reduced if necessary to bring flowering forward. Southern South Korea is at an advantage, and finishing indoors makes it more reliable still.

### 13-3. Overwintering Cuttings

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;For lines whose seed does not ripen well, such as aquatic-type varieties, keeping the stems alive through the winter is the only way.

| Step | Method | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Choosing cuttings | Two weeks before frost is due, cut several healthy stems 20-30cm long | Once frost has hit, it is already too late |
| Rooting | Stand them in a container of water and keep them in a bright room | Easier to manage than soil, and the state of the roots is visible |
| Keeping warm | Do not let the temperature fall below 10°C. 15°C or above is better | They do not grow below 10°C and die below 5°C |
| Light | Place them at a sunny window | In the dark the internodes lengthen and weaken |
| Changing the water | Change the water every 3-5 days | If the water goes foul, the stem bases turn soft |
| Winter care | Cut the stems that have grown long and stick them in again | Cutting and re-sticking again and again increases the number of plants |
| Spring planting | Move them outside in mid-May, once frost is completely over (South Korea) | Do not put them out suddenly; harden them off by putting them out only during the day for a few days |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Keeping the indoor temperature at 10°C is not easy when heating has been cut off. In that case, place them in the warmest spot in the living space, that is, in the kitchen or near a brazier, and divide them among several small containers so that if one freezes, everything is not lost. This is the practical meaning of the advice to do both seed storage and overwintering of cuttings.

> **In South Korea, water spinach is a crop that disappears unless people carry it on every year. Nothing is left in the field by itself, so autumn seed saving and winter care of cuttings are the very beginning of next year's cultivation.**

## 14. Toxicity and Compounds of Concern - Hazards That Water Brings

### 14-1. The Plant Itself Contains No Poison

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The water spinach plant itself contains no toxic components harmful to humans. It has nothing like the solanine of potatoes, the cyanogenic glycosides of cassava, or the trypsin inhibitors of beans. This crop does, however, carry a kind of risk that other vegetables do not, and that risk comes not from the plant but from the water.

### 14-2. Heavy Metal Accumulation

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Water spinach absorbs metal ions from the water so well that it is even used to purify polluted water. The problem is that being used for purification means precisely that the contaminants accumulate in the body of the plant.

| Substance | Where it comes from | How it acts on the body |
|---|---|---|
| Cadmium | Wastewater from plating, battery and pigment plants; mine drainage; phosphate fertilizer; sewage sludge | Accumulates in the kidneys and ruins kidney function. Pulls calcium out of the bones, so the bones soften and break. It takes decades to leave the body |
| Lead | Old water pipes, paint, batteries, road dust, bullets | Acts on the nervous system. Harms brain development in children and causes anemia and nerve damage in adults |
| Arsenic | Mine drainage, wood preservatives, some groundwater | Chronic poisoning causes skin lesions and damage to several organs |
| Mercury | Factory wastewater, discarded fluorescent lamps, discarded batteries | Damages the nervous system |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The most important fact about this risk is that it cannot be removed by washing or cooking. Heavy metals are held bound inside the cells, so they are not washed away by water and are not broken down by heat. They cannot be detected by appearance either. Water spinach grown in polluted water often grows even lusher and greener, because some of the metals stimulate growth in trace amounts.

> **The most important decision with water spinach is made not when choosing seed or when applying fertilizer but when deciding which water to plant in. If it is not known where that water came from, it is better not to use that spot.**

| Site | Judgment |
|---|---|
| Spots where rainwater has pooled, well water, valley water coming down from the mountains | Relatively safe |
| Irrigation water used in rice paddies | Usually safe, but check upstream |
| Lower reaches of a stream, city drainage ditches, puddles beside roads | Avoid. They are mixed with lead from road dust and household wastewater |
| Water downstream of a factory, mine or waste landfill | Never use |
| Old stagnant water, water with an oily film on it or an unusual color | Do not use |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In a disaster, matters get worse. When management breaks down, substances leak out of factories, warehouses and gas stations, are washed away by rain, and collect in the standing water of low ground. Pools left after a flood look like good spots for water spinach, but it is necessary to first think about where that water has passed through.

### 14-3. Waterborne Parasites

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The second risk is parasites. Aquatic plants grown in water mixed with feces become places where flukes attach themselves.

| Parasite | How it is transmitted | Symptoms |
|---|---|---|
| Giant intestinal fluke (Fasciolopsis buski) | Larvae that have passed through freshwater snails cling to the surface of aquatic plants, wearing a protective shell (encysted larvae, metacercariae). If the plant is eaten raw, they settle in the human intestine | Diarrhea, abdominal pain, digestive disorders. In heavy infection, malnutrition and edema |
| Liver fluke (Fasciola) | Attach to aquatic plants by the same route. When swallowed, they pierce the intestinal wall and go to the liver and bile ducts | Fever, pain in the upper right abdomen, liver damage, bile duct obstruction |
| Blood fluke (Schistosoma) | Not transmitted by eating; larvae in the water penetrate the skin. Infection occurs during work that involves entering the water with bare feet and bare hands | Fever, blood in the urine or stool, and in long-standing cases damage to the liver and bladder |
| Bacteria from fecal contamination | Human and livestock feces mix into the water and remain on the plant surface | Diarrhea and abdominal pain caused by E. coli, dysentery bacteria and the like |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The first two differ from the blood fluke in that they travel by the oral route while the blood fluke travels by the skin route, and so the responses also differ. The oral route is cut off by cooking. Encysted larvae are weak against heat and die even with a brief blanching in boiling water. The skin route has nothing to do with cooking, and is reduced only by keeping bare skin from being in contact with the water for long when working in it.

| Response | What it prevents |
|---|---|
| Always eat it cooked. Whether blanched, stir-fried or in soup, heat it thoroughly | Giant intestinal fluke, liver fluke, fecal bacteria |
| Rinse several times in running water, including inside the stems and in the leaf axils | Reduces what clings to the surface. This alone is not enough, however |
| When working in the water, cover bare skin with tall boots and clothing | Blood fluke |
| Do not use water mixed with feces or water downstream of a livestock barn | Cuts off the source of all three |
| Cook especially thoroughly anything grown in water where ducks or fish were also raised | The risk by the fecal route is high |

> **Water spinach is a vegetable that must always be eaten cooked. The habit of washing and eating it as is, like lettuce, must not be applied to this crop. The loss on the cooked side is part of the vitamin C; the loss on the uncooked side is parasites.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This is why Cooking & processing in the evaluation table is A, one step below the SS of crops that are washed and eaten as is. Water spinach must always be cooked over a fire, and where fuel is scarce this is a real cost. Since stir-frying is over within 5 minutes, however, it is incomparably lighter than boiling beans for an hour.

### 14-4. Other Cautions

| Item | Details |
|---|---|
| Nitrate | Accumulates in leafy vegetables given a lot of nitrogen fertilizer. Section 10 describes how to reduce it |
| Oxalic acid | Present in trace amounts, but far less than in spinach or Swiss chard, so it is not a practical problem |
| Potassium | At 489mg per 100g, it is on the high side. For people with reduced kidney function, it is better to blanch it and discard the water |
| Excessive intake | No notable side effects have been reported at normal amounts |

## 15. Storage Without Refrigeration

### 15-1. Why It Is So Difficult

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The Storability in the evaluation table is E, 2-3 days at room temperature without power. The cause is simple: the water content exceeds 90% and the tissue is tender. After harvest the cells stay alive for a while, breathing and burning their own sugars, and because no more water arrives from the roots the tissue dries out, while at the same time bacteria and mold burrow into the tender tissue. The structure in which the stem is hollow and air reaches the inside also speeds spoilage.

| Method | How | Duration |
|---|---|:---:|
| Leaving as is | Unwashed, with water shaken off, in a shaded place | 1-2 days |
| Wrapping in paper | Wrapped in wet paper or cloth and kept in a cool place | 2-3 days |
| Standing in water | Stand the bases in water like cut flowers | 2-3 days |
| Earth cellar or beside a well | Kept in a cool place, it lasts a little longer | 3-4 days |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Standing the bases in water works on the principle of letting the stems keep drawing up water, which slows wilting. It can gain a day or two, but if the water warms up the bases begin to rot, so the water has to be changed often.

### 15-2. The Field Is the Storehouse

> **The surest answer to the storage problem of water spinach is not to store it. It does not spoil while standing in the field, so cut only as much as will be eaten that day, on that day.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;For a crop that has to be harvested all at once, the E for Storability would be fatal, but water spinach regrows the more it is cut. If twenty plants are kept standing and a few stems are cut each day, the problem of storage never arises. There is only one time when storage becomes necessary: just before frost, when what remains has to be dealt with all at once.

### 15-3. Ways to Keep It Longer

| Method | Procedure | Duration | Principle |
|---|---|:---:|---|
| Drying | Blanch in boiling water for 30 seconds, rinse in cold water, squeeze out the water and dry thoroughly in the shade | 6 months to 1 year | With the water removed, bacteria and mold cannot multiply. The reason for blanching is to kill enzymes and keep the color and flavor from changing |
| Salting | Layer with salt at 3-5% of the weight and press down | 2-3 months | Salt draws out water and suppresses microbes through osmotic pressure |
| Making kimchi | Salt it, season it, pack it in a jar and keep it in a cool place | 2-3 months | Lactic acid bacteria produce acid, which holds other microbes in check |
| Jangajji (pickled vegetables in soy sauce or paste) | Blanch, then bury in soy sauce or doenjang (Korean soybean paste) | Several months | Salt and fermentation act together |
| Freezing | Blanch, squeeze out the water and freeze | Several months | Possible only when electricity is available. Midwinter cold can substitute, but by then the crop is already gone |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The most practical method in a disaster is drying. It needs no electricity, no salt and no jar, only sunlight and wind, and the volume shrinks to 10%, so it takes up little storage space. However, during drying, vitamin C almost disappears and beta-carotene also falls considerably. What remains is fiber, minerals and a little protein, so it is good as a winter soup ingredient but hard to count on as a source of vitamins.

| What to observe when drying | Why |
|---|---|
| Do not dry directly in the sun; dry in shade and wind | Direct sunlight destroys pigments and vitamins |
| Spread thinly, without overlapping | Where pieces overlap, the space between them does not dry and mold forms |
| Dry until completely dry. If it crumbles when rubbed by hand, it is done | If moisture remains, mold appears during storage |
| Seal it and keep it in a dark, cool place | If it takes up moisture again, it spoils |

## 16. Cooking and Processing

### 16-1. Saving Fuel

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Water spinach must always be cooked, but it does not need to be cooked for long. The tissue is tender, so heat passes through quickly, and long cooking actually makes it mushy. Where fuel is scarce, this trait is an advantage.

| Method | Time | Fuel | Nutrition retained |
|---|:---:|:---:|---|
| High-heat stir-frying | 2-5 minutes | Least | A relatively large share of vitamin C remains. Beta-carotene is absorbed better when eaten with oil |
| Blanching | 30 seconds to 1 minute | Little | Water-soluble vitamins leach into the water. Using that water recovers them |
| Soup or stew | 5 minutes | Moderate | The nutrients that leach out stay in the broth and are eaten with it |
| Steaming | 3-5 minutes | Little | Water-soluble losses are smaller than with blanching |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There is a reason water spinach is mostly stir-fried in Southeast Asia. It is done in a short time and uses little fuel, nutrient loss is small, oil helps the absorption of beta-carotene, and the crisp texture of the stems survives. If there is no oil, it can be stir-fried with just a little water and the lid on, in the manner of steaming.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The knack is to add the stems and leaves separately. If the stems are cooked first for about a minute and the leaves added afterward, both cook just right. If they go in together, the stems are still undercooked by the time the leaves are mashed.

### 16-2. How to Eat It

| Method | Details |
|---|---|
| Stir-fry | Stir-fry over high heat with garlic. The most common and the most efficient |
| Soup and stew | Add it to doenjang soup or clear soup. It turns mushy if boiled long, so add it last |
| Seasoned side dish | Blanch, squeeze out the water and toss with seasoning. This is the way it is eaten as namul (seasoned vegetable side dish) |
| Porridge | Chop finely and add to grain porridge. Suited to people with weak digestion |
| Kimchi | Salt it and pack it. Doubles as storage |
| Rehydrating dried leaves | Soak in warm water for 20-30 minutes and use in soup or stir-fry |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The qualifier "off-putting texture" attached to the Taste grade in the evaluation table comes mostly from the stems. Cutting off tough stems, or slicing them thin on the diagonal to sever the fibers, improves things considerably, and splitting thick stems in half before adding them makes them cook faster and easier to chew.

### 16-3. Cooking That Protects Nutrition

| Principle | Reason |
|---|---|
| Wash before cutting | If washed after cutting, vitamins leach into the water through the cut surfaces |
| Do not soak for long after washing | Water-soluble nutrients keep leaching out |
| Cover with a lid and cook briefly | Steam is trapped, so it cooks quickly and uses less fuel |
| Use the blanching water as broth | Recovers what leached out. Where nitrate is a concern, however, discard it |
| Use at least a little oil | Beta-carotene is absorbed only when dissolved in oil |

## 17. Annual Work Calendar

### 17-1. Monthly Table

| Month (South Korea) | Flooded cultivation | Field cultivation | Other work |
|---|---|---|---|
| January | Rest | Rest | Indoor care of cuttings. Change the water; keep at 10°C or above |
| February | Rest | Rest | Indoor care of cuttings. Cut the stems that have grown long and stick them in again to increase the number of plants |
| March | Rest | Rest | Prepare compost. Check the condition of the seed |
| April | Tidy the plot, repair the banks | Tillage and adding compost | Raising seedlings indoors can begin |
| May | After mid-month, bring in water; sow or take cuttings | After mid-month, sow or take cuttings | Confirm that frost is over. Harden off the indoor cuttings outdoors |
| June | Keep water at 15-20cm; first harvest | Watering, mulching; first harvest | Increase side branches with the first pinching |
| July | Repeated harvests; top-dressing after each harvest | Repeated harvests; watering 1-2 times a day | Secure ventilation in the monsoon season (jangma, late June to July in South Korea). Watch for downy mildew |
| August | Repeated harvesting is at its most vigorous | Repeated harvests | In early August, choose the seed plants and stop harvesting them. From late August, pick off flower buds |
| September | Harvest continues but slows | Same | Watch the flowers and capsules of the seed plants. Reduce water to help flowering |
| October | Finish before the first frost | Same | Harvest seed. Cut cuttings and bring them indoors. Blanch and dry what remains |
| November | Drain the water and dry the plot | Clean up; remaining stems to compost | Dry and store seed |
| December | Rest | Rest | Start indoor care of cuttings |

### 17-2. The Axis Running Through the Year

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The year of water spinach is divided into five months in the field and seven months indoors. From mid-May to early October it works vigorously in the field, and for the remaining seven months it waits for the next year inside a seed packet or in a bowl of water on a windowsill. If this structure is missed, one year succeeds and the next year there is nothing.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;So the most important cells in this table are not the summer harvest entries but early August and October. If seed plants are not set apart in early August, there will be no seed, and if cuttings are not cut and brought in during October, there will be no living stems.

> **The harvest of the five summer months rests on the judgment to set aside a few plants in early August and the hands that cut a few stems and bring them in during October. With this crop, the skill of carrying it on is harder than the skill of harvesting.**
