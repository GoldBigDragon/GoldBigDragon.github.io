## Introduction

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Kale (Brassica oleracea var. acephala) is a leafy vegetable in the cabbage family. It is the same species as cabbage, but the branch that never forms a head — the lineage that stays closest to the wild cabbage that existed before humans began selecting it. Its original form was a plant growing on sea cliffs along the Mediterranean coast, and that origin explains almost everything about kale's character. The thick leaves built to withstand cool winds and salt air, the cells that do not freeze even in winter, and the growth habit of raising a single central stem and continually pushing out new leaves from the bottom — all of it comes from those cliffs.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;When you look at this crop through the lens of disaster preparedness, the first thing to understand is that kale is not a crop that fills the stomach. Raw kale has only 28kcal per 100g. Getting an adult's full daily requirement of 2,100kcal from kale alone would mean eating 7.5kg, far more than a human stomach can hold. That is why its calories-per-area grade is C. Yet on the same table, its Supplementary grade is SS at 71.1 points, among the highest in this entire crop table. The gap between these two numbers is kale's true identity. Kale is not a staple; it is a crop that fills the gap that grains and tuber crops (tubers/storage roots) can never fill.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;What that gap actually is shows up in the micronutrients criterion. Kale's micronutrients grade is SS, and when converted to daily intake, it covers more than 10% of the recommended amount for 18 of the 21 nutrients evaluated — the highest count among every crop covered in this document. Even after rice, potatoes, and soybeans have covered calories and protein, vitamin A, vitamin C, vitamin K, folate, and calcium are still missing. If that gap continues long enough, a person falls ill on a full stomach. Scurvy, night blindness, and osteomalacia come not from starvation but from an unbalanced diet, and in disaster situations it is often the latter that actually kills people. A few kale plants can prevent that death.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The second strength lies in time. Kale is not a crop you pull up by the root and harvest once. Picking the mature lower leaves off the central stem makes new leaves keep coming from the top. A single plant can be harvested from spring to the following spring, and in southern South Korea, for more than two years. The Perenniality grade of S on the evaluation table points to exactly this. Cold hardiness of S overlaps with it. Kale does not die even at minus 10°C, and frost actually makes the leaves sweeter. In a winter field where nothing else is left, kale alone stands green. It amounts to a living store of vitamin C standing in a corner of the field, a capability that no storage technique can imitate.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The weaknesses are just as clear. A Pest and disease pressure grade of C, with an estimated yield loss without pest control of 51.7%, means that without any control at all you lose half your crop. Cabbage white butterfly caterpillars and diamondback moths eat the leaves, and because the leaves themselves are the harvest in kale, that damage becomes a direct loss. Storability is D, and picked leaves do not last a week at room temperature. Yield retention without synthetic inputs is also low, at 40.8%, so once fertilizer stops the leaves grow small and tough. And seed saving is tricky. Being biennial, it must go through winter before it flowers, and because of the cross-pollination typical of the cabbage family, it crosses freely with neighboring brassica crops. Section 13 deals with this problem separately.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In short, kale is not a crop meant to take over the whole field but one meant to stand ten to twenty plants deep in a corner of it. Those ten plants fill, all year round, the nutritional gap that no grain store can fill.
