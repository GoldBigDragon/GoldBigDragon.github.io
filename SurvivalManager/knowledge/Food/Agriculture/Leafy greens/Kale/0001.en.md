## Introduction
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Kale (Brassica oleracea var. acephala) is the same species as cabbage, but a strain that does not form a head, and it is the leafy vegetable closest to wild cabbage. Its overall grade is A (50.0 points, 17th of 183 valid species), and its supplementary-food grade is SS (71.1 points). Raw kale has only 28 kcal per 100 g, so covering 2,100 kcal a day with kale alone would take 7.5 kg, which is why calories per area is C. Kale is not a staple; it fills the nutritional gaps that grains and tubers cannot. Micronutrients are SS, and 18 of 21 nutrients exceed 10% of the daily recommended amount. Even when rice, potatoes, and beans cover calories and protein, vitamins A, C, and K, folate, and calcium remain empty, and if that gap lasts long it brings scurvy, night blindness, and osteomalacia. Picking the fully grown leaves at the bottom of the stem makes new leaves keep emerging at the top, so a single plant can be harvested from spring to the following spring, and it withstands -10℃, with frost making the leaves sweeter as sugars accumulate. It is a living vitamin C store standing in the winter field. Its weaknesses are the pests and diseases common to the cabbage family (estimated loss without control 51.7%), storability that does not last a week at room temperature (D), yield that drops sharply once fertilizer stops (yield retention without synthetic inputs 40.8%), and seed saving made difficult by being a biennial and cross-pollinating. It is a crop to keep as ten to twenty plants in a corner of the field, not across the whole field.

## 2. Plant Overview
| Item | Content |
|:---|:---|
| Classification | Biennial herb of the cabbage family (Brassicaceae), genus Brassica. Native to the Mediterranean coast and the Atlantic coast of western Europe, so it is weak in summer heat, likes calcium, and dislikes acidic soil |
| Leaves, stem | Leaves are large and thick with a waxy layer, either flat or heavily ruffled (green is the base color; reddish-purple strains also exist). A single thick central stem stands straight at 60-150 cm, and nodes remain where leaves were picked |
| Roots, flowers, pods | The taproot goes 30-60 cm deep, which helps in drought. Yellow flowers bloom in racemes in the spring of the second year, and the ripe pods dry to brown and split lengthwise |

| Stage | Period | Key points |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Germination to vegetative growth | Sowing to 8 true leaves (about 8 weeks) | The taproot goes down, the number of leaves increases, and the stem thickens. Phosphorus, nitrogen |
| Harvest stage | After 8 true leaves | Lower leaves mature and new leaves keep emerging at the top. Balance of nitrogen and potassium |
| Bolting and flowering | March-May of the following year | The stem grows rapidly and yellow flowers bloom. Phosphorus, potassium, boron |

> **What makes kale send up a flower stalk is not day length but winter cold. If young seedlings go through several weeks below 10℃, the flower stalk rises when temperatures climb the following spring.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Vernalization is essential for seed saving but is an accident to avoid for those who want to harvest leaves. Below 5℃ the plant hardly grows, but sugars and amino acids dissolve in the cell sap and lower its freezing point, so the leaves do not freeze down to -10℃.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Per 100 g: 28 kcal, protein 3.35 g (47.9% of calories), dietary fiber 3-4 g, vitamin C around 90 mg, calcium 200-330 mg, iron 1.5-2 mg, and potassium 400-500 mg. Vitamins A (beta-carotene) and K are among the highest of any vegetable, and lutein and zeaxanthin are also abundant. Low oxalic acid gives calcium a high absorption rate (reportedly around 5% for spinach and over 50% for kale), and iron is absorbed well because vitamin C comes with it, so kale is a source of calcium and iron where dairy and meat are gone.

## 3. Growing Environment and Varieties
| Condition | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Temperature | Germination 5-30℃ (optimum 20-25℃), growth 5-30℃ (optimum 15-20℃). Above 30℃ the leaves turn stiff and bitterness increases. It does not die even at -10℃ |
| Light | 6 hours or more is good; at 3-4 hours the leaves are thin and leggy. It keeps 86% of yield even under 40% shade, so it suits scrap land beside walls, in tree shade, and between buildings |
| Water, soil | Total evapotranspiration about 400 mm per crop cycle; soil moisture 70-80% of maximum water capacity. Loam or sandy loam rich in organic matter. Salt tolerance medium. pH 6.5 or higher (below 5.5, clubroot rises sharply) |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The temperature range reaches 25℃, so kale grows at almost any time except the height of summer, but even the same kale is a completely different food in an August leaf and a December leaf. For winter vitamins choose curly kale (very strong cold tolerance and the most widely grown) or a red strain (especially cold-hardy but low yield); for total yield, a large juicing variety (1.5 times curly kale); and if summer harvest is needed, a Tuscan type (relatively tolerant of heat). To keep saving seed, secure fixed varieties rather than hybrids (F1). Hybrids scatter their traits from the next year, and seed with no F1 label, or labeled fixed, landrace, or open-pollinated (OP), can be carried on from generation to generation.

## 4. Field Preparation, Sowing, and Transplanting
| Item | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Preparing the soil | Dig 20-30 cm deep (to make a path for the taproot), bed width 90-120 cm (two rows), and a drainage ditch is a must (excess water triggers root rot and clubroot) |
| Fertilization (1,000㎡) | Nitrogen 10-12 kg, phosphorus 6-8 kg, potassium 8-10 kg, fully matured compost 1,500-2,000 kg, lime 100-150 kg (if none, wood ash or burned and crushed shell, eggshell, or oyster shell powder). Basal fertilizer 1-2 weeks before transplanting: all the compost and phosphorus, and half of the nitrogen and potassium. Top dressing at 3-4 weeks after transplanting (mainly nitrogen), after the first harvest (nitrogen, potassium), and then a little every 3-4 weeks |
| Substitute resources | Urine diluted 8-10 times (pour in the furrows between plants; it burns if it touches the leaves; stop 2 weeks before harvest), fully matured compost, wood ash (a handful or two per 1㎡, no more than 2-3 times a year; too much blocks absorption of boron, iron, and manganese), legume green manure (plow under and transplant 3-4 weeks later), and grass liquid fertilizer (fermented 2-3 weeks, diluted 5 times) |

> **Picking leaves means removing nitrogen from the field. If a little fertilizer is not fed back at every harvest, new leaves get smaller and paler.**

| Cropping type | Sowing | Transplanting | Harvest |
|:---|:---|:---|:---|
| Autumn crop | July-Aug | Aug-Sep | Sep to the following spring. Best quality |
| Overwintering crop | Aug-Sep | Sep-Oct | Nov to April of the following year. Central region and south |
| Spring crop | Late March-April | Late April-May | June-Oct. Quality drops in summer |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In disaster conditions the autumn crop is the core. It supplies leaves through winter until the following spring, and that is exactly the period when every other vegetable is gone. Spring sowing is put off until after late March to avoid premature bolting, in which seedlings that spend more than 3 weeks below 10℃ send up a flower stalk that same spring.

| Planting | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Sowing | Depth 0.5-1 cm, germination in 4-7 days at 20-25℃. Seedlings are raised for 30-40 days (4-5 true leaves); direct sowing uses a row spacing of 40-50 cm (the taproot is not cut, so it is stronger against drought) |
| Thinning, transplanting | For direct sowing, thin twice: to 10 cm at 2-3 true leaves and to 30-40 cm at 4-5 true leaves. Transplanting spacing 30-40 cm. The thinned young seedlings can be eaten as they are |
| Seed treatment | Soak in 50℃ water for 20 minutes, then cool at once in cold water (above 55℃ the seeds die) |

## 5. Water Management and Cultivation Management
| Period | Water management |
|:---|:---|
| Sowing to seedling stage | Daily until germination so the topsoil does not dry; at the seedling stage once every 2-3 days, letting it dry slightly so roots reach downward |
| Vegetative growth, harvest stage | 1-2 times a week, deeply each time; during harvest once after picking leaves (shallow, frequent watering makes roots shallow) |
| Dormant period | Almost none (if wet soil freezes, roots are damaged) |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;If it wilts at midday and recovers by evening, that is normal; if it is still wilted in the morning, it lacks water; if the leaves are small, thick, and tough, the shortage is persistent. If lower leaves turn yellow and soft, it is excess water, so dig a drainage ditch and stop watering; if a whole plant suddenly wilts, dig up the root and check for galls. Excess water collapses plants within a few days, so prevent it structurally by raising the beds. Mulch of straw, dry grass, fallen leaves, or rice hulls (5-10 cm) reduces evaporation so watering is nearly halved, suppresses weeds, and prevents freezing in winter. Slugs hide easily underneath, so lift it now and then.

| Management | Method |
|:---|:---|
| Hilling | Pile 5 cm of soil around the base 3-4 weeks after transplanting (picking raises the plant and makes it tall, and wind knocks it over, so adventitious roots hold it) |
| Weeds | Weeks 4-6 after transplanting are the critical period. Mulch right after transplanting, then scrape only the surface 2-3 cm with a hoe every 2 weeks (digging deep cuts fine roots and brings weed seeds up) |
| Wintering | Hill the base, cover with straw or fallen leaves, set windbreaks of sorghum or corn stalks on the northwest, and use a nonwoven fabric tunnel in the central-northern region |

> **Winter damage to kale comes from wind rather than temperature. Cold wind strips moisture from the leaves, and if the ground freezes so that roots cannot lift water, the leaves dry out. Blocking the wind comes before covering.**

## 6. Pests, Diseases, and Nutrient Problems
| Pest or disease | Symptoms and season | Control without chemicals |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Cabbage white caterpillar | Eats everything but the veins. April-June, Sep-Oct | Pick by hand from the underside of leaves in early morning or at dusk; rub off the yellow eggs on the underside |
| Diamondback moth, cabbage moth | Gnaws the underside of leaves leaving a thin membrane, or bores into the heart. May-Oct | Insect netting, diluted wood vinegar, frequent scouting, removing infested plants |
| Flea beetle | Pinhole-like holes in leaves. Seedling stage | Laying straw, thinly dusting dry ash (again after rain), insect netting |
| Aphids, thrips, slugs | Sucks sap from shoots / silvery scars / irregular holes at night | Soapy water and water jets, protecting ladybugs / keeping humidity up by watering, removing weeds / catching at night, bands of ash or rice hulls |
| Clubroot | Galls form on the roots and plants wilt in the daytime. Acid soil, excess water | Cannot be cured. Keep pH at 6.5 or above, drainage, rotation |
| Downy mildew, powdery mildew, soft rot | Yellow spots on leaves with mold on the underside / white powder / tissue softens and smells foul | Ventilation, spacing, removing diseased leaves / diluted milk (milk to water 1:5) / avoiding wounds, drainage |

> **Clubroot cannot be cured with chemicals and lives in the soil for over 7 years. A field for cabbage-family crops can only be protected in advance by three things: soil pH, drainage, and rotation.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;If you pull up a wilting plant and the root has club-like galls, it is clubroot. Do not plant cabbage-family crops (kale, cabbage, Chinese cabbage, radish, leaf mustard, rapeseed, broccoli) in that field for at least 5 years, preferably 7, and do not put diseased plants in compost; burn them or bury them deep and far away. Against butterflies and moths, the surest way is to cover the field on hoops with netting of about 1 mm mesh (a mosquito net or old curtain also works) and seal the edges with soil; remove eggs and larvae before covering and open one side during the day for ventilation. Make soapy water by dissolving 20 g of unscented soap in 1 L of water and spray it so that it directly touches aphids (because the wax layer makes it run off, raise the concentration slightly or spray in several rounds); use a garlic-and-chili infusion as a repellent and wood vinegar at 500-1,000 times dilution. Spraying broadly kills slow-breeding natural enemies first, so protect ladybugs, lacewings, spiders, and parasitic wasps.

| Symptom | Cause and remedy |
|:---|:---|
| Yellowing from the lower leaves, small plant | Nitrogen deficiency. Diluted urine, fully matured compost, grass liquid fertilizer |
| Deformed new leaves, dead growing point | Boron deficiency (common in fields given too much ash). Stop the ash and raise organic matter with matured compost |
| New leaves with green veins and yellow between them | Iron or magnesium deficiency. Check whether pH is too high (above 7.5, boron, iron, and manganese cannot be absorbed) |
| Soft, large, tender leaves that attract pests | Nitrogen excess. Stop the nitrogen supply |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, and magnesium move around inside the plant, so deficiency appears first on lower leaves, while calcium, boron, and iron cannot move, so it appears first on new leaves.

## 7. Intercropping and Crop Rotation
| Compatibility | Companions |
|:---|:---|
| Good | Legumes (peas, kidney beans: fix nitrogen), spring onion, onion, and garlic (the smell confuses butterflies and aphids), potatoes and beets (different root depths mean little competition for nutrients) |
| Bad | Cabbage-family crops such as Chinese cabbage, radish, leaf mustard, and broccoli (they share pests and diseases, which build up), strawberries (they hinder each other's growth) |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Kale takes a lot of nitrogen out of the soil, so legumes follow it. Going around in the order kale (cabbage family) → peas and kidney beans (legumes) → potatoes and sweet potatoes → spring onion and onion → kale again, kale returns to the same spot only every 4 years. Planting peas or kidney beans in the furrows between kale beds and plowing the stems and leaves in after harvest creates nitrogen inside the field without chemical fertilizer.

## 8. Harvest and Seed Saving
| Harvest | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Timing | Baby leaves 30-40 days after sowing, first harvest at 60-70 days (8 or more true leaves, leaf length 20-25 cm), then repeated every 1-2 weeks. In winter (Nov to March of the following year) it does not grow, so pick the existing leaves one at a time |
| Technique | In early morning, pick 3-5 fully grown leaves from the bottom up by snapping the leaf stalk down toward the stem (a clean cut keeps it from rotting). Always leave 6-8 or more leaves at the top, and water after picking |
| Duration | Planted in autumn, it can be harvested for 7-9 months until the flower stalk rises the next spring, and ten to twenty plants can supply one person's winter greens |
| Flower stalk | Flower stalks at the bud stage taste like broccoli and become a hungry-spring vegetable. Leave only the seed plants and eat the rest |

> **There is only one growing point, at the top of the stem, so if the top is cut, that plant is finished. The leaves you leave are the photosynthesis factory, and that factory makes the next leaves.**

| Seed saving | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Two barriers | It is a biennial that must pass a winter to flower. It is cross-pollinating and crosses completely with all of Brassica oleracea (cabbage, broccoli, cauliflower, kohlrabi, Brussels sprouts). Chinese cabbage, radish, and leaf mustard are different species and do not matter |
| Preventing crossing | Staggered cultivation is the most realistic: this year let only kale flower and eat all the cabbage, and do the reverse next year (seed life 4-5 years). Isolation distance 500 m or more. In a small field, cover three or four seed plants with netting before the flower buds form and move pollen with a brush (it is self-incompatible, so several plants are needed) |
| Procedure | In autumn choose plants with large, disease-free leaves. Overwinter Nov-Feb (hill the base and cover with straw and fallen leaves), bolting March-April (stake), and in June-July when two thirds of the lower pods are brown, cut the whole stem and after-ripen it in shade. After full drying, winnow and seal |
| Central-northern region | If overwintering in the open is difficult, pull the plants up with roots and some soil before frost, bury them in a pit or storeroom at 0-5℃, and replant in spring |

> **Keep at least 5 plants for seed, preferably 10 or more. If seed of a cross-pollinating crop is taken from only one or two plants, inbreeding depression appears within a few generations.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Keep the seed sealed in a cool, dark place, and every year place 20 seeds on a damp cloth to check the germination rate. Save seed once every 3-4 years, with proper isolation.

## 9. Storage
| Method | Conditions and period |
|:---|:---|
| Leaving it standing in the field | Down to -10℃, Nov to March of the following year (covers and windbreaks raise the wintering success rate) |
| Picking ahead | Pick a few days' worth in advance, do not wash, wrap in a damp cloth, and keep in a cool storeroom or under a north-facing eave for 5-7 days (washing starts spoilage) |
| Drying | Wash and shake off the water, blanch in boiling water for 30 seconds to 1 minute, and dry in shade until it crumbles by hand. Keep 6 months to 1 year in a sealed container with charcoal or roasted rice. Make it into powder and add a spoonful at a time to soup or porridge |
| Lactic fermentation | Salt 2-3%, several weeks to several months. Cut coarsely and salt, press down once wilted so the juice covers it. A good part of the vitamin C remains |

> **Drying keeps vitamin A, calcium, iron, and dietary fiber but loses most of the vitamin C. Winter vitamin C comes not from dried leaves but from fresh leaves standing in the field and from lactic fermentation.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;If white film yeast floats on the surface of a ferment, skim it off, and if it smells rotten instead of sour and fresh, throw it away. Kale leaves are tougher than cabbage, so cut them fine and knead thoroughly.

## 10. Cooking and Precautions
| Method | Fuel and nutrition |
|:---|:---|
| Raw (wraps, salad) | No fuel, maximum nutrient retention. Remove tough veins |
| Blanching for 30 seconds to 1 minute | Very little fuel. Goitrogens decrease and the volume shrinks greatly |
| Adding to soup or porridge | Add at the end to a fire already burning. Eating the broth too preserves nutrients |
| Stir-frying | Vitamin A (beta-carotene), K, and lutein are fat-soluble, so absorption improves when there is oil. Ground perilla or sesame seeds or soybean milk work the same way |

| Component | Problem and response |
|:---|:---|
| Goitrogens (breakdown products of glucosinolates) | They interfere with iodine use and can lower thyroid function. The risk is eating a large amount of raw kale every day, especially as juice, for a long time on an iodine-poor diet. Alternate raw and cooked, add seaweed (sea mustard, kelp, laver), and cook it if thyroid function is low |
| Oxalic acid | Kale has 20-50 mg per 100 g, far below spinach (around 700 mg). Eating nearly 1 kg of kale alone every day makes it accumulate, so mix various vegetables |
| Nitrate | Accumulates in leaves given a lot of nitrogen. Do not overapply nitrogen and harvest in the afternoon |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Kale has no acute toxicity and can be eaten as it is after washing.

## 11. Annual Work Schedule
| Month | Work |
|:---|:---|
| Jan-Feb | Harvest winter leaves, check windbreaks and covers, turn the compost in February |
| Mar-Apr | Spring sowing in late March, stakes for seed plants. April: seed plants flower and need isolation, harvest flower stalks |
| May-June | Transplant spring seedlings and install insect netting in May. First harvest and peak pest season in June |
| July-Aug | Seed harvest in early July, mulching and watering, start of autumn sowing. August: raising seedlings, clearing out spring plants |
| Sep-Oct | Transplant autumn seedlings, second pest peak, and top dressing in September. Autumn kale harvest begins and wintering preparation (hilling) in October |
| Nov-Dec | When sugars rise after frost in November, cover with straw and fallen leaves and set up windbreaks. Harvest winter leaves and check seed plants' wintering in December |

> **There is no resting month for kale. It stands in the field all year and is harvested all year. Even in January and February, when there are no other vegetables, the kale column has a harvest written in it, and the reason to keep kale in a corner of the field lies in those two columns.**
