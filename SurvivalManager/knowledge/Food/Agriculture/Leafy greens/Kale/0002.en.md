## 1. Disaster-Preparedness Evaluation

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Kale's Overall grade is A, with a score of 50.0. Broken down by scenario, its Short-term ability to endure the early phase of a disaster is S (51.0), its Long-term ability to be self-sufficient over several years is A (49.0), and its Supplementary value as a side-dish resource beyond a staple food is SS (71.1). A Supplementary score of 71.1 is among the highest in this entire crop table. Two facts sum up kale's character in a single line: that Short-term outranks Long-term, and that Supplementary outranks Overall by as much as 21 points.

### 1-3. Why the Calorie Grade Is C but the Supplementary Grade Is SS

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The key to reading this table is that Calories per area C sits side by side with a Supplementary score of SS at 71.1. The two figures look contradictory, but they are really the same story told from two sides. 100g of kale contains 28kcal. Set against the same weight of cooked rice at 130kcal or dried soybeans at 401kcal, it is nearly as light as water. Even harvesting 4kg from 1m² converts to only 1,120kcal, just 0.43× the self-sufficiency line. A plan to fill the stomach with kale never works to begin with.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Yet that very low calorie count is what lifts the grades of other criteria. The 47.9% P:E behind the SS in protein density is not a result of kale having a lot of protein. At 3.35g per 100g, the absolute amount is small. It is because the denominator, calories, is so small that the ratio comes out large. The SS in micronutrients works the same way. It means the food has an extremely high density of nutrients per calorie, and that is exactly the definition of a supplementary crop.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;So this crop's place is not the center of the diet but its edge. Calories are established by crops like potatoes, sweet potatoes, rice, and maize. Protein is added on top by soybeans, adzuki beans, and peas. After that, there is still a gap left over: vitamin C, vitamin A, vitamin K, folate, and calcium. Grains, tuber crops (tubers/storage roots), and legumes alone barely fill these five. Kale slots in exactly there.

> **The purpose of planting kale is not to fill the rice bowl, but to stop the slow-developing deficiency in someone who lives on rice alone.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In numbers, it looks like this. Kale's micronutrients criterion covers 10% or more of the daily recommended amount for 18 of the 21 nutrients evaluated — the highest count of any crop covered in this document. Few crops earn an SS in micronutrients, and 18 nutrients puts it at the very top even among those. Eating 200g of kale a day — about five or six large leaves — exceeds the daily recommended amount of vitamin C and covers several times the recommended amount of vitamin K. Having ten kale plants standing in the field is, in effect, the same as having a bottle of multivitamins planted there.

### 1-4. How to Read the Strengths

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The criteria that earned an SS are five: protein density, micronutrients, infrastructure needs, cooking and processing, and temperature range. An S went to speed to harvest, perenniality, cold hardiness, continuous-cropping tolerance, taste, and prevalence.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Temperature range SS and cold hardiness S together create kale's most practical value. Its viable growth range spans 25°C, from 5 to 30°C, and it does not die even at minus 10°C. Frosted kale actually tastes better, which comes from a physiological response in which the plant dissolves sugar into its cell fluid to lower the freezing point so it will not freeze to death. In effect, it makes its own antifreeze, and that antifreeze registers on the human tongue as sweetness. Thanks to this trait, from November to March of the following year — the period when every other vegetable has disappeared — kale becomes the only green thing still standing in the field.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;History answers why winter greens matter. The human body cannot make vitamin C and can barely store it. The body's reserves run out in roughly one to three months, after which scurvy sets in — bleeding gums, wounds that will not heal, and old scars reopening. It is a disease that has repeatedly killed people on long sea voyages, in besieged cities, and in armies wintering in the field, and the cause was not a shortage of calories but the absence of fresh plant food. A winter of nothing but grain, dried beans, and salted meat reproduces exactly that condition. A single kale plant, alive even below freezing, breaks that condition.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Perenniality S and speed to harvest S should also be read together. From sowing to the first harvest is 60-70 days, and after that harvest continues every 1-2 weeks. Unlike radish or cabbage, which are planted once and pulled once, a single kale plant keeps working. This matters in a disaster because the harvest is never concentrated at one point in time. Without storage technology, a crop harvested all at once mostly rots. With kale, you only need to pick as much as you will eat that day. This is why the low Storability grade of D is, in practice, considerably eased.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Cooking-and-processing SS and infrastructure-needs SS are especially valuable once fuel and materials run out. It can be washed and eaten as is, so fuel use is zero. It just needs to be planted in the open field — no stakes, no plastic sheeting, no greenhouse required. That contrasts with soybeans, which need eight hours of soaking and an hour of boiling.

### 1-5. How to Read the Weaknesses

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The only criterion with an E is non-food value; D went to storability; and C went to calories per area, pest and disease pressure, and yield retention without synthetic inputs.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Pest and disease pressure C is the most substantial burden. An estimated yield loss without pest control of 51.7% means that with no control at all, you lose half the crop. This is a shared fate among cabbage-family crops, and because the leaves themselves are the harvest in kale, this loss is immediately visible. When cabbage white butterfly caterpillars chew the leaves, holes remain; when aphids attach, the leaves curl. The saving grace is that pest-control self-reliance is B. Even without chemicals, insect netting, adjusted sowing dates, and hand-picking can block a substantial share of the damage. Section 9 covers these methods in detail.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;A yield retention without synthetic inputs of C, at 40.8%, also has to be read seriously. It means that stopping chemical fertilizer cuts yield to 40%. Kale is a heavy feeder, especially hungry for nitrogen, so when fertilizer runs short the leaves grow smaller, paler, and tougher — the exact opposite position from beans, which make their own nitrogen through root nodules. To keep kale going in a disaster, you have to build a separate cycle that steadily feeds in compost and diluted urine, and section 5 covers this problem.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Storability D is a story about the picked leaf. At room temperature it turns soft within a week. But as noted above, picking only as much as needed straight from the field largely neutralizes this weakness. Even so, you still need drying or pickling techniques for the days when deep snow keeps you out of the field, and section 15 covers that.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;A seed-saving grade of A looks like a decent grade, but in practice it is tricky. Being biennial, kale must go through winter to set seed, and being cross-pollinated, it crosses freely with nearby cabbage, broccoli, kohlrabi, and Brussels sprouts. Because they are all varieties of the same species (Brassica oleracea), whatever pollen a bee carries simply mixes in. If this problem is not solved, unidentifiable hybrids grow in the field the following year. Section 13 is the solution.
