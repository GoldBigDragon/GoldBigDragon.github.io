## 12. Harvest — The Technique of Continuous Picking

| Use | Timing | State |
|---|:---:|---|
| Baby leaves | 30-40 days after sowing | Leaves smaller than a palm |
| First harvest | 60-70 days after sowing | 8 or more true leaves, leaf length 20-25cm |
| Repeat harvest | Every 1-2 weeks after that | Fully grown leaves, from the bottom up |
| Winter harvest | Nov-Mar of the following year | Since it is not growing, take existing leaves one at a time |

### 12-1. Why You Pick From the Bottom

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Kale has only one growth point, at the top of the stem. New leaves keep rising from there, and the farther down you go, the older the leaves are. So picking the fully grown lower leaves costs the plant almost nothing - you are only taking an old leaf whose photosynthetic capacity has already declined, and the plant redirects the nutrients it was spending there to new leaves.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Cut the top, on the other hand, and that plant is finished. With the growth point gone, it can no longer produce leaves. Side shoots may emerge and branch out, but the leaves get smaller and the yield drops sharply. Only harvest a whole plant at once when you have decided to give up on it.

| Item | Technique |
|---|---|
| Leaves to pick | 3-5 fully grown leaves from the bottom up |
| Leaves to leave | Always 6-8 or more at the top |
| How to pick | Snap the petiole downward toward the stem. The break needs to be clean or it will rot |
| When to pick | Early morning. Freshest after holding overnight moisture |
| After picking | Water it, and top-dress every 3-4 weeks |

> **Always leave six to eight leaves at the top. The leaves you leave are the photosynthesis factory, and that factory is what makes the next leaves. Get greedy and pick them all, and that plant simply stops.**

### 12-2. How Long One Plant Keeps Going

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Kale planted in autumn keeps yielding for roughly 7-9 months, right up until the flower stalk rises the following spring. In southern South Korea, or in a year with a mild winter, it can even carry on into the second summer. The perenniality grade of S on the evaluation table refers to this staying power. Harvest three or four leaves a week from one plant for 30 weeks and you pass 100 leaves, and if one adult eats three or four big leaves a day, ten to twenty kale plants can cover one person's winter greens.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Once spring comes and the flower stalk rises, the leaves toughen and the bitterness intensifies. That is the end for that plant. The flower stalk itself is edible, though. Before the flowers open, while it is still in bud, the stalk tastes similar to broccoli and is tender. It earns its keep during the empty stretch between the tail end of winter and the arrival of spring vegetables - the so-called spring famine gap. Leave only the few plants you want for seed, and pick and eat the flower stalks from the rest.

## 13. Seed Saving — The Biennial and Cross-Pollination Barriers

### 13-1. Two Barriers

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There are two barriers to saving kale seed. First, it is biennial, so it has to pass through winter before it flowers. Second, it is cross-pollinated, so it crosses freely with any nearby brassica crop. That is why its seed-saving grade on the evaluation table stays at A, in contrast to soybean, which gets an S for self-pollination.

| Barrier | Details | Solution |
|---|---|---|
| Biennial habit | The flower stalk rises only after exposure to cold | Overwinter it in place, or pull it up, store it, and replant it in spring |
| Cross-pollination | Bees carry pollen and mix it with other varieties | Isolation distance, staggered timing, netting |

### 13-2. What It Crosses With

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Kale (Brassica oleracea) crosses completely with every variety of the same species. Cabbage, broccoli, cauliflower, kohlrabi, Brussels sprouts, and kale are all one species, so there is no barrier between them at all. If cabbage flowers next to kale, the seed you collect that year will be a hybrid that is neither kale nor cabbage.

| Crop | Scientific Name | Crosses With Kale |
|---|---|:---:|
| Cabbage, broccoli, cauliflower, kohlrabi, Brussels sprouts | Brassica oleracea | Crosses completely |
| Napa cabbage, bok choy, turnip, rapeseed | Brassica rapa | Almost never crosses |
| Leaf mustard, mustard | Brassica juncea | Almost never crosses |
| Radish | Raphanus sativus | Different genus - does not cross |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This table matters in practice. It is fine for napa cabbage, radish, or leaf mustard to flower the same year you are saving kale seed, because they are different species. The only real problem is the Brassica oleracea group - cabbage, broccoli, kohlrabi, and the rest.

### 13-3. Three Ways to Prevent Crossing

| Method | Details | Practicality |
|---|---|---|
| Isolation distance | Keep it 500m or more from any other flowering Brassica oleracea | Hard, since you cannot control your neighbor's field |
| Staggered timing | Let only one type flower in a given year | The most realistic |
| Netting | Cover it with mesh before flowering and hand-pollinate with a brush inside | Reliable at a small scale |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Staggered timing is the most realistic solution. This year, save seed only from kale, and eat all the cabbage before it can flower. Next year, do the opposite. Since kale seed keeps for 4-5 years, rotating which one species you save each year never leaves you without seed. This approach protects purity with the least effort.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Netting is the method for when your plot is small and your neighbors are close. Before the buds form, cover three or four plants you plan to save seed from all together with mesh to keep bees out. That blocks outside pollen, but it also blocks pollination altogether. Kale has self-incompatibility, meaning it rarely fertilizes well with its own pollen, so plants inside the net have to pollinate each other. Just move a brush or a small paintbrush back and forth between the flowers. That is why you need several seed-saving plants, not just one.

> **Leave at least 5 plants for seed, and ideally 10 or more. For a cross-pollinated crop, saving seed from only one or two plants brings on inbreeding depression within a few generations, and the line weakens.**

### 13-4. Overwintering and Seed-Saving Steps

| Step | Timing | Details |
|---|---|---|
| Selecting seed plants | Autumn | Choose plants with large leaves, no disease, and clear varietal traits |
| Overwintering | Nov-Feb | Mound soil around the base and cover with straw and fallen leaves |
| Bolting | Mar-Apr | The flower stalk rises. Stake it so it does not fall over |
| Flowering | Apr-May | Yellow flowers bloom. Maintain isolation now |
| Pod set | May-Jun | Pods form |
| Harvest | Jun-Jul | Once two-thirds of the pods have dried brown, cut the whole stalk |
| After-ripening, threshing | Right after harvest | Dry it 1-2 weeks in the shade, then put it in a sack and beat it |
| Sorting, storage | After it is fully dry | Winnow away the husks and seal it somewhere cool |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In regions with harsh winters, such as central and northern South Korea, overwintering in the open field can fail. In that case, pull the plant up by the roots before the first frost, with a little soil still clinging to it, bury it in a pit or a cool shed, and replant it the following spring. You have to keep it at 0-5°C, cold enough that it will not freeze but will not sprout either. Since experiencing cold in storage is itself vernalization, a plant that has gone through this will send up its flower stalk as soon as it is replanted.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Do not leave the pods in the field until they are all completely dry. Brassica pods split open on their own once fully dry and spill their seed onto the ground. The trick is to cut the whole stalk while the lower two-thirds of the pods have turned brown and the upper ones are still green, then let it after-ripen in the shade. If you lay the cut stalks on a cloth or sack while they dry, you can even catch the seed from pods that split open and fall.

| Item | Standard |
|---|---|
| Seed longevity | 4-5 years sealed at room temperature |
| Optimal storage | Somewhere cool, dark, and dry, in a sealed container |
| Desiccant | Roasted, cooled rice, charcoal, dry ash |
| Checking germination | Each year, place 20 seeds on a damp cloth and count the germination rate after a few days |
| Yield per plant | A few thousand seeds |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;One kale plant yields a few thousand seeds. Save seed from ten plants and you secure several years' worth of seed at once. Seed saving is not an every-year task - it is something you do once every 3-4 years, and do properly, with isolation, in that one year.

## 14. Toxicity and Compounds of Concern

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Kale has no acute toxicity. You can wash it and eat it as is, and the evaluation table's cooking-and-processing grade of SS reflects that. Still, there are two compounds that can become a problem if eaten a lot and for a long time: goitrogens and oxalic acid.

| Compound | What It Is | What Problem It Causes | How to Reduce It |
|---|---|---|---|
| Goitrogens (glucosinolate breakdown products) | The source of the pungent smell and bitterness characteristic of brassicas | Can interfere with iodine uptake and lower thyroid function | Blanching or cooking breaks down a substantial share of them |
| Oxalic acid | An organic acid the plant stores bound to calcium | Eating a lot raises the risk of kidney stones | Kale's level is on the low side. Blanching and discarding the water reduces it further |
| Nitrates | Accumulate in leaves given heavy nitrogen fertilizer | A problem with excessive intake | Do not over-fertilize with nitrogen, and harvest in the afternoon |

### 14-1. Goitrogens

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Brassica plants contain sulfur compounds called glucosinolates. When a leaf is damaged, an enzyme kicks in and converts these into pungent-tasting compounds, whose original purpose is to drive off the insects eating it. Some of these breakdown products interfere with how the human thyroid uses iodine. That is why they are called goitrogens.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;How real the risk is depends on the conditions. Someone eating enough iodine has no problem eating a normal amount of kale. It becomes risky when a diet is short on iodine and someone eats large amounts of raw kale every day for a long time, especially juiced. In a disaster situation, cut off from the sea with no more seaweed, kelp, or salt, an actual iodine deficiency can develop, and the problem compounds if kale becomes your main vegetable under those conditions.

> **The fix is simple. Blanching destroys the glucosinolate-breaking enzyme and leaches a substantial share of the goitrogens into the water. Do not eat it only raw - alternate with cooked forms.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;If you can get seaweed, eating it alongside kale is the fundamental solution. Miyeok (a Korean edible brown seaweed), dasima (kelp), and gim (dried laver) are iodine sources, and a small amount is enough. Anyone whose thyroid function is already low is safer cutting back on raw kale and eating it cooked.

### 14-2. Oxalic Acid

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Oxalic acid is a compound that matters for spinach, Swiss chard, and purslane, and kale's level is dramatically lower than that group's. Spinach holds around 700mg per 100g, while kale runs 20-50mg - a difference not of single digits but of more than tenfold. That is why kale's calcium absorption rate is high: with little oxalic acid around, the calcium stays free rather than being bound up. Still, an extreme diet of eating close to 1kg of kale alone every day would let the total accumulate. Mixing in a variety of vegetables is always the safer approach.

## 15. Storage Without Refrigeration

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Kale's storability grade is D. A picked leaf will not last a week at room temperature with no electricity. This weakness is largely solved in the field, though, because if you pick only as much as you need that day, there is nothing to store.

### 15-1. The Field Is the Storehouse

| Method | Conditions | Period |
|---|---|---|
| Leave it standing in the field | Down to -10°C | Nov-Mar of the following year |
| Cover with straw or fallen leaves | Central South Korea and farther north | Raises the odds of successful overwintering |
| Set up a windbreak | On the northwest side | Reduces damage from winter drought |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Deep winter snow can keep you out of the field for several days running. Just pick a few days' worth ahead of time. Left unwashed, wrapped in a damp cloth, and kept in a cool shed, under the floor, or under a north-facing eave, it lasts five to seven days. Washing it leaves surface moisture that becomes the starting point for decay, so wash it only right before eating.

### 15-2. Drying

| Step | Method |
|---|---|
| Prep | Wash it and shake off the water completely |
| Blanching | 30 seconds to 1 minute in boiling water. Pull it out once the color brightens |
| Hanging | Spread it out or hang it on a line somewhere shaded and breezy |
| Full drying | Until it crumbles by hand |
| Storage | In a sealed container, together with charcoal or roasted rice |
| Duration | 6 months to 1 year |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Blanching before drying reduces color and nutrient loss and cuts down on discoloration during storage. Vitamin C, though, is mostly lost during the drying process. Dried kale is a way of preserving vitamin A, calcium, iron, and fiber - not a way of preserving vitamin C. This distinction matters. Your winter vitamin C comes not from the dried leaves but from fresh leaves still standing in the field and from the pickled forms covered next.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Fully dried kale can be rubbed by hand into a powder. Added a spoonful at a time to soup or porridge, it takes up little space while still supplying nutrition. You can also rehydrate it in water like mungnamul (dried, rehydrated preserved greens), season it as a side dish, or stir-fry it.

### 15-3. Salt-Packing and Fermentation

| Method | Salt Concentration | Duration | Characteristics |
|---|:---:|---|---|
| Jjanji-style salt-packing | 10-15% salt | Several months | Keeps a long time, but must be soaked in water before eating |
| Lactic fermentation (kimchi-style) | 2-3% salt | Weeks to months | Retains a substantial share of the vitamin C |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Lactic fermentation is the second route to winter vitamin C. You can ferment kale the same way you make kimchi from napa cabbage. Cut the leaves into coarse pieces, salt them at 2-3% of their weight, and once they have wilted, pack them down so the brine covers the material. Cut off from air, lactic acid bacteria grow and produce acid, and that acid keeps other microbes out.

> **Salt-packing and boiling for storage lose most of the vitamin C, but lactic fermentation retains a substantial amount. This is the historical reason fermented foods have protected against scurvy when nothing fresh was available in winter.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;German sauerkraut and Korean kimchi work on the same principle, and it is no accident that this kind of food developed in regions with long winters. Kale's leaves are tougher than cabbage's, so fermentation runs a little slower, but cutting it small and kneading it thoroughly takes care of that. The trick is to pack it into a jar or glass container and weigh it down with a stone or a water-filled bag so it stays submerged under the brine. Skim off any white kahm yeast that forms on the surface, and discard the batch if it smells rotten rather than sour and fresh.

## 16. Cooking and Processing

| Method | Fuel | Vitamin C Retention | Notes |
|---|:---:|:---:|---|
| Raw (wraps, salads) | None | Maximum | The most efficient. Remove the tough midribs |
| Blanching, 30 sec-1 min | Very little | Over half | Reduces goitrogens and shrinks the volume substantially |
| Adding to soup or porridge | Combined with other cooking | Retained if you drink the broth too | A method that uses no extra fuel |
| Stir-frying | Little | Medium | Improves absorption of fat-soluble vitamins A and K |
| Fermentation | None | Substantial retention | Doubles as storage and preparation |
| Drying into powder | Sun and wind while drying | Almost none | Minerals and vitamin A remain |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;When fuel is scarce, kale's biggest advantage is that it does not need cooking at all. Compare that to soybean, which needs eight hours of soaking and an hour of boiling, and the difference is huge. Still, as noted above, eating it exclusively raw is not advisable because of the goitrogens, and tossing it in at the end of a fire already cooking something else is the way to get a cooked form without spending any extra fuel.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;If you have even a little oil, mixing in some stir-frying is nutritionally worthwhile. Kale's vitamin A (as beta-carotene), vitamin K, and lutein are all fat-soluble and need oil present to be absorbed. Eaten without oil, the share of those compounds that actually gets into your body drops sharply. Grinding in perilla or sesame seeds, or adding it to a broth made from ground soybeans, has the same effect.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The tough central midrib is normally removed, but there is no need to throw it away. Chopped fine and simmered a long time in soup or porridge, it softens up, and its fiber and minerals stay intact. Thick stems work the same way - peel them, slice them, and boil them, and they are edible too.

## 17. Annual Work Calendar

| Month | Work |
|---|---|
| Jan | Harvest winter leaves. Check the condition of the windbreak and covering |
| Feb | Harvest winter leaves. Prepare for spring sowing and turn the compost |
| Mar | Spring sowing in late month. Seed plants begin bolting; set up stakes |
| Apr | Manage spring-sown seedlings. Seed plants flower; maintain isolation. Harvest flower stalks |
| May | Transplant spring seedlings. Start monitoring for cabbage white butterflies. Set up insect netting |
| Jun | First harvest of spring kale. Seed plants form pods. Peak pest season |
| Jul | Save seed in early month. Manage the hot season (mulching, watering). Start autumn sowing |
| Aug | Continue autumn sowing. Manage seedlings. Clear out spring plants |
| Sep | Transplant autumn seedlings. Second peak pest season. Top-dress |
| Oct | Start harvesting autumn kale. Prepare for winter (hilling) |
| Nov | Sweetness rises after frost. Cover with straw and fallen leaves; set up windbreak |
| Dec | Harvest winter leaves. Check that seed plants are overwintering well |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;What this table shows is that kale has no off month. It stands in the field all year round, and you harvest it all year round. Even in January and February, when no other vegetable is available, kale's row still shows a harvest. Those two entries alone are the reason to keep a corner of the field for kale.
