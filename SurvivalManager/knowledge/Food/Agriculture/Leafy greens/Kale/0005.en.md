## 8. Crop Management

### 8-1. Thinning and Hilling

| Task | Timing | Details |
|---|---|---|
| 1st thinning | 2-3 true leaves | Reduce to 10cm plant spacing |
| 2nd thinning | 4-5 true leaves | Final spacing 30-40cm |
| 1st hilling | 3-4 weeks after transplanting | Mound soil 5cm up around the base of the stem |
| 2nd hilling | After the plant has grown larger | Add more if needed |
| Removing lower leaves | As needed | Remove yellowed and diseased leaves |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Hilling is especially important for kale. As you keep picking leaves upward, the stem grows longer and leaves remain only at the top, raising the plant's center of gravity. When the wind blows, it topples, and a toppled plant, once its stem bends, is finished right there. Mounding soil around the base lets adventitious roots emerge at that spot and anchor the plant. Staking the stem with bamboo or a thick branch serves the same purpose. In regions where winter snow piles up, this single task makes a large difference to overwintering success.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;It also matters not to leave yellowed lower leaves in place. An old leaf does almost no photosynthesis while still consuming nutrients, and it becomes the spot where fungal disease gets started in humid weather. Take removed leaves out of the field and add them to the compost pile. If a leaf is diseased, do not even put it in the compost — burn it or bury it far away instead.

### 8-2. Weed Management

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Without herbicides, timing is everything in weed management. The 4-6 weeks after transplanting are the critical window. If weeds outgrow the kale during this period, the plant is robbed of light and nutrients and struggles to recover. Once this window has passed, on the other hand, and the kale's leaves cover the ground, that shade suppresses the weeds on its own.

| Timing | Method |
|---|---|
| Right after transplanting | Suppress in advance with mulch |
| 2-6 weeks after transplanting | Hoe shallowly every 2 weeks |
| After that | Pull only the large weeds |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Hoe shallowly. Digging deep cuts kale's fine roots and brings buried weed seeds up to the surface, where they germinate anew. Scraping just the top 2-3cm to sever the roots of newly sprouted weeds is enough. Do it in the morning on a sunny day, and the severed weeds dry out and die that same day.

### 8-3. Winter Care

| Method | Material | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Hilling the base | Soil | Protects the area near the roots and the growing point |
| Straw covering | Rice straw, dry grass | Slows the drop in soil temperature |
| Leaf-litter covering | Oak leaves | Same effect as straw, and becomes fertilizer in spring as is |
| Windbreak | Sorghum stalks, corn stalks, reed screens | Blocks the northwest wind. Much of cold damage comes from wind |
| Tunnel | Row-cover fabric, plastic sheeting (if available) | Optional in southern South Korea; extends the harvest period in the central and northern regions |

> **Kale's winter damage comes more from wind than from temperature. Leaves suffer more in a strong wind at minus 5°C than in still air at minus 10°C. Blocking the wind comes before covering the plant.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Cold wind draws moisture out of the leaves, and if the ground freezes so the roots cannot draw up water, the leaves dry out and turn brown. This is what is called winter drought. Standing sorghum or corn stalks woven into a screen on the northwest side greatly reduces this damage, and since the material is a byproduct of summer crops, there is no need to source it separately.

## 9. Pests and Diseases — Controlling Them Without Chemicals

### 9-1. Major Pests

| Pest | Damage | Season | Non-chemical control |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cabbage white butterfly caterpillar | Green caterpillars eat everything but the leaf veins | April-June, September-October | Hand-picking, insect netting, rubbing out the yellow eggs on the underside of leaves |
| Diamondback moth | Small caterpillars scrape the underside of leaves, leaving a thin membrane | May-October | Insect netting, diluted wood vinegar, frequent monitoring |
| Flea beetle | Small black insects riddle the leaves with pinhole holes | April-June | Insect netting during the seedling stage, scattering ash, covering the ridge with straw |
| Aphids | Cluster on the undersides of leaves and new shoots, sucking sap. Leaves curl | April-June, September-October | Soapy water, rinsing off with a jet of water, protecting ladybird beetles |
| Cabbage webworm | Bores into the growing point and new shoots | Summer-autumn | Insect netting, removing infested plants |
| Slugs | Leave irregular holes in leaves at night | The monsoon season | Hand-picking at night, laying a band of ash or rice husks around the plant |
| Thrips | Leave silvery-white scarring on leaves | Hot, dry weather | Maintaining humidity with irrigation, removing weeds |

### 9-2. Why Hand-Picking Works

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Cabbage white butterfly caterpillars are large and slow. They cling along the leaf veins and are hard to spot because they match the leaf color, but if you follow the holes in a leaf, they are usually nearby. Sweeping the undersides of leaves in the early morning or at dusk can remove a substantial number of them in just a few minutes a day. In a garden plot of ten to twenty kale plants, this method alone can serve as control. The 51.7% yield loss without pest control is the figure for doing nothing at all; in a field where someone hand-picks daily, that number drops sharply.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Even more efficient is stopping them at the egg stage. Cabbage white butterflies attach one elongated yellow egg at a time to the underside of a leaf. If you spot a white butterfly flying over the field and check the undersides of leaves the next day, you can find these eggs, and rubbing one out with a finger erases a caterpillar before it exists. Wiping out one egg is far easier than catching one caterpillar, and no damage has occurred yet at all.

### 9-3. Netting and Physical Barriers

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The surest way to stop butterflies and moths is to keep them from laying eggs at all. Stretching netting with a mesh of around 1mm over hoops to cover the whole plot, and sealing the edges down with soil, blocks egg-laying by both cabbage white butterflies and diamondback moths. In documented organic-growing field trials, insect-netting cover is rated as the single most reliably effective measure.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Without netting on hand, you need to think of a substitute. A fine-mesh mosquito net, an old curtain, thin cloth, and worn-out screen material can all be used. Because poor ventilation increases fungal disease, avoid sealing it completely — instead, open one side during the day to regulate airflow. If eggs or caterpillars are already present before you cover the plants, you end up raising them inside the enclosure, so always check the leaves before covering.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Flea beetles need a different response. This insect emerges from the soil and travels by jumping, and it especially favors young plants. Laying straw or dry grass on the surface of the field blocks the beetles' path up out of the soil, and dusting a thin layer of dry ash on the leaves makes the surface rough enough that the beetles are reluctant to feed. Because rain washes the ash away, reapply it after it rains.

### 9-4. Natural Control Agents

| Agent | How to make it | Target | Caution |
|---|---|---|---|
| Soapy water | Dissolve 20g of unscented soap in 1L of water | Aphids, mites, thrips | Spraying at midday burns the leaves. Spray in the evening and rinse with water the next morning |
| Garlic-and-chili infusion | Grind 100g of garlic and dried chili into 1L of water, let sit a day, then strain | Repellent. Aphids, adult moths | For prevention only; it will not kill pests already established |
| Wood vinegar | Dilute 500-1,000× | General repellent | Leaves are damaged if it is too concentrated |
| Wood ash | Sift and dust thinly on leaves and soil | Flea beetles, slugs | Alkaline, so overuse skews soil pH |
| Diluted milk | Mix milk and water 1 to 5 | Aphids, powdery mildew | Only applicable when milk is available |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The reason soapy water works on aphids is not toxicity but a physical action. The soap dissolves the wax coating over the aphid's breathing pores and body, and it suffocates. So it only works if the liquid touches the insect's body directly, and once it dries after spraying, its effect is spent. The trick is to aim at the aphids clustered on the undersides of leaves and spray from below upward. Because kale's waxy leaf coating makes soapy water prone to running off, raise the soap concentration slightly or apply it in two or three separate rounds.

### 9-5. Major Diseases

| Disease | Symptoms | Conditions | Response |
|---|---|---|---|
| Clubroot | Galls form on the roots, and the plant wilts during the day | Acidic soil, waterlogging | Cannot be cured. Keep pH at 6.5 or above, ensure drainage, rotate 7 years or more |
| Downy mildew | Yellow spots on leaves, grayish-white mold on the underside | Cool, humid conditions | Ventilation, wider plant spacing, removing diseased leaves |
| Sclerotinia rot | The base softens, with white mycelium and black sclerotia | Low temperature, high humidity | Drainage, removing infected plants, crop rotation |
| Black rot | Leaf margins turn yellow in a V shape | High temperature, high humidity | Hot-water seed treatment, removing diseased plants |
| Soft rot | Tissue turns mushy and gives off a foul smell | High temperature, high humidity, wounds | Avoid wounding the plant, ensure drainage |
| Powdery mildew | Leaves are covered with white powder | Dry, cool conditions | Ventilation, diluted milk |

> **Clubroot cannot be cured with chemicals and survives in the soil for more than seven years. For any field where cabbage-family crops will be planted, the only option is to prevent it in advance with three things: soil pH, drainage, and crop rotation.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Checking whether a field already has clubroot is simple. Pull up a plant that wilts while growing and look at its roots. If it has club-shaped or gall-like swollen lumps attached, it is clubroot. Do not plant cabbage-family crops in that field for at least 5 years, preferably 7. That includes kale, cabbage, napa cabbage, radish, leaf mustard, rapeseed, and broccoli. Do not leave a diseased plant where it stood or put it in the compost — burn it or bury it deep, far from the field. The route of spread is the resting spores inside the galls scattering into the soil.

### 9-6. Cultural Control Principles

| Principle | Details |
|---|---|
| Adjusting sowing time | Plant to avoid pest population peaks. Cabbage white butterflies peak in May-June and September |
| Crop rotation | Do not plant cabbage-family crops in succession. At least 2 years; 7 years if clubroot is present |
| Maintaining plant spacing | Keep to 30-40cm. Too narrow and airflow is blocked, increasing fungal disease |
| Removing plant debris | Do not leave spent plants or diseased leaves in the field |
| Companion planting | Flowers of the carrot family — dill, coriander, and carrot flowers among them — attract parasitoid wasps and hoverflies, which cut down aphid numbers |
| Protecting natural predators | Do not kill ladybird beetles, lacewings, or spiders. This is why broad-spectrum spraying should be avoided |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Protecting natural predators matters especially in a long-term disaster. A single ladybird beetle eats dozens of aphids a day. Parasitoid wasps lay eggs inside cabbage white butterfly caterpillars and kill them from within. In a field where these relationships have taken hold, pest density suppresses itself. Spray broadly, on the other hand, and the slower-breeding predators disappear first while the fast-breeding pests come back alone, becoming a bigger problem the following year. Having no chemicals is a constraint, but it is also, at the same time, the condition that lets this balance recover.

## 10. Nutrient Deficiency and Excess

| Symptom | Cause | Natural remedy |
|---|---|---|
| Whole lower leaves turn yellow first, plant stays small | Nitrogen deficiency | Urine diluted 8-10×, well-rotted compost, grass liquid fertilizer |
| Leaf margins scorch brown | Potassium deficiency | Scatter wood ash around the base of the plant |
| Leaves are dark blue-green and stop growing | Phosphorus deficiency | Bone meal, wood ash |
| New leaves are malformed and the growing point dies | Boron deficiency | Raise organic matter with well-rotted compost. Common in fields given too much ash |
| New leaves yellow between the veins while the veins stay green | Iron or magnesium deficiency | Check whether pH is too high. Cut back on ash |
| Leaves are pale, large, and soft, drawing pests | Nitrogen excess | Stop nitrogen. The plant also becomes more vulnerable to disease |
| White blotches appear on leaves and growth stops | Excess salts | Increase watering to flush them out. Lower the concentration of urine used |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The first fork in diagnosis is whether the deficiency symptom starts in old leaves or in new ones. Nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, and magnesium are elements that can move around within the plant, so when they run short, the plant pulls them out of old leaves and sends them to new ones. That is why symptoms appear in the lower leaves first. Calcium, boron, and iron, by contrast, cannot move once they settle in place, so symptoms show up in new leaves first. If the lower leaves are yellow, suspect nitrogen; if the new leaves are malformed, suspect boron or calcium first.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Boron and iron deficiency often show up in fields that have had too much ash. Ash is alkaline and raises soil pH, and once pH goes above 7.5, boron, iron, and manganese lock into forms in the soil that will not dissolve in water, so roots cannot absorb them. It is not that the soil lacks these elements — they are there, but unavailable. In this case, the fix is not to add more ash but to stop it altogether, raising organic matter with well-rotted compost to bring the soil back gradually.

## 11. Intercropping and Crop Rotation

| Compatibility | Partner | Effect |
|:---:|---|---|
| Good | Legumes (peas, kidney beans) | Fix nitrogen, leaving nitrogen behind for kale to use |
| Good | Welsh onion, onion, garlic | Their smell confuses butterflies and aphids |
| Good | Dill, coriander, carrot flowers | Attract parasitoid wasps and hoverflies, reducing aphids |
| Good | Potato, beet | Different rooting depths mean less competition for nutrients |
| Neutral | Lettuce, spinach | Cover the ground and suppress weeds |
| Bad | Cabbage-family crops such as napa cabbage, radish, leaf mustard, broccoli | Share pests and diseases, which accumulate |
| Bad | Strawberry | The two hinder each other's growth |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The principle behind a rotation sequence is not to plant the same family in succession, and to follow a heavy-feeding crop with one that builds nutrients back up. Kale is a heavy feeder that draws a lot of nitrogen out of the field, so a legume is placed right after it.

```
Kale (cabbage family, heavy feeder) → Peas/kidney beans (legumes, nitrogen recovery) → Potato/sweet potato (nightshade/morning-glory family) →
Welsh onion/onion (allium family) → back to kale
```

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Following this sequence, it takes 4 years for kale to return to the same spot. If a field has confirmed clubroot, this cycle must be extended to 7 years or more, and cabbage-family crops must never be added in between. The key point is to keep radish and leaf mustard out of the same cycle too, precisely because they are cabbage-family crops.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Pairing kale with legumes in intercropping is especially valuable. If peas or kidney beans are planted in the furrow between kale beds, some of the nitrogen produced by the root nodules of the beans returns to the soil through root exudates and fallen leaves. The effect is even greater if the stems and leaves are tilled back into the same spot after the beans are harvested. Without chemical fertilizer, this combination is one of the few ways to actually generate nitrogen within the field itself.
