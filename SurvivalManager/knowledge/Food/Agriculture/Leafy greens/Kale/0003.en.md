## 2. What Kind of Plant Is This?

### 2-1. Taxonomy and Origin

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Kale is a biennial herb in the genus Brassica, family Brassicaceae (the cabbage family). Its scientific name is Brassica oleracea var. acephala, and acephala means "headless." Within the same species, Brassica oleracea, humans have bred varieties by enlarging different organs: cabbage (leaves layered into a head), broccoli (flower buds clustered together), cauliflower (a swollen flower stalk), kohlrabi (a swollen stem), and Brussels sprouts (clustered side buds). Kale is the branch that never particularly enlarged any of these organs — the one that stays closest to the original form.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This lineage actually implies two things. One is that kale, cabbage, and broccoli exchange pollen with each other and their seed mixes. Since they are the same species, there is no barrier to crossing at all — a constraint that must be considered when saving seed, and the subject of section 13. The other is that they share pests and diseases. Cabbage white butterflies, diamondback moths, flea beetles, and clubroot attack the cabbage family as a whole. Napa cabbage, radish, leaf mustard, and rapeseed belong to the same family too, so they share the same diseases. Growing cabbage-family crops in the same spot year after year lets pathogens and pests build up.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Its native range is the Mediterranean coast and the Atlantic coast of Western Europe. Wild cabbage still grows today on limestone sea cliffs in Britain and France. This origin determines kale's growing requirements.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;First, being a plant of cool maritime climates, it is vulnerable to summer heat. Above 25°C the leaves toughen and turn bitter.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Second, being a plant of limestone country, it favors calcium and dislikes acidic soil. pH 6.0 to 7.0 is optimal, and strongly acidic soil triggers a clubroot outbreak.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Third, being a plant of land where winters are mild but frost still falls, it has strong cold hardiness, and it must go through winter cold before it sends up a flower stalk. This is called vernalization, and it is the cause of kale's biennial nature.

### 2-2. Appearance

| Organ | Characteristics |
|---|---|
| Leaf | Large and thick, with a waxy layer on the surface. Depending on the variety, it may be flat or deeply crinkled. Green is the base color, but purplish-red types also exist |
| Stem | Grows a single thick, sturdy central stem upright. Nodes remain where leaves were picked, so the lower part looks like a palm-tree trunk. Height 60-150cm |
| Root | A straight taproot reaches down 30-60cm, with fine roots spreading sideways. It goes deeper than napa cabbage or lettuce, which is an advantage in drought |
| Flower | Four yellow petals arranged in a cross. Blooms in a raceme in the spring of the second year |
| Pod | A long, slender silique. When ripe it dries brown and splits lengthwise |
| Seed | Round grains 1.5-2mm across, ranging from reddish-brown to black |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The waxy layer on the leaf is worth remembering. This white, slippery coating on the surface of a kale leaf is a defensive structure that blocks moisture loss and makes it hard for pathogens to invade. It is the reason kale tolerates drought relatively well, and also the reason water or sprays simply run right off the leaf. When soapy water is mixed to control aphids, the point of adding a little soap is not cleaning — it is to make the liquid stick to this waxy layer instead of running off.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The structure of leaves spiraling up a single central stem determines how kale is harvested. There is always exactly one growing point, at the top of the stem, and new leaves keep emerging from there. The lower a leaf sits, the older it is. So if you pick from the bottom upward, the plant keeps growing taller and keeps producing leaves. Cut off the growing point at the top, on the other hand, and that plant is finished right there. Every harvesting technique in section 12 comes out of this structure.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The deep rooting also has practical significance. A shallow-rooted leafy vegetable like lettuce wilts as soon as the topsoil dries out, but kale can draw water from below 30cm, so it holds up even if watering is delayed by several days. This structure is part of why its Water use grade is A.

### 2-3. Growth Stages

| Stage | Timing | Key physiology | Key nutrients |
|---|---|---|---|
| Germination & seedling | From sowing to 2-3 true leaves (2-3 weeks) | Cotyledons unfold and the taproot goes down | Phosphorus, a small amount of nitrogen |
| Vegetative growth | 4 to 8 true leaves (3-8 weeks) | Leaf number increases rapidly and the stem thickens | Nitrogen |
| Harvest | After 8 true leaves (from 8-10 weeks) | Lower leaves finish maturing while new ones keep forming at the top | Balance of nitrogen and potassium |
| Overwintering | November to February of the following year | Growth nearly stops and sugar accumulates in the cells | Watering and fertilizing stop |
| Bolting & flowering | March-May of the following year | The stem grows rapidly and yellow flowers bloom | Phosphorus, potassium, boron |

> **What triggers kale to send up a flower stalk is not day length but winter cold. Once a young plant goes through several weeks of temperatures at or below 10°C, that signal is recorded in its tissue, and when temperatures rise the following spring, the flower stalk comes up.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This phenomenon, called vernalization, is an essential step when saving seed but an accident to avoid when harvesting leaves. If you sow too early in spring and the young plants go through a long spell of late cold, the flower stalk comes up that same spring. This is called premature bolting, and once the flower stalk stands, the leaves toughen, turn bitter, and the plant stops making new leaves. This is why spring sowing in central South Korea is set for late March or later.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The physiology of the overwintering stage is also worth understanding. Once the temperature drops below 5°C, kale barely grows at all. But it does not die either. A plant normally dies when the water in its leaf cells freezes and tears the cell membranes, but kale lowers its freezing point by dissolving solutes such as sugars and amino acids into its cell fluid. Down to minus 10 degrees, the water inside its cells does not freeze, which is why the leaves stay alive. This sugar is exactly why frosted kale tastes sweet. However, no new leaves form in winter, so in effect you spend the whole winter picking, one by one, the leaves you grew large enough back in autumn.

### 2-4. Nutrients per 100g

| Nutrient | Amount | Notes |
|---|:---:|---|
| Calories | 28kcal | Per the Korean Food Composition Table, raw. Very low |
| Protein | 3.35g | 47.9% of calories. The absolute amount is small, but the density ranks at the very top |
| Carbohydrates | 5-9g | A large share is dietary fiber |
| Fat | 0.6-1.0g | Negligible |
| Dietary fiber | 3-4g | On the high side |
| Vitamin C | around 90mg | 100g exceeds the daily recommended amount |
| Vitamin A (beta-carotene) | Very high | Among the highest of any vegetable |
| Vitamin K | Very high | 100g provides several times the recommended amount |
| Calcium | 200-330mg | Among the highest levels of any vegetable |
| Iron | 1.5-2mg | Absorbed relatively well because it is paired with vitamin C |
| Potassium | 400-500mg | Involved in balancing sodium |
| Folate | Abundant | Involved in blood formation |
| Lutein & zeaxanthin | Abundant | Pigments that accumulate in the macula of the eye |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This is the table behind the SS in micronutrients on the evaluation sheet. Eighteen of the 21 nutrients exceed 10% of the daily recommended amount. Four deserve particular attention: vitamin C, vitamin A, calcium, and vitamin K. These four are the biggest gaps in a diet built on grains, tuber crops (tubers/storage roots), and legumes. Rice, potatoes, and soybeans have almost no vitamin C, or lose most of what they have during cooking, and beta-carotene, the precursor of vitamin A, is essentially absent from grains. As for calcium, plant-based sources drop sharply once milk and anchovies are gone.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;One qualification needs to be added regarding calcium. Kale's calcium content is among the highest of any vegetable, and its absorption rate is also relatively good. The calcium in spinach or Swiss chard binds to oxalic acid and is poorly absorbed, but kale is different because its oxalic acid content is low. Some reports put spinach's calcium absorption rate at around 5%, against more than 50% for kale. This is why kale carries special value as a calcium source once dairy products are gone.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Iron follows the same pattern. Plant-based iron (non-heme iron) has a naturally low absorption rate, but that absorption rises sharply in the presence of vitamin C. Because kale carries both iron and vitamin C in the same leaf, it helps delay anemia in a diet without meat.

## 3. Growing Conditions

### 3-1. Temperature

| Category | Limit | Optimum | Notes |
|---|:---:|:---:|---|
| Germination temperature | 5-30°C | 20-25°C | Below 15°C, germination slows |
| Growing temperature | 5-30°C | 15-20°C | The SS in temperature range on the evaluation table comes from this 25°C span |
| Heat limit | 30°C and above | - | Leaves toughen and bitterness intensifies |
| Cold limit | minus 10°C | - | Leaves stay alive but growth stops |
| Vernalization trigger | Several weeks at 10°C or below | - | The trigger for the flower stalk to rise |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;That kale's temperature range spans 25°C means it grows at almost any time of year except for the height of summer. Planted in spring, it carries through to autumn; planted in autumn, it carries through to the following spring. Quality diverges at the two extremes, though. In summer above 30°C, the leaves grow thick and stiff, glucosinolates increase, and bitterness intensifies. Frost, by contrast, makes it sweeter. So even though it is the same kale, an August leaf and a December leaf are entirely different foods.

### 3-2. Light

| Category | Standard | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Required sunlight | 6 hours or more a day | Leaves grow thick and darker in color |
| Minimum sunlight | 3-4 hours a day | Still grows, but the leaves are thin and the plant grows lanky |
| At 40% shade | Relative yield 86% | A value common to leafy vegetables, and the basis for the B grade on the evaluation table |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Shade tolerance is highly valuable in a disaster. Holding on to 86% of yield even at 40% shade is a major advantage compared with soybeans (50%) or purslane (45%). This is because a leaf crop has no conversion step to begin with, turning sunlight into fruit or roots. It makes sense to place kale in odd corners that never get sun all day — the base of a wall, the south edge of a large tree, a narrow strip of land between buildings — and give the sunny spots to grain or beans instead.

### 3-3. Water and Soil

| Item | Standard | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Total evapotranspiration | About 400mm per season | On the low side for leafy vegetables, and the basis for the A grade on the evaluation table |
| Soil moisture | 70-80% of field capacity | Neither dry nor waterlogged |
| Soil texture | Loam or sandy loam rich in organic matter | Needs both drainage and water retention together |
| Soil depth | 30cm or more | The taproot goes down deep |
| pH | 6.0-7.0 | Around 6.5 is best |
| pH lower limit | 5.5 | Below this, clubroot increases sharply |
| Salt tolerance | Moderate | Being of coastal origin, it is relatively strong among leafy vegetables |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;It is important to remember that managing pH in kale cultivation is directly tied to disease management. Clubroot forms galls on the roots of cabbage-family crops that block the passage of water and nutrients, and once it takes hold, it persists in the soil for more than seven years. It is not a disease that can be cured with chemicals, and it spreads explosively in acidic soil. Raising pH to 6.5 or above greatly suppresses the pathogen's activity. When lime is not available, wood ash, powder from burned and ground shell and eggshell, and oyster-shell powder serve as substitutes. Section 5 covers this method.
