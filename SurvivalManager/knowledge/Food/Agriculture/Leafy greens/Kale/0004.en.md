## 4. Varieties

### 4-1. Characteristics by Type

| Type | Leaf shape | Cold hardiness | Yield | Suitability for disaster use |
|---|---|:---:|:---:|---|
| Curly kale | Deeply crinkled leaves | Very strong | Medium | The most widely grown type, and well suited to winter harvest |
| Flat-leaf kale (collard type) | Flat, broad leaves | Strong | High | Because the leaves are large, more is harvested at once |
| Large juicing type | Broad, thick leaves | Strong | Highest | Widely grown in South Korea, and favorable for repeated harvest |
| Wrap-salad type | Thin, tender leaves | Medium | Medium | Good for eating raw, but less persistent through winter |
| Red type | Purple crinkled leaves | Very strong | Low | High in pigment and especially cold-hardy |
| Tuscan type (dinosaur kale) | Long, bumpy, dark leaves | Strong | Medium | Relatively tolerant of heat |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The most important criterion in choosing a variety is what to prioritize. If the goal is securing winter greens, choose curly kale or a red type. It might seem that the deeper the crinkling, the larger the surface area and the more vulnerable to cold damage, but the opposite is actually true: air trapped between the folds provides insulation, and the cell structure itself is denser. If yield is the goal, on the other hand, the large juicing type has the advantage. The total leaf weight from a single plant can reach 1.5 times that of a curly type.

### 4-2. Selection Criteria by Situation

| Situation | Choice | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Securing winter vitamins is the top priority | Curly kale, red type | Leaves stay alive even at minus 10°C or below |
| Total yield is the top priority | Large juicing type | Leaves are large and regrow quickly |
| Planning to keep saving your own seed | Any type, as long as it is open-pollinated | Hybrid (F1) seed loses its traits the following year |
| When you must harvest through summer too | Tuscan type | Less bitterness in hot weather |
| When the plot is narrow | Curly kale | Spreads sideways less |

> **If disaster preparedness is the premise, you should secure open-pollinated seed rather than hybrid seed. A hybrid gives a good yield in its first year, but if you save and replant its seed, the traits scatter and it stops performing properly from the following year on.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;If a commercial seed packet says F1, it is a hybrid. Seed with no such marking, or labeled open-pollinated, heirloom, or OP, is seed you can carry on for generations. If you are stockpiling seed on the premise of disaster, this distinction takes priority over how outstanding the variety is. Kale's seed longevity is 4-5 years sealed at room temperature, so once you secure it, it lasts you several years.

## 5. Field Preparation and Fertilizer

### 5-1. Preparing the Field

| Item | Standard | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Tilling depth | 20-30cm | Opens a path for the taproot to go down |
| Bed height | 15-20cm | 25cm or more on poorly drained land |
| Bed width | 90-120cm | Based on planting two rows |
| Drainage channel | Essential | Waterlogging triggers root rot and clubroot |
| pH correction | Target pH 6.5 | Apply 2-3 weeks before transplanting |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The evaluation table grades tillage and digging energy as B (based on 10cm deep tillage), but in practice it is better to till 20cm or deeper. Kale's taproot goes down more than 30cm, and a deeply rooted plant builds up resistance to drought and wind. Turning the soil with a single spade is within reach of human labor, and an energy return of 12.9× means that labor comes back well rewarded.

### 5-2. Fertilizer Amounts

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Kale is a heavy feeder, and among its needs, nitrogen demand is especially large — unsurprising for a crop grown for its leaves. The Fertilizer demand grade of B on the evaluation table comes from the figure that N, P, and K combined require about 24g per 1m².

| Nutrient | Per 1,000m² | Per m² | Notes |
|---|:---:|:---:|---|
| Nitrogen (N) | 10-12kg | 10-12g | Split between base fertilizer and top-dressing |
| Phosphorus (P) | 6-8kg | 6-8g | All as base fertilizer |
| Potassium (K) | 8-10kg | 8-10g | Split between base fertilizer and top-dressing |
| Compost | 1,500-2,000kg | 1.5-2kg | Well-rotted compost |
| Lime | 100-150kg | 100-150g | For correcting acidic soil |

| Stage | Timing | Details |
|---|---|---|
| Base fertilizer | 1-2 weeks before transplanting | All the compost, all the phosphorus, half the nitrogen and potassium |
| 1st top-dressing | 3-4 weeks after transplanting | Mainly nitrogen |
| 2nd top-dressing | After the first harvest | Nitrogen and potassium |
| Later top-dressing | Every 3-4 weeks | A little each time leaves are picked |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Adding a little fertilizer at every harvest is the key to managing kale. Picking a leaf is, in effect, removing nitrogen from the field. Without replacing it, the new leaves that come in grow progressively smaller and paler. The advantage of a crop you harvest from a single plant for a long time is preserved only when there is a steady feedback cycle of replenishment.

### 5-3. Disaster Substitute Fertilizers

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;When chemical fertilizer plants stop and distribution breaks down, nitrogen is the first thing to disappear. Phosphorus and potassium can be replaced to some extent with ash and bone, but nitrogen cannot. Because kale is a crop with a low yield retention without synthetic inputs of 40.8%, securing a nitrogen source is, in practice, what decides success or failure in growing it.

| Substitute resource | Nutrients supplied | How to use | Caution |
|---|---|---|---|
| Human urine | Nitrogen (mainly), potassium, phosphorus | Dilute 8-10× with water and pour onto the soil between plants | Burns leaves on direct contact. Stop 2 weeks before harvest |
| Well-rotted compost | Nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, organic matter | Generously as base fertilizer. As top-dressing, place around the base of the plant | Under-rotted compost burns roots and invites disease |
| Wood ash | Potassium, calcium, phosphorus, magnesium | Scatter one or two handfuls per m² | Alkaline, so it raises pH. Helpful for suppressing clubroot, but too much blocks micronutrient uptake |
| Bone meal | Phosphorus, calcium | Burn, grind, and mix into the base fertilizer | Effects appear slowly |
| Leguminous green manure | Nitrogen | Grow peas or Chinese milkvetch as a preceding crop and till it under | Till it under and let it break down 3-4 weeks before planting |
| Oil cake & rice bran | Nitrogen, phosphorus | Mix into base fertilizer, or soak in water and use the fermented liquid | Applied directly, fermentation heat damages the roots |
| Shell & eggshell | Calcium | Burn and grind, use for pH correction | A substitute for lime |
| Weed/grass liquid fertilizer | Nitrogen, potassium | Soak cut grass in water, let it ferment 2-3 weeks, then dilute 5× | Smells strongly. Keep it away from where people live |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;It is worth stating plainly that diluted urine is a genuinely practical nitrogen source in a disaster. A healthy person's urine is nearly sterile right after it is passed, and it carries roughly 7-10g of nitrogen per liter in the form of urea. Diluted 8-10× with water, it becomes a mild liquid fertilizer. Applied undiluted, the high concentration of salts and urea burns the roots. Pour it into the furrow between plants, away from the leaves, then follow with a bit more water to rinse it down into the soil. Because the leaves of this crop are eaten raw, it is safer, for hygiene, to stop applying it from 2 weeks before harvest.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Wood ash does two things at once: it supplies potassium and raises soil pH. The latter matters especially for cabbage-family crops. But if the alkalinity goes too far, it blocks the uptake of boron, iron, and manganese, and other deficiency symptoms appear on the leaves. One or two handfuls per m², no more than two or three times a year, is about right. Scattered on wet ground after rain, the potassium dissolves right in, which makes it especially effective.

## 6. Sowing and Transplanting

### 6-1. Cropping Systems

| System | Sowing | Transplanting | Harvest | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Spring growing | Late March-April | Late April-May | June-October | Quality declines in summer |
| Autumn growing | July-August | August-September | September-following spring | Gives the best quality |
| Overwintering growing | August-September | September-October | November-following April | Possible in central South Korea and further south |

> **In a disaster situation, center your planting on autumn growing. Kale planted in autumn goes through winter and keeps providing leaves until the following spring, and that period is exactly when every other vegetable disappears.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The reason spring sowing is pushed back to late March or later is premature bolting. If a young plant goes through more than 3 weeks of temperatures at or below 10°C, vernalization occurs and the flower stalk comes up that same spring. A plant meant to be harvested for its leaves ends up just sending up a flower stalk and finishing there. Autumn sowing, by contrast, has no such problem. By the time winter sets in, the plant has already grown large enough that even after going through the cold, it keeps producing leaves until the following spring.

### 6-2. Sowing and Raising Seedlings

| Item | Standard |
|---|---|
| Sowing depth | 0.5-1cm |
| Days to germination | 4-7 days at 20-25°C |
| Seedling period | 30-40 days (4-5 true leaves) |
| Row spacing for direct sowing | 40-50cm |
| Plant spacing at transplanting | 30-40cm |
| 1st thinning after direct sowing | 2-3 true leaves, plant spacing 10cm |
| 2nd thinning after direct sowing | 4-5 true leaves, final spacing 30-40cm |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Either direct sowing or raising seedlings works. Raising seedlings shortens the time the field is occupied, which makes it easier to rotate the plot with other crops, and it is also easier to protect young plants from flea beetles that way. Direct sowing leaves the root uncut, so the taproot goes down deeper and the plant becomes more drought-resistant. When water is scarce, direct sowing has the advantage.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Do not throw away the young plants you thin out. A kale seedling with three or four true leaves is already an edible vegetable, tender because it is young, and dense in nutrients. The thinnings are often the very first fresh vegetable to reach the table after you start the plot.

### 6-3. Seed Treatment

| Method | Procedure | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Hot-water soak | Soak in 50°C water for 20 minutes, then cool immediately in cold water | Kills pathogens attached to the seed |
| Ash coating | Coat wet seeds with sifted wood ash | Combines surface disinfection with alkaline treatment |
| Water soak | 4-6 hours in room-temperature water | Speeds up germination by a day or two |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;For the hot-water soak, temperature control is everything. Above 55°C the seed dies; below 45°C it is not disinfected. Without a thermometer, mixing roughly equal parts boiling and cold water to a point where it feels hot but bearable to the hand is approximately 50°C. Still, doing this by feel risks losing the entire batch of seed, so it is safer to treat only part of what you have and plant it in separate batches.

## 7. Water Management

### 7-1. Water by Growth Stage

| Stage | Management | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Sowing to germination | Daily, keeping the surface soil from drying | If it dries out at this stage, germination rate drops sharply |
| Seedling stage | Once every 2-3 days | Better to let it dry slightly so roots are encouraged to reach downward |
| Vegetative growth | 1-2 times a week, deeply each time | Shallow, frequent watering keeps roots shallow |
| Harvest | Once after picking leaves | New leaves need water to form |
| Overwintering | Almost none | Roots are damaged if wet soil freezes |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;When water is scarce, how you water matters more than how much. Watering daily but only wetting the topsoil concentrates roots near the surface, and the plant collapses at the slightest dry spell. Instead, watering deeply enough every few days to soak the soil down to 20cm lets the roots follow the water down. This difference is especially large in a crop like kale, whose taproot reaches deep.

### 7-2. Symptoms of Water Shortage and Excess

| Symptom | Cause | Response |
|---|---|---|
| Wilts at midday, recovers by evening | Normal range | No response needed |
| Still wilted in the morning | Water shortage | Water thoroughly right away |
| Leaves small, thick, and tough | Chronic water shortage | Shorten the watering interval and mulch |
| Leaf margins dry and turn brown | Water shortage or excess salts | Water, then check drainage |
| Lower leaves yellow first and turn soft | Waterlogging / root rot | Dig a drainage channel and stop watering |
| The whole plant wilts suddenly | Clubroot or root rot | Dig up the root and check for galls |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;When water is short, kale's response develops slowly. The leaves grow smaller and thicker, and the waxy layer thickens. It is an adaptation for survival, and the plant does not die, but the quantity and quality of the harvest clearly decline. Waterlogging, by contrast, is far faster and more deadly. Once the roots are cut off from oxygen and start to rot, the whole plant collapses within days. In a disaster, without irrigation infrastructure, a shortage of water is the easier failure to manage, so it is better to build the beds high and structurally prevent waterlogging in the first place.

### 7-3. Mulching

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Without plastic sheeting, mulching materials are straw, dry grass, fallen leaves, rice husks, and sawdust. Covering the ground around the plant to a depth of 5-10cm produces three effects at once: evaporation from the topsoil drops, cutting watering frequency by nearly half; weeds are suppressed; and in summer, soil temperature drops, easing heat stress. In winter, this covering becomes a blanket that keeps the area near the roots from freezing. Slugs and grubs like to hide underneath the mulch, though, so check under it now and then.
