## 9. Pests and Diseases — Protection Without Chemicals

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Leaf mustard's pest & disease pressure grade is C, with an estimated yield loss without pest control of 51.7%. That means losing nearly half the crop with no control measures at all, a figure that applies across Brassicaceae crops generally. Leaf mustard does, however, have one defense mechanism that other Brassicaceae crops lack.

### 9-1. Why Pungency Is a Defense Compound

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Leaf mustard's leaves contain a mustard-oil glycoside called sinigrin. The compound itself is neither pungent nor has any taste at all. The instant a leaf is wounded and its cells rupture, an enzyme called myrosinase, kept stored separately in another compartment, meets sinigrin and converts it into allyl isothiocyanate. This is the true source of the pungency and the sharp sting in the nose.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;From the plant's perspective, this is a remarkably sophisticated defense system. It stores the compound in a safe form that costs nothing under normal conditions, and it converts to a toxic substance only at the moment of being eaten. An insect chewing on the leaf suddenly experiences an irritant bursting in its mouth and backs off. At a cut or wound, the same compound acts as a disinfectant, blocking the entry of fungi and bacteria.

| Situation | What leaf mustard's pungency does |
|:---|:---|
| Ordinary plant-eating insects | A good many avoid it. In the same field, it suffers damage later than napa cabbage |
| Adapted pests | Diamondback moth and others actually use this smell as a signal to find it |
| Fungi, bacteria | Suppressed. Also why pickled products have such good storability |
| Soil pathogens | Tilling leaf mustard into the soil produces breakdown products with a soil-disinfecting effect |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The last row has real practical value in a disaster. Chopping up the stems and roots left after harvesting leaf mustard, or plants that bolted and became inedible, and tilling them into the field lets the breaking-down mustard-oil glycosides suppress soil pathogens and nematodes. Commercial agriculture calls this method biofumigation, and it is one of the few ways to manage soil without chemicals. Watering after tilling it in and covering the ground for a few days increases the effect.

### 9-2. Major Pests

| Pest | Symptoms | Chemical-free control |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Aphids | Cluster on leaf undersides sucking sap; leaves curl. Transmit viruses | Soapy-water spray, wood-ash dusting, protecting ladybugs and parasitic wasps, reflective silver material |
| Turnip sawfly (larva) | Chews holes in leaves | Hand-picking, insect netting, adjusted sowing dates |
| Diamondback moth | Scrapes the underside of leaves thin, leaving translucent patches | Netting is most reliable. Combine with hand-picking |
| Cabbage white butterfly caterpillar | Leaves large holes in leaves | Cover with netting to keep butterflies from landing, and rub off the yellow eggs on leaf undersides |
| Flea beetle | Riddles leaves with tiny, needle-prick-like holes | Damage is greatest at a young age. Netting early on, wood ash |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Flea beetles are a pest seen especially often on leaf mustard. They are drawn by the smell of mustard-oil glycosides in Brassicaceae plants, a case where leaf mustard's own defense compound turns into an attractant instead. A fully grown plant can tolerate some holes, but a plant hit at the cotyledon or young true-leaf stage dies outright. Covering it with insect netting from right after germination through three or four true leaves is most effective, and once that period is past, damage drops sharply.

### 9-3. Why Physical Barriers Are Most Reliable

| Method | Details | Limitation |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Insect netting | Cover from right after sowing to block moths and butterflies from laying eggs | Impossible without netting |
| Non-woven fabric, mosquito netting | Old mosquito netting or cloth also works | Poor airflow invites disease |
| Adjusted sowing dates | Sow to avoid the peak period of pest occurrence | Limits the choice of season |
| Hand-picking | Check leaf undersides in early morning and remove eggs and larvae | Labor-intensive |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Why insect netting is overwhelmingly effective without chemicals becomes clear once you look at the pests' life cycle. For both the cabbage white butterfly and the diamondback moth, adults fly in and lay eggs, and it is the larvae that hatch from those eggs that cause the damage. So if adults are kept from reaching the leaves, the larval stage never occurs at all. Preventing egg-laying takes far less effort than catching larvae that have already appeared.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Without netting, adjusting the sowing date is the alternative. Since butterfly and moth activity is governed by temperature, sowing later reduces damage: mid-September sowing has an advantage over late August in terms of pest damage. But sowing later also shortens the growing period, cutting yield and raising the risk of encountering frost. This trade-off is the real substance of deciding when to sow in autumn.

### 9-4. Natural Control Agents

| Agent | Preparation | Target |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Plant-ash solution | Pour 75kg of water over 15kg of plant ash, steep for 24 hours, then strain | Aphids, stink bugs |
| Soapy water | Dissolve 20g of unscented soap in 1L of water | Aphids, mites |
| Wood-ash dusting | Sift fine ash and dust it on leaves while morning dew is still on them | Pest deterrence, potassium supply |
| Wood vinegar | Dilute 300 to 500 times and spray | Deterrent effect, disease suppression |
| Leaf mustard's own extract | Crush leaf mustard leaves, soak in water, strain, and spray on other crops | Deterrent effect |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The way soapy water works on aphids is physical, not toxic. The surfactant in soap dissolves the waxy layer on an aphid's body, letting moisture escape, and clogs its breathing pores, the spiracles. Because of this, only insects directly touched by the spray die, and there is no residual effect. It has to soak the leaf undersides thoroughly and be repeated two or three times a few days apart. Raising the concentration also damages leaf mustard's own leaves, so keep to the line of 20g per liter.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The effect of plant-ash solution comes from its alkalinity and potassium. Soaking ash in water dissolves out potassium carbonate to make an alkaline solution, and this solution irritates soft-bodied insects. At the same time, the potassium left on the leaves acts as a foliar feed. The time to spray it is early morning or evening; sprayed at midday, the water droplets act as lenses and scorch the leaves.

### 9-5. Major Diseases

| Disease | Symptoms | Conditions | Chemical-free response |
|:---|:---|:---|:---|
| Downy mildew | White-to-gray mold on leaf undersides; upper side turns yellow | Cool, humid conditions | Ensure airflow, avoid overcrowding, remove diseased leaves |
| Soft rot | The base turns soft and foul-smelling | Heat and humidity, wounds | Ensure drainage, avoid wounding plants, remove infected plants immediately |
| Clubroot | Galls form on roots and the plant wilts | Acidic soil, continuous Brassicaceae cropping | Correct pH with wood ash, rotate for 2+ years |
| Viral disease | Mosaic patterning appears on leaves, which curl | Spread by aphids | Control aphids, pull and burn infected plants |
| Gray mold | Gray mold develops on leaves and stems | Cold, humid conditions, poor airflow | Airflow, remove diseased parts |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The one to take most seriously in this table is clubroot. Other diseases only ruin that year's crop, but clubroot ruins the field itself, because the pathogen's resting spores survive in the soil for 7-10 years. Once it takes hold, Brassicaceae crops cannot be planted in that field for a long time.

> **There is no cure for clubroot. Keeping it from getting in is everything; once it has, waiting it out through crop rotation is the only option.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There are three ways to keep it out. First, pH management: the pathogen is active below pH 6.0 and its activity drops off sharply above pH 7.2, so steadily adding wood ash to keep the soil closer to neutral suppresses outbreaks. Second, drainage: the pathogen spreads in spots where water pools. Third, crop rotation: the rule is to rest Brassicaceae crops for 2 years or more, and a field where the disease is already present needs even longer.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The pathogen travels on soil and water. A hoe or a pair of shoes used in an infected field can carry the disease into a clean one, and it flows downhill with water from an upper field to a lower one. This kind of sanitation is easy to neglect in a disaster, but it is a matter of losing an entire field or not.

### 9-6. Principles of Cultural Control

| Principle | Details |
|:---|:---|
| Healthy seed | Do not use seed collected from diseased plants. Break seed-borne transmission with hot-water soaking |
| Crop rotation | The basic rule is to rest Brassicaceae for 2 years or more |
| Airflow | Thinning and adequate spacing are the cheapest way to reduce fungal disease |
| Drainage | Build raised beds and maintain drainage channels |
| Residue disposal | Do not put diseased leaves and plants in compost; burn them or bury them far away |
| Timing adjustment | Sow to avoid the pest's peak occurrence period |
| Multi-variety mixed planting | Mix varieties with different traits to reduce the risk of total loss |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Cultural control means suppressing pests and diseases through the cultivation method itself, without using chemicals. Where chemical control is impossible, this becomes the whole of pest management. The effect is neither immediate nor visible, but accumulated over several years it changes the condition of the field. Neglect it, on the other hand, and pathogens and pests build up year after year until, past some point, the field can no longer support a crop.

## 10. Nutrient Deficiency and Excess

| Symptom | Cause | Response with natural materials |
|:---|:---|:---|
| The whole leaf turns yellow and the plant stays small | Nitrogen deficiency | Work well-rotted compost between plants, or apply urine diluted 10-15 times |
| Leaf edges dry out and turn brown | Potassium deficiency | Scatter a handful of wood ash per m² and mix with the soil |
| Growth lags and leaves take on a purplish tint | Phosphorus deficiency | Add bone meal or wood ash. The same symptom also appears when soil temperature is low |
| The tissue between leaf veins yellows while the veins stay green | Magnesium deficiency | If no magnesium source is available, increase compost |
| The tips of new leaves dry out and curl inward | Calcium deficiency or uneven moisture | Supplement calcium with wood ash and water evenly |
| Leaves grow lush but pungency and aroma are weak | Nitrogen excess | Stop the nitrogen supply. Becomes more vulnerable to disease and pests |
| Leaves are deep green but thick and stiff | Nitrogen excess | Bring the harvest forward and reduce fertilizer for the next cropping |
| No growth, and galls form on the roots | Clubroot | Not a nutrient problem. See section 9 |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There is something to watch for when reading deficiency symptoms in leaf mustard. Because the growing period is short, by the time a symptom appears, there is no longer any time to respond. Even if you see yellowing leaves and add fertilizer, it takes about ten days for it to be absorbed and take effect, and by then harvest is already underway. So nutrient management for leaf mustard cannot work by watching for symptoms and correcting them — it has to work by filling the need in advance, at the base-fertilizer stage.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Nitrogen excess is harder to notice than deficiency, because leaves that are large and deep green look like they are growing well. But leaf mustard that has taken up too much nitrogen has thin cell walls and soft tissue. Aphids find it easier to suck the sap, and fungal hyphae find it easier to penetrate. This is why soft rot and downy mildew are worse in fields with excess nitrogen. What's more, synthesizing the mustard-oil glycoside that gives pungency requires sulfur, and when nitrogen is abundant but sulfur is scarce, the result is leaf mustard with big leaves but a flat, dull taste — less useful for kimchi.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;One more note on sulfur. Brassicaceae crops need more sulfur than other crops, because the mustard-oil glycoside itself is a sulfur-containing compound. In farming that uses chemical fertilizer, sulfur is supplied incidentally through ammonium sulfate or potassium sulfate, but where only organic materials are used, it can run short. Compost mixed with animal manure, oil cake, and chicken manure all contain sulfur, and wood ash has a small amount too. If leaf mustard's flavor turns flatter year after year, sulfur deficiency is worth suspecting.

## 11. Intercropping and Crop Rotation

| Compatibility | Partner | Effect |
|:---:|:---|:---|
| Good | Legumes (pea, kidney bean, cowpea) | Root-nodule bacteria fix nitrogen and leave it behind |
| Good | Lettuce, spinach | Different growing period and root depth mean less competition |
| Good | Welsh onion, garlic chive | Their smells confuse each other's pests |
| Good | Along the edge of potato or tomato beds | Leaf mustard's smell repels some pests |
| Bad | Napa cabbage, radish, cabbage, kale, and other Brassicaceae | Share the same pests and diseases |
| Bad | Strawberry | Known to suppress each other's growth |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The rule against planting Brassicaceae crops side by side comes from two reasons. First is pests and disease: clubroot, cabbage white butterfly, diamondback moth, and flea beetles all attack Brassicaceae as a whole, so grouping them together lets pests move between them and multiply. Second is cross-pollination: if you plan to save seed, no Brassicaceae crop flowering at the same time should be nearby. This problem is covered separately in section 13.

| Example rotation | Year | Crop | What happens to the field |
|:---|:---:|:---|:---|
| 4-year cycle | Year 1 | Leaf mustard, napa cabbage, and other Brassicaceae | Consumes a lot of nitrogen |
| | Year 2 | Legumes (soybean, pea, cowpea) | Nitrogen is fixed and accumulates |
| | Year 3 | Potato, sweet potato, and other tuber crops (tubers/storage roots) | Turns over the soil, improving its physical structure |
| | Year 4 | Maize, sorghum, and other grasses | Deep roots draw up nutrients from lower layers |
| | Back to Year 1 | Brassicaceae | Starts again with pathogen density reduced |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The evaluation table's continuous-cropping tolerance grade of A means a 2-year break is recommended, and the 4-year cycle above is a more generous arrangement than that. In a field where clubroot is already present, even 4 years may not be enough, because the disease's resting spores survive for 7-10 years. In such a field, it is more realistic to move Brassicaceae to a different plot entirely.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Placing legumes before or after leaf mustard is especially efficient. Leaf mustard is a crop that uses a lot of nitrogen quickly and then leaves, while legumes fix nitrogen from the air and leave it in the soil. Harvesting peas in spring, planting a summer crop in that spot, and then sowing leaf mustard there in autumn completes a cycle within a single year. Placing legumes in the rotation is nearly the only structural way to secure nitrogen where chemical fertilizer is unavailable.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There is also a way to use leaf mustard as green manure — the biofumigation mentioned earlier. Sow it thickly in late autumn or early spring, and till the whole stand under right before the flower stalk rises: the decomposing mustard-oil glycosides suppress soil pathogens and nematodes while also turning into organic matter. It yields no food, but serves to repair the field, and is a method worth using when seed is plentiful.

## 12. Harvest

| Use | After sowing | Condition |
|:---|:---:|:---|
| Baby leaves (wraps, quick salad) | 15-20 days | Leaf length 5-10cm. Combined with thinning |
| Mid-stage harvest | 25-35 days | Leaf length 15-20cm. Tender |
| Main harvest (namul, kimchi) | 45-60 days | Leaf length 20-30cm. Juicy and not yet tough |
| For seed | 80-100 days | Flower stalk has risen and pods have dried |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The standard for judging the right time to harvest is not leaf size but leaf condition. The right moment is when the leaf feels thick and springy in the hand, and breaking it releases moisture. Past that point, the veins toughen and pungency becomes excessive. Being a few days late does not make it inedible, but it turns tough for namul and coarse for kimchi.

> **The right time is when the leaves have grown enough, are juicy, and the pungency is moderate. Left too long, they turn tough and only the pungency remains. For leaf mustard, harvesting a little early is better than harvesting late.**

| Item | Tips |
|:---|:---|
| Harvest time of day | Early morning on a clear day. Freshest after holding moisture overnight |
| Harvest method | Pull up with the root, or cut with a knife leaving 1-2cm above the ground |
| Regrowth | Leaving the base gives almost no regrowth harvest |
| Trimming | Shake off soil and remove any yellowed leaves |
| Pre-drying | Do not wash with water; spread in the shade to let surface moisture evaporate |
| Storage prep | Leave what is destined for pickling unwashed |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The reason not to wash leaf mustard right after harvest is that surface moisture becomes the starting point for spoilage. A leafy vegetable's cells begin dying the moment it is harvested, and bacteria multiply at wound sites and on wet surfaces. Leaving it unwashed and letting surface moisture evaporate in the shade buys several extra days. This matters even more for leaf mustard headed for pickling: the salting process draws out water and effectively washes it anyway.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The fact that leaf mustard gives almost no regrowth harvest is the decisive point where it differs from kale or Swiss chard. Kale has a single growing point at the tip of its stem and keeps sending up new leaves even as the lower ones are picked, but leaf mustard grows as a single unit as a whole plant, and cutting the base finishes it. Small leaves do sprout from side buds, but they never grow to a usable size. The perenniality grade of C points to exactly this trait. This is why the design of leaf mustard cultivation relies not on repeated harvesting but on staggered sowing.
