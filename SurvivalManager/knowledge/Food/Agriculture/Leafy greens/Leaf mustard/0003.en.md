## 2. What Kind of Plant Is This?

### 2-1. Taxonomy and Origin

| Item | Details |
|:---|:---|
| Scientific name | Brassica juncea |
| Taxonomy | Magnoliophyta Magnoliopsida / Brassicales / Brassicaceae / Brassica |
| Other names | mustard, leaf mustard, Indian mustard, cheongchae |
| Life form | Annual (bolting habit). When sown in autumn, it becomes an overwintering annual that survives the winter |
| Native range | The region from Central Asia to the Himalayas |
| Origin | Natural cross between napa cabbage (Brassica rapa) and black mustard (Brassica nigra) |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Leaf mustard's pedigree is unusual even among Brassicaceae crops, because it is not a species that evolved on its own in the wild but one that arose when two species met. One full set of napa cabbage chromosomes (the A genome) and one full set of black mustard chromosomes (the B genome) settled together in a single organism, and this state of carrying both species' chromosomes is called an allotetraploid. Rapeseed (Brassica napus) arose the same way, from a cross between napa cabbage and cabbage. Within the Brassicaceae, the relationship among three basic species and three hybrid species is known as the Triangle of U, and leaf mustard sits on one side of that triangle.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This fact has two implications for cultivation. One is that leaf mustard carries a mix of traits from napa cabbage and black mustard: its leaves open quickly and stay tender like napa cabbage, while it produces plenty of sinigrin and turns pungent like black mustard. The other is the boundary of cross-pollination. Because leaf mustard shares chromosomes with both napa cabbage and black mustard, there is room for it to cross with either, while it effectively does not cross with the cabbage group (Brassica oleracea), which shares no chromosomes with it at all. This distinction is the core of seed saving in section 13.

### 2-2. Appearance

| Part | Characteristics |
|:---|:---|
| Leaf | Serrated edges, slightly wrinkled surface. Varies by cultivar: green, reddish-purple, or a mix of colors |
| Stem | Grows upright, branching later in growth |
| Root | A taproot grows down but stays relatively shallow |
| Flower | Typical of Brassicaceae: four yellow petals arranged in a cross |
| Fruit | An elongated pod (silique). It dries brown as it matures, with seeds lined up inside |
| Seed | Round grains 1-2mm across. Brown to reddish-brown |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The shallow distribution of leaf mustard's roots links directly to water management. Since it cannot draw water from deep in the soil, growth stops as soon as the topsoil dries out. On the other hand, it is not a crop that uses a great deal of water either: its roots are shallow, but its growing period is short, so total water demand comes to only 300mm. Watering often in small amounts suits leaf mustard; watering deeply all at once does not.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Leaf color varies by cultivar, and that difference is also a difference in composition. The red of red leaf mustard comes from a pigment called anthocyanin, which deepens in response to UV light and cold. That is why red leaf mustard grown in autumn comes out a deeper color than the same cultivar grown in spring, in the same field. Because this pigment acts as an antioxidant, planting green and red leaf mustard together widens the range of compounds obtained from the same area.

### 2-3. Growth Stages

| Stage | Timing (from sowing) | Key physiology | Main nutrients used |
|:---|:---|:---|:---|
| Germination & seedling stage | Sowing to day 7 | The seed absorbs water and the cotyledons open | Phosphorus, nitrogen |
| Early vegetative stage | Day 7-20 | True leaves emerge and roots become established | Nitrogen |
| Rapid vegetative stage | Day 20-45 | Leaves and stem grow quickly | Nitrogen, potassium |
| Optimal harvest window | Day 45-60 | Leaves are fully grown but not yet tough | Balanced nitrogen and potassium |
| Bolting & flowering stage | Day 60-100 | The flower stalk rises and yellow flowers bloom. Leaves toughen | Phosphorus, potassium |
| Fruiting & ripening stage | 50-55 days after flowering | Pods mature and seeds ripen | Phosphorus, potassium |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The point worth noting in this table is that the entire life cycle wraps up in around 100 days. That also means the window for human intervention is narrow. If nutrients and water fall short within the first 20 days, there is no time left afterward to make up that loss. This schedule is why leaf mustard has become a crop that stakes everything on base fertilizer and barely uses top-dressing at all.

> **Because leaf mustard's growing period is short, the nutrient supply in the early stage all but determines the yield. There is no time to make up for it with top-dressing, so the outcome is decided at the base-fertilizer stage.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;When bolting occurs also matters. Leaf mustard's flower stalk rises under two conditions: one is after it has gone through a period of cold followed by lengthening days, and the other is under heat and drought stress. Leaf mustard sown early in spring that stops growing and sends up a flower stalk is the first case; leaf mustard sown in midsummer that bolts before its leaves have even properly spread out is the second. In both cases, the leaves toughen and only the pungency strengthens, making it hard to eat. This is why leaf mustard's cropping falls into spring and autumn, leaving summer empty.

### 2-4. Nutrition per 100g

| Component | Amount | Notes |
|:---|:---:|:---|
| Calories | 36kcal | 24-36kcal depending on cultivar and part |
| Moisture | 87.8g | |
| Protein | 3.50g | 38.9% of calories |
| Carbohydrates | 4.5-7.3g | |
| Dietary fiber | 2.6g | |
| Beta-carotene | 533 μg | Converted to vitamin A in the body |
| Vitamin B1 (thiamine) | 0.07mg | |
| Sugars | 1.51g | Imputed as the median of 8 measured species in the same genus |
| Calcium, iron, phosphorus, potassium | Meets 10% of the RDA | Absolute amounts are at the average level for leafy vegetables |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The number to hold onto when reading this table is the ratio between 3.50g of protein and 36kcal of energy. It measures how much protein comes with each kcal of energy, and for leaf mustard that is 38.9% — nearly four times the 10% that people need. This does not mean leaf mustard is high in protein: its absolute amount, 3.5g, is less than 10% of soybean's. The ratio is large simply because the denominator, calories, is so small.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Even so, this ratio has real meaning. A disaster-time diet usually fills its calories with grains and tuber crops (tubers/storage roots), and these foods have a low protein share of calories: rice runs around 8%, potato around 9%. Both barely reach, or fall short of, the 10% line people need. Adding a vegetable with a high ratio, like leaf mustard, raises the ratio for the whole diet. It is a way to raise the protein share without raising calories, and it is the hidden role leafy vegetables play in a grain-based diet.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Beta-carotene at 533μg is also worth noting. Vitamin A does not dissolve in water but does dissolve in oil, so absorption drops sharply if it is eaten without any fat. Dressing leaf mustard with perilla oil or sesame oil is not only for flavor — it is also for absorption. Even when oil is scarce in a disaster, crushing and mixing in perilla or sesame seeds can achieve the same effect.

## 3. Growing Conditions

### 3-1. Temperature

| Category | Temperature | Explanation |
|:---|:---:|:---|
| Optimal growth temperature | 15-20°C | Leaves are most tender and yield is highest |
| Viable growth range | 10-25°C | The evaluation table's span is over 15°C, but 12°C is applied due to the bolting constraint |
| Minimum germination temperature | 6°C | Below this, germination becomes extremely slow |
| Optimal germination temperature | 25°C | Seedlings emerge in 3-5 days |
| Maximum germination temperature | 35°C | Above this, germination rate drops |
| High-temperature limit | Above 25°C | Pungency intensifies and leaves toughen |
| Low-temperature limit | -5°C | Below this, leaves freeze and turn mushy |
| Bolting-inducing cold | 0-10°C for a set period | The flower stalk rises afterward once days lengthen |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The most practical piece of information in leaf mustard's temperature requirements is how wide its germination range is: seeds sprout anywhere from 6-35°C. That means sowing at any point from early spring to late summer will germinate one way or another, and in a disaster that is considerable flexibility. Leaf mustard is a seed you can toss into an empty spot in the field without worrying much about the season.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The problem comes after germination. Leaf mustard grown above 25°C develops thin, tough leaves and excessively strong pungency. From the plant's point of view, this is a normal response: heat and drought are stress, and a stressed Brassicaceae plant produces more of its defensive mustard-oil glycosides. In effect, it shifts toward being worse to eat. The rule of thumb that summer leaf mustard tastes bad rests on this physiology.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Conversely, leaf mustard grown in cool conditions, especially during autumn when the gap between day and night temperatures is wide, strikes a balance between pungency and sweetness. When the temperature drops at night, less sugar is consumed by respiration, so sugar builds up in the leaves. The saying that leaf mustard tastes best during gimjang (making kimchi for the winter) season points to this phenomenon.

> **Leaf mustard is a crop of the cool season. It will germinate in summer too, but it will not produce leaves worth eating. In a disaster, sowing leaf mustard in summer easily becomes a waste of seed and labor.**

### 3-2. Light

| Item | Standard | Notes |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Sunlight requirement | 6+ hours of direct sunlight a day | |
| Partial-shade tolerance | Possible | Relative yield for leafy vegetables at 40% shade: 86% |
| Seed germination | Positively photoblastic | Germination rate is higher with light exposure |
| Symptoms of deficiency | Stems stretch and become leggy, leaves thin out | |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The fact that leaf mustard seed is positively photoblastic is where sowing mistakes happen most often. Because germination is promoted by exposure to light, covering the soil too thickly causes the germination rate to drop sharply. 0.5cm to 1cm, just enough to barely cover the seed, is right. This is the same trait as lettuce, and the opposite of crops like potato or beans that are buried deep.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The figure of 86% relative yield under shade also has meaning in a disaster. Even in a spot that gets no sun all day, such as in a building's shadow or under a tree, leaf mustard still produces some yield. This is a general trait of leafy vegetables, because the leaf itself is the harvest, with no need to move the products of photosynthesis into a root or fruit. That said, leaf mustard grown in shade has weaker pungency and thinner leaves. Whether that counts as a drawback or an advantage depends on the use: for leaf mustard destined for kimchi, a sunny spot is better, while for tender leaves to eat as namul (a seasoned vegetable side dish), partial shade is not bad at all.

### 3-3. Water and Soil

| Item | Standard | Notes |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Total evapotranspiration | About 300mm | On the low side among leafy vegetables |
| Soil moisture | Maintain good water retention | Roots are shallow, so growth stops as soon as the topsoil dries |
| Excess moisture | Risk of root rot | Ensure drainage channels |
| Soil | Fertile ground rich in organic matter with good water retention | Yield and quality are best on fertile, clay-rich soil |
| Soil pH | pH 6.0-7.5 | Slightly acidic to neutral |
| Raised bed height | 15-20cm | For drainage |
| Deep tillage | 20-30cm | The evaluation table's digging-energy figure is based on full-surface tillage at 10cm |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Below pH 6.0, leaf mustard visibly declines. Clubroot, a problem common to all Brassicaceae crops, becomes much worse in acidic soil. The protist that causes clubroot is active below pH 6.0 and its activity drops off sharply above pH 7.2. In a disaster where lime is unavailable, wood ash is the way to deal with this. The ash left after burning wood contains potassium carbonate and calcium oxide and is alkaline, so spreading it on the field raises the soil's pH. Wood ash, however, works fast and strong, and too much of it tips the soil toward alkaline, blocking the uptake of micronutrients. About a handful per square meter is what the soil can take in a year, and it must be mixed well into the soil after spreading.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;When it comes to water, there are two conflicting needs. Leaf mustard likes moisture but is weak against waterlogging. Its shallow, spreading roots are sensitive to the moisture state of the topsoil, and at the same time, if those same shallow roots sit submerged, they cannot get oxygen and start rotting right away. The solution is to build raised beds. Planting on a bed 15-20cm high lets water drain away from around the roots while the inside of the bed stays moist. Checking that drainage channels have not clogged during the monsoon season is the single most practical piece of water management in growing leaf mustard.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;When plastic mulch is not available, straw, dry grass, fallen leaves, or rice hulls take its place. Organic mulch is highly effective for a shallow-rooted crop like leaf mustard: it blocks evaporation from the topsoil, keeps raindrops from pounding the surface into a crust, and becomes nutrients as it decomposes. It should not be laid right after sowing, though, because the seed is positively photoblastic and blocking light hurts germination. The right order is to lay it between the plants after three or four true leaves have come out.

## 4. Varieties

### 4-1. Characteristics by Type

| Variety | Characteristics | Taste and texture | Main use |
|:---|:---|:---|:---|
| Cheonggat | Leaves are green with strong pungency. Strong cold hardiness and disease resistance | Strongly pungent | Most widely used for kimchi |
| Jeokgat (Honggat) | Leaves are red, rich in anthocyanins | Medium pungency | Kimchi, wraps, and pickles that showcase the color |
| Dolsan gat | A late-maturing type with large, broad leaves | Tender | Used exclusively for gat-kimchi |
| Eolcheonggat | Cold-tolerant enough for overwintering cultivation | Mild taste | Winter growing and early-spring harvest |
| Deulgat | Close to the wild type | Strong, coarse aroma | Poor soil and low-maintenance growing |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In the end, the differences among varieties come down to two axes: how strong the pungency is, and how well the plant withstands cold. Cheonggat leans toward pungency, Dolsan gat toward tenderness, and Eolcheonggat toward cold hardiness. This choice matters in a disaster because the strength of the pungency is also the strength of its keeping power.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Allyl isothiocyanate, the breakdown product of mustard-oil glycosides, has antimicrobial action — naturally enough, since plants make it to fend off bacteria and fungi. Kimchi made from leaf mustard with plenty of this compound keeps longer because spoilage microbes multiply more slowly. Kimchi made from a milder variety, by contrast, is pleasant to eat but sours and softens quickly. So Cheonggat suits kimchi meant to be eaten all through winter as stored kimchi, while Dolsan gat suits kimchi meant to be eaten right away at the start of winter.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Deulgat is a line that has been pushed out of commercial cultivation, but it is worth a second look in a disaster. Being less improved, it resists pests and diseases well, grows reasonably even with little fertilizer, and drops its own seed to come back the following year. Let deulgat loose on an unmanaged spot like a field edge or roadside, and it will carry on for several years without anyone tending it. The taste is coarse but edible, and above all, its seed supply never runs out.

### 4-2. Selection Criteria by Situation

| Situation | Choice | Reason |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Early disaster, fastest possible harvest | Cheonggat, Jeokgat | Fast-growing; baby leaves can be harvested in 20-30 days |
| Kimchi and long-term storage | Cheonggat, Dolsan gat | Cheonggat's strong pungency gives good storability; Dolsan gat's large leaves are easy to handle |
| Widening the nutritional range | Jeokgat | Adds anthocyanins |
| Winter growing and overwintering | Eolcheonggat | Has the strongest cold hardiness |
| Poor soil, low-maintenance growing | Deulgat | Needs less attention and continues through self-sowing |
| Spreading risk | Sow Cheonggat and Jeokgat together | Different bolting times and disease resistance reduce the risk of total loss |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The mixed planting in the last row has particular value in a disaster. Planting only one variety means the whole field collapses if a disease that variety is vulnerable to shows up. Mixing two or three varieties with different traits means that even if one is hit, the others remain. Spreading out the harvest time is also an advantage: planting late-maturing Dolsan gat together with early-maturing Cheonggat splits the harvest across 2-3 weeks.

> **In a disaster, the first criterion for choosing a variety is not yield but risk spread. Two or three varieties with different traits have a higher chance of surviving than any single best variety.**
