## 1. Disaster-Preparedness Evaluation

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Leaf mustard's Overall grade is B, 44.3 points. Broken down by scenario, its Short-term ability to get through the early stage of a disaster is B (45.1), its Long-term ability to sustain itself over several years is C (43.5), and its Supplementary value as a side-dish resource beyond staple food is SS (58.7). Two things summarize leaf mustard's character: Short-term outranks Long-term, and Supplementary outranks Overall by more than 14 points. It grows fast and helps in the early stage, but it is not a crop that sustains a field year after year, and its value as a side dish sits near the top of the grading scale.

### 1-3. Why Micronutrients D and Supplementary SS Appear Together

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The first combination that stands out in this table is micronutrients D alongside Supplementary SS at 58.7 points. A crop graded as nutrient-poor ends up near the top for side-dish value. It looks like a contradiction, but the way the score is calculated explains it.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The micronutrient criterion assumes eating an amount equal to 2,100kcal a day, and counts how many nutrients exceed 10% of the daily recommended intake at that amount. For leaf mustard, that is 8: vitamin A, vitamin C, thiamine, riboflavin, calcium, iron, phosphorus, and potassium. That is on the low side for a leafy vegetable. Compared with kale's 18, Swiss chard's 17, and lettuce's 16, leaf mustard's nutritional range is narrow. It fell short of the threshold for nutrients such as vitamin K, vitamin E, folate, and pantothenic acid.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;But the Supplementary score is not set by micronutrients alone. Protein density, ease of cooking and processing, taste, storage/processing potential, and speed to harvest all go into it. Leaf mustard's protein density is SS: with 3.50g of protein and 36kcal per 100g, its protein share of calories is 38.9%, nearly four times the requirement line of 10%. Cooking and processing is also SS — it can be washed and eaten as is, or turned straight into kimchi. These two SS scores lift the D in micronutrients and produce the SS in Supplementary.

> **Leaf mustard does not compete on the number of nutrients it offers; it earns its value by carrying protein on top of few calories, and by becoming a preserved food once it meets salt.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This distinction matters for actual meal planning. Planting leaf mustard does not by itself solve vitamin supply. Keep a nutritionally broad leafy vegetable such as kale or Swiss chard on the side, and let leaf mustard handle a different job next to it. That job is quick recovery and storage.

### 1-4. How to Read the Strengths

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;SS goes to three criteria: protein density, infrastructure needs, and cooking & processing. S goes to speed to harvest, prevalence in South Korea, and seed longevity. A goes to water use, temperature range, cold hardiness, pest-control self-reliance, continuous-cropping tolerance, yield stability, and seed saving.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Speed to harvest S is leaf mustard's first advantage. It takes 45-60 days from sowing to a full harvest, and thinnings of baby leaves can start at 20-30 days. If a disaster begins and you sow into a field with nothing in it, leaf mustard is one of the few options that puts something green in your mouth within a month. Set against potato's 100 days, soybean's 120, and cabbage's over 90, leaf mustard's one month belongs to a different order of time. This is why its Short-term grade of B outranks its Long-term grade of C.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Seed longevity S is another criterion worth weighing carefully. It stands at 5 years in airtight storage at room temperature. Compared with lettuce's mere 2-3 years or Welsh onion's 1 year, leaf mustard's seed waits a long time. Disaster preparedness, in the end, is the ability to wait, and a seed that lasts 5 years is itself a stockpile. Seal a packet of leaf mustard seed and keep it somewhere cool, and it can still be sown 5 years later. Its annual habit compounds this advantage. Leaf mustard sown in spring flowers and sets seed that same summer, so unlike kale or cabbage, which must survive a winter to give seed, its seed cycle completes within the first year.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Water use A also carries real, practical value. Total evapotranspiration is about 300mm, clearly less than radish's 500mm or iceberg lettuce's 500mm. When water has to be hauled by hand, this difference translates directly into labor. The main reason water use is low is the short growing period: the crop simply stands in the field for less time, so there is less time for water to evaporate.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Cold hardiness A and temperature range A pay off in autumn and winter. Leaf mustard maintains growth down to -5°C, and in southern South Korea it overwinters outdoors. In a field cleared of other vegetables in late autumn, leaf mustard keeps its leaves through several frosts. Frost-touched leaf mustard turns sweeter and slightly less pungent, a response to the plant dissolving sugar into its cell fluid to lower its freezing point. The custom of making gat-kimchi during gimjang (making kimchi for the winter) season rests on this physiological response.

### 1-5. How to Read the Weaknesses

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;D goes to four criteria: micronutrients, storability, non-food value, and taste. C goes to calories per area, perenniality, pest & disease pressure, yield retention without synthetic inputs, and tillage & digging energy.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Look first at taste D. The basis given is that the smell and aroma are off-putting. This looks like a matter of preference, but in a disaster it is a practical problem. Food people don't want to eat gets left over, leftover food gets thrown out, and a crop that gets thrown out gives you no reason to plant it. Leaf mustard's pungency comes from allyl isothiocyanate, released when sinigrin — a mustard-oil glycoside — breaks down. Chewing the leaf ruptures its cells, and an enzyme called myrosinase meets sinigrin to produce this compound. The stinging sensation rises through the nose rather than the tongue because the compound is volatile.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Fortunately, processing resolves much of this weakness. Heating destroys myrosinase, so no pungency develops, and salting and fermenting shift the sensation toward umami. That is why gat-kimchi is far easier to eat than raw leaf mustard. Taste D is a grade set on the raw product, and once processing is assumed, the actual experience changes considerably.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Perenniality C is a structural weakness. Leaf mustard does not lend itself well to the continuous method of picking lower leaves used with kale or Swiss chard. Its growing point sits at the tip of the stem and the whole plant grows as one mass, so while picking a few leaves is possible, stretching repeated harvests over several months is not. Once you cut it, that plant is finished, and twice a year — spring and autumn — is the limit. This is one of the main reasons its Long-term grade is pulled down to C. The countermeasure is to sow in staggered batches every ten to fifteen days rather than all at once. This keeps the harvest coming instead of concentrated at a single point in time.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Pest & disease pressure C, with an estimated yield loss without pest control of 51.7%, is a burden shared by all Brassicaceae crops. Leaf mustard does have one mitigating factor, though: its pungent compound is itself a defense substance the plant makes to deter plant-eating insects. In practice, if napa cabbage and leaf mustard are planted side by side in the same field, cabbage white butterfly caterpillars go for the napa cabbage first. But this defense is not complete. Pests such as the diamondback moth that have adapted to mustard-oil glycosides actually use the smell of leaf mustard as a signal to find it. A pest-control self-reliance grade of B means that even without chemicals, netting and adjusted sowing dates can block a large share of the damage; section 9 covers the methods.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Yield retention without synthetic inputs C, at 40.8%, means yield drops to 40% once chemical fertilizer is cut off. Leaf mustard is a heavy nitrogen feeder, and pushing out leaves in such a short time requires enough nutrients early on. When fertilizer is short, the leaves come out small, tough, and only more pungent. Fortunately, because the growing period is short, the total amount needed is not large, and unlike other heavy-feeding crops, a single application of base fertilizer can carry it through its whole life. Section 5 covers this in detail.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Storability D is a grade based on raw leaves: they do not last a week without power at room temperature. But for leaf mustard, this grade does not represent its real value, because it was never a crop meant to be kept fresh for long — it is a crop meant to be pickled. Once salt and a crock are assumed, the storage period stretches from several months to more than a year. Section 15 tells that story.
