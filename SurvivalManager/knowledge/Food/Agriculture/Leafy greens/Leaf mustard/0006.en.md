## 13. Seed Saving — The Advantage of Being an Annual and the Pitfall of Cross-Pollination

### 13-1. Leaf Mustard Does Not Need to Survive Winter

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The biggest factor that makes seed saving hard for Brassicaceae crops is their biennial habit. Cabbage, kale, napa cabbage, and radish must go through winter cold before their flower stalk will rise. In the first year they only produce leaves or roots, and only after wintering do they flower the following spring. That ties up a bed for a year and a half, and risks the plant freezing to death somewhere in that span.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;For leaf mustard, this barrier is low. Leaf mustard sown in spring sends up a flower stalk, blooms, and sets seed within that same year as temperatures rise and days lengthen. Either there is no absolute requirement for cold exposure, or it acts only very weakly. In practice it can be treated as close to a true annual, and this is leaf mustard's greatest strategic advantage in a disaster.

| Crop | Life form | Time to seed | Winter care |
|:---|:---|:---|:---|
| Leaf mustard | Annual (bolting habit) | About 100 days after spring sowing | Not needed |
| Radish | Biennial | 1.5 years after sowing | The root must be overwintered |
| Napa cabbage, cabbage, kale | Biennial | 1.5 years after sowing | The plant must be overwintered |
| Lettuce | Annual | About 100 days after sowing | Not needed |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;So leaf mustard holds a special place in seed planning for the first year of a disaster. Leave a few spring-sown plants unpulled, and they will give seed by June or July. Since seed longevity is 5 years, one harvest of seed lasts for several years. Autumn-sown leaf mustard works differently: it winters over and sends up its flower stalk the following spring. Both paths are open — seed saving from a spring sowing, and overwintering seed saving from an autumn sowing.

### 13-2. What It Crosses With

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The difficulty in saving leaf mustard seed is cross-pollination. Leaf mustard is capable of self-pollination and sets seed with its own pollen, but bees and flies moving between plants cause a substantial share of cross-pollination as well. This is where the evaluation table's Seed saving grade of A, with a 500m isolation standard, comes from. It is easier than crops like kale or cabbage, which have self-incompatibility and absolutely require another plant, but trickier than a crop like lettuce, which is practically fully self-pollinating.

| Crop | Scientific name | Crossing with leaf mustard |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Leaf mustard, mustard greens, quick-growing mustard types | Brassica juncea | Cross completely |
| Napa cabbage, bok choy, turnip, rapeseed | Brassica rapa | Can cross at low frequency |
| Black mustard | Brassica nigra | Can cross at low frequency |
| Cabbage, kale, broccoli, kohlrabi | Brassica oleracea | Effectively do not cross |
| Radish | Raphanus sativus | Different genus; does not cross naturally |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The basis for this table is chromosomes. Leaf mustard is an allotetraploid carrying one set of napa cabbage chromosomes (the A genome) together with one set of black mustard chromosomes (the B genome). So its chromosomes can partially pair with those of the napa cabbage group, which carries the A genome, and with black mustard, which carries the B genome, producing hybrids at low probability. The cabbage group, by contrast, carries an entirely different chromosome set, the C genome, so no crossing occurs under natural conditions.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In practical terms, this comes down to the following. In a year you are saving leaf mustard seed, kale, cabbage, or broccoli flowering nearby is nothing to worry about. What matters is, first, other leaf mustard varieties, and second, napa cabbage, turnip, and rapeseed. Other leaf mustard varieties in particular mix 100% of the time. Plant Cheonggat and Jeokgat side by side and let both flower, and the next year you get leaf mustard of indeterminate identity with colors all mixed together.

### 13-3. Three Ways to Prevent Cross-Pollination

| Method | Details | Practicality |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Isolation distance | Keep 500m or more from other flowering plants | Difficult, since neighboring fields cannot be controlled |
| Staggered cultivation | Let only one variety flower per year | Most practical |
| Netting | Cover with mesh netting before flowering to keep bees out | Reliable for leaf mustard, since it self-pollinates |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;For leaf mustard, netting is especially effective. Kale or cabbage have self-incompatibility, so someone has to move pollen by hand with a brush inside the netting, but leaf mustard fertilizes with its own pollen, so covering it with netting and simply leaving it alone still sets seed. Keep bees out, and purity is preserved; even with no bees at all, fruiting is unaffected. This combination is a rare condition among Brassicaceae crops.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Without netting, staggered cultivation is the way to go. This year, let only Cheonggat flower and harvest all the Jeokgat to eat; next year, do the reverse. Since seed longevity is 5 years, saving seed of a given variety just once every 3 years keeps the supply unbroken. This method preserves purity with the least effort.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;An isolation distance of 500m is the rule, but hard to maintain on a small plot. Even short of full isolation, though, distance still has value: the farther apart, the lower the crossing rate, and an obstacle between the two, such as a building, trees, or a hill, that blocks bee movement effectively extends that distance.

> **Leave at least ten plants for seed. Even with a self-pollinating crop, saving seed from just one or two plants year after year narrows genetic diversity and weakens vigor.**

### 13-4. Seed-Saving Procedure

| Stage | Timing | Details |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Seed-plant selection | Harvest time | Choose plants with large leaves, clear varietal traits, and no disease |
| Bolting | May-June for spring sowing, March-April the following year for autumn sowing | The flower stalk rises. Stake it to keep it from toppling |
| Flowering | 2-3 weeks after bolting | Yellow flowers bloom. Maintain isolation at this point |
| Pod set | After flowering | Elongated pods form |
| Harvest | 50-55 days after flowering | Cut the whole stalk once two-thirds of the pods have dried brown |
| Post-ripening, threshing | Right after harvest | Dry in the shade for 1-2 weeks, then put in a sack and beat it |
| Sorting | After fully drying | Winnow to blow away empty husks |
| Storage | Moisture 10% or below | Put in an airtight container in a cool, dark place |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Do not leave the pods in the field until they are completely dry. Brassicaceae pods split open on their own once fully dry, spilling their seed onto the ground. Leaf mustard has this trait especially strongly: being a day or two late can leave nothing to harvest. The trick is to cut the whole stalk when the lower two-thirds of the pods have turned brown while the upper ones are still green, and let it post-ripen in the shade. Laying the cut stalks on cloth or a sack while drying also catches any seed that pops out and falls.

| Item | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Seed longevity | 5 years in airtight storage at room temperature |
| Optimal storage | A cool, dark, dry place, in an airtight container |
| Desiccant | Roasted and cooled rice, charcoal, dry wood ash |
| Germination check | Each year, place 20 seeds on a wet cloth and count the germination rate after a few days |
| Yield per plant | Several thousand seeds |
| Seed-saving cycle | Once every 3-4 years, with proper isolation maintained that year |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;One leaf mustard plant yields several thousand seeds, so ten plants secure several years' worth of seed at once. Combine that with a 5-year seed longevity, and seed saving stops being an annual task: it only needs to happen once every 3-4 years, with proper isolation maintained that one year. In the other years, all the leaf mustard can go toward eating.

## 14. Toxicity and Compounds of Concern

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Leaf mustard has no acute toxicity. It can be washed and eaten as is, and the evaluation table's Cooking & processing grade of SS reflects that fact. There are, however, compounds worth considering when it is eaten in large amounts over a long time.

| Compound | What it is | What problem it causes | How to reduce it |
|:---|:---|:---|:---|
| Mustard-oil glycoside (sinigrin) | The source of Brassicaceae's pungency and aroma | Irritates the stomach if eaten in large amounts | Heating destroys the enzyme, so no pungency develops |
| Goitrogens | Some of the breakdown products of mustard-oil glycosides | Can interfere with iodine uptake and lower thyroid function | Blanching, eating seaweed |
| Oxalic acid | An organic acid the plant stores bound to calcium | Raises kidney stone risk if eaten in large amounts | Leaf mustard's content is on the low side. Blanching and discarding the water reduces it further |
| Nitrate | Builds up in leaves given a lot of nitrogen | Becomes a problem with excessive intake | Avoid over-fertilizing with nitrogen and avoid fertilizing right before harvest |

### 14-1. How Safe Is the Pungency?

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Mustard-oil glycosides are safe at ordinary intake levels. What matters is amount and form. Eating a lot of raw leaf mustard at once can let allyl isothiocyanate irritate the stomach lining and cause heartburn. For people with a sensitive stomach, cooked or pickled forms are safer than raw.

> **When trying it for the first time, start with a small amount. Reactions to pungency vary greatly between individuals, and in a disaster, getting sick from a new food is itself a real danger.**

### 14-2. Goitrogens and Iodine

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Some of the substances produced as mustard-oil glycosides break down interfere with how the thyroid uses iodine. These substances are called goitrogens. For someone getting enough iodine, eating an ordinary amount of leaf mustard is not a problem. The risky condition is eating large amounts of raw Brassicaceae vegetables over a long time on a diet already short on iodine.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This condition can genuinely arise in a disaster. When logistics break down and people find themselves far from the sea, sea mustard, kelp, and laver disappear, and refined salt often contains no iodine either. In that state, relying mainly on Brassicaceae vegetables can lower thyroid function. There are two responses. One is to mix in cooked forms: heating destroys the myrosinase enzyme, cutting goitrogen formation itself, and blanching and discarding the water removes water-soluble compounds. The other is to secure seaweed whenever it is available. Dried sea mustard and kelp keep for a long time at room temperature, and a small amount is enough.

## 15. Storage Without Refrigeration — Where Leaf Mustard Comes Into Its Own

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Leaf mustard's storability grade is D, and it is hard to keep past a week without power at room temperature. But that grade is the story for raw leaves left as they are. Leaf mustard was never a crop meant to be kept fresh from the start — it is a crop meant to be pickled, and the moment it meets salt, its storage period stretches from several months to more than a year.

### 15-1. Short-Term Storage Fresh

| Method | How | Duration |
|:---|:---|:---:|
| Cool storage | Without washing, let surface moisture dry, then keep in a cool, ventilated place | 3-5 days |
| Wet-cloth storage | Wrap in a damp cloth and keep in a root cellar, earthen pit, or under a north-facing eave | 5-7 days |
| Leaving in the field | Leave standing in the field until frost, cutting only as much as needed | Until early winter |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The last row is the most practically useful method. Since leaf mustard tolerates temperatures down to -5°C, it can be left standing in the field until early winter and cut whenever needed — the field itself acts as a storehouse. It cannot get through the depth of winter the way kale can, though, so it must be cleared and moved into pickling before the real cold sets in.

### 15-2. Gat-kimchi

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Kimchi is the centerpiece among ways to preserve leaf mustard. There is a reason gat-kimchi keeps longer than napa cabbage kimchi: leaf mustard's allyl isothiocyanate has antimicrobial action that suppresses the growth of contaminating microbes, while the lactic acid bacteria that drive fermentation are comparatively less affected. Well-made gat-kimchi lasts from several months to more than a year in a cool place.

| Stage | Details |
|:---|:---|
| Salting | Soak in 3-4% brine for 3-4 hours. Ready once the stems bend |
| Rinsing | Rinse once or twice and drain |
| Seasoning | Mix in chili powder, garlic, ginger, and salted fish/seafood |
| Packing | Press into a crock so the brine covers the material |
| Fermenting | Ferment in a cool place |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;It has to be assumed there may be no seasoning ingredients at all. Kimchi can still be made without chili powder or salted seafood. Set the salt concentration at 2-3% and keep the material fully submerged in brine, and lactic fermentation will proceed. Because leaf mustard has its own pungency, the flavor is not bland even without any other seasoning.

> **Lactic fermentation comes down to two essentials: the material must stay fully submerged under the brine, and the salt concentration must not fall below 2%. Keep these two conditions, and lactic acid bacteria grow instead of contaminating microbes.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The greatest value of lactic fermentation in a disaster is preserving vitamin C. Drying or boiling as a storage method loses most of the vitamin C, but fermentation keeps a substantial amount. It is no accident that pickling cultures developed in regions with long winters.

### 15-3. Salt Curing

| Method | How | Duration |
|:---|:---|:---:|
| Salt packing | Layer with 10-15% salt and press into a crock | Several months to over 1 year |
| How to use | Before use, soak in water two or three times to remove salt | |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Salt curing is the simplest and longest-lasting storage method. Once salt concentration exceeds 10%, even lactic acid bacteria cannot function and all microbial activity stops. It becomes a stalled state where neither fermentation nor spoilage occurs, and that is why it lasts past a year. In exchange, almost no vitamin C survives.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;How much salt is on hand determines the choice between salt curing and kimchi. If salt is plentiful, go with salt curing for the long haul; if it is scarce, go with 2-3% kimchi-style fermentation instead, since salt use differs by a factor of five between the two.

### 15-4. Drying and Dried Greens (Mungnamul)

| Step | Method |
|:---|:---|
| Blanching | Blanch in boiling water for 30 seconds and rinse in cold water |
| Wringing out water | Squeeze firmly by hand |
| Drying | Hang in a shaded, breezy spot and dry completely |
| Storage | Put in an airtight container along with charcoal or roasted rice |
| Duration | 6 months to over 1 year |
| Use | Soak in water, boil, then dress as namul or add to soup |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There are two reasons to blanch before drying. One is to destroy enzymes and prevent discoloration and spoilage during storage; the other is to soften the tissue so it rehydrates well later. Leaf mustard dried without blanching turns yellowish and stays tough even after soaking. That said, dried leaf mustard retains almost no vitamin C. What drying leaves behind is dietary fiber, minerals, and beta-carotene, which is relatively heat-stable. In a winter diet, dried leaf mustard is a means of storing it in reduced bulk, while vitamin C comes from fermented foods and any fresh leaves still left in the field.

### 15-5. Jangajji

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;If soy sauce or doenjang (Korean soybean paste) is available, jangajji is another route. Lightly salt leaf mustard's thick stems, drain them, and bury them in the paste, and they will keep for months. The principle is the same as salt curing: the high salt concentration together with the antimicrobial compounds in the paste stop microbial activity.

| Method | Expected duration | Materials needed | Characteristics |
|:---|:---:|:---|:---|
| Fresh, short-term | 3-5 days | None | Maximum freshness |
| Gat-kimchi | Several months to over 1 year | Salt and seasoning | Retains a substantial amount of vitamin C |
| Salt curing | Several months to over 1 year | Salt (a lot) | Simplest and most reliable |
| Drying, mungnamul | 6 months to 1 year | None | Greatly reduces bulk and weight |
| Jangajji | Several months | Soy sauce or doenjang | A salty side dish. Also serves as a salt source |

## 16. Cooking and Processing

| Method | Fuel | Pungency | Vitamin C retention | Notes |
|:---|:---:|:---|:---:|:---|
| Raw (wraps, quick salad) | None | Strongest | Maximum | Baby leaves are suitable |
| Blanching, 30 sec-1 min | Very little | Greatly reduced | Over half | Goitrogens are also reduced |
| Adding to soup or porridge | Shared with other cooking | Reduced | Preserved if the broth is eaten too | Uses no separate fuel |
| Stir-frying | Little | Reduced | Medium | Improves beta-carotene absorption |
| Kimchi, fermentation | None | Turns into umami | Substantial amount retained | Doubles as storage and preparation |
| Drying and grinding to powder | Sun and wind for drying | Almost disappears | Almost none | Minerals and beta-carotene remain |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;When fuel is scarce, the standard for designing how to cook leaf mustard is how to handle its pungency. If raw leaf mustard's pungency is too much to manage, there are two routes: heating or fermenting. Heating spends fuel; fermenting spends time. In a disaster, time is usually more abundant than fuel, so fermentation is structurally the more favorable choice.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The principle behind pungency disappearing with heat is enzyme destruction. Allyl isothiocyanate only forms when sinigrin meets myrosinase, and this enzyme, being a protein, is fragile against heat: even 30 seconds in boiling water destroys most of its activity. That is why blanched leaf mustard is barely pungent at all. Conversely, to keep the pungency, crush or chop the leaves and let them sit at room temperature for a while before cooking. During that time the enzyme does its work and the pungent compound is already formed, and even after heating afterward, a substantial share of that compound remains.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Making mustard sauce works on the same principle. Grind leaf mustard seeds and mix them with lukewarm water, and within 10-15 minutes the enzyme goes to work and the pungency rises. Add vinegar at that point, and the enzyme stops, fixing the pungency at that level. Mix with hot water instead, and the enzyme dies, leaving no pungency, only bitterness. Using the seed as well means a single leaf mustard plant yields both a leafy vegetable and a condiment.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;If even a little oil is available, mixing in stir-fried preparations is nutritionally advantageous. Leaf mustard's beta-carotene is fat-soluble and needs oil to be absorbed. Without oil, crushing and mixing in perilla or sesame seeds can substitute.

## 17. Annual Work Calendar

| Month | Tasks |
|:---|:---|
| January | Check the condition of stored kimchi and salt-cured goods. Check seed germination rate |
| February | Turn the compost and prepare the field for spring sowing. Collect wood ash |
| March | Sow spring leaf mustard in late March. Remove covering from overwintered leaf mustard |
| April | Thin spring leaf mustard and harvest baby leaves. Harvest overwintered leaf mustard. Overwintered seed plants bolt |
| May | Main harvest of spring leaf mustard. Seed plants flower; maintain isolation |
| June | Flowering and pod set of spring-sown seed plants. Summer highland sowing (above 400m elevation) |
| July | Seed harvest. Cut when two-thirds of pods have turned brown. Post-ripening and threshing |
| August | Autumn leaf mustard sowing begins in late August. Apply base fertilizer |
| September | Autumn sowing continues (staggered). Thinning. Watch for flea beetles and moths |
| October | Autumn harvest begins. Overwintering sowing in late October (southern South Korea) |
| November | Main harvest and gimjang. Making gat-kimchi and salt-curing. Cover overwintering leaf mustard |
| December | Manage stored goods. Clear the field and dispose of residue. Till harvest residue into the soil |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;What this table shows is that leaf mustard's year has two peaks. The spring peak is short and early: sown in late March, harvested in May, and, left standing, giving seed by July. The autumn peak is long and heavy: sown in staggered batches from late August, harvested in October and November, with that harvest moving into kimchi and salt curing to get through winter.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The two peaks serve different roles. Spring leaf mustard is closer to farming for seed, while autumn leaf mustard is farming for food. In the first year of a disaster, this distinction matters especially.

> **In the first year of a disaster, eat half the spring leaf mustard and leave half for seed. The half you leave decides that autumn and the whole of the following year.**
