## Introduction

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Leaf mustard (Brassica juncea) is a leafy vegetable in the cabbage family (Brassicaceae). It arose in Central Asia as a natural cross between napa cabbage (Brassica rapa) and black mustard (Brassica nigra), making it an allotetraploid that inherited the chromosomes of both ancestors. This ancestry explains leaf mustard's two faces: fast-growing leaves inherited from napa cabbage, and pungency inherited from black mustard. So it is not surprising that the vegetable we call leaf mustard and the raw material behind mustard sauce belong to the same species.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;By the disaster-preparedness yardstick, leaf mustard's Overall grade is B, 44.3 points — middling. But look at the Supplementary grade alone, and it jumps sharply to SS, 58.7 points. That gap is leaf mustard's real identity. A leafy vegetable with 36kcal per 100g cannot fill a stomach. Covering one adult's daily 2,100kcal with leaf mustard alone would require eating 5.8kg of it, and eating that much pungent leaf is physically and physiologically impossible. Leaf mustard is not a crop that fills the rice bowl — it is one that fills another dish on the table.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;It fills that other dish differently from other leafy vegetables. Where kale or Swiss chard supply micronutrients as raw leaves, leaf mustard specializes in becoming a preserved food once it meets salt. Sinigrin, the compound behind leaf mustard's pungency, and its breakdown product allyl isothiocyanate, irritate the human tongue but act as a disinfectant against contaminating microbes. That is why gat-kimchi (leaf mustard kimchi) turns sour later and keeps longer than napa cabbage kimchi, and why leaf mustard jangajji (vegetables pickled in soy sauce or paste) survives the summer. With just salt and a crock, leaf mustard harvested in autumn stays on the table until the following spring. When electricity and refrigeration are gone, that is no small ability.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The weaknesses are just as clear. First, the micronutrient grade is D. Of 21 nutrients, only 8 exceed 10% of the daily recommended intake in leaf mustard. Set against kale's 18 and Swiss chard's 17, that is less than half. Eating leaf mustard alone will not prevent vitamin deficiency.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Second, the taste grade is D. This reflects an assessment that its smell and aroma are off-putting, which simply mirrors the reality that it is hard to eat much raw leaf mustard.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Third, perenniality is C. Unlike kale, whose lower leaves can be picked continuously, leaf mustard is a crop that is done once harvested, and twice a year — spring and autumn — is the limit.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;But leaf mustard has three things other leafy vegetables lack. Speed: 45-60 days from sowing to harvest, or 20-30 days for baby leaves. Seed longevity: 5 years in airtight storage at room temperature. And its annual habit, which means it does not need to survive a winter to set seed. Unlike kale and cabbage, which make you wait two years for seed, leaf mustard sown in spring returns seed that same summer. It is one of the few Brassicaceae crops that can achieve seed self-sufficiency in the first year of a disaster.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In short, leaf mustard is not the star of the field but its insurance policy. It fills an empty plot within a month, becomes a winter side dish if there is salt, and its seed endures for 5 years, multiplying on its own year after year. Even so, nutrition should not be entrusted to this one crop alone, and if there is no way to process it to handle the pungency, its value drops by half.
