## 5. Field Preparation and Fertilizer

### 5-1. Preparing the Field

| Item | Method |
|:---|:---|
| Tilling | Till to a depth of 20-30cm, break up clods, and level the ground evenly |
| Raised bed | Height 15-20cm. Cut drainage channels alongside |
| Soil pH | Adjust to pH 6.0-7.5. Mix in wood ash if acidic |
| Organic matter | Work in well-rotted compost 1-2 weeks before sowing and mix with the soil |
| Surface preparation | Since the seed is positively photoblastic, large clods bury it and hurt germination |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There is good reason to insist on well-rotted compost. If under-composted material is worked in, decomposition keeps going right in the field, and this process generates heat and releases ammonia that damages young roots. A crop with a growing period of over 100 days can make up for this early damage later, but leaf mustard, which finishes in 45-60 days, has no time to recover. This is why the sequence is to add compost and wait at least 1 week, preferably 2, before sowing.

### 5-2. Fertilizer Rates

| Fertilizer | Per 1,000m² | Per m² | Timing |
|:---|:---:|:---:|:---|
| Nitrogen (N) | 8-10kg | 8-10g | All as base fertilizer |
| Phosphorus (P) | 6-8kg | 6-8g | All as base fertilizer |
| Potassium (K) | 6-8kg | 6-8g | All as base fertilizer |
| Compost | 1,000-1,500kg | 1-1.5kg | 1-2 weeks before sowing |

| Category | Timing | Details |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Base fertilizer | 1-2 weeks before sowing | All compost and fertilizer |
| Top-dressing | Omitted in principle | Only a small amount, and only if growth is visibly poor |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Leaf mustard's fertilization pattern differs decisively from other leafy vegetables. Most vegetables split their fertilizer between a base application and top-dressing, but leaf mustard gets it all as base fertilizer. The reason is time. After top-dressing is applied, it takes roughly ten days for the roots to absorb it and move it into the leaves, and for leaf mustard, those ten days are 20% of its entire growing period. By the time a top-dressing would show any effect, harvest is already underway.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This structure turns into an unexpected advantage in a disaster, because it cuts down the number of trips to the field. The labor of hauling and spreading fertilizer is finished in one go, and after that only watering needs attention. When labor is scarce, or when the field is far away or unsafe to visit often, a crop like this carries an extra kind of value.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The figures of 8-10g nitrogen, 6-8g phosphorus, and 6-8g potassium are based on the nutrient content of chemical fertilizer. These are the values behind the evaluation table's Fertilizer demand grade of B, with N+P+K totaling about 24g. Where chemical fertilizer is unavailable, this nutrient content has to be converted into natural materials, which is the subject of the next item.

### 5-3. Emergency Fertilizer Substitutes

| Substitute | Nutrients supplied | How to use | Caution |
|:---|:---|:---|:---|
| Well-rotted compost | Nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, and micronutrients generally | Work in 1-1.5kg/m² as base fertilizer and mix with the soil | Under-composted material damages young roots |
| Wood ash | Potassium, phosphorus, calcium | Scatter a handful per m² and mix with the soil | Strongly alkaline; excess blocks micronutrient uptake |
| Diluted urine | Nitrogen | Dilute 10 to 15 times with water and pour between plants | Burns leaves on direct contact. Stop 2 weeks before harvest |
| Bone meal | Phosphorus, calcium | Mix into compost and apply together | Breaks down slowly; use only as base fertilizer |
| Leaf and grass humus | Organic matter and micronutrients | Spread on the field or till it in | Takes time to break down, so apply well in advance |
| Sesame/perilla oil cake, soybean cake | Nitrogen, phosphorus | Ferment before applying as base fertilizer | Unfermented cake damages roots with fermentation heat |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;A bit more on urine. Human urine is a genuinely usable resource as a nitrogen fertilizer. In a healthy person, urine is nearly sterile inside the bladder, and its main component, urea, is converted by soil microbes through ammonia into nitrate, a form plants can use. The problem is concentration: undiluted, its salt and nitrogen levels are too high and will burn roots. That is where the standard of diluting it 10 to 15 times comes from.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;For leaf mustard, though, the opportunity to use diluted urine is limited in itself, since it is a crop that barely gets top-dressed at all. If it is used, limit it to a single application around the time five or six true leaves have appeared, and only if growth is visibly lagging. Do not apply it from 2 weeks before harvest onward. Late nitrogen causes nitrate to build up in the leaves, and that is not a compound that does anyone good in large amounts.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Wood ash is a resource that suits leaf mustard particularly well. It supplies potassium that firms up the leaves, neutralizes acidic soil to lower the risk of clubroot, and, dusted onto the leaves, physically repels pests — one resource doing three jobs. That said, it washes away after rain and needs reapplying, and if stored wet, potassium dissolves out of it, so it must be kept in a dry place.

## 6. Sowing and Thinning

### 6-1. Cropping Schedule

| Cropping type | Sowing time | Harvest time | Notes |
|:---|:---|:---|:---|
| Spring cropping | Late March-early April | Mid-April-early May | Right after the ground thaws; sowing late risks bolting |
| Autumn cropping | Late August-mid-September | Late September-late October | The most stable cropping type. 40-60 days |
| Overwintering cropping | Late October-mid-November | The following spring | Possible in southern South Korea; central South Korea needs covering |
| Summer/highland | Early June-early July | July-August | Only above 400m elevation |

> **Sowing in the open field at low or middle elevation between June and August lets the flower stalk rise first, so no edible leaves develop. The seed and labor are lost outright.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Of the four cropping types, autumn cropping is the mainstay in a disaster. There are several reasons. First, since temperatures are falling, there is no risk of bolting: in spring cropping, temperatures rise and days lengthen as the plant grows, so bolting pressure keeps building, while autumn runs the opposite way. Second, it falls in a period when pest activity is declining. Third, the harvest flows straight into gimjang (making kimchi for the winter) and storage. Fourth, it fills the gap left in a field once summer crops are done.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Spring cropping is a race against time. Sowing has to happen the moment the ground thaws, and even a slight delay lets temperatures rise enough that the flower stalk rises before the leaves have spread out. In exchange, leaf mustard sown in spring sets seed that same summer. Treating a spring sowing as a way to get seed rather than food is one valid strategy: sow a little spring leaf mustard in a corner of the field and let it flower as it will, and it will yield several years' worth of seed by June or July.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Overwintering cropping works in the southern provinces and coastal areas of South Korea. Since the plant tolerates temperatures down to -5°C, if left unprotected in the central inland regions, where winter lows often drop below that, it freezes to death. A thick cover of straw and fallen leaves, or a windbreak, raises the success rate. Plastic is not required: piling dry grass generously over the plants creates an air layer inside that acts as insulation, and if snow falls, the snow itself becomes an insulating layer.

### 6-2. Sowing Method

| Item | Standard | Notes |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Sowing depth | 0.5-1cm | The seed is positively photoblastic, so burying it deep sharply cuts the germination rate |
| Row spacing | 15-20cm | Spacing chosen for airflow and ease of management |
| Plant spacing | 10-15cm final | Reached through thinning |
| Sowing method | Row sowing or hill sowing | Broadcast sowing is also possible if the goal is baby-leaf harvest |
| Seeding rate | 2-3kg per 1,000m² | |
| Covering soil | Just enough fine soil to cover the seed | Do not cover thickly |
| Firming | Press lightly with a hand or board | The seed must be in close contact with the soil to absorb water |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Here is the technique for handling positively photoblastic seed once more. Because germination is promoted by light exposure, the soil must not be covered thickly — but leaving it completely uncovered lets the seed dry out or wash away in rain. The compromise is 0.5-1cm. Pressing lightly after sowing matters because the seed must be in close contact with soil particles to draw water through capillary action; if there is an air gap between soil and seed, no amount of watering will reach it.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Not sowing everything at once is the key technique in growing leaf mustard, because staggered sowing is the only way to offset the weakness of perenniality C — the fact that the plant is done once harvested. Sowing in batches ten to fifteen days apart keeps the harvest coming ten to fifteen days apart as well. For autumn sowing, that might mean splitting it into three rounds: late August, early September, and mid-September.

### 6-3. Seed Treatment

| Treatment | Procedure | Effect |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Selection | Remove diseased or damaged seed and empty husks | Raises germination rate and early vigor |
| Water sorting | Soak in water for 3-4 hours and use only what sinks | Leaves only well-filled seed |
| Hot-water soaking | Soak in 50-55°C water for 10-15 minutes, then cool in cold water | Kills pathogens on and inside the seed |
| Ash treatment | Coat the seed with fine wood ash | Doubles as disinfection and drying, and keeps seeds from clumping |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Hot-water soaking is the most reliable seed disinfection method available without chemicals. It works on a difference in tolerance: the seed itself withstands 50-55°C for 10 to 15 minutes, while pathogens on its surface and inside die under those same conditions. Because this margin is narrow, temperature matters a great deal. Above 60°C the seed dies too, and below 45°C the pathogens survive.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Without a thermometer, you have to judge by feel. 50-55°C is hot enough that dipping your hand in stings, but you can still stand it for a few seconds. Mixing boiling water and cold water at roughly a 1:1 ratio gets you into this range. After treatment, always cool the seed in cold water and dry it in the shade. Left wet, germination starts and then the seed dries out and dies.

## 7. Water Management

### 7-1. Water by Growth Stage

| Stage | Management | Notes |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Sowing-germination | Light, frequent watering to keep the topsoil from drying out | Germination succeeds or fails right here |
| Seedling stage | Water when the topsoil dries | Shallow roots make it very vulnerable to drought |
| Rapid growth stage | Water thoroughly when the topsoil dries | Moisture at this stage determines leaf size |
| Just before harvest | Reduce somewhat | Firmer leaves are better for storage and pickling |
| Monsoon season | Ensure drainage channels | Root rot from waterlogging is the bigger risk |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The riskiest stretch in leaf mustard's water management is from sowing to germination. Once a seed has absorbed water and begun to swell, drying out kills it outright — a seed that has started absorbing water cannot go back. So for the first few days after sowing, everything comes down to keeping the soil surface from drying out. Watering often in small amounts is right, rather than a lot at once, and if water is scarce, a very thin covering of straw or dry grass can cut evaporation while still letting some light through.

### 7-2. When Water Is Too Little or Too Much

| Condition | Symptom | Cause | Outcome |
|:---|:---|:---|:---|
| Deficiency | Leaves wilt during the day and recover by evening | Temporary moisture shortage | Still recoverable |
| Deficiency | Leaves become small and thick, pungency intensifies | Sustained drought stress | Mustard-oil glycosides increase |
| Deficiency | Leaf edges dry out and the flower stalk rises | Severe drought | Difficult to recover |
| Excess | Lower leaves turn yellow | Root oxygen deficiency | Root function declines |
| Excess | The base softens and smells | Root rot progressing | That plant is hard to save |
| Excess | The stem grows leggy and leaves thin out | Excess moisture combined with insufficient sunlight | Becomes more vulnerable to disease |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The fact that drought stress intensifies pungency is also put to practical use in growing leaf mustard: give it plenty of water for tender leaves, or cut back on water starting a week before harvest for leaves with pungency intact for kimchi. This is a matter of control, though, not of neglect. If water runs seriously short, pungency does intensify, but the leaves themselves turn small and tough and yield drops.

### 7-3. Mulching

| Material | Effect | Caution |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Straw | Retains moisture, suppresses weeds, supplies organic matter as it decomposes | Do not apply right after sowing |
| Dry grass, fallen leaves | Same effect as straw. Easy to obtain | Grass with seed mixed in invites weeds |
| Rice hulls | Light and long-lasting | Temporarily ties up nitrogen |
| Wood ash | Combines pest deterrence with potassium supply | Too thick a layer makes it excessively alkaline |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Organic mulch suits leaf mustard well because of its root depth. A crop with shallowly spread roots experiences the full swing of the topsoil's temperature and moisture. When topsoil temperature exceeds 40°C on a summer day, the roots within it stop functioning. A single layer of straw lowers this temperature by several degrees and cuts water evaporation by nearly half. Without plastic mulch, that difference is not small.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;However, mixing carbon-rich materials like rice hulls or sawdust into the soil causes decomposing microbes to draw on the soil's nitrogen, creating a temporary nitrogen shortage. This is called nitrogen immobilization. Leaving the material simply on the surface causes little trouble, but tilling it in hits a crop like leaf mustard hard right away, since it needs to use a lot of nitrogen quickly. The rule is to lay rice hulls on top without mixing them in.

## 8. Crop Management

### 8-1. Thinning Determines the Yield

| Timing | Task | Spacing left |
|:---|:---|:---:|
| 2-3 true leaves | First thinning | 3-5cm |
| 4-5 true leaves | Second thinning | 10-15cm (final) |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In growing leaf mustard, thinning is not optional but essential, and it has the greatest influence on yield and quality. The reason is competition. Overcrowded leaf mustard stretches its stem upward as it competes with neighboring plants for light, spending on height the resources that should go into widening its leaves. The result is a thin, tall stem carrying a few small leaves. The same area ends up with more plants but, paradoxically, less total harvest weight.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The second problem with overcrowding is airflow. When leaves overlap and air cannot circulate, humidity stays high on the leaf surface, and this condition becomes a breeding ground for downy mildew and gray mold. A good share of the diseases that occur in Brassicaceae leafy vegetables start from the combination of overcrowding and excess moisture. Without chemicals, securing enough spacing is the most reliable way to prevent disease.

> **Thinned young leaf mustard is not waste — it is the first harvest. Baby leaves around day 20 after sowing are still mild and tender enough to eat as is, in wraps or a quick fresh salad.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;For this reason, thinning leaf mustard is different in character from thinning other crops. Thinning radish or napa cabbage produces a discarded portion, but with leaf mustard what is thinned out is food outright. Worked out on a schedule, that gives a first harvest (thinning) at day 20 after sowing, a second harvest (thinning) at day 30, and the main harvest following at day 45-60. In a disaster, these three harvest opportunities are not small.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;When thinning, do not pull the plants up; cut them close to the ground with scissors or a fingernail. Pulling disturbs and damages the shallow roots of neighboring plants as well. Cutting instead leaves the remaining root to rot in the ground and become organic matter.

### 8-2. Hilling and Weeds

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Leaf mustard is a crop that needs almost no hilling. It does not grow its harvest underground like potato, nor is there any reason to lengthen a blanched stem base as with Welsh onion. Its growing period is short enough that harvest finishes before the plant grows large enough to topple over. The exception is overwintering cultivation: for plants that must get through winter, mounding a little soil at the base stabilizes the temperature around the roots and reduces heaving from frost.

| Weed control | Method | Timing |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Hand weeding | Pull weeds between plants | Along with thinning |
| Mulching | Cover with straw, dry grass, or fallen leaves | After 3-4 true leaves |
| Dense sowing then thinning | Let leaf mustard cover the ground first | Early stage |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The fight against weeds is concentrated in the first three weeks. Once leaf mustard's leaves cover the ground, the shade they cast keeps weeds from coming up easily. So the period from sowing until five or six true leaves have appeared is where the battle is decided. Fail to control weeds in this window, and they take the ground first, leaving shallow-rooted leaf mustard to lose the competition for nutrients and water.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;When pulling weeds, take care not to disturb leaf mustard's root zone. Most of its roots sit within 10-15cm of the surface, so pushing a hoe in deep will cut them. The trick is to scrape shallowly, starting from the side farther from the plant.

### 8-3. Managing to Delay Bolting

| Factor | Effect on bolting | Response |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Cold followed by long days | The strongest signal inducing the flower stalk | Move spring sowing earlier and hasten harvest |
| Heat and drought | Triggers stress-induced bolting | Stabilize soil temperature and moisture with mulching and watering |
| Nitrogen deficiency | Speeds the shift to reproductive growth | Apply enough base fertilizer |
| Overcrowding | Competition stress speeds up bolting | Thin on time |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Bolting is not a disease but a normal part of the plant's life cycle; it simply conflicts with the grower's purpose. Leaf mustard is grown for its leaves, but once the flower stalk rises, the plant redirects all its resources to flowers and seed, so the leaves toughen and only bitterness and pungency remain. A plant that has begun bolting cannot be reversed, and even if the flower stalk is cut off, another rises from beside it.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;So the essence of managing bolting is not delaying it but choosing the timing. The only reliable method is to time sowing so that harvest is finished before leaf mustard sends up its flower stalk. This is why autumn cropping is the default cropping type in a disaster: with temperatures moving downward, the signal for bolting never arises.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Still, there is no need to throw out leaf mustard entirely once it has bolted. A young flower stalk still in bud is tender and edible, tasting something like rapeseed greens or broccoli. In spring, when stored food is running out and new vegetables have not yet come in, this flower stalk earns its keep. Keeping only the plants meant for seed and picking the rest of the flower stalks to eat is the way to make the most of the resource.
