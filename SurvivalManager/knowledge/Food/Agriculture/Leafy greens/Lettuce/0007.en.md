## 15. Storage Without Refrigeration

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The evaluation table gives Storability an E, with 2-3 days at room temperature without power, the lowest value among all the crops this document covers. This section is not a place to present ways of overturning that fact. Lettuce does not store. The purpose of this section is to distinguish precisely what is possible from what is impossible.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Three things combine to produce this result. First, the water content is above 95%, and the leaves are thin and broad, so the surface area relative to volume is large and moisture evaporates quickly. Second, what holds the tissue up is mainly the water pressure inside the cells, so the leaf wilts right away when even a little water is lost. There is no structure like that of napa cabbage, where thick leaf veins hold the shape. Third, latex flows from cut surfaces, and the spot browns and becomes the starting point of rot. So the whole of the technique for keeping lettuce going for a few days comes down to two things: not letting it lose water, and lowering the temperature to slow respiration.

| Method | Procedure | Duration | Limitation |
|:---|:---|:---:|:---|
| Stem in water | Cut 1cm off the base and stand the plant in a container with shallow water | 3-5 days | The water must be changed daily and it takes up space |
| Wrapping in a wet cloth | Wrap unwashed in a wet cloth and keep in a cool place | 3-4 days | The effect disappears once the cloth dries |
| Burying in wet straw | Lay the leaves flat in layers between wet straw | 3-5 days | Dirty straw brings mold |
| Earth cellar or root-cellar pit | Keep in a cool underground space together with a wet cloth | 5-7 days | Requires a root-cellar pit |
| Sealing in an earthenware jar | Place in a jar lined with a wet cloth and keep in the shade | 3-5 days | Without ventilation it turns soft |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The principle of keeping stems in water is simple. The cut vessels keep drawing water up and maintain turgor in the leaves. However, bacteria grow on the submerged cut surface and clog the vessels, so changing the water daily and re-cutting a few mm off the base each time makes it last longer. The rule of storing unwashed is also important. Washing leaves a film of water on the leaf surface, and under it decay organisms multiply quickly. The cuticle layer and wax on the leaf surface are lettuce's own protective barrier, and part of it can be stripped off during washing.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The temperature of an earth cellar or root-cellar pit stays between 10-15°C all year at 1.5-2m underground. Even in summer it is more than 10°C lower than outside. Even this much noticeably slows the respiration rate, so two days become five. However, the humidity inside a pit is high and mold grows easily, so do not block ventilation completely, and do not let spoiled leaves get mixed in. The ethylene given off by a single spoiled leaf ages the sound leaves next to it.

| Processing for storage | Procedure | Duration | Assessment |
|:---|:---|:---:|:---|
| Blanching and drying | Blanch in boiling water for 20-30 seconds and dry in the shade | 6 months-1 year | Possible, but the volume shrinks extremely and the texture collapses |
| Drying as is | Spread out unwashed to dry | A few months | Browns and crumbles |
| Mulgimchi (water kimchi) | Soak in 2-3% salt water and ferment | 1-2 weeks | Only in a cool place. The leaves turn soft |
| Jjanji-style pickling (jjanji: heavily salted pickle) | Salt at 10% or more | A few months | The shape collapses completely and it is used only as soup material |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Blanching and drying is physically possible, but the result is not satisfactory. Since 95% is water, drying 1kg leaves less than 50g, and even that is thin tissue that crumbles. Even after soaking, the original texture does not return. It is more accurate to think of it as a last resort for not throwing away lettuce that has started bolting in summer and has to be dealt with all at once.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The limits of salt pickling come from the structure. In napa cabbage and radish, the cell walls keep their shape even when salted, but lettuce goes limp and floppy the moment it is salted. Even made into kimchi, no crunch remains and it turns soft and sour within a few days. This is why lettuce mulgimchi exists only as a dish eaten within a few days of making.

> **The way to solve lettuce's storage problem lies not in storage technique but in the sowing plan. Sow in batches at intervals of ten to fifteen days and pick only the outer leaves, and there is no need to store anything in the first place. Lettuce standing in the field is the best storehouse.**

## 16. Cooking and Processing

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The Cooking & processing grade in the evaluation table is SS, and its basis is one short phrase: "eaten raw after washing." It is worth weighing precisely how much this short phrase is worth in a disaster.

| Crop | Processing needed before eating | Fuel |
|:---|:---|:---:|
| Lettuce | Washing | None |
| Soybean | Soaking for 8 hours and boiling for 1 hour or more | A lot |
| Potato | Boiling or baking for 20-30 minutes | Medium |
| Rice | 30 minutes of cooking | Medium |
| Swiss chard | 5 minutes of blanching | A little |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;When electricity and gas are cut off, cooking fuel is firewood or charcoal, and that is something people must cut, haul, dry and split. The amount of wood needed to boil three meals a day is more than one would expect, and near cities the dry wood in the surrounding area runs out in a few months. In such conditions, having a vegetable in the field that can be eaten without using fire at all is the kind of advantage that does not show up in a calorie table.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;A nutritional advantage is layered on top of this. Vitamin C, folate and some of the B vitamins are heat-labile and water-soluble, so when blanched a considerable share disappears and drains into the cooking water. Lettuce has no such loss. It is one of the few crops for which the evaluation of Micronutrients S, meeting 16 of 21, translates almost directly into actual intake.

| Style | Tips | Notes |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Ssam (leaf wraps) | Use whole leaves to wrap rice and side dishes and eat | The simplest way to break the monotony of a grain-centered diet |
| Geotjeori (fresh-salted side dish) | Cut coarsely, salt briefly, then toss with seasoning | The leaves wilt and shrink in volume, so you end up eating more |
| Salad | Mix in oil or ground perilla seeds | Raises absorption of fat-soluble vitamins |
| Mulgimchi | Soak in 2-3% salt water and leave for a day or two | Lactic fermentation preserves a good part of the vitamin C |
| Blanching | 20-30 seconds in boiling water | Bitterness leaches into the water and the texture softens |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Geotjeori is especially practical in a disaster. Raw leaves are bulky, so there is a limit to how much can be eaten at one time, but lightly salted, they wilt and the same amount goes down much more easily. Since lettuce's greatest weakness is its calorie density, this simple treatment that raises the amount actually eaten makes a difference in nutrition supply. If salt is scarce, salt briefly with a very small amount and use the brine in soup. Mixing in even a little oil is also advantageous. Vitamin A, vitamin E and vitamin K are fat-soluble, so their absorption drops sharply if eaten without oil, and sprinkling on ground perilla or sesame seeds gives the same effect.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There is also a way to eat it cooked. Cooking lettuce is unfamiliar, but when bolting has begun and the leaves have turned tough and bitter, this is actually the better option. Blanching in boiling water for 20 to 30 seconds leaches a good part of the bitter compounds into the water and softens the texture. Added at the very end to the fire that is already boiling soup, it uses no separate fuel.

### 16-1. Using the Lettuce Stalk

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;It is a waste to simply pull out lettuce whose flower stalk has risen. The stem of bolted lettuce, the lettuce stalk, can itself be eaten. In China, varieties grown specifically for the stem are cultivated, and in South Korea too, lettuce stems have long been used as namul (seasoned vegetable side dish) and jangajji (pickled vegetables in soy sauce or paste).

| Part | Treatment | Taste and use |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Thick stem | Peel, slice on the diagonal, soak in salt water to remove the bitterness, then stir-fry or toss with seasoning | Crisp and mild |
| Stem skin | Tough and holds concentrated latex, so peel it off | Return it to compost |
| Flower buds | Blanch buds that have not opened yet and toss with seasoning | Pleasantly bitter |
| Stem jangajji | Slice, salt, then bury in doenjang (Korean soybean paste) or soy sauce | Keeps for several months |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The trick is to always peel off the skin. The outer skin holds the most lactucarium and its fibers are also tough. Once peeled, the flesh is actually less bitter and crisp. After slicing, soaking in salt water or rice-rinsing water for about 30 minutes removes more of the remaining bitterness. Made into jangajji, it keeps for several months. In effect, a storage form that can never be made from the leaves becomes possible with the stems. The side-shoot stems that come from seed plants can be used the same way, so combining seed saving with stem harvest reduces the opportunity cost of seed plants that tie up their spot for four months.

## 17. Annual Work Calendar

| Month (South Korea) | Spring crop | Autumn crop |
|:---:|:---|:---|
| January | Check seed stock, manage compost, plan crop rotation | Field empty |
| February | Check germination rate, turn compost, prepare base fertilizer | |
| March | Sowing in late March. Base fertilizer and raised beds | |
| April | Thinning and transplanting. Harvest of young leaves. Watch for slugs | |
| May | Full harvest, cut-and-come-again harvest every 10-14 days. Top-dressing | |
| June | Bolting starts. Mark seed plants, then clear the rest | |
| July | Seed plants flower and set seed. Summer crops in the empty spots | |
| August | Seed harvest and cleaning in early August | Sowing from early August. Countering high-temperature dormancy |
| September | | Thinning and transplanting, second sowing. Watch for downy mildew |
| October | | Full harvest. The month with the longest cut-and-come-again harvest |
| November | | Harvest continues. Straw cover and windbreak. Clearing in late November |
| December | | Field cleanup and crop residue disposal. Diseased plants go out of the field |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Reading this table vertically reveals the character of lettuce. The spring crop has a short harvest period, and its end is closed by bolting. It harvests well in May, then ends abruptly in June as temperatures rise. In exchange, only the spring crop can leave seed. From sowing to seed takes four months, and only in spring is that time available.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The autumn crop is the opposite. Temperatures fall throughout the growing period, so there is no bolting pressure, and cut-and-come-again harvest continues for more than two months until frost arrives. The leaves are thick and gain sweetness. However, seed cannot be saved.

| Category | Spring crop | Autumn crop |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Harvest period | 4-6 weeks | 8-10 weeks |
| Reason it ends | Bolting | Frost |
| Leaf quality | Increasingly bitter toward the end | Tender to the end, and sweetness develops |
| Disease pressure | Low, then rises late | Heavy downy mildew pressure |
| Seed saving | Possible | Difficult |
| Germination conditions | Easy | Needs countering of high-temperature dormancy |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;So the actual arrangement becomes this. In spring you plant to secure seed, and in autumn you plant to eat. The two summer months and the three winter months between the two cropping seasons are a period with no lettuce, and other crops fill that gap. In summer, leafy vegetables that tolerate heat, such as purslane and water spinach, and in winter, crops that withstand cold, such as kale and leaf mustard, take that place.

> **Of the twelve months of the year, lettuce reaches the table for only a little over four. Knowing this and planning what to fill the other eight months with matters more in a disaster than the skill of growing lettuce well.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Finally, let us return to the principle of staggering sowing dates. Lettuce does not store, so if everything is sown at once, some part is bound to be wasted. Sowing in three or four batches at intervals of ten to fifteen days spreads the harvest out by the same amount, and even if one cropping fails from disease, the next sowing remains. For a crop that cannot be stored, staggered sowing is not a convenience but a way of spreading risk.
