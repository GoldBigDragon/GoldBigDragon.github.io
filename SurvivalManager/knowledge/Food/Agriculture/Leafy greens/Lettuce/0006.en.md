## 13. Seed Saving - The Advantage of Self-Pollination

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The Seed saving grade in the evaluation table is S, and the Basis column carries a short phrase: "self-pollinating, pure line maintained." This one line explains almost everything about saving lettuce seed. Where kale calls for a 500m isolation distance and several plants flowering at the same time, and radish calls for 1km, lettuce can give seed from a single plant left standing, and that seed grows into the same lettuce.

### 13-1. Why the Varieties Do Not Mix

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The lettuce flower is the head-type flower (capitulum) typical of the daisy family (Asteraceae). Ten to twenty small florets gather into one yellow flower head, and the whole head opens for only an hour or two in the morning before closing again. What matters is the timing of that opening. In lettuce, before the flower opens, while the bud is still closed, the stamens have already released their pollen and the pistil beneath takes it up. By the time the flower opens, fertilization is already over. So even when bees visit, the genes hardly mix. The measured cross-pollination rate is between 0 and 5% depending on conditions, and usually around 1%.

| Crop | Pollination mode | Practical isolation distance | Minimum number of seed plants |
|:---|:---|:---:|:---:|
| Lettuce | Self-pollinating (close to cleistogamy) | 1-2m | Even 1 plant is enough |
| Kale, cabbage | Cross-pollinating (insect-pollinated), self-incompatible | 500m or more | 5-10 plants |
| Radish | Cross-pollinating (insect-pollinated), self-incompatible | 1,000m or more | 10 or more plants |
| Swiss chard, beet | Cross-pollinating (wind-pollinated) | 1,000m or more | 10 or more plants |

> **The isolation needed for saving lettuce seed is 1-2m, just enough that the flower stalks of different varieties do not touch each other. This means seed of several varieties can be saved in the same year even in a small field.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The rate is not a perfect 0%, so to keep a variety going for many years you add two more steps: placing a row of another crop between the seed plants to widen the gap, and pulling out any plant among the following year's seedlings that differs from the variety's traits. Repeating this for a few years actually raises purity.

### 13-2. From Bolting to Seed: The Timeline

| Stage | After sowing | State |
|:---|:---:|:---|
| Leaf harvest | 40-60 days | The stage for eating |
| Start of bolting | 60-80 days | The center rises and the stem shoots up |
| Flower stalk complete | 80-100 days | 60-120cm tall, branching |
| Flowering | 90-110 days | Opens in turn from the bottom upward over 20-30 days |
| Seed maturity | 12-24 days after flowering | Counted flower by flower |
| Seed harvest | 120-150 days after sowing | The pappus begins to burst open |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The line to read here is the last one. From sowing to seed takes four to five months. In South Korea, spring lettuce sown in late March gives seed from late July to August, whereas with an autumn crop the frost comes first and the seed cannot ripen. Seed saving is therefore done on the spring crop. Autumn lettuce is for eating, and a few of the spring lettuce plants are for seed.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This timetable also affects the field layout. The spot where a seed plant stands is tied up for more than four months, and flower stalks that grow over 1m tall cast shade on neighboring crops. It makes sense to gather the seed plants where shade is not a problem, such as the edge of the field or its northern end.

### 13-3. The Battle with the Pappus

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Lettuce seed has the same structure as a dandelion seed. At the tip of each seed sits a pappus, a tuft of white down that, once spread out, carries the seed away on the wind. Lettuce belongs to the same family (Asteraceae) and so uses the same strategy. The problem is that the pappus is both the sign that the seed is ripe and the very moment the seed is lost. About ten to twenty days after the flower fades, the closed calyx opens and a white ball of fluff swells up. Leave it one or two more days and it scatters. On top of that, the flowers open and fade in turn from the bottom, so the ripening within a single plant is spread over more than three weeks. If you wait for everything to ripen, you lose what ripened first.

| Method | Procedure | Characteristics |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Stripping in rounds | Every 2-3 days, strip by hand only the flowers whose fluff has swelled and collect them | Highest recovery rate. Takes a lot of labor |
| Bagging | Put a paper bag or cloth bag over the flower stalk and tie it below | Seed that blows away stays inside the bag |
| Cutting whole | When more than half of the flower heads have swelled, cut the whole stalk | Finished in one go, but unripe seed gets mixed in |
| Spreading a cloth | Spread a cloth or a meongseok (straw drying mat) under the flower stalks | A supplementary measure. Effective only on windless days |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Bagging gives the best balance between labor and recovery. Use a breathable paper bag or a loosely woven cloth bag, and avoid plastic. Inside plastic, moisture builds up and the seeds rot or sprout in the bag. The time to put the bag on is just before the flower fades and the fluff becomes visible; bagged too early, it molds. Stalks cut whole are hung upside down over a sack or cloth and left to after-ripen for a week to ten days, and a good number of the seeds that looked underripe also fill out on the nutrients remaining in the stalk.

### 13-4. Cleaning and Yield

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;What you shake out is a mixture of seed, pappus and bits of calyx. The pappus is much lighter than the seed, so wind separates them. On a windy day, set out two wide containers and pour slowly from a height: the heavy seeds fall straight down and the fluff blows away. Rubbing the material in your hands first to break off the pappus makes the separation work better.

| Item | Value |
|:---|:---|
| Seeds per plant | 3,000-10,000 |
| Weight of 1,000 seeds | about 0.8-1.2g |
| Amount needed to sow 1m² | 0.3-0.5g |
| Seed longevity | 2-3 years sealed at room temperature; 3-5 years if cool and dry |
| Practical minimum germination rate | 70% |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The important fact is that a single plant yields thousands of seeds. The lettuce seed a family uses in a year is a few grams even when generously reckoned, which is the amount from one or two seed plants. Seed saving does not mean giving up a large part of the field. Mark the spring lettuce plants that have large leaves, no disease and flower stalks that rose latest, and simply leave them standing.

> **Choose the seed plants from among those whose flower stalks rose latest. The late-bolting trait, that is, the ability to hold out in the heat for longer, is inherited. Repeating selection on this basis for a few years makes the lettuce in that field a little more heat-tolerant each year.**

### 13-5. Storage and High-Temperature Dormancy

| Item | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Dryness | Until it crumbles when rubbed in the hand and snaps with a crack when pressed between the teeth |
| Container | Glass jar, earthenware jar, airtight metal tin |
| Desiccant | Roasted and cooled rice, charcoal, dry wood ash |
| Storage place | Cool, dark and not damp. Root-cellar pit, north-facing storeroom, basement |
| Checking | Each year, place 20 seeds on a wet cloth and count how many have germinated a few days later |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There is one special point to watch in storing lettuce seed. Freshly harvested seed in fact shows strong high-temperature dormancy. Freshly matured seed has not yet finished physiological after-ripening, so germination-inhibiting substances remain in it, and these raise its sensitivity to temperature. As a result, seed from the year of harvest and early the following year stops germinating at lower temperatures than older seed does.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;What this means in practice is clear. Autumn sowing in August comes when soil temperature is already high, a head-on collision with high-temperature dormancy, and the seed you happen to use then is the new seed collected a few months earlier. When the two conditions coincide, the germination rate drops sharply. Because the seed lasts two to three years, keeping two years' worth on hand and using the older seed first resolves the conflict naturally. If new seed must be used right away, hasten its after-ripening by keeping it completely dry in a dry place at 30-40°C for a few days. Heating moist seed kills it, so always do this only after the seed is bone dry.

## 14. Toxicity and Compounds of Concern

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Lettuce has no acute toxicity. It is a vegetable eaten raw after washing, and the SS for Cooking & processing in the evaluation table reflects that fact. Still, it is worth knowing about one compound specific to lettuce and one problem common to leafy vegetables in general.

| Compound | What it is | What effect it has | When it becomes a problem |
|:---|:---|:---|:---|
| Lactucarium (lactucin, lactucopicrin) | The bitter compounds in the white latex that flows when the stem is cut | Mild sedative effect and bitter taste | Rises sharply after bolting, making the leaves hard to eat |
| Nitrate | The form in which nitrogen absorbed by the roots is stored in the leaves | A problem if eaten in excess | Shade, low light, excess nitrogen |
| Oxalic acid | An organic acid that binds with calcium | Risk of stones if eaten in large amounts | Lettuce has a low content, so it is not a practical problem |

### 14-1. Lactucarium

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Cut a lettuce stem or leaf vein and white sap seeps out. This latex, which hardens to brown as it dries, is lactucarium, and the scientific name Lactuca comes from it. Its original purpose is defense. It gives a bitter taste to whatever gnaws the leaf and seals the wound. Its main constituents are the sesquiterpene lactones called lactucin and lactucopicrin, which produce mild sedative and pain-relieving effects in people. There are records from 19th-century Europe of preparations made by hardening lettuce latex being used as a sedative, and the common belief that eating lettuce makes you sleepy also arose from this. However, the amount in the leaves eaten at the table falls far short of a level at which that effect would be felt.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;What matters in practice is not the medicinal effect but the bitterness. The concentration varies greatly with the growth stage.

| Stage | Latex concentration | Taste |
|:---|:---:|:---|
| Young leaves | Very low | Tender, with a hint of sweetness |
| Mature leaves (before bolting) | Low | Pleasant to eat |
| Just after bolting | Rises sharply | Bitter aftertaste |
| Flowering | Very high | Hard to eat |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The answer to why bitterness rises along with bolting lies in a switch in the plant's priorities. The moment the flower stalk rises, the lettuce's goal changes from growing leaves to making seed. From then on the leaves become a nutrient source for the seed, and the plant increases its production of defensive compounds to protect that asset. At the same time lignin builds up in the leaf cells and the leaves toughen. This is why bitterness and toughness arrive together. Drought stress and high temperature work in the same direction: when water runs short, defensive compounds increase even before bolting. This is why lettuce that was not watered on time in summer is unusually bitter, and why autumn lettuce, grown in cool conditions with even moisture, has almost no bitterness.

> **Bitterness is not a sign of toxicity but a sign that the time has passed. It does no harm to eat, but it becomes hard to keep eating, and that point is where the harvest of that plant ends.**

### 14-2. Nitrate

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Among leafy vegetables, lettuce is relatively prone to accumulating nitrate. Nitrogen taken up by the roots rises as nitrate and is stored in the leaf vacuoles, and then, when light is present, enzymes convert it into amino acids and proteins. This conversion requires the energy that photosynthesis produces. So when light is short, the nitrate is not converted and stays in the leaf as it is.

| Condition | Nitrate accumulation |
|:---|:---|
| Excess nitrogen fertilization | Increases greatly |
| A run of cloudy days | Increases |
| Growing in shade | Increases |
| Harvest in early morning | High, because conversion stops overnight |
| Harvest in the afternoon of a clear day | Low, because conversion has taken place during the day |
| Outer leaves and petioles | Higher than inner leaves |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;One thing needs to be made clear here. Eating normally grown lettuce in ordinary amounts is not a problem. Nitrate itself is not a poison, a considerable part of the nitrate people eat naturally comes from vegetables, and the current general understanding is that the benefits of eating vegetables far outweigh that burden. What should cause concern is a particular combination: lettuce grown in a field given too much nitrogen fertilizer, in a spot with poor sunlight, and eaten daily in large quantities without other vegetables.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In a disaster this combination is formed more easily than one would expect. Diluted urine applied often concentrates nitrogen, lettuce ends up grown in places with limited sunlight such as the shade of a building or a small yard, and the diet becomes lopsided because lettuce is the only vegetable to eat. The response is to keep the three conditions from overlapping. Stop nitrogen sources a few days before harvest, give sunny spots to lettuce first, harvest in the afternoon, and alternate with other leafy vegetables. Avoid drinking juice made by grinding leaves as if it were a staple, since the concentration rises.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Oxalic acid is not a practical problem in lettuce. Where Swiss chard and spinach hold several hundred mg per 100g, lettuce holds only a few tens of mg. This is one reason it can be eaten raw without blanching. A more frequent risk in practice is not a compound but hygiene. Because lettuce is eaten raw, whatever is on the soil goes straight into the mouth. Lettuce grown in a field where excrement was applied without enough fermentation, or on land a flood has passed over, is dangerous to eat raw. This is the point where lettuce's greatest strength, raw eating, becomes its greatest weakness at the same time, and the response is to compost thoroughly until fully rotted.
