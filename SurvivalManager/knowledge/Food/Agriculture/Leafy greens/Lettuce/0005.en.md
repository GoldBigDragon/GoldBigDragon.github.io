## 9. Pests and Diseases - Protection Without Pesticides

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Lettuce's Pest & disease pressure grade is C, with an estimated yield loss without pest control of 50.6%. Pest-control self-reliance is B, but the evaluation basis carries a heavy caveat. The empirical papers on organic lettuce tested mixed planting of varieties against downy mildew, yet mulching alone could not provide commercial-level control, and at moderate infestation losses were at least 10% and returns fell by more than 25%. This means growing lettuce without pesticides is no easy task.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Lettuce does have one advantage, though. Because it belongs to the daisy family, the pests of cabbage-family crops do not attack it. The cabbage white butterfly, diamondback moth, flea beetles, and clubroot leave lettuce alone. When a field is crowded with cabbage-family crops, lettuce stands outside that pressure. Lettuce's problem is not insects but fungi.

### 9-1. Major Diseases

| Disease | Symptoms | Conditions favoring it | Response without pesticides |
|:---|:---|:---|:---|
| Sclerotinia rot | The base turns soft and is covered with white mycelium, and black grains like fly specks appear | Cool and humid conditions, continuous cropping of daisy-family plants | Deep tillage, mulching, 2-3 year rotation, complete removal of infected plants |
| Downy mildew | Yellow-brown spots on the upper surface of the leaf and white mold on the underside | When cool and damp, when leaves stay wet for long | Ensure ventilation, morning watering, mixed planting of varieties, remove diseased leaves |
| Gray mold | Brown spots appear and are covered with gray mold | Cool and humid conditions, poor ventilation | Manage humidity, secure spacing, remove diseased parts |
| Bacterial leaf spot | Dark brown spots appear on the leaf margins, spread, and the tissue dies and dries | Hot and humid conditions, wounds | Lower humidity and ensure ventilation |
| Bottom rot | The crown area turns brown and the plant falls over | Excess moisture, soil covering the base | Drainage; keep the area around the base clear |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Sclerotinia rot is the most serious disease of lettuce. Other diseases stop at reducing that year's harvest, but sclerotinia rot contaminates the field. The mycelium growing from the base of an infected plant forms hard black lumps, and these are the sclerotia. Sclerotia fall into the soil and survive for several years, and when conditions are right they germinate again and infect new plants.

> **If you find a plant infected with sclerotinia rot, dig it up together with the soil around the roots and carry it out of the field. Do not put it on the compost pile. Sclerotia survive the fermentation heat of compost and spread across the whole field.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The Continuous-cropping tolerance B and the 2-3 year rotation break in the evaluation table are precisely because of these sclerotia. On a field where sclerotia have already built up, you must avoid not only lettuce but also daisy-family crops such as garland chrysanthemum, chicory, and burdock. Plant crops of the grass family (Poaceae) or the legume family (Fabaceae) for a few years instead. The reason a field with heavy sclerotia needs a 3-5 year rotation with grass-family crops is the same.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Downy mildew spreads by airborne spores, so it cannot be stopped completely. But its conditions are clear: it gets worse the longer the leaves stay wet. So the response is also clear. Water in the early morning so the leaves dry during the day, thin the plants to widen spacing and let air through, and avoid crowding and excess moisture. Planting a mix of varieties can slow the spread across the whole field of a pathogen strain to which one line is vulnerable.

### 9-2. Major Insect Pests

| Pest | Damage | Control without pesticides |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Aphids | Suck sap from the undersides of leaves and spread viruses | Spraying soapy water, protecting ladybugs and aphid parasitoid wasps, silver reflective material |
| Thrips | Scrape the leaves and suck the sap, leaving silvery-white marks | Yellow sticky traps, removing weeds |
| Moth caterpillars | Chew the leaves | Hand-picking in the early morning, insect netting |
| Slugs | Make holes in the leaves and leave mucus trails | Hand-picking at night, bands of ash or rice hulls |
| Root mites | Gnaw the roots, so the plant wilts | Crop rotation, better drainage |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Slugs are an especially troublesome presence on lettuce, because its leaves are close to the ground and tender. They hide under straw or beneath stones by day and come out at night to chew, so they cannot be seen even if you look for them in the daytime. Going out at night with a lantern and picking them off is the most reliable method, and if you lay a band of dry wood ash, rice hulls, or eggshell around the plant, the slugs shun drying out their bodies and do not cross it. However, the effect disappears when it rains, so the band must be laid again.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The awkward point is that mulch becomes a hiding place for slugs. Mulching blocks rain splash and reduces fungal disease, but at the same time it draws in slugs. In fields with heavy slug damage, a compromise is to use a coarse, dry material such as rice hulls in place of straw, or to leave the ground right next to the plants bare and cover only the area a little farther away.

### 9-3. Natural Control Agents

| Control agent | Preparation | Target |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Soapy water | Dissolve 20g of unscented soap in 1L of water | Aphids, mites |
| Plant-ash solution | Soak plant ash in water for 24 hours, then strain and use | Aphids |
| Wood-ash band | Scatter dry ash around the plant | Blocks slugs |
| Yellow sticky trap | Coat a yellow board with something sticky and stand it up | Thrips, winged aphids |
| Wood vinegar | Dilute 300-500 times and spray | Repellent effect, disease suppression |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;When using soapy water on lettuce, you need to be more careful than with other crops. The leaves are thin and tender, so they are damaged even if the concentration is only slightly high. Do not exceed 20g per 1L, and rinsing once with clean water a few hours after spraying can reduce leaf damage. Avoid midday and spray in the early morning or after sunset.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The way yellow attracts flying insects is used to monitor thrips and aphids. If you coat a yellow board with something sticky and stand it in the field, you can tell which insects arrive and from when. When no pesticides are available, the first step of control is knowing what arrives and when, and this simple device serves that role.

### 9-4. Principles of Cultural Control

| Principle | Details |
|:---|:---|
| Crop rotation | Rest the field from daisy-family crops for 2-3 years, or longer if sclerotinia rot is present |
| Ventilation | Secure spacing by thinning. The cheapest means of control in lettuce |
| Drainage | Build raised beds and keep water from pooling |
| Watering time | Water in the early morning so the leaves dry during the day |
| Mulching | Blocks rain splash and cuts the route of soil-borne pathogens |
| Nitrogen management | Too much makes the leaves soft and draws aphids |
| Residue disposal | Dig up diseased plants with the soil and burn them or bury them far away |
| Avoiding hot periods | Avoiding summer cropping is itself a form of control |
| Mixed planting of varieties | Mixing varieties with different resistance prevents total loss |

## 10. Nutrient Deficiency and Excess

| Symptom | Cause | Remedy with natural materials |
|:---|:---|:---|
| The whole leaf turns yellow and the plant is small | Nitrogen deficiency | Apply well-rotted compost, or give urine diluted 15-20 times |
| Leaf margins dry out brown | Potassium deficiency | Scatter a small amount of wood ash |
| Growth is poor and leaves take on a purplish tint | Phosphate deficiency | Add bone meal or wood ash. The same symptom appears when soil temperature is low |
| Tips of the inner leaves dry out brown | Calcium deficiency or uneven moisture | Eggshell powder, even watering |
| Yellowing between the leaf veins | Magnesium deficiency | Increase compost |
| Leaves are excessively soft and tender | Nitrogen excess | Stop supplying nitrogen |
| Leaves are dark green and strongly bitter | Nitrogen excess or drought stress | Tell the cause apart and respond accordingly |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The deficiency symptom characteristic of lettuce is the tips of the inner leaves scorching brown. It is called tipburn, and it occurs when calcium fails to reach the tips of newly growing leaves. It more often results from poor movement of water than from a lack of calcium in the soil. Calcium is an element that moves with water, and the inner leaves are shaded by the outer leaves, transpire little, and so have a weak flow of water.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;So the response to tipburn lies less in adding calcium than in keeping moisture even and keeping humidity from getting too high. It gets worse when the inner leaves are shut in by crowding, so securing spacing also helps. Crushing eggshell finely and working it in is meaningful as long-term calcium supplementation, but an immediate effect should not be expected.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Nitrogen excess is a problem to guard against especially in lettuce. The leaves are large and deep green, so the plant looks as if it is growing well, but the tissue is soft and the cell walls are thin, so it becomes vulnerable to fungal disease and draws aphids. Moreover, lettuce is eaten raw, so the nitrate accumulated in the leaves goes straight into the mouth. The rule is not to give nitrogen late, and especially not in the few days before harvest.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The causes of bitterness should be divided into two branches. One is that lactucarium has increased because of bolting and drought stress; the other is that the tissue has changed because of nitrogen excess. The former is handled by managing water and temperature, the latter by adjusting fertilization. You can tell them apart by checking whether a flower stalk is visible, whether the soil is dry, and whether the leaf color is excessively dark.

## 11. Intercropping and Crop Rotation

| Compatibility | Companion | Effect |
|:---:|:---|:---|
| Good | Legume-family crops (pea, kidney bean, cowpea) | Fix nitrogen and leave it behind |
| Good | Small scallion, Welsh onion, carrot | The smell confuses pests |
| Good | Radish, napa cabbage, and other cabbage-family crops | They share no pests or diseases, and their rooting depth differs |
| Good | Tall crops such as maize and sunflower | Cast shade and delay bolting |
| Bad | Daisy-family crops such as garland chrysanthemum, chicory, and burdock | They share sclerotinia rot and downy mildew |
| Bad | Continuous cropping of lettuce | Sclerotia accumulate |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Planting lettuce and radish mixed together is efficient in its use of space. Lettuce has shallow roots that spread near the surface and vacates its spot in 40-60 days, while radish sends its root deep and lasts 70 to 90 days. While the radish leaves are still small, the lettuce between them covers the ground and holds back weeds, and by the time the lettuce has all been harvested, the radish has grown and takes up the space. You get two crops from the same area. The fact that they share no pests or diseases is also an advantage.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Placing lettuce on the north side of tall crops is a way to get through the summer. Maize or sunflowers block the strong afternoon sun, soil temperature falls by a few degrees, and bolting is delayed by that much. However, if it is too dark the lettuce grows leggy, so keep a distance that gives partial shade rather than full shade.

| Example rotation sequence | Year | Crop | What happens in the field |
|:---|:---:|:---|:---|
| 4-year cycle | Year 1 | Daisy-family crops such as lettuce | The risk of sclerotinia rot begins to build |
| | Year 2 | Legume family (soybean, pea, cowpea) | Nitrogen is fixed and accumulates |
| | Year 3 | Grass-family crops such as maize and sorghum | Daisy-family pathogens starve |
| | Year 4 | Cabbage-family crops such as radish and napa cabbage | Deep roots break up the soil |
| | Year 1 again | Lettuce | Starts with a lowered sclerotia density |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Putting grass-family crops after daisy-family crops is especially effective. The sclerotinia pathogen has a wide host range but does not infect grasses well. Rice, maize, sorghum, wheat, and barley fall into this group. This is the basis for the 3-5 year rotation with grasses needed in a field where sclerotia have already built up.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;If the field is too small for rotation, simply moving the planting spot makes a difference at the very least. Sclerotia do not move through the soil, so just swapping this year's spot with the spot where lettuce was planted last year reduces the chance of infection. If you grow it in pots or boxes, replacing the soil is the most reliable method.

## 12. Harvest - How Cut-and-Come-Again Harvesting Works

### 12-1. When to Harvest

| Purpose | After sowing | Condition |
|:---|:---:|:---|
| Young leaves (salad, ssam leaf wraps) | 25-30 days | Tender young leaves. Combined with thinning |
| Leaf-picking harvest | 30-40 days | Outer leaves 15-20cm long |
| Whole-plant harvest | 40-60 days | Fully grown |
| For seed saving | 60-100 days | The flower stalk rises and the seeds ripen |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The Speed to harvest of S in the evaluation table is set at 50-60 days from sowing to first harvest, but in practice you start eating much sooner, because harvesting young leaves together with thinning is possible from around day 25. If you sow lettuce early in a disaster, when there is nothing in the field, something green reaches your mouth within a month.

### 12-2. Why Only the Outer Leaves Are Picked

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The growing point of lettuce sits at the top of the compressed short stem, in the very center of the plant. New leaves keep rising from there, pushed outward as they grow. So when you pick the fully grown outer leaves, all the plant loses is old leaves whose photosynthetic capacity has already declined. The growing point remains and keeps making new leaves.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Conversely, if you cut out the center, that plant is finished. The growing point is gone and no more leaves can be made. A few small leaves may come out of the side buds, but they do not grow to a usable size. The reason head lettuce is a one-time harvest is the same: to harvest the head, you must take the entire growing point with it.

| Item | Tip |
|:---|:---|
| Leaves to pick | Fully grown leaves, starting from the lower outer ones |
| Amount per picking | No more than half of all leaves |
| Leaves to leave | Always at least six center leaves |
| Tool | Do not tear by hand; cut near the stem with scissors |
| Time of picking | Early morning. Having taken up water overnight, the leaves are at their freshest |
| Re-harvest interval | 10-14 days |
| Number of repeats | 2-3 times on average, 4-5 times if conditions are good |

> **Always leave at least six center leaves. The leaves you leave are the photosynthesis factory, and that factory makes the next leaves. If you get greedy and pick them all, that plant simply stops.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There is a reason for using scissors instead of tearing by hand. When leaves are torn off by hand, the leaf stalk tears from the stem and leaves a lengthwise wound, and that wound becomes an entrance for pathogens. Sclerotinia rot and gray mold in particular enter through wounds. Cut cleanly with scissors, the cut surface is small and heals quickly. It is safer still to dab a little dry wood ash on the cut after cutting.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Water after picking leaves. A plant that has lost leaves uses water and nutrients to make new ones, and if the soil dries out at this point, recovery is delayed and stress can bring bolting forward. To keep cut-and-come-again harvesting going for a long time, it is good to water every time you pick and to apply a dilute top-dressing once every 2-3 weeks.

### 12-3. How Long One Plant Lasts

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The Perenniality A in the evaluation table means that leaf lettuce keeps producing for 2-3 months through repeated harvesting of its outer leaves. You harvest 2-3 times at intervals of 10-14 days, or 4-5 times if conditions are good. What determines this period is, in the end, bolting. The moment the flower stalk rises, cut-and-come-again harvesting is over.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This is why autumn lettuce lasts longer than spring lettuce. In spring, temperatures rise while the plants grow, so bolting begins in one to one and a half months, but in autumn temperatures fall, so harvesting continues until frost arrives. The advice to center disaster cropping on autumn comes from this difference in duration.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;If one person eats five or six lettuce leaves a day, then ten to twenty plants under cut-and-come-again harvesting can cover one person's daily needs. Because this is a crop you take a little of each day, not one you harvest all at once and stockpile, the number of plants needed has to be calculated from the regrowth rate, not from the amount eaten.
