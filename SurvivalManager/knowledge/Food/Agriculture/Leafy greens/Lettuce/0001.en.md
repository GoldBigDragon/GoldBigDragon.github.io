## Introduction

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Lettuce (Lactuca sativa) is a leafy vegetable of the daisy family (Asteraceae). Unlike kale and leaf mustard, which belong to the cabbage family (Brassicaceae), it comes from the same family as dandelion, mugwort, and sunflower. Its ancestor is prickly lettuce, which grew wild around the Mediterranean and western Asia, and records show it was already being cultivated in ancient Egypt. The Lactuca in its scientific name comes from lac, the Latin word for milk. When a lettuce stem is cut, white latex oozes out, and that latex explains the plant's name, its nature, and its weakness all at once.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Measured by the yardstick of disaster preparedness, lettuce's Overall grade is D, with a score of 37.7. Short-term is D (38.5) and Long-term is D (36.9). It sits on the lower end of the crops covered in this document, and there is no reason to blur that fact. Calories per area is D: even if you harvest 3,000kg from 1,000m², at 20kcal per 100g that is only 600kcal per m², just 0.23 times the self-sufficiency line. Storability is E, the lowest in this entire document; at room temperature without electricity, lettuce spoils in 2-3 days. Temperature range is also C: step outside the narrow band of 15-22°C and the plant bolts and turns bitter.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Yet in the same table, the Supplementary grade is SS, with a score of 62.1. Micronutrients is S, covering 16 of 21 nutrients. Protein density is SS, and Seed saving is S. Understanding this gap is the key to making proper use of lettuce. Lettuce is not food to stack up in a storehouse but food that goes straight from the field to the mouth. The problem is not that it cannot be stored; it is a crop eaten in a way that leaves no reason to store it.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This crop has three practical strengths. First, it is washed and eaten as it is. The Cooking & processing grade of SS refers to this. Not a drop of fuel is needed. In a disaster, the value of a vegetable that can be eaten without lighting a fire is not easy to see in a nutrition table, but in reality it is large.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Second, if you pick only the outer leaves, the plant keeps growing. This is what the Perenniality grade of A reflects. A single planting is harvested a little at a time for two to three months, and since the harvest is not concentrated at one point in time, the storage problem never arises in the first place.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Third, lettuce is self-pollinating. Saved seed keeps the plant's characteristics, and there is almost no need to worry about cross-pollination. This contrasts with cabbage-family crops, whose seed saving is tricky because they are held back by cross-pollination and a biennial life cycle.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The weaknesses are concentrated in temperature and time. Lettuce seed stops germinating above 25°C. This phenomenon is called high-temperature dormancy (thermodormancy), and it is the first wall you run into when trying to plant lettuce in summer. Even if germination succeeds, continued heat sends up the flower stalk, and once the stalk rises, the bitter latex in the leaves increases and the plant becomes inedible. Seed longevity is also only B, at 2-3 years, which falls short of the 5 years of the cabbage family. And because of sclerotinia rot, a disease that afflicts the daisy family, lettuce cannot be replanted in the same spot for 2-3 years.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In short, lettuce is not the pillar of the field but its everyday crop. While grains and tuber crops (tubers/storage roots) provide the calories and radish and napa cabbage take charge of winter storage, lettuce supplies something fresh, a little at a time every day, through the two seasons of spring and autumn. Without that freshness, people fall ill even on a full stomach.
