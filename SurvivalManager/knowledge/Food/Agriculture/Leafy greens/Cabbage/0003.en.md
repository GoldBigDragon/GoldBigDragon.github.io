## 2. What Kind of Plant Is This?

### 2-1. Taxonomy and Origin

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Cabbage is a biennial herb of the family Brassicaceae, genus Brassica. Its scientific name is Brassica oleracea var. capitata, and it is a different variety of the same species as kale, broccoli, cauliflower, kohlrabi, and Brussels sprouts. Today's head is the result of people selecting, over thousands of years, the trait of leaves wrapping over each other from wild cabbage.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Being the same species has two practical consequences. First, cabbage exchanges pollen with kale, broccoli, and kohlrabi, so their seeds mix. This is a constraint you must factor in when saving seed, and it is the subject of Section 13. Second, crucifers as a whole share pests and diseases. Cabbage white butterflies, diamondback moths, flea beetles, and clubroot attack kale, napa cabbage, radish, and leaf mustard together.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Cabbage originates from the Mediterranean coast and the Atlantic coast of Western Europe. Coming from a cool maritime climate, it is weak against high heat. Above 25°C heading does not occur, and if 30°C continues, a head that has already formed can even split open. Originating from limestone country, it favors calcium and dislikes acidic soil. Its biennial habit, needing to go through winter cold before sending up a flower stalk, also comes from that climate.

### 2-2. Physical Form

| Organ | Characteristics |
|---|---|
| Outer leaves | Broad, thick, egg-shaped, with a white waxy layer on the surface. They are the factory that builds the head and the protective shield during storage |
| Head (heading) | A ball shape made of inner leaves wrapped layer over layer. Depending on the variety it can be flat, pointed, or round |
| Core (central stalk) | A short, thick stem that runs vertically through the center of the head. The growing point is at its tip |
| Stem | Very short and thick. The leaf stalks are packed so closely that it does not look like a stem |
| Roots | A straight taproot goes down 40-60cm while fine roots spread shallowly |
| Flowers | Four yellow petals arranged in a cross. They bloom in the spring of the second year |
| Pod & seed | Elongated siliques hold reddish-brown seeds inside |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Understanding the structure of the head explains almost everything about growing cabbage. The five or six outer leaves spread out and catch sunlight. The nutrients these leaves produce gather at the center and push out new inner leaves. Because the inner leaves grow without receiving light, they are pale and soft, and instead of unfolding upward they curl inward. As the inner leaves stack up layer after layer, they press against each other and the head becomes firm.

> **The outer leaves are what build the head. If the outer leaves are not large enough, there is no nutrient supply to push the inner leaves out, and then no head forms. Growing five or six outer leaves large and healthy is essentially the whole of heading management.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The core is the thick stem running vertically through the center of the head, with the growing point at its tip. You cut through the core at harvest, but if you leave part of the core and some outer leaves at the base, a few small new cabbages can sprout from the side buds at that spot. They are only fist-sized and low in number, but they are valuable when they appear at a time when nothing else is available. Still, as the D for perenniality in the evaluation table indicates, this is a bonus, not a full-scale second harvest.

### 2-3. Growth Stages

| Stage | Timing | Key Physiology | Key Nutrients |
|---|---|---|---|
| Germination & seedling stage | 40 days from sowing | Grown up to 4-5 true leaves | Phosphorus, a small amount of nitrogen |
| Establishment stage | 1-2 weeks after transplanting | Severed roots put out new growth | Phosphorus |
| Vegetative-growth stage (outer-leaf formation) | 3-6 weeks after transplanting | Forms 10-15 outer leaves | Nitrogen |
| Heading stage | 6-12 weeks after transplanting | Inner leaves curl inward and the head grows | Potassium, calcium, nitrogen |
| Maturation stage | 2-3 weeks after heading | The head firms up | Stop irrigation and fertilizing |
| Bolting & flowering stage | The following spring | The head splits and a flower stalk rises | Phosphorus, potassium, boron |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The boundary between the vegetative-growth stage and the heading stage is the watershed of growing cabbage. Around 6 weeks after transplanting, once about 10-15 outer leaves have grown, the plant changes direction. Instead of unfolding new leaves, it starts curling them inward. For this switch to happen, the conditions have to be right: the temperature must be between 15-20°C, and nutrients and water must be sufficient. If the conditions are off, the switch does not happen and the plant just keeps unfolding leaves. This is what is commonly called poor heading.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There is also something worth knowing about bolting. In cabbage, vernalization occurs when a young seedling that has grown past a certain thickness goes through cold. Once a seedling with a stem diameter thicker than a pencil (about 6mm) experiences 10 degrees or below for 3 weeks or more, that signal gets recorded. This is why sowing too early or transplanting overly large seedlings is risky in spring cultivation: the plant skips forming a head that spring and simply bolts. Young seedlings cannot yet sense cold, so planting small seedlings later is actually the safer choice.

### 2-4. Nutrition per 100g

| Nutrient | Amount | Notes |
|---|:---:|---|
| Calories | 29kcal | Based on the Korean Food Composition Table, raw |
| Water | About 92% | Mostly water |
| Protein | 1.36g | 18.8% of calories |
| Carbohydrates | 5-6g | Sugars are 4.40g, on the sweeter side among leafy vegetables |
| Fat | 0.1g | Nearly none |
| Dietary fiber | 2-3g | Supports bowel movement |
| Vitamin C | 30-40mg | One head exceeds a full day's worth for several people |
| Vitamin K | High | Involved in blood clotting and bone formation |
| Vitamin U (S-methylmethionine) | Present | An amino-acid derivative reported to be linked to protecting the stomach lining |
| Folate | Abundant | Involved in blood formation |
| Calcium | 40-50mg | Less than kale, but well absorbed |
| Potassium | About 200mg | Helps balance things out when salt intake rises |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;One practical fact about vitamin C is worth noting. Cabbage's vitamin C is highest in the outer leaves and decreases as you move inward, because more of it is synthesized in leaves that receive sunlight. The outer leaves are tough and darker in color, so they are commonly thrown away, but nutritionally they are not a part to discard. Chopped finely into soup or mixed into sauerkraut, that vitamin C can be put to use as is.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Vitamin U is an amino-acid derivative whose formal name is S-methylmethionine, and it was named from the observation that cabbage juice acts on the stomach lining. It is heat-sensitive and much of it disappears on cooking, so eating cabbage raw or juicing it is what preserves this compound.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;And the fact that fermentation changes nutrition matters especially for cabbage. Fermenting it into sauerkraut generally keeps vitamin C intact or even increases it, creates new B vitamins, and adds lactic-acid bacteria. Storage by cooking or drying loses vitamin C, but fermentation does not. Section 15 covers this.

## 3. Growing Conditions

### 3-1. Temperature

| Category | Limit | Optimum | Notes |
|---|:---:|:---:|---|
| Germination temperature | 15-30°C | 25°C | Germinates in 3-4 days at 25°C |
| Growing temperature | 5-27°C | 15-20°C | The B for temperature range in the evaluation table comes from the 12°C range for heading |
| Optimal heading temperature | 15-27°C | 15-18°C | Outside this range, the leaves do not wrap |
| High-temperature limit | 25°C and above | - | Poor heading, premature bolting, head splitting |
| Low-temperature limit | -5°C | - | Can overwinter in open fields in southern South Korea. Below this, the head freezes and is damaged |
| Vernalization trigger | Stem diameter 6mm or more, 10°C or below for 3 weeks | - | Bolts that same spring, not the following year |

> **Heading is a temperature problem. No matter how much fertilizer and water you give it, the leaves will not wrap if 25°C continues. When working out sowing dates, look at the temperature during heading, not at harvest.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This principle determines the cropping pattern. Spring cultivation is planted in March-April and harvested from late June through July, but its heading period falls in May-June, which cuts it close. Autumn cultivation is planted in July-August and harvested from late October through November, and its heading period falls in September-October, exactly matching the right temperature. This is why autumn cultivation is ahead in both quality and storability.

### 3-2. Light

| Category | Standard | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Required sunlight | 6+ hours a day | The outer leaves must grow large for heading to occur |
| Minimum sunlight | 4 hours a day | There are separate low-light heading varieties |
| At 40% shade | Relative yield 86% | The common value for leafy vegetables and the basis for the B grade in the evaluation table |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The figure showing that 86% of yield is kept even under shade is measured by leaf weight. Head quality, however, is more sensitive than that. When light is insufficient, the outer leaves spread thin and wide and less nutrient reaches the inside, so the head ends up small and loose. Even if you give the sunniest spots to calorie crops, it is better to make sure cabbage still gets five or six hours of direct sun a day.

### 3-3. Water and Soil

| Item | Standard | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Total evapotranspiration | About 550mm per season | On the high side among leafy vegetables, and the basis for the B grade in the evaluation table |
| Soil moisture | 70-80% of field capacity | Especially important during heading |
| Soil texture | Loam or clay loam with good water retention | Sandy soil runs short of water during heading |
| Soil depth | 30cm or more | The taproot goes down deep |
| pH | 6.0-6.8 | Around 6.5 is best |
| Lower pH limit | 5.5 | Below this, clubroot increases sharply |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Cabbage uses more water than kale. Its total evapotranspiration of 550mm is 1.4× kale's 400mm. If water is cut off during heading the head becomes small, and if water is cut off and then suddenly resumed, the inside grows rapidly while the outside cannot keep up, so the head splits. Uniform water supply matters more for cabbage than for any other crop, and where there is no irrigation equipment, you have no choice but to rely on mulching to cut evaporation.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Managing soil acidity is the same task as defending against clubroot. Keeping pH at 6.5 or above greatly suppresses the pathogen's activity. Without lime, wood ash, burnt and crushed shell and eggshell, or oyster-shell powder can substitute. However, if cabbage's pH rises well above 7.0, boron uptake is blocked and internal browning appears, so you should not simply keep pushing the acidity up.
