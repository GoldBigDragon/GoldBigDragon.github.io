## 1. Disaster-Preparedness Evaluation

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Cabbage's overall grade is A, with a score of 48.6 points. Broken down by scenario, its short-term ability to endure the early stage of a disaster is A (49.4), its long-term, multi-year self-reliance ability is B (47.7), and its supplementary value as a side-dish resource beyond staple food is SS (58.8). It stands out that the short-term score is 1.7 points higher than the long-term score. Stored heads can be taken out and eaten in winter, but the burden of fertilizing and seed saving accumulates as the years go by.

### 1-3. Why Calories Are B and Supplementary Is SS

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Cabbage's calories-per-area grade of B is unusually high for a leafy vegetable. Lettuce is D, Swiss chard is D, kale is C, and only cabbage reaches B. But this B was not earned through high calorie density. At 29kcal per 100g, cabbage is practically the same as kale's 28kcal. The difference is yield: 6,000kg from 1,000m², 1.5× more than kale's 4,000kg. This is because the structure of leaves stacked into layers like a ball produces more weight per unit area.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Even so, this is only 0.67× the self-sufficiency line. To cover 2,100kcal a day from cabbage alone, you would have to eat 7.2kg, which is three or four large heads. That is not something a person can do every day. The conclusion is the same as with kale: cabbage is not a crop that supplies calories, but a crop that supplies everything else.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;What that "everything else" is shows up in the Micronutrients grade of S. Cabbage exceeds 10% of the daily recommended intake for 16 of 21 nutrients. Vitamin C matters especially. 100g of cabbage contains 30mg to 40mg of vitamin C, and a single average-sized head covers a full day's worth for several adults.

> **What sets cabbage apart from other leafy vegetables is that it can hold on to this vitamin C all winter long. Lettuce's vitamin C is gone within three days, while cabbage's vitamin C lasts three months in a root-cellar pit.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;It is worth revisiting why vitamin C deficiency is a real threat in a disaster. The human body cannot make vitamin C and cannot store it either. If fresh plant foods are cut off, the body's reserves run out within one to three months, and scurvy begins: bleeding gums, wounds that will not heal, and old scars that reopen. A winter diet of nothing but grain, dried beans, and salted meat is exactly that condition. Putting ten heads of cabbage into a root-cellar pit, and fermenting some of it into sauerkraut, actually removes this risk.

### 1-4. How to Read the Strengths

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;SS went to infrastructure needs and cooking & processing; S went to protein density, micronutrients, taste, and prevalence; A went to cold hardiness, continuous-cropping tolerance, yield stability, seed saving, and seed longevity. What stands out is that, even with a C for storability, the whole reason this crop exists is storage.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The C for storability should not be misread. This grade is measured on the same scale as dried beans or grain. On a table where soybeans hold up for 3 years and earn an SS, 2-3 months can only come out as a C. But compared among leafy vegetables, the picture is completely different.

| Crop | Storability Grade | Unpowered Room-Temp Period |
|---|:---:|---|
| Cabbage | C | 2-3 months whole, 3-4 months in a root-cellar pit |
| Radish | C | 3-4 months in a root-cellar pit |
| Kale | D | 1 week |
| Leaf mustard | D | About 1 week |
| Swiss chard | D | 1 week |
| Iceberg lettuce | D | 1 week |
| Lettuce | E | 2-3 days |
| Purslane | E | 2-3 days |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Among leafy vegetables, cabbage is the only one whose storage period crosses into months, putting it on a par with radish, a root vegetable. The structure in which outer leaves wrap the inside, in other words the head itself, acts as a storage container.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The A for cold hardiness is worth noting too. Cabbage tolerates down to -5°C and overwinters in open fields in southern South Korea. It falls short of kale's -10°C, but it still gives you the freedom not to rush the late-autumn harvest. The way cabbage actually gets sweeter after a few frosts follows the same logic as kale: it is a response that dissolves sugar into the cell sap to lower the freezing point.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The S for taste and the SS for cooking & processing matter in practice. At 4.40g of sugars per 100g, cabbage is on the sweeter side among leafy vegetables, and it can be eaten as is after washing. Being a vegetable with a fuel cost of 0 that you can eat every day without any resistance carries more value than it seems in a disaster that drags on. This contrasts with leaf mustard, which received a D for taste.

### 1-5. How to Read the Weaknesses

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;E went to the single criterion of non-food value; D went to perenniality; C went to speed to harvest, fertilizer demand, pest & disease pressure, storability, yield retention without synthetic inputs, and tillage & digging energy.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The C for speed to harvest and the D for perenniality pair up to create cabbage's biggest constraint. You have to wait four months after planting, and once you harvest it, that is the end. This is the exact opposite of kale, which you can harvest thirty weeks running from a single plant. Planting cabbage right after a disaster begins still leaves a four-month gap until the first harvest, and something else has to supply food during that time. That is why cabbage is always placed alongside other crops. Crops like leaf mustard and radish, which are ready in two months, fill the early stretch, while cabbage backs it up later.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The C for fertilizer demand is a real risk. Cabbage needs 32g of N, P, and K combined per 1m², the largest amount among the leafy vegetables covered in this document. Compare that with 24g for kale, leaf mustard, and radish, and 22g for lettuce and Swiss chard. The reason is that forming the head itself demands a large amount of nutrients, and if fertilizer runs short, heading simply does not happen at all. Reading this together with the 40.8% yield retention without synthetic inputs makes the problem clear. Once chemical fertilizer is cut off, cabbage collapses before the other crops do. Section 5 addresses this problem.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The C for tillage & digging energy is also worth recording. The whole plot has to be worked to a depth of 22cm, and the annual digging energy comes to 239kcal per 1m², more than double kale's 109kcal. Under conditions where a person is working the field with a spade, this difference shows up as real labor time. The energy return multiple is still positive at 6.3×, but that is only about half of kale's 12.9×.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The C for pest & disease pressure and the 51.7% yield loss are the same as kale's. This is the shared fate of all crucifers. Cabbage does have a trickier side to it, though. Once a moth caterpillar burrows into the head, it cannot be seen from outside, and a head eaten out from within cannot be stored either. The key is stopping them before heading begins, and that is the subject of Section 9.
