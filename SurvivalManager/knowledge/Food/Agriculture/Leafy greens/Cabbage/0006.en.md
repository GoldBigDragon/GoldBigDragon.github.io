## 12. Harvest

### 12-1. Judging Harvest Time

| Criterion | Details |
|---|---|
| Feel | If the head resists firmly when pressed with your palm, it is ready |
| Timing | Spring cultivation late June-July, autumn cultivation late October-November |
| If delayed | The head splits lengthwise and a flower stalk rises from inside |
| Before harvest | Stop irrigation starting 10 days ahead |
| Harvest time of day | Early morning on a clear day |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;If it feels soft when pressed, the inner leaves have not filled out yet. Leave it a little longer. On the other hand, if you leave it too long, the inner leaves keep growing and push outward, splitting the head. Germs enter a split head through the crack and it cannot be stored, so eat it right away or turn it into ferment.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There is a reason for harvesting early in the morning on a clear day. If you harvest right after rain or while the dew has not yet dried, moisture stays between the outer leaves, and that moisture becomes the starting point of decay during storage. On the other hand, if you harvest at midday, the head's temperature is high, and once placed in storage its respiration is active and it spoils quickly. It is best to take the morning's cool head straight into a cool place.

### 12-2. Harvesting Technique

| Step | Method |
|---|---|
| 1 | Hold the plant with one hand and cut the base with a knife. For storage, you can also pull it up roots and all |
| 2 | Leave 2-3 outer leaves and remove the rest |
| 3 | Handle without causing wounds. Do not throw or stack and press them |
| 4 | Leave in the field for about half a day to dry the surface (curing) |
| 5 | Move to a cool, dark place |

> **Always leave two or three outer leaves on. They keep moisture from evaporating during storage and absorb physical impact, and if the outside gets damaged, you can just peel off those leaves and the inside will still be fine.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;If you are aiming for storage, pulling the plant up roots and all is better. Moisture remaining in the roots moves slowly into the head and slows the rate of drying out. Shake off the soil but do not cut the roots, and store it either hanging upside down as is or reburied in soil.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Do not skip curing - drying the harvested head in the shade for about half a day. If it goes into storage with a wet surface, the trapped moisture becomes the starting point of soft rot. Leaving it too long in direct sun scorches the outer leaves, so dry it in the shade.

### 12-3. Managing the Stump After Harvest

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;If you leave the stump in the field, a few small cabbages can sprout from the side buds. They are only fist-sized and few in number, but they make one meal's worth of side dish when they appear at a time when nothing else is available. However, this stump can also become a winter refuge for moths, aphids, and viruses. In a field with high pest and disease pressure, it is better for the following year to give up on that side-bud harvest and dig out and remove the stump.

## 13. Seed Saving - The Biennial and Cross-Pollination Barrier

### 13-1. A Two-Layered Difficulty

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Saving cabbage seed ranks among the trickiest of the crops covered in this document. Being biennial, it only flowers after going through winter, and being cross-pollinated, it freely mixes with other varieties of the same species. On top of that, the very structure of the head gets in the way of seed saving, because the flower stalk has to push its way through leaves wrapped layer over layer.

| Barrier | Details | Solution |
|---|---|---|
| Biennial habit | The flower stalk rises only after going through cold | Overwinter in the open field, or store in a root-cellar pit and replant in spring |
| Heading | The flower stalk cannot pierce a firm head | Cut a cross into the top of the head in spring |
| Cross-pollination | Bees carry pollen and mix it with other varieties | Isolation distance, staggered cultivation, netting |
| Self-incompatibility | Its own pollen does not fertilize it well | Leave several seed-parent plants |

### 13-2. What It Crosses With

| Crop | Scientific Name | Crosses with Cabbage |
|---|---|:---:|
| Kale, broccoli, cauliflower, kohlrabi, Brussels sprouts | Brassica oleracea | Crosses completely |
| Napa cabbage, bok choy, turnip, rapeseed | Brassica rapa | Barely crosses |
| Leaf mustard, mustard | Brassica juncea | Barely crosses |
| Radish | Raphanus sativus | Different genus; does not cross |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;It is fine for napa cabbage, radish, or leaf mustard to flower at the same time in a year you are saving cabbage seed, because they are different species. The only problem is the Brassica oleracea group - that is, kale, broccoli, and kohlrabi.

### 13-3. Three Ways to Prevent Crossing

| Method | Details | Practicality |
|---|---|---|
| Isolation distance | 500m or more from other flowering Brassica oleracea plants | Hard, since you cannot control neighboring fields |
| Staggered cultivation | Let only one variety flower per year | The most practical option |
| Netting | Cover with netting before flowering and transfer pollen by hand with a brush inside | Reliable at small scale |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Staggered cultivation is the most practical. This year, save seed only from cabbage and eat all the kale so it never flowers. Next year, do the opposite. Since cabbage seed lasts 4 to 5 years, rotating which crop you save seed from each year still keeps your seed supply unbroken.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;When netting, it is essential to remember that cabbage is self-incompatible. Its own pollen does not fertilize it well, so you need to put several plants together inside the netting and move a brush back and forth between the flowers. If you net only a single plant, hardly any pods will set.

> **Leave at least 5 seed-parent plants, and ideally 10 or more. If a cross-pollinated crop is carried forward through only one or two plants, inbreeding depression appears within a few generations, weakening vigor and disrupting heading.**

### 13-4. Overwintering and Seed-Saving Procedure

| Stage | Timing | Details |
|---|---|---|
| Selecting seed-parent plants | Harvest time | Choose plants with firm, large heads, clear varietal traits, and no disease |
| Overwintering (open field) | November-February | Southern South Korea only. Mound soil at the base and cover with straw and fallen leaves |
| Overwintering (storage) | November-February | Central South Korea and north. Pull up roots and all and bury in a 0-5 degree root-cellar pit |
| Replanting | March | Replant the stored plants |
| Cutting the cross | March-April | Cut a cross 3-5cm deep into the top of the head |
| Bolting | April | The flower stalk rises up through the cut. Set up a stake |
| Flowering | April-May | Yellow flowers bloom. Maintain isolation at this stage |
| Pod set | May-June | Pods form |
| Harvest | June-July | Once two-thirds of the pods turn brown, cut the whole stalk |
| After-ripening & threshing | Right after harvest | Dry in the shade for 1-2 weeks, then put in a sack and beat it |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Cutting the cross is a task unique to saving cabbage seed. When spring comes and the flower stalk starts growing, if the head is too firm it cannot push through and instead bends and rots inside. Cutting a cross 3-5cm deep into the top of the head lets the flower stalk rise up through that path. Cutting too deep starts decay inside, so go only deep enough to split a few layers of outer leaves.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Overwintering in the open field is prone to failure in central South Korea and further north. Cabbage tolerates down to -5°C, but if it stays below that for long, the head freezes, thaws, and turns soft. Before frost sets in seriously, pull the plants up roots and all with a little soil still attached, bury them in a root-cellar pit or a cool shed, and keep them at 0-5°C. This storage period is itself the vernalization process, so replanting in spring makes them send up a flower stalk right away.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Do not leave the pods in the field until they are completely dry. Crucifer pods, once fully dry, split open on their own and spill their seed onto the ground. If you cut the whole stalk when the lower two-thirds have turned brown and let it after-ripen on cloth or in a sack, you can catch even the seeds that split open and fall.

| Item | Standard |
|---|---|
| Seed longevity | 4-5 years sealed at room temperature |
| Storage conditions | A cool, dark, dry place, in a sealed container |
| Desiccant | Roasted and cooled rice, charcoal, dry ash |
| Checking germination | Each year, place 20 seeds on a wet cloth and count the germination rate |
| Yield per plant | Several thousand seeds |

## 14. Toxicity and Compounds of Concern

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Cabbage has no acute toxicity. It can be washed and eaten as is, and the SS for cooking & processing in the evaluation table reflects that fact. There are two things to watch for.

| Item | Details | Response |
|---|---|---|
| Gas and bloating | Oligosaccharides like raffinose ferment in the large intestine | Eat small amounts at first; cooking reduces it |
| Goitrogens | Common to all crucifers. Can interfere with iodine uptake | Cooking reduces them. Eat seaweed alongside |
| Spoilage | Discoloration, sliminess, foul smell | If only the outer leaves are spoiled, peel them off; if it is soft all the way in, discard it |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Goitrogens are the same compound found in kale, and cabbage tends to have a lower content than kale. It is not a problem for someone getting enough iodine to eat a normal amount. The condition that becomes dangerous is eating large amounts of raw cabbage daily, especially as juice, over a long period on an iodine-poor diet. This is a condition that could actually arise in a disaster, cut off from the sea with no access to miyeok (a type of edible brown seaweed) and dasima (kelp), so alternate with cooked forms and eat seaweed alongside it whenever you can get it. Fermenting into sauerkraut also reduces goitrogens considerably.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Oxalic acid is not a concern in cabbage. Unlike spinach or Swiss chard, its content is very low and does not interfere with calcium absorption.

## 15. Storage Without Refrigeration - Root-Cellar Pits and Sauerkraut

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The biggest reason cabbage is included in this document lies in this section. It is a leafy vegetable that keeps for months, and fermented, it lasts past a year.

### 15-1. Comparing Storage Methods

| Method | Conditions | Duration | Vitamin C |
|---|---|:---:|---|
| Room-temperature shade | A cool, dark place | 2-4 weeks | Gradually decreases |
| Root-cellar pit | 0-4 degrees, 85-95% humidity | 3-4 months | Largely retained |
| Burying in sand | Cool shed, damp sand | 2-3 months | Retained |
| Sauerkraut | Ferment at room temperature, then a cool place | 6 months to over a year | Retained or increased |
| Drying | Shredded and dried in the shade | 6 months to a year | Nearly gone |
| Salt-curing | 10-15% salt | A few months | Greatly reduced |

### 15-2. Root-Cellar Pit Storage

| Item | Method |
|---|---|
| Site | Dig a pit 1-1.5m deep in a well-drained spot |
| Floor | Lay down 10cm of straw or dry grass |
| Loading | Place upside down with roots facing up, or lay on their sides. Leave space so they do not touch each other |
| Covering | Cover with straw and mound 30-50cm of soil on top |
| Ventilation | Stand a straw bundle or a pipe in the middle to let air through |
| Checking | Open once every 2 weeks and remove any spoiled ones |

> **The target for a root-cellar pit is a temperature that neither freezes nor lets sprouting begin - that is, 0 to 4 degrees. Too shallow and it freezes; too deep and moisture builds up and causes rot.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There are also many spots that can substitute for a root-cellar pit. Under north-facing eaves, under a raised floor, a basement, the shade by a well, or the north corner of a shed are all candidates. The conditions are the same: it must not freeze, temperature must not swing widely, light must not reach it, and air must circulate very faintly.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;It matters to keep the heads from touching each other. Once one starts rotting, the ones touching it follow one after another. Tuck straw between them, space them out on shelves, or, if pulled up roots and all, tie the roots together and hang them upside down. Hanging is best for airflow.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Skipping the check every 2 weeks brings down the whole storage. If only the outer leaves are spoiled, just peel them off, but if the base is soft and smells, take it out right away and eat it or discard it. This is a structure where one bad head ruins ten.

### 15-3. Sauerkraut - A Year of Storage with Nothing but Salt

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This is the most powerful method for handling cabbage in a disaster. All you need is salt and a crock, no fire or refrigeration is required, and vitamin C is retained or even increases during fermentation.

| Step | Method | Caution |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Preparing | Remove two or three outer leaves and set them aside, cut out the core, and shred the rest as finely as possible | The finer the shreds, the better the juice releases |
| 2. Measuring | Weigh the shredded cabbage and prepare salt equal to 2-2.5% of that weight | 20-25g of salt per 1kg |
| 3. Massaging | Sprinkle on the salt and knead by hand for about 30 minutes | Until juice pools abundantly |
| 4. Packing | Pack tightly into a crock or glass jar. Press down layer by layer so no air remains | The brine must rise above the vegetable |
| 5. Covering | Cover the surface with the outer leaves you set aside and place a weight stone on top | The vegetable must be completely submerged in the brine |
| 6. Fermenting | 1-3 weeks at 15-20°C. Bubbles rise | Cover with cloth so air can escape |
| 7. Storing | Once it turns sour, move it to a cool place (root-cellar pit, cellar, north-facing shed) | 6 months to over a year |

> **The single condition that separates success from failure is whether the vegetable stays completely submerged in the brine. Mold only grows on the part that touches air.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Knowing the underlying principle makes it easy to adapt. Cabbage leaves naturally carry lactic-acid bacteria on them. Salt draws water out of the cells to make brine, and at the same time creates a concentration that suppresses the growth of unwanted microbes while lactic-acid bacteria can still tolerate it. When the lactic-acid bacteria consume sugar and produce lactic acid, the mixture turns acidic and other microbes cannot survive. This acid is what acts as the preservative, which is why it lasts a long time without refrigeration. It is the same principle as Korean kimchi.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Salt concentration is the key variable. Below 2%, unwanted microbes win out, and above 3%, even the lactic-acid bacteria are suppressed and fermentation slows. Without a scale, a rule of thumb is about one and a half to two tablespoons of salt per head of cabbage (roughly 1.5kg). Sun-dried salt is better than refined salt, but refined salt works fine if that is what you have.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;A white film sometimes forms on the surface; it is usually kahm yeast and is harmless. Just skim it off and continue. If black or blue mold grows instead, cut that part out thoroughly, and if the smell is rotten rather than sour and fresh, discard the whole batch. Normal sauerkraut is sour and salty and still carries the smell of cabbage.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;History proves the value of sauerkraut as a winter source of vitamin C. It was this salted cabbage that prevented scurvy on long sea voyages, and it is the same reason winter tables in Central Europe and East Asia have long relied on fermented vegetables. Drying makes vitamin C disappear and boiling leaches it into the water, but fermentation keeps it. If you have harvested ten heads of cabbage, a reasonable split is to put half into a root-cellar pit and turn the other half into ferment.

### 15-4. Drying and Salt-Curing

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Shredded, blanched, and then fully dried in the shade, cabbage keeps for 6 months to a year. Its volume shrinks to 10%, which is convenient for storage and transport, and you use a handful at a time in soup or porridge. However, vitamin C is nearly all gone. It should be clear that dried cabbage is a storage method that preserves fiber and minerals, not one that preserves vitamin C.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Cured heavily with 10-15% salt, cabbage keeps for a few months. At this concentration fermentation barely happens, so it comes out closer to a heavy salt-pickle, and you need to soak it in water before eating. This is the method to use when salt is plentiful and you do not have the means to manage fermentation.

## 16. Cooking and Processing

| Method | Fuel | Vitamin C Retention | Notes |
|---|:---:|:---:|---|
| Raw (shredded) | None | Maximum | Vitamin U is preserved too |
| Fermented (sauerkraut) | None | Retained or increased | Doubles as both storage and cooking |
| Blanching 1-2 min | Very little | Half or less | Volume shrinks and goitrogens decrease |
| Added to soup or stew | Shared with other cooking | Preserved if you eat the broth too | Uses no extra fuel |
| Stir-frying | Little | Medium | Sweetness increases |
| Steaming | Medium | Medium | Good for eating as a wrap |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Where fuel is scarce, cabbage's greatest advantage is that it does not need to be cooked. Shredded and lightly salted, it becomes a side dish for a meal, and you can also tear off a whole leaf to wrap rice in. Tossing it in at the very end of a fire that is already cooking something else is the way to get a cooked form without using any extra fuel.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Putting easily discarded parts to use is also practical. The outer leaves are tough but are the part with the most vitamin C. Chopped fine into soup or mixed into sauerkraut, they can be used as is. The core is crisp and sweet, so slice it thin to eat or add it to soup. From one head of cabbage, the only part actually worth discarding is the one or two outer leaves with soil on them.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Sauerkraut is eaten as is, but it is also used in cooking. Added to soup along with its brine, it makes a sour-tasting soup, and stir-frying mellows the sourness. However, applying heat kills the lactic-acid bacteria and also reduces vitamin C, so if you are counting on it for vitamins in winter, it is better to eat it uncooked, as is.

## 17. Annual Work Calendar

| Month | Tasks |
|---|---|
| January | Check root-cellar pit (every 2 weeks). Eat sauerkraut. Plan the field |
| February | Check root-cellar pit. Prepare for spring sowing, turn compost. Check overwintering seed-parent plants |
| March | Begin spring sowing and raising seedlings. Replant seed-parent plants and cut the cross |
| April | Harden off and transplant spring seedlings. Seed-parent plants flower; manage isolation |
| May | Manage vegetative growth, 1st top-dressing. Begin butterfly scouting, insect netting |
| June | Heading stage for spring cabbage, 2nd top-dressing. Harvest from late June |
| July | Finish spring harvest. Save seed. Begin autumn sowing and raising seedlings |
| August | Autumn seedling-raising and transplanting. Manage the hot period (mulching, irrigation) |
| September | Manage vegetative growth, top-dressing. 2nd moth peak, insect netting |
| October | Manage heading stage, top-dressing. Harvest begins from late October |
| November | Autumn harvest and curing. Root-cellar storage, making sauerkraut |
| December | Check root-cellar pit. Prepare seed-parent plants for winter |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The most important slot in this schedule is November. How you sort what you have harvested determines the whole winter. Send firm, unblemished heads to the root-cellar pit, and turn split or damaged heads into sauerkraut right away. Putting something that will not keep into storage ruins the ones next to it, while turning it into ferment instead makes it last a whole year. The trick is to do this sorting right there at the harvest site.
