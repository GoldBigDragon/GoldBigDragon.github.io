## 4. Varieties

### 4-1. Characteristics by Maturity Group

| Group | Growing Period | Head Size | Storability | Characteristics |
|---|:---:|---|:---:|---|
| Early-maturing | 80-100 days | Small (1-1.5kg) | Low | Harvested before the heat arrives. Used for spring cultivation |
| Mid-maturing | 100-120 days | Medium (1.5-2.5kg) | Medium | Used for both spring and autumn |
| Late-maturing | 120-150 days | Large (2.5-4kg) | Highest | The mainstay of autumn cultivation and winter storage |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;If storage is the goal, choose late-maturing varieties. Their leaves are packed tight, the head is firm, and moisture content is low, so under the same conditions they keep two to four times longer than early-maturing varieties. Early-maturing varieties have soft leaves and a loose head, which tastes good but turns soft within days. From a disaster-preparedness standpoint, this choice is clear.

### 4-2. Varieties by Cropping Pattern

| Cropping Pattern | Sowing | Transplanting | Harvest | Required Variety Traits |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Spring cultivation | March-April | Mid-April to mid-May | Late June-July | Early-maturing, late-bolting (trait of putting up a flower stalk late) |
| Summer cultivation | May-June | June-July | August-September | Heat tolerance. Easy to fail unless grown at high elevation |
| Autumn cultivation | July-August | Mid-August to September | Late October-November | Late-maturing, storability |
| Overwintering cultivation | September | October-November | April-May the following year | Cold hardiness. Limited to southern South Korea |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The reason late-bolting matters in spring cultivation is the vernalization discussed in Section 2. Planting in spring means the young seedlings inevitably go through a late cold snap, and varieties sensitive to bolting skip forming a head and send up a flower stalk instead. Late-bolting varieties have been selected specifically not to respond readily even to the same cold. If you have seed of unknown variety, it is safer to cut back on spring cultivation and focus on autumn cultivation.

### 4-3. Selection Criteria by Situation

| Situation | Choice | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Winter storage is the goal | Late-maturing, autumn cultivation | Storage period is two to three times longer |
| Sauerkraut is the goal | Late-maturing | Low moisture and firm tissue keep it from turning soft during fermentation |
| Need to eat quickly in the first year | Early-maturing | Ready to harvest in 80-100 days |
| Planning to keep saving seed | True-breeding variety | Hybrid traits scatter in the following generation |
| Field with insufficient sunlight | Low-light heading variety | Heads even in shade |

> **If a seed packet says F1, it is a hybrid. First-year yield is good, but if you save seed from it and replant, the traits scatter. If you plan to use the seed across generations, look for the labels true-breeding variety, heirloom, or open-pollinated (OP).**

## 5. Field Preparation and Fertilizer

### 5-1. Preparing the Field

| Item | Standard | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Tillage depth | 25-30cm | The taproot goes down deep |
| Bed height | 20cm or more | 30cm if drainage is poor |
| Bed width | 120-140cm | Based on planting two rows |
| Drainage ditch | Essential | Waterlogging invites soft rot and clubroot |
| Acidity correction | Target pH 6.5 | Apply 2-3 weeks before transplanting |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The C for tillage & digging energy in the evaluation table comes from this 25-30cm deep tillage. Doing it with a spade is considerable labor, but it is hard to skip. Cabbage's taproot goes down more than 40cm, and in a shallowly worked field it cannot draw up enough water and nutrients during heading, so the head ends up small.

### 5-2. Fertilizer Amounts

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Cabbage is the heaviest feeder among the leafy vegetables covered in this document. The C for fertilizer demand in the evaluation table, and the figure of 32g per 1m², show that burden.

| Nutrient | Per 1,000m² | Per 1m² | Split |
|---|:---:|:---:|---|
| Nitrogen (N) | 10-12kg | 10-12g | 50% base fertilizer, 50% top-dressing |
| Phosphorus (P) | 7-8kg | 7-8g | All as base fertilizer |
| Potassium (K) | 8-10kg | 8-10g | 60% base fertilizer, 40% top-dressing |
| Compost | 1,500-2,000kg | 1.5-2kg | Well-rotted compost |
| Lime | 150-200kg | 150-200g | For correcting acidic soil |

| Stage | Timing | Details |
|---|---|---|
| Base fertilizer | 1-2 weeks before transplanting | All compost, all phosphorus, half the nitrogen and potassium |
| Top-dressing 1 | About 20 days after transplanting | Nitrogen and potassium |
| Top-dressing 2 | At the start of heading | Nitrogen and potassium. The most important one |
| Top-dressing 3 | 15-20 days after the 2nd | Mainly potassium |

> **Top-dressing during heading decides whether cabbage growing succeeds or fails. If nutrients run short at this stage, there is not enough force to push out the inner leaves, and the head either fails to form or ends up loose.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There is a specific reason potassium and calcium matter so much during heading. Potassium moves water in and out of cells so the leaves stand taut, while calcium strengthens the cell walls. Both the firmness of the head and its resistance to going soft during storage are the work of these two elements. If you are aiming for storability, the potassium supply during heading matters especially.

### 5-3. Disaster Substitute Fertilizers

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;A zero-input yield retention of 40.8% means yield drops to 40% once chemical fertilizer is cut off. In cabbage, this does not show up as a simple yield reduction but as failed heading. If fertilizer runs short, the head may not just become small, it may not form at all. Securing substitute fertilizer is more urgent for cabbage than for any other crop.

| Substitute Resource | Nutrients Supplied | How to Use | Caution |
|---|---|---|---|
| Human urine | Nitrogen (mainly), potassium, phosphorus | Dilute 8-10× with water and pour between plants | Burns leaves on contact. Stop 3 weeks before harvest |
| Well-rotted compost | All-around | Generously as base fertilizer, around plants as top-dressing | Under-composted material burns roots |
| Wood ash | Potassium, calcium, phosphorus | One or two handfuls per 1m². Especially useful during heading | Alkaline; boron uptake is blocked once pH passes 7 |
| Bone meal | Phosphorus, calcium | Burn, crush, and mix into base fertilizer | Effect appears slowly |
| Eggshell & shellfish shell | Calcium | Burn and crush before use | A lime substitute and a calcium source during heading |
| Legume green manure | Nitrogen | Grow peas, Chinese milk vetch, or hairy vetch as a preceding crop and turn them under | Transplant after 3-4 weeks of breaking down |
| Sesame/perilla oil cake & rice bran | Nitrogen, phosphorus | Mix into base fertilizer or use as a water-steeped liquid | Fresh material near roots damages them with fermentation heat |
| Weed liquid fertilizer | Nitrogen, potassium | Soak cut weeds in water for 2-3 weeks to ferment, then dilute 5× | Smells strongly |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Meeting cabbage's nutrient demand without chemical fertilizer takes more than a single application; you have to design a cycle for the whole field. A structure that actually works looks like this: grow a legume green manure as a preceding crop and turn it under, add plenty of well-rotted compost on top of that, give diluted urine every 3 weeks during growth, and add wood ash during heading. Only by layering these four together does heading happen without chemical fertilizer. Any one alone is not enough.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;One more point about diluted urine. Because cabbage's leaves stack in layers, once heading begins, liquid gets trapped between the leaves and does not dry well. If decay starts inside there, it leads to soft rot. Once heading begins, pour diluted urine only into the furrows, never letting it touch the leaves, and stop it entirely from 3 weeks before harvest.

## 6. Sowing and Transplanting

### 6-1. Raising Seedlings

| Stage | Details |
|---|---|
| Sowing | 1-2cm spacing in a box or tray, 0.5-1cm deep |
| Germination conditions | 15-25°C, keep the soil from drying out |
| Days to germinate | 3-4 days at 25°C, 7-10 days at 15°C |
| Seedling-raising period | 30-40 days, 4-5 true leaves |
| Hardening off | Starting 1 week before transplanting, gradually expose to sun and wind |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Raising seedlings is more advantageous for cabbage than direct sowing. The growing period is long and occupies the field for a long time, but if you handle 40 days of seedling raising separately, you can use the field for other crops during that time. It is also much easier to protect young seedlings from flea beetles and moths in a small space.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Hardening off is a step that is easy to skip, but it has a real effect. If a seedling that has grown under protection is suddenly put out into the open field, its leaves scorch and establishment is delayed. Starting one week before transplanting, repeatedly taking it outside during the day and bringing it in at night thickens the leaves and develops the wax layer, reducing transplant shock afterward.

### 6-2. Transplanting

| Item | Standard | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Planting depth | Down to below the seed leaves. Keep the growing point from being buried | Planting too deep rots the lower leaves |
| Early-maturing spacing | 60 x 35cm | Dense planting is possible |
| Mid/late-maturing spacing | 70 x 40cm | Wider, since the head is larger |
| Planting density | 4,500-5,000 plants per 1,000m² | 4-5 plants per 1m² |
| Transplanting time | Overcast afternoon or dusk | Plants wilt if transplanted at midday |
| Right after transplanting | Water thoroughly | Brings roots and soil into close contact |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;It matters not to skimp on plant spacing. Planted too close, the outer leaves overlap each other and miss light, and small outer leaves mean no heading. Poor airflow also increases soft rot and downy mildew. Planting generously and letting the heads form properly, rather than cramming more plants into the same area, wins out on total yield as well.

### 6-3. Seed Treatment

| Method | Procedure | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Hot-water soak | 20 minutes in 50°C water, then cool immediately in cold water | Kills pathogens on the seed surface and inside it |
| Ash coating | Coat wetted seed with sifted wood ash | Combines surface disinfection with alkaline treatment |
| Water soak | 4-6 hours in room-temperature water | Speeds germination by a day or two |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Hot-water soaking is genuinely effective at preventing seed-borne diseases like black rot. However, above 55°C the seed dies, so doing it by feel without a thermometer is risky. The safe approach is not to treat all of your seed at once, but to treat only part of it and plant in batches.

## 7. Water Management

### 7-1. Water by Growth Stage

| Stage | Management | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Right after transplanting to establishment | Once thoroughly, then every 2-3 days | Until the roots take hold |
| Vegetative-growth stage | When the topsoil dries. 1-2 times a week | Water deeply each time |
| Heading stage | Steadily, without letting it dry out | The most important period |
| 10 days before harvest | Stop irrigation | Improves storability and prevents head splitting |

> **A water shortage during heading shows up immediately in head size. What is worse, though, is letting it dry out and then suddenly watering. The inside grows rapidly while the outer leaves cannot keep up, and the head splits lengthwise.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Once a head splits, it can no longer be stored. Pathogens enter through the split and it starts rotting within days. This damage clusters around the time heavy rain suddenly falls after the monsoon (jangma, late June to July in South Korea) ends. If a plant's head is nearly finished forming, one option is to harvest it early when heavy rain is forecast, and another is to lift the roots slightly to reduce water uptake.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Stopping irrigation 10 days before harvest is a measure aimed at storage. A head with high water content has soft tissue and a high respiration rate during storage, so it spoils quickly. Cutting off water raises the solute concentration inside the cells, which firms up the tissue and also raises sugar content.

### 7-2. Symptoms of Too Little or Too Much Water

| Symptom | Cause | Response |
|---|---|---|
| Outer leaves droop at midday and recover by evening | Normal range | No response needed |
| Still drooping in the morning | Water shortage | Water thoroughly right away |
| Head is small and loose | Water or nutrient shortage during heading | Combine top-dressing with irrigation |
| Head splits lengthwise | Sudden watering after drying out | Water evenly. Eat split heads first |
| Outer leaves yellow and the base softens | Waterlogging, soft rot | Dig a drainage ditch and remove diseased plants |
| Lower leaves wilt first, drooping badly by day | Suspected clubroot | Dig up the roots to check for galls |

### 7-3. Mulching

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Without plastic film, use straw, dry grass, fallen leaves, or rice hulls. Covering the bed to a thickness of 5-10cm cuts evaporation, greatly reducing how often you need to water, suppresses weeds, and lowers soil temperature in summer. Mulching is especially useful for cabbage because it produces, without special equipment, the uniform moisture that heading requires. Where you cannot water often, mulching is less an option than a near necessity.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;However, the space under mulch is also where slugs hide. They gnaw on cabbage's young leaves and the outer surface of the head, reducing storability, so lifting the cover occasionally to check, and keeping about 5cm around each plant clear of cover, reduces the damage.
