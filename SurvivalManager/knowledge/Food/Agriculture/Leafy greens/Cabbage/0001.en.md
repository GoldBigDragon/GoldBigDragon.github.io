## Introduction
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Cabbage (Brassica oleracea var. capitata) is the same species as kale, and heading, rolling the leaves inward, is what separates the character of the two crops. The outer leaves wrap the inner ones and act as natural packaging that blocks water loss and the entry of pathogens, so even though it is a leafy vegetable its storability is C: it lasts 2-3 months whole at room temperature and 3-4 months in a storage pit, and fermented into sauerkraut it lasts beyond a year. Its overall grade is A (48.6 points, rank 28 of 183 valid species) and its supplementary food grade is SS (58.8 points). Calories per area is B (6 kg per ㎡, 29 kcal per 100 g, 0.67 times the self-sufficiency line), but as a leafy vegetable it cannot fill the stomach on its own and takes the other half of the table. Its weak points are time (harvest speed C; 120-150 days converted to direct sowing, including 40 days of seedling raising), perenniality D (it does not regrow once cut), fertilizer (fertilization C; yield retention without synthetic inputs 40.8%), and heading itself: above 25℃, or with too little fertilizer or water during heading, the leaves do not roll. So it is a crop aimed at winter from the start: sown in summer, harvested in autumn, and the heads put in a pit to be eaten until the next spring.

> **What sets cabbage apart from other leafy vegetables is that it can hold on to its vitamin C all winter. The vitamin C of lettuce is gone in three days; the vitamin C of cabbage lasts three months in a pit.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Cabbage is the only leafy vegetable whose storage period is counted in months. Harvest speed C and perenniality D pair up to form the biggest constraint, and fertilization C is a real risk. The grade for each item is in the Crop Evaluation Results document.

## 2. Plant Overview
| Part | Characteristics |
|:---|:---|
| Outer leaves | Wide, thick, and egg-shaped, with a white wax layer. The factory that builds the head and the protective wrapper during storage |
| Head, core | A ball of inner leaves rolled layer on layer (flat, pointed, or round by variety). The core (central stalk) is a short, thick stem running vertically through the middle of the head, with the growing point at its tip |
| Roots, flowers | A straight taproot goes down 40-60 cm and fine roots spread shallowly. In the second spring it bears yellow flowers with four petals, and the long pods hold reddish brown seeds |

> **The outer leaves make the head. If the outer leaves are not big enough, there are no nutrients to push the inner leaves out and no head forms. Growing 10-15 outer leaves big and healthy is almost all there is to managing heading.**

| Stage | Timing | Key point |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Seedling and establishment | 40 days from sowing (4-5 true leaves), 1-2 weeks after transplanting | Phosphorus |
| Vegetative growth | 3-6 weeks after transplanting. Makes 10-15 outer leaves | Nitrogen |
| Heading | 6-12 weeks after transplanting. Inner leaves roll inward and the head grows | Potassium, calcium, nitrogen |
| Maturity, bolting | The head firms up 2-3 weeks after heading (stop watering and fertilizing). A flower stalk the next spring | Phosphorus, potassium, boron |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Per 100 g: 29 kcal, about 92% water, 1.36 g of protein (18.8% of calories), 4.40 g of sugars, 2-3 g of dietary fiber, 30-40 mg of vitamin C (one head exceeds the daily need of several people), 40-50 mg of calcium, and around 200 mg of potassium, and it is rich in vitamin K and folate. Vitamin U (S-methylmethionine) has been reported to relate to protecting the stomach lining.

## 3. Growing Environment and Varieties
| Condition | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Temperature | Germination 15-30℃ (3-4 days at 25℃), growth 5-27℃ (15-20℃), best heading 15-27℃ (15-18℃). Above 25℃ means poor heading, early bolting, and split heads. It withstands down to -5℃, but below that the head freezes. If 10℃ or lower lasts 3 weeks on stems 6 mm or thicker, it bolts that same spring |
| Light | 6 hours or more a day (minimum 4 hours; there are low-light heading varieties) |
| Water, soil | Total evapotranspiration about 550 mm per season; soil moisture 70-80% of maximum water capacity during heading. Loam with good water retention, pH 6.0-6.8 (best near 6.5); below pH 5.5 clubroot surges |

> **Heading is a temperature problem. However much fertilizer and water there is, it will not roll while 25℃ persists. When calculating the sowing date, look at the temperature at heading, not at harvest.**

| Item | Details |
|:---|:---|
| Maturity | Early 80-100 days (head 1-1.5 kg, low storability, spring crop), mid 100-120 days (1.5-2.5 kg), late 120-150 days (2.5-4 kg, highest storability; for autumn crop and winter storage, and best for sauerkraut because it is low in water and firm, so it does not soften during fermentation) |
| Planting types | Spring: sow March-April, transplant mid April-mid May, harvest late June-July (early varieties, late-bolting). Autumn: sow July-August, transplant mid August-September, harvest late October-November (late varieties). Overwintering: sow September, transplant October-November, harvest April-May next year (southern regions only). A summer crop needs heat tolerance and easily fails outside the highlands |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;A seed packet marked F1 is a hybrid, and its seed saved will scatter in type. If you plan to carry the crop on, look for fixed, landrace, or open-pollinated (OP) varieties.

## 4. Field Preparation, Sowing, and Transplanting
| Item | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Preparing the soil | Dig 25-30 cm deep; bed height 20 cm or more (30 cm if drainage is poor), width 120-140 cm (two rows); a drainage ditch is a must (excess water invites soft rot and clubroot). Add lime 2-3 weeks before transplanting to reach pH 6.5 |
| Fertilization (1,000㎡) | Nitrogen 10-12 kg (basal 50%, top dressing 50%), phosphorus 7-8 kg (all basal), potassium 8-10 kg (basal 60%, top dressing 40%), lime 150-200 kg. Basal fertilizer 1-2 weeks before transplanting: all the compost, all the phosphorus, and half of the nitrogen and potassium |
| Top dressing | First about 20 days after transplanting (nitrogen, potassium), second at the start of heading (nitrogen, potassium; the most important), third 15-20 days after the second (mainly potassium) |
| Substitute resources | Urine diluted 8-10 times (poured between plants; it burns leaves if it touches them, and stops 3 weeks before harvest), well-rotted compost (half-rotted compost burns roots), wood ash, a handful or two per ㎡ (useful at heading, but above pH 7 it blocks boron uptake), burnt and ground eggshells and shells (calcium), a leguminous green manure crop before it (peas, Chinese milk vetch, or hairy vetch plowed under and left 3-4 weeks before transplanting), oil cake and rice bran, grass liquid fertilizer (cut grass steeped in water for 2-3 weeks and diluted 5 times) |

> **Top dressing at heading decides the success of cabbage. If nutrients run short at this stage, there is no strength to push out the inner leaves and the head does not form or ends up loose.**

| Planting | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Seedlings | Sow in boxes or trays 1-2 cm apart and 0.5-1 cm deep. Germination takes 3-4 days at 25℃ and 7-10 days at 15℃. Raise for 30-40 days and transplant at 4-5 true leaves, hardening off by putting them out gradually in sun and wind from 1 week before transplanting. Soaking seed in 50℃ water for 20 minutes and cooling it at once in cold water prevents seed-borne diseases such as black rot |
| Transplanting | Plant down to the cotyledons (so the growing point is not buried). Early varieties 60 x 35 cm, mid and late varieties 70 x 40 cm (4,500-5,000 plants per 1,000㎡). Plant in the afternoon of a cloudy day or at dusk and water thoroughly at once |

## 5. Water Management and Cultivation Management
| Period | Water management |
|:---|:---|
| Right after transplanting to establishment | Soak thoroughly once, then once every 2-3 days |
| Vegetative growth | When the topsoil dries, 1-2 times a week, deeply each time |
| Heading | Steadily so it does not dry (the most important) |
| 10 days before harvest | Stop watering (storability rises and heads do not split) |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Water shortage at heading shows directly in head size, but worse is watering suddenly after drying out. The inside swells quickly, the outer leaves cannot keep up, and the head splits lengthwise; split heads cannot be stored, so eat them first. If the outer leaves are still drooping in the morning, it is water shortage; if the outer leaves turn yellow and the base softens, it is excess water and soft rot; if the lower leaves wilt first and droop in the daytime, suspect clubroot and dig up a root to check for galls. Without plastic, mulch with straw, dry grass, fallen leaves, or rice hulls, though under mulch is where slugs hide.

| Management | Details |
|:---|:---|
| Causes of failed heading | Heat (above 25℃ at heading: adjust the sowing date so heading falls at 15-20℃), lack of nutrients (outer leaves small and pale: top dressing at heading), lack of water (head small and hard: steady watering and mulch), crowding (outer leaves overlap and miss the light: 70 x 40 cm), damage to the growing point (when moth larvae eat the core, heading does not start: insect net and removing larvae before heading) |
| Plant care | Fill gaps 1 week after transplanting, first hilling at 3-4 weeks (5 cm of soil at the base), second hilling just before heading starts, and remove only diseased, yellowing outer leaves as needed. Stake in windy fields. Weeds are most critical 2-6 weeks after transplanting |

## 6. Pests, Diseases, and Nutrient Problems
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The pests are cabbage white larvae (April-June, September-October: hand-picking, insect net, rubbing off the yellow eggs on leaf undersides), diamondback moths (May-October: insect net, wood vinegar dilution), cabbage heart caterpillars (eating the growing point early after transplanting means no heading: early detection and removal), cabbage armyworms and tobacco cutworms (hide in the soil or behind outer leaves by day and come out at night: hand-picking at night), flea beetles (scatter ash, cover beds with straw), aphids (carry viruses: soapy water, washing off with a water jet, protecting ladybugs), and slugs (rainy season: catching at night, a ring of ash or rice hulls). The insect net (a net with 1 mm openings over hoops, edges sealed with soil, mind the ventilation) must be put on before heading starts, after removing every egg and larva on the leaves; put on after heading, it traps and raises the pests inside. Control agents are soapy water (20 g of unscented soap in 1 L of water, sprayed in the evening and washed off the next day), garlic and chili water (100 g of garlic and dried chili ground in 1 L of water and left a day; a repellent), wood vinegar diluted 500-1,000 times, and wood ash.

| Disease | Symptoms and conditions | Response |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Clubroot | Galls on roots, wilting by day. Acid soil, excess water | Cannot be cured (lives in soil over 7 years). pH 6.5 or higher, drainage, 7-year rotation |
| Soft rot | Base and head soften and stink. Hot and humid, wounds | Drainage, no wounds, remove diseased plants at once (especially fatal for stored cabbage) |
| Downy mildew | Yellow spots on leaves, gray-white mold underneath. Cool and damp | Ventilation, spacing, remove diseased leaves |
| Sclerotinia rot, black rot, leaf spot | White mycelium and black grains at the base / leaf edges yellowing in a V / spots with concentric rings | Drainage, removing infected plants, rotation / hot-water seed disinfection / ventilation and removing crop residue |

> **Clubroot cannot be cured with medicine and lives in the soil for over 7 years. For brassica fields, the only way is to prevent it in advance with acidity, drainage, and rotation.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;For cultural control, do not plant brassicas in succession for at least 2 years (7 years if there is clubroot), leave no base or outer leaves in the field after harvest (they host the next year's pathogens), mix in dill, coriander, and carrot flowers to call parasitic wasps and hoverflies, and protect ladybugs, lacewings, and spiders.

| Symptom | Cause and action |
|:---|:---|
| Lower leaves yellow and plant small | Nitrogen deficiency. Urine diluted 8-10 times, well-rotted compost, grass liquid fertilizer |
| Leaf edges scorch brown | Potassium deficiency. Wood ash around the plant |
| Inner leaf edges scorch (core browning) | Calcium deficiency or a sudden change in moisture (a physiological disorder, not a disease). Eggshell and shell powder, even watering |
| Head interior turns brown and hollow | Boron deficiency (common in fields given too much ash). Well-rotted compost |
| Leaves soft and tender and pests gather | Excess nitrogen (storability also falls). Stop nitrogen |

## 7. Intercropping and Crop Rotation
| Compatibility | Companions |
|:---|:---|
| Good | Legumes (peas, kidney beans, fava beans: fix nitrogen), Welsh onion, onion, garlic (the smell confuses moths and aphids), dill, coriander, carrot flowers (parasitic wasps, hoverflies), potato, beet, celery (different rooting depths, so little competition) |
| Poor | Kale, napa cabbage, radish, mustard, broccoli (share pests and diseases), strawberry and tomato (hinder each other's growth) |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Rotating cabbage (brassica, heavy feeder), peas and kidney beans (legume, restores nitrogen), potato and sweet potato, and Welsh onion and onion takes 4 years to bring cabbage back to the same plot. It is good to plant beans before growing cabbage.

## 8. Harvest and Seed Saving
| Harvest | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Timing | It is ready when the head holds firm under the palm (soft means the inner leaves are not yet full). Spring crop late June-July, autumn crop late October-November. If late, the head splits lengthwise and a flower stalk rises inside. Stop watering 10 days before harvest and harvest early on a clear morning |
| Method | Hold with one hand and cut the base with a knife (for storage, pulling up with the roots is better). Leave 2-3 outer leaves (they prevent water loss in storage and absorb shocks, and if the outside spoils, peeling off only that leaf leaves the inside sound). Handle without wounds, leave the head half a day in shade in the field to dry the surface moisture (pre-drying), then move it to a cool, dark place. If the base is left, a few small cabbages sometimes sprout from the side buds |

| Seed saving | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Difficulties | It is a biennial and needs cold to bolt (overwinter in the open or in a storage pit and transplant again in spring). The flower stalk cannot break through the hard head, so in spring cut a cross 3-5 cm deep in the top of the head. It is cross-pollinated and self-incompatible, so keep at least 5 and preferably 10 or more seed plants (carrying on from only one or two plants leads to inbreeding depression within a few generations and loose heads) |
| Crossing | It crosses completely with kale, broccoli, cauliflower, kohlrabi, and Brussels sprouts (B. oleracea). It hardly crosses with napa cabbage, bok choy, turnip, and rape (B. rapa) or with leaf mustard and mustard (B. juncea), and radish (Raphanus) is a different genus and does not cross |
| Prevention | Staggered growing (let only one flower in a given year; the most realistic), isolation of 500 m or more (hard, since neighboring fields cannot be controlled), bagging with a net (put a net on before flowering and move pollen inside with a brush) |
| Procedure | Select plants at harvest whose heads are firm, large, and disease-free. Overwinter in the open only in the south (hill soil at the base and cover with straw and leaves); further north, pull up with the roots and bury in a 0-5℃ pit. Transplant again in March, cut the head in March-April, bolt in April (stake), flower in April-May, and when two thirds of the pods turn brown in June-July, cut the whole stalk, dry it in shade for 1-2 weeks, then put it in a sack and beat it |
| Longevity | 4-5 years sealed at room temperature. Check germination every year by laying 20 seeds on wet cloth |

## 9. Storage
| Method | Conditions | Period | Vitamin C |
|:---|:---|:---|:---|
| Room-temperature shade | Cool, dark place | 2-4 weeks | Falls gradually |
| Storage pit | 0-4℃, humidity 85-95% | 3-4 months | Mostly kept |
| Sauerkraut | Ferment at room temperature, then a cool place | 6 months to 1 year or more | Kept or increased |
| Drying, salting | Shredded, blanched, shade-dried / salt 10-15% | 6 months to 1 year / a few months | Almost gone / greatly reduced |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;For a storage pit, dig a pit 1-1.5 m deep in a well-drained place, lay 10 cm of straw or dry grass on the bottom, set the heads upside down or on their sides with spaces so they do not touch, cover with straw and pile 30-50 cm of soil on top, stand a straw bundle or tube in the middle for ventilation, and open it once every 2 weeks to pick out spoiled ones. The goal is 0-4℃, where it does not freeze or sprout (too shallow and it freezes; too deep and it rots in the damp).

| Sauerkraut | Method |
|:---|:---|
| Preparing, salting | Set aside two or three outer leaves, cut out the core, and shred as finely as possible (the finer, the more juice comes out). Sprinkle on salt at 2-2.5% of the weight (20-25 g per kg) and knead by hand for about 30 minutes until the juice runs freely |
| Packing, covering | Pack it hard into a crock or glass jar with no air left so the brine rises above the material, cover with the set-aside outer leaves, and put a weight on top so the material is fully submerged in brine |
| Fermenting, keeping | 1-3 weeks at 15-20℃ (cover with cloth so air can escape). Once it turns sour, move it to a cool place (pit, basement, north-facing storehouse) and keep it 6 months to 1 year or more |

> **The single condition that separates success from failure is whether the material is completely submerged in brine. Mold grows only where it touches air.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;A white film floating on the surface is usually kahm yeast and is harmless.

## 10. Cooking and Precautions
| Method | Fuel | Vitamin C retention |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Raw (shredded) | None | Maximum (vitamin U is alive too) |
| Fermented (sauerkraut) | None | Kept or increased. Serves as storage and cooking at once |
| Blanching for 1-2 minutes | Very little | Half or less (volume shrinks and goitrogens decrease) |
| Soups and stews | Shared with other cooking | Kept if the broth is eaten too |
| Stir-frying, steaming | Little to moderate | Moderate (stir-frying raises sweetness and steaming suits wraps) |

| Precaution | Details |
|:---|:---|
| Gas, goitrogens | No acute toxicity. Oligosaccharides (raffinose) ferment in the colon and cause gas and bloating, so eat a little at first and cooked. The goitrogens common to brassicas can interfere with iodine uptake, but they decrease with cooking, seaweed can be eaten with it, and the content is lower than in kale |
| Spoiled cabbage | If it discolors, turns slimy, or stinks, peel off the outer leaves if only they have spoiled, and throw it away if the softness reaches the inside |

## 11. Annual Work Schedule
| Month | Work |
|:---|:---|
| Jan-Feb | Check the storage pit (once every 2 weeks), eat sauerkraut, plan the field, turn compost, check overwintering seed plants |
| Mar-Apr | Start spring sowing and seedlings, transplant seed plants again and cut their heads. In April harden off and transplant spring seedlings, and manage flowering seed plants and isolation |
| May-June | Vegetative growth management, first top dressing, start watching for butterflies and put on insect net (May). Second top dressing at heading, harvest from late month (June) |
| July-Aug | Finish the spring harvest and save seed, start autumn sowing and seedlings (July). Autumn seedlings and transplanting, mulch and water in the hot season (August) |
| Sep-Oct | Vegetative growth management and top dressing, insect net at the second moth peak (September). Heading management and top dressing, harvest from late month (October) |
| Nov-Dec | Autumn harvest and pre-drying, storage pit, making sauerkraut (November). Check the pit, overwinter seed plants (December) |

> **Five things not to forget: fit heading to 15-20℃, top dress at heading, put on the insect net before heading, put late varieties in the pit, and keep winter vitamin C with sauerkraut.**
