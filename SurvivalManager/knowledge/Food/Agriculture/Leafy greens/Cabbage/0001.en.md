## Introduction

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Cabbage (Brassica oleracea var. capitata) is the same species as kale. Among the several lines that branched off from wild cabbage on Mediterranean coastal cliffs, cabbage is the one people selected and bred for its tendency to wrap its leaves into layers. Capitata means "forming a head," and that head is what heading (head formation) produces. Kale keeps its leaves spread open, while cabbage rolls them inward, and this single difference sets the two crops completely apart in character.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The fact that the leaves are bundled together like a ball has a clear meaning from a disaster-preparedness standpoint. The outer leaves wrap around the inner leaves and become natural packaging that blocks moisture loss and pathogen entry, so even though cabbage is a leafy vegetable, it holds up for months. Cabbage's storability grade is C, lasting 2-3 months whole at unpowered room temperature and 3-4 months in a root-cellar pit. Compared with kale's D, lettuce's E, and leaf mustard's D, cabbage is clearly ahead of the other leafy vegetables. Fermented into sauerkraut on top of that, the period stretches past a year. Cabbage is nearly the only route to keeping a leafy vegetable's nutrition through the whole winter without a refrigerator.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Looked at by calories, though, cabbage is still a leafy vegetable in the end. It has 29kcal per 100g, and its calories-per-area grade is B, but that B comes from a heavy yield of 6kg per 1m², not from calorie density. At 0.67× the self-sufficiency line, cabbage alone cannot fill a stomach. The Supplementary grade, by contrast, is SS at 58.8 points. Cabbage follows the same pattern that repeats through this table: low calories paired with high value as a side dish. Cabbage's place is not in the rice bowl but in the other half of the table.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The biggest weakness is time. The speed-to-harvest grade is C, at 120-150 days converted to direct sowing. That figure includes 40 days of raising seedlings, and compared with kale's 60-70 days and leaf mustard's 45-60 days, it is more than double. Perenniality is D. Once cut, that is the end of it, and it does not regrow. This means cabbage is not a crop to plant for something to eat right away, at the moment a disaster has just begun. From the start, cabbage is a crop planted with winter in mind. You plant it in summer, harvest in autumn, put the head in a root-cellar pit, and eat it through to the following spring.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There is also the peculiar difficulty of heading itself. The process by which leaves wrap into a firm head only happens when temperature, nutrients, and water are all in the right range. Above 25°C the leaves stop wrapping, and if fertilizer or water runs short during heading, the leaves stop wrapping too. The most common failure in growing cabbage is ending up with nothing but a lush spread of leaves and no head. This is where the low fertilizer-demand grade of C and cabbage's heavy nutrient requirement of 32g per 1m² come from. Its zero-input yield retention is also only 40.8%, so once the fertilizer supply is cut off, heading collapses. Section 8 takes this problem on directly.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In short, cabbage takes a great deal of work and a long time, but in exchange it is a crop that lets you hold on to what you harvest all winter long. Leaf mustard, lettuce, and radish supply what you eat quickly, while cabbage prepares what you eat in winter.
