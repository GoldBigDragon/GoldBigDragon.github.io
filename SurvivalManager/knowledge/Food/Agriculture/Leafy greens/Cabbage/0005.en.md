## 8. Crop Management - Forming the Head

### 8-1. Why Heading Fails

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The most common failure in growing cabbage is neither disease nor pests, but poor heading. The leaves grow lush but no head forms, or if one does form, it is only fist-sized and loose. The cause is usually one of five things.

| Cause | How It Shows Up | Response |
|---|---|---|
| High temperature | If the temperature during heading exceeds 25°C, the leaves keep unfolding | Adjust sowing time so heading falls in the 15-20°C range |
| Nutrient shortage | Outer leaves are small and pale, and inner leaves are not pushed out | Be sure to apply top-dressing during heading |
| Water shortage | The head is small and firm but does not grow | Water steadily and mulch during heading |
| Overcrowding | Outer leaves overlap each other and miss light | Keep to a 70 x 40cm spacing |
| Growing-point damage | If moth caterpillars eat the core, heading never starts at all | Cover with insect netting before heading and remove caterpillars |

> **Heading is the work of pushing the nutrients the outer leaves make into the inside. Growing ten to fifteen outer leaves large and dark is almost the whole of heading management.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This principle gives rise to a practical rule. Up to 6 weeks after transplanting, focus on growing the outer leaves. Give plenty of nitrogen and keep to proper plant spacing so light reaches evenly. Do not pick and eat outer leaves during this period. If you treat cabbage with the habit of picking lower leaves the way you do with kale, heading collapses. The outer leaves are cabbage's factory, and if you tear the factory apart to eat it, no product comes out.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Once 6 weeks have passed since transplanting and the inside starts wrapping, change direction. Keep giving nitrogen but increase potassium. Keep water uniform. The 4 to 6 weeks from this point to harvest determine the head's size and firmness.

### 8-2. Hilling and Plant Care

| Task | Timing | Details |
|---|---|---|
| Filling gaps | 1 week after transplanting | Fill dead spots with spare seedlings |
| 1st hilling | 3-4 weeks after transplanting | Mound 5cm of soil at the base |
| 2nd hilling | Just before heading begins | Mound again to protect the roots |
| Removing outer leaves | As needed | Remove only diseased or fully yellowed outer leaves |
| Staking | Windy fields | Once the head grows large and heavy, plants can lean over |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Hilling protects the roots and stabilizes the plant. Cabbage's fine roots spread shallowly, so they are easily exposed by weeding or wind and rain, and exposed roots dry out or get damaged. Mounding soil at the base can also cause new roots to grow from that spot, increasing nutrient uptake.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Removing outer leaves is done with care. It is not a task of diligently picking leaves the way you do with kale. Remove only diseased leaves and ones that have turned completely yellow. Leave outer leaves that are still green. Even at harvest, leave two or three outer leaves attached, because they become a protective layer during storage.

### 8-3. Weed Management

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The critical period is 2-6 weeks after transplanting. If weeds grow taller than the outer leaves during this time, cabbage loses the competition for light and heading becomes difficult. Every 2 weeks, scrape only the surface 2-3cm shallowly with a hoe. Digging deep cuts the shallowly spread fine roots. Once the outer leaves grow large enough to cover the bed, their own shade suppresses weeds, so after that you only need to pull the large ones.

## 9. Pests and Diseases - Managing Without Pesticides

### 9-1. Major Pests

| Pest | Damage | Timing | How to Manage Without Pesticides |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cabbage white butterfly caterpillar | Green caterpillars eat everything but the leaf veins | April-June, September-October | Hand-picking, insect netting, rubbing out yellow eggs on leaf undersides |
| Diamondback moth | Small caterpillars scrape the leaf underside, leaving a thin film | May-October | Insect netting, frequent scouting, diluted wood vinegar |
| Cabbage shoot moth | Bores into the growing point of young seedlings, so no head forms at all | Early after transplanting | Early detection and removal, insect netting |
| Cutworm & tobacco cutworm | Large caterpillars devour leaves and heads | Summer-autumn | Hand-picking at night, killing early in the morning |
| Flea beetles | Riddle young seedling leaves with millet-sized holes | April-June | Insect netting, dusting with ash, covering the bed with straw |
| Aphids | Cluster on leaf undersides and inside the head, sucking sap. Transmit viruses | April-June, September-October | Soapy water, rinsing with a stream of water, protecting ladybugs |
| Slugs | Chew holes in outer leaves and the head's surface | Monsoon season | Picking at night, a band of ash or rice hulls |

### 9-2. Why You Must Stop Them Before Heading

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Pest management in cabbage has a deadline. Once the leaves start wrapping, there is no way to catch a caterpillar that has gotten inside. This is where you get a head that looks fine from the outside but, once split open, is full of droppings and holes. A head like that can neither be eaten nor stored.

> **Cover with insect netting before heading begins, and before that, remove every egg and caterpillar stuck to the leaves. Covering it after heading has begun just traps the pests inside and raises them there.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The cabbage shoot moth is especially dangerous early on. If this pest's caterpillar bores into the growing point of a young seedling, that plant cannot even begin heading at all. It shows up as an abnormally forked stem or several small shoots appearing. If you see a plant like this within 2 weeks of transplanting, pull it out and replace it with a spare seedling.

### 9-3. Netting and Physical Barriers

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;If you stretch netting with a mesh of about 1mm over hoops to cover the whole bed and seal the edges down with soil, egg-laying by butterflies and moths is blocked. Organic-farming field-trial literature also records insect-netting cover as the most reliable method. Without netting, fine-mesh mosquito netting, thin cloth, or an old curtain can substitute.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The thing to watch is ventilation. If moisture gets trapped in cabbage during heading, soft rot explodes. Do not seal it completely; instead open one side during the day, or set the hoops high to keep an air layer. After a long rain, take the netting off and let it dry for about a day.

### 9-4. Hand-Picking and Natural Remedies

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;On a plot of about twenty cabbage plants, hand-picking alone provides substantial control. Cabbage white butterfly caterpillars are large and slow, so they stand out, and following the holes in the leaves usually leads you right to one nearby. Even more efficient is targeting the egg stage. If you see white butterflies flying around, the next day look on the undersides of leaves for elongated yellow eggs and rub them out, erasing a caterpillar before it even hatches.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The large caterpillars of cutworms and tobacco cutworms hide in the soil or behind outer leaves during the day and come out at night. To find them during the day, dig a little into the soil at the base of a badly damaged plant. Going out with a light after sunset makes them much easier to catch.

| Remedy | How to Make It | Target | Caution |
|---|---|---|---|
| Soapy water | Dissolve 20g of unscented soap in 1L of water | Aphids, mites | Spray in the evening and rinse off the next day |
| Garlic-chili infusion | Grind 100g garlic and dried chili peppers in 1L water and let sit for a day | Repellent | For prevention; it does not kill pests already present |
| Wood vinegar | Diluted 500-1,000× | General repellent | Leaves are damaged if too concentrated |
| Wood ash | Sift and dust onto leaves and soil | Flea beetles, slugs | Reapply after rain |

### 9-5. Major Diseases

| Disease | Symptoms | Conditions | Response |
|---|---|---|---|
| Clubroot | Galls on roots, wilting during the day | Acidic soil, waterlogging | Not curable. Keep pH at 6.5 or above, ensure drainage, rotate on a 7-year cycle |
| Soft rot | The base and head turn mushy and smell foul | High heat and humidity, wounds | Drainage, avoid wounding plants, remove diseased plants immediately |
| Downy mildew | Yellow spots on leaves, grayish-white mold underneath | Cool, humid weather | Ventilation, adequate spacing, remove diseased leaves |
| Sclerotinia rot | Base turns mushy with white mycelium and black sclerotia | Cool, humid conditions | Drainage, remove infected plants, crop rotation |
| Black rot | Leaf edges turn yellow in a V-shape | High heat and humidity | Hot-water seed treatment, remove diseased plants |
| Alternaria leaf spot | Concentric-ring spots on leaves | Humid conditions | Ventilation, removing plant debris |

> **Clubroot cannot be cured with chemicals, and it survives in the soil for more than 7 years. For a crucifer plot, the only option is to prevent it in advance with three things: soil acidity, drainage, and crop rotation.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Soft rot is especially fatal for cabbage intended for storage. A head already infected in the field rots after going into the root-cellar pit, and that one head ruins the ones next to it as well. Since wounds are the entry point, do not throw or gouge heads at harvest, and do not harvest right after rain. Before storing, check each head individually, and pull out and eat first any whose base is soft or smells bad.

### 9-6. Cultural Control Principles

| Principle | Details |
|---|---|
| Adjusting sowing time | Avoid the peak period of moth activity. In autumn cultivation, transplanting in August overlaps with the September egg-laying period |
| Crop rotation | Do not plant crucifers back to back. At least 2 years, or 7 years if clubroot is present |
| Adequate spacing | Keep to 70 x 40cm. Airflow reduces fungal disease |
| Removing debris | Do not leave stumps and outer leaves in the field after harvest. They become a host for pathogens the following year |
| Companion planting | Dill, coriander, and carrot flowers attract parasitic wasps and hoverflies |
| Protecting natural predators | Do not kill ladybugs, lacewings, spiders, or parasitic wasps |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Removing debris matters especially for cabbage. Stumps and roots left in the field after harvest can survive until the following year and put out new shoots, and those shoots become a winter refuge for moths, aphids, and viruses. Once harvest is finished, dig out the stumps, carry them out of the field, and put them in compost or feed them to livestock. If clubroot was present in the field, do not even put them in compost; burn them or bury them deep, far away.

## 10. Nutrient Deficiency and Excess

| Symptom | Cause | Natural Remedy |
|---|---|---|
| Lower leaves yellow first, plant stays small | Nitrogen deficiency | Urine diluted 8-10×, well-rotted compost, weed liquid fertilizer |
| Leaf edges scorch brown | Potassium deficiency | Scatter wood ash around the plants |
| Leaves are dark blue-green and stop growing | Phosphorus deficiency | Bone meal, wood ash |
| No heading, or a loose head | Nitrogen and potassium shortage | Be sure to apply top-dressing during heading |
| Inner-leaf edges scorch brown (internal browning) | Calcium deficiency or abrupt moisture change | Eggshell/shellfish-shell powder, uniform watering |
| The inside of the head browns and hollows out | Boron deficiency | Well-rotted compost. Common in fields given too much ash |
| New leaves yellow between the veins while veins stay green | Magnesium or iron deficiency | Check whether pH is too high and cut back on ash |
| Leaves are soft and tender, attracting pests | Nitrogen excess | Stop nitrogen. Storability also drops |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Internal browning is a physiological disorder that occurs often in cabbage, not a disease. It happens because calcium fails to reach all the way inside the head. Calcium travels along the flow of water, and leaves inside the head have almost no transpiration, so water barely reaches them. This is why it can appear even when the soil has plenty of calcium. It gets worse when the plant swings between sudden dryness and waterlogging, so uniform watering is often more effective than adding calcium.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Boron deficiency is common in fields that have had a lot of ash applied. Ash is alkaline and raises pH, and once it passes 7.5, boron locks into a form that will not dissolve in water, so roots cannot absorb it. If the inside of the head starts browning and hollowing out, the answer is not to add more ash but to stop it, and gradually bring the soil back with well-rotted compost to raise organic matter.

## 11. Intercropping and Crop Rotation

| Compatibility | Partner | Effect |
|:---:|---|---|
| Good | Legumes (peas, kidney beans, fava beans) | Fix nitrogen, leaving nitrogen for cabbage to use |
| Good | Welsh onion, onion, garlic | Their smell confuses moths and aphids |
| Good | Dill, coriander, carrot flowers | Attract parasitic wasps and hoverflies, reducing aphids |
| Good | Potato, beet, celery | Different root depths mean less competition |
| Neutral | Lettuce, spinach | Cover the space between beds early on, suppressing weeds |
| Bad | Kale, napa cabbage, radish, leaf mustard, broccoli | Share pests and diseases, which build up |
| Bad | Strawberry, tomato | Hinder each other's growth |

```
Cabbage (crucifer, heavy feeder) → Pea/kidney bean (legume, nitrogen recovery) → Potato/sweet potato (nightshade family/bindweed family) →
Welsh onion/onion (lily family) → back to cabbage
```

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Cycling through this order takes 4 years for cabbage to return to the same spot. The A for continuous-cropping tolerance in the evaluation table means a 2-year break is recommended, but if clubroot has been confirmed in the field, that should be extended to 7 years or more. The key is not to insert radish, leaf mustard, or kale in between, because they are the same crucifer family.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Before growing cabbage, it is good to plant beans first. Cabbage is the heaviest feeder among the leafy vegetables covered in this document, and legumes make their own nitrogen. If you grow peas or fava beans from winter into spring and turn them under around flowering, the nitrogen held in their root nodules returns to the soil. Transplanting cabbage 3-4 weeks after turning them under, once they have broken down, replaces a substantial part of the base fertilizer. Under conditions with no chemical fertilizer, this one sequence alone can decide whether heading succeeds or fails.
