## 1. Disaster-Preparedness Evaluation

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Welsh onion's overall grade is C, with a score of 44.2. By scenario, its short-term ability to get through the early stage of a disaster is C (44.0), its long-term self-reliance over several years is B (44.4), and its Supplementary value, which treats it as a side-dish resource beyond the staple food, is SS (61.0). Two facts sum up the character of Welsh onion: the long-term score is higher than the short-term one, and the Supplementary score is 17 points higher than the overall score. It is not a crop for use in a hurry but a side-dish crop to keep and use over a long time.

### 1-3. How the D in Speed to Harvest Shaped the Score

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Why Welsh onion's overall score is a full 8 points lower than garlic chive's becomes clear quickly when the criteria are compared one by one. Because the two crops belong to the same genus, they receive the same grade for pest & disease pressure, pest-control self-reliance, yield retention without synthetic inputs, yield stability, prevalence in South Korea and seed longevity. There are also criteria where Welsh onion does better. Micronutrients is A versus D, a large lead for Welsh onion, and protein density is SS versus B, again in its favor. Infrastructure needs also favor Welsh onion, SS versus A.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Yet the result is reversed. Speed to harvest is far apart at D versus A, and the grades also split on perenniality at S versus SS, cold hardiness at S versus SS, continuous-cropping tolerance at S versus SS, fertilizer demand at C versus B, and storability at D versus E. Of these, the one that weighed heaviest is speed to harvest. The figure of 200 days is among the slowest values for the leafy vegetables in this table, and compared with the 30 days of purslane or the 30 days of water spinach, it takes seven times as long.

> **Welsh onion is not a crop that can be planted after a disaster begins and eaten that same year. In disaster preparedness, its value holds only when it is already standing in the field.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Understanding this structure also explains why the short-term 44.0 and the long-term 44.4 came out almost the same. Its ability to respond early is low, but once it has taken hold, it gets through the winter, carries on through tillering, and lasts for several years. The pattern is one of recovering early losses later, and that is why the two values meet at a similar point.

### 1-4. How to Read the Strengths

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Five criteria carry an SS: Protein density, Infrastructure needs, Cooking & processing, Temperature range and Prevalence in South Korea. S went to Perenniality, Cold hardiness and Continuous-cropping tolerance, and A to Micronutrients, Tillage & digging energy and Yield stability.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Micronutrients, graded A, is the criterion most worth a close look. The figure of 15 nutrients is near the top among leafy vegetables and well ahead of the 8 of garlic chive, which belongs to the same genus Allium. Vitamin A, vitamin C, vitamin K, thiamin, riboflavin, pantothenic acid, folate and calcium head that list. The value runs against the perception of Welsh onion as only a seasoning, and in practice there is a nutritional difference between eating the Welsh onion cooked in a soup and fishing it out.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The D for Storability also looks low on its own, but reading the details shows a different story. The period grade itself was C, but the FAO storage loss rate for the same genus is high at 6.50%, which lowered it by one grade to D. One month at room temperature without power is very long by leafy-vegetable standards. Compared with kale at 1 week and garlic chive, purslane and water spinach at 2-3 days, it is ten times as long. On top of this comes the further point that Welsh onion can be stored by overwintering while standing in the field.

> **Welsh onion standing in a winter field works the same as a vegetable put in a storehouse. It does not freeze, it does not spoil, and a single stalk can be pulled and used on the day it is needed.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Tillage & digging energy A, with an energy return of 43.0×, is the highest value among the four crops this document covers. The 12cm deep tillage is done, but that labor is amortized over 5 years, and because Welsh onion's calories per area are on the high side for a leafy vegetable, the multiple becomes large. Where farming is done with a single spade, this multiple expresses the calories returned relative to the physical effort put in, and 43 times is a very good result for a leafy vegetable.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The basis for the S in continuous-cropping tolerance, that Allium is a group unharmed by continuous cropping, is also practical. Most crops suffer damage from soil diseases that build up when they are planted repeatedly in the same spot, but in Allium the root exudates actually suppress soil diseases. Where the field is small and a rotation is hard to arrange, a crop that can be grown in the same spot year after year is precious.

### 1-5. How to Read the Weaknesses

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Five criteria carry a D: Speed to harvest, Storability, Non-food value, Taste and Seed longevity. C went to Calories per area, Fertilizer demand and Yield retention without synthetic inputs.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The D for Speed to harvest is the heaviest weakness, as discussed above. However, knowing how those 200 days are made up leaves some room to respond. They consist of 60 days for raising seedlings and four to five months in the main field. But Welsh onion can be eaten without waiting out the whole period. By 40-50 days after planting, it reaches the thickness of silpa (thin spring onions), and even in that state it is an excellent vegetable. The 200 days are needed only to obtain a Welsh onion with a thick, white blanched shaft; if in a hurry, the plants can simply be pulled and eaten while still thin. The grade is a value assigned on the basis of the final product.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Fertilizer demand C, 30g per 1m², is on the heavy side in this table. Welsh onion is a heavy-feeding crop and takes up especially large amounts of nitrogen. This is why it is customary to apply top-dressing each time hilling is done. On top of that comes Yield retention without synthetic inputs of C, 40.8%. When chemical fertilizer stops, yield falls to 40%, and in Welsh onion this shows up as a blanched white shaft that is thinner and shorter. Section 5 deals with this problem.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The D for Seed longevity is exactly the same problem as in garlic chive. Allium seed contains fats that oxidize easily, so it does not last beyond 1 year at room temperature. No plan can be built on putting Welsh onion seed in a jar and using it several years later. Added to this is the matter of Seed saving, graded A. Welsh onion is cross-pollinated, so it crosses with other Allium crops nearby. This means seed can be collected but keeping the traits pure is not easy; Section 13 deals with it.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The D for Taste came from the finding that the smell and aroma are off-putting. It also reflects the fact that the measured sugars, 2.63g, fall short of 8.0, the baseline for the sweetness bonus. This grade, however, is an evaluation of Welsh onion eaten by itself. Welsh onion is not a vegetable eaten alone but one that adds aroma to other ingredients, and its value in that use is not captured in this grade. Section 16 returns to this.
