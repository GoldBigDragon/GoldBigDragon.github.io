## 8. Crop Management - Hilling and Tillering

### 8-1. The Principle of Hilling

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Hilling is the work of piling soil up around the base of growing Welsh onion. It is the most important management task in growing Welsh onion, and in effect it is this work that makes Welsh onion what it is.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The principle is simple. A plant's leaves make chlorophyll and turn green when they receive light, and when they receive no light they make no chlorophyll and stay white. In the column of leaf sheaths stacked layer upon layer in Welsh onion, the part buried in soil receives no light and so stays white, and that part becomes the blanched white shaft. The higher the soil is piled, the longer the blanched white shaft becomes.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The white color, however, is only a result; the change that matters to people lies elsewhere. Tissue that receives no light makes less tough fiber and instead accumulates sugar. This is why the blanched white shaft is tender and sweet. The difference whereby the green leaves are pungent and tough while the white part feels tender and sweet comes from here.

> **The length and thickness of the blanched white shaft are decided not by the seed but by how many times, and how high, a person has piled up the soil. A single-stalk Welsh onion that has never been hilled is close to silpa (thin young green onion).**

### 8-2. Timing and Depth of Hilling

| Round | Timing | Height piled | Done together |
|---|---|:---:|---|
| 1st | 1 month after transplanting | 5-6cm | Put fertilizer in the furrow and pile soil over it |
| 2nd | 1 month after the 1st | 10cm | Add fertilizer and pile soil |
| 3rd | 1 month after the 2nd | Enough to cover about 10cm of the part where the leaves split | Add fertilizer and pile soil |
| Finishing | 3-4 weeks before harvest or before overwintering | As needed | Also serves as winter insulation |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The most important reference line is the point where the leaves split off. The leaf sheaths of Welsh onion form a column, and at a certain height the leaves branch outward; that branching point must not be covered with soil. If soil gets into that spot, soil and water seep into the gaps between the leaf sheaths, and soft rot starts in that damp, airless space.

| Improper hilling | Result |
|---|---|
| Starting too early | Young plants are pressed down by the soil and growth is delayed |
| Piling too high at once | The leaf-split point is buried and soft rot develops |
| Piling only too shallowly | The blanched white shaft is short and there is little to eat |
| Piling wet soil | The soil hardens and air cannot pass through |
| Piling soil without fertilizer | Even when new roots form, they have nothing to feed on |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This is why the soil is piled up little by little in three rounds. Covering 30cm at once seems as if it would lengthen the blanched white shaft, but at that height the leaf-split point is already buried. As Welsh onion grows, the leaf-split point also rises, so the soil must be piled gradually, following that movement. It is accurate to see the work as matching the speed at which the plant grows with the speed at which a person raises the soil.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The condition of piling dry soil should also be noted. If wet soil right after rain is piled up, it hardens into clods, air cannot get through, and the blanched white shaft is damaged. The best time for hilling is when several clear days in a row have left the soil crumbly.

### 8-3. Dividing Tillered Plants

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This is the work that actually realizes the Perenniality S of the evaluation table. Welsh onion is a biennial, but if tillered plants are separated and replanted, the stand carries on without seed.

| Item | Details |
|---|---|
| Timing | In South Korea, early spring (March-April) or autumn (September-October) |
| Target | Plants with several stalks split off from the base |
| Method | Dig up the whole plant and split it apart by hand |
| Unit of division | One stalk at a time, or two or three stalks at a time |
| Trimming | Cut the leaves back by about half and tidy the ends of long roots |
| Replanting | Plant in a new furrow in the same way as transplanting |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The reason for planting with the leaves cut back by about half is the same as for garlic chive. When moved, the roots are severed and their ability to draw up water drops; if the leaves are left as they are, more water goes out than comes in, and the plant withers. Reducing the leaves restores this balance.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;What this cycle produces matters in disaster preparedness. When one plant splits into eight stalks, half of them are eaten and half are replanted. The following year, that half splits into several stalks again. It is a structure in which the field produces its own seedlings without any seed packet, and it goes straight around the weakness of Seed longevity D.

| Round | Time | Result |
|---|---|---|
| First transplanting | Year 0 | One plant |
| 1st division | Year 1 | Three or four plants, half of them for eating |
| 2nd division | Year 2 | About a dozen plants |
| 3rd division | Years 3-4 | A whole patch of field |

### 8-4. Weeds and Overwintering Management

| Timing (South Korea) | Task | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Early growth | Pull weeds while they are young | Welsh onion has narrow leaves that do not cover the ground, so it is weak against weeds |
| At every hilling | Bury them along with the soil piled up | Weed control and hilling become a single task |
| Late October | Finish the last hilling | The blanched white shaft is covered with soil and insulated |
| November | Apply fertilizer, mainly potassium | Raises cold hardiness |
| December to February | Cover with straw or fallen leaves | Especially needed north of central South Korea |
| All winter | Pull one stalk at a time when needed | The field is the storehouse |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;That hilling doubles as weed control is an efficient aspect of growing Welsh onion. In scraping soil from the furrow and piling it on the base, the young weeds in that spot are buried or cut off at the roots. A single operation does three jobs at once: it makes the blanched white shaft, buries the fertilizer, and removes weeds.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;It is also worth knowing that Welsh onion is weak against weeds. Its leaves are thin and stand straight up without covering the ground, so sunlight reaches the bare ground between plants unimpeded. If a creeping plant such as purslane gets in, it is covered over in no time. If weeding in the first month or two is neglected, afterward it becomes hard to get a hand in.

## 9. Pests and Diseases - Preventing Them Without Chemicals

### 9-1. Main Diseases

| Disease | Symptoms | Conditions | Response without chemicals |
|---|---|---|---|
| Downy mildew | Gray mold and yellow streaks on the leaves; leaves break over | Cool and humid, spring and autumn | Secure drainage and ventilation. Remove diseased leaves |
| Rust | Spots on the leaves that give off orange powder | Monsoon season, excess nitrogen | Carry diseased leaves out of the field. Supplement potassium |
| Soft rot | The inside of the blanched white shaft turns mushy and smells foul | Excess moisture, wounds, soil getting between the leaves | Cannot be treated. Prevent with drainage and hilling height |
| Leaf blight | Leaves dry from the tip and black spots form | Poor ventilation | Remove diseased leaves and widen plant spacing |
| Black spot | Oval black spots on the leaves | Hot and humid | Remove diseased leaves |
| Black sclerotial rot | The base rots and black granules form | Cool and humid, continuous cropping | Do not plant Allium in that spot for several years |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The Pest-control self-reliance B in the evaluation table was assigned on the basis of six papers on German organic onion trials, in which downy mildew was identified as the biggest constraint on Allium. The figure of 96% disease severity in the untreated control means that nearly everything gets infected if no chemicals are used. The situation is the same for Welsh onion.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;What matters, however, is that the countermeasures those papers presented were not chemicals. They consisted of nothing more than using resistant strains, widening plant spacing so air can pass through, and securing drainage. These can be done without chemicals, and although these three do not completely prevent the disease, they greatly reduce the speed of progress and the scale of damage.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Moreover, unlike leafy vegetables, the fact that something edible remains in Welsh onion even when the leaves are damaged acts as a buffer. Even when downy mildew damages half of the leaves, the blanched white shaft is often untouched, and the blanched white shaft is the main harvest of Welsh onion. This differs from kale, where damaged leaves mean losing everything.

### 9-2. Main Pests

| Pest | Damage | Season | Response without chemicals |
|---|---|---|---|
| Onion fly | Larvae eat the base and underground part, and the plant dies | Spring, autumn | Do not use insufficiently rotted compost. Scatter ash around the base |
| Onion leafminer | Larvae tunnel inside the leaves, leaving white lines | Spring, autumn | Remove damaged leaves. Covering with netting is the sure way |
| Beet armyworm | Larvae gnaw the leaves and bore into them | Summer to autumn | Pick off egg masses and young larvae by hand |
| Onion thrips | White spots form on the leaves and they take on a silvery sheen | Hot, dry periods | Keep the plants from drying out. Blue sticky traps help |
| Bulb mite | Clings to the underground part, sucks sap, and brings on rot | When overly wet | Fix drainage and remove diseased plants |

> **Among the pests of Welsh onion, the only one that actually kills plants is the onion fly. And the biggest cause that attracts that insect is insufficiently rotted compost.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In a disaster, when there is no chemical fertilizer and one must depend on compost, this point is especially dangerous. When compost is scarce, one is tempted to use even compost that has not rotted enough, but with Welsh onion that amounts to inviting onion flies into the field. Adults of this insect fly in on the smell of decaying organic matter and lay eggs in the soil nearby. If compost runs short, it is better to reduce the amount than to use compost that is not rotted enough.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Hand-picking works well against the beet armyworm. Because the mother lays eggs on the leaves in a mass, rubbing off a single mass amounts to catching dozens of pests. Once the larvae have grown and bored into the leaves they can no longer be reached by hand, so the knack is not to miss the stage when they are young and clustered on the outside of the leaves.

### 9-3. Control Without Chemicals

| Method | How to make and use | Target |
|---|---|---|
| Securing drainage | Raise the beds and dig the furrows deep | Soft rot, downy mildew, bulb mite, onion fly |
| Securing plant spacing | Keep to the plant spacing and do not plant densely | Downy mildew, leaf blight |
| Using only well-rotted compost | Let it rot until it gives off no heat and smells of earth | Onion fly |
| Wood ash | Sift it and scatter around the base and on the leaves | Onion fly, slugs. Also supplies potassium |
| Removing diseased leaves | Carry them out of the field and burn or bury them deep | Rust, downy mildew, black spot |
| Soapy water | 20g of unscented soap per 1L of water | Aphids, mites |
| Diluted garlic extract | Grind 100g of garlic in 1L of water, strain, and use | Repels pests |
| Cinnamon infusion | Steep 50g of cinnamon in 1L of water and use | Repels pests, suppresses mold |
| Wood vinegar | Dilute 300-500 times and spray | For prevention |
| Bordeaux mixture | Made from copper sulfate and quicklime | Downy mildew, rust. Only when the ingredients are available |
| Insect netting | Put on in spring and autumn | Adult onion flies, leafminers |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Here there is one stumbling block peculiar to Welsh onion. Because of the wax layer on the leaf surface, liquid control agents do not stick well and simply run off. Even if soapy water or wood vinegar is sprayed, it has no effect unless it stays on the leaf. The way to solve this problem is soap. Soap not only acts on aphids by itself but also serves as a spreader-sticker, lowering the surface tension of the liquid so that it spreads over the leaf. When garlic extract or wood vinegar is used, mixing in a very small amount of soap makes it cling to the leaf and stay.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;It is worth pointing out again that wood ash is especially useful in a Welsh onion field. Scattered around the base, it dries and alkalizes the surface so that egg-laying by onion flies decreases, and at the same time it supplies potassium, which raises disease resistance and cold hardiness. Scattered in the furrow before hilling and covered with soil, it does two jobs at once: disinfecting the underground part and fertilizing.

## 10. Nutrient Deficiency and Excess

| Symptom | Cause | Remedy using natural materials |
|---|---|---|
| The leaves are pale green all over and short, and the blanched white shaft is thin | Nitrogen deficiency | Pour urine diluted 8-10 times into the furrow before hilling and cover with soil |
| The leaves are unusually large and soft and fall over easily | Nitrogen excess | Stop fertilizing. The plant has become vulnerable to rust and soft rot |
| Older leaves scorch brown from the tips | Potassium deficiency | Scatter a handful or two of wood ash per 1m² |
| Growth is slow and the leaves are dark green with a purplish tinge | Phosphorus deficiency | Add bone meal and wood ash. The same symptom appears in cold periods too |
| New leaves twist and the tips dry out | Calcium deficiency | Burn and crush seashells and eggshells and add them |
| The areas between the veins turn yellow and only the veins stay green | Magnesium deficiency | Wood ash contains magnesium |
| The blanched white shaft does not thicken and only the leaves lengthen | Insufficient hilling or insufficient light | Not a nutrition problem. Check the hilling and the planting spot |
| Rust is unusually severe | Nitrogen excess, potassium deficiency | Reduce nitrogen and add wood ash |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The last two rows are items peculiar to Welsh onion. The seventh row is the case most easily mistaken for a deficiency symptom. If the blanched white shaft is thin and only the leaves are long, the cause is usually not fertilizer but that hilling was not done enough or that dense planting left too little light. Adding more nitrogen then only lengthens the leaves further and softens the tissue, making the plant vulnerable to disease. Misidentifying the cause doubles the loss.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The eighth row shows the link between disease and nutrition. When nitrogen is in excess, cell walls become thin and the tissue soft, and in that state rust and soft rot increase sharply. Potassium, conversely, thickens cell walls and firms up the tissue. So if disease is severe in a Welsh onion field, the balance of fertilizer should be examined before looking for chemicals. It is common for disease to appear especially severe in a field where only nitrogen has been applied and no potassium.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;It should also be noted that the symptom of phosphorus deficiency in the fourth row cannot be told apart from the symptom of a cold period. In early spring, when soil temperature is low, the roots cannot take up phosphorus even when the soil has plenty, and the leaves take on a purplish tinge. Recovery comes on its own as temperatures rise, so a purplish color seen in March and April (early spring in South Korea) usually just calls for waiting.

## 11. Intercropping and Crop Rotation

### 11-1. Compatibility

| Compatibility | Crop | Reason |
|:---:|---|---|
| Good | Carrot | The smell of carrot drives off flies, and the smell of Welsh onion drives off the carrot fly |
| Good | Tomato, eggplant, chili pepper | Allium root exudates suppress wilt and late blight |
| Good | Strawberry | The observation that it reduces root rot has long been made |
| Good | Lettuce, spinach | They use the empty space between ridges and do not compete with each other |
| Bad | Legume crops (soybean, pea, kidney bean) | Allium exudates suppress root-nodule bacteria |
| Bad | Plants of the same genus Allium (garlic chive, garlic, onion) | Pathogens and pests build up together |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The combination of carrot and Welsh onion in the first row is a rare case of each protecting the other. The carrot fly that attacks carrots comes looking after the smell of carrot but is thrown off by the smell of onion, and the onion fly that attacks Welsh onion comes after the smell of onion but is thrown off by the smell of carrot. If the two crops are planted in alternating rows, damage drops on both sides. This is the most efficient of the control methods that need no chemicals, and it does not use any additional field area either.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The fifth row is a taboo that must be observed. The sulfur compounds that Allium plants give off affect not only pathogens but also the root-nodule bacteria living on the roots of legume crops. Because the ability of soybean to make its own nitrogen comes from these bacteria, soybean planted next to Welsh onion forms few root nodules and fixes less nitrogen. In a situation without chemical fertilizer, nitrogen fixation by soybean is a pillar that maintains the fertility of the whole field, so avoiding this combination is not a mere matter of compatibility.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Placing them before and after each other with time in between is a different matter. If soybean is harvested, its residue buried, and Welsh onion planted in that spot, the Welsh onion receives that nitrogen. The problem is planting them side by side at the same time; linking them in sequence is actually beneficial.

### 11-2. Crop Rotation

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Continuous-cropping tolerance in the evaluation table is S, and the basis is that Allium is a group unharmed by continuous cropping. Even when planted repeatedly in the same spot there is no serious damage, and in fact soil where Allium has grown has a lower density of soil diseases, which favors the next crop.

| Situation | Response |
|---|---|
| Normal continuous cropping | There is no problem planting repeatedly in the same spot |
| A spot where black sclerotial rot appeared | Do not plant Allium for several years |
| A spot with severe onion fly damage | Rest it for a year and correct the compost management |
| A spot where Welsh onion has been cleared | Soil diseases are reduced, which favors the next crop |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;One example of a rotation order is a sequence in which a legume crop is planted where Welsh onion was harvested to restore nitrogen, then a leafy or fruit vegetable is brought in, and after that the cycle returns to Welsh onion. In this order, Welsh onion plays the role of cleaning up the soil, the legume plays the role of replenishing nitrogen, and the fruit vegetable makes use of the result.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The value that the fact of Allium being a group unharmed by continuous cropping has in a disaster situation deserves a separate mention. When the field is small and there is no room to work out a rotation chart, crops for which one can designate a plot and keep planting Welsh onion there are rare. The very fact that there is a crop that can simply be planted even when plans have collapsed is a safeguard.

### 11-3. Siting in Practice

| Criterion | Details |
|---|---|
| A place where water does not stand | The top-priority condition |
| A place with deep soil and few stones | Plenty of soil is needed for hilling |
| A place where the space between ridges can be used wide | Hilling soil is taken from alongside |
| A place where less snow piles up in winter and that is easy to reach | It is the spot to pull from all winter |
| Next to carrot or fruit vegetable ridges | Also gives the intercropping benefit |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The fourth row is a criterion that exists only for Welsh onion. Welsh onion stands in the field even in winter, and that is when it is most needed, so if it is placed where deep snow piles up or the path freezes and is hard to reach, it cannot be used in the very season it is needed. A spot close to the house with good sun, where the snow melts quickly, suits Welsh onion for winter.
