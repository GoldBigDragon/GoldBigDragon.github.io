## Introduction

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Welsh onion (Allium fistulosum) is a biennial plant of the genus Allium in the Amaryllidaceae family. Its natural life runs as follows: it sprouts from seed, grows for one year, goes through the cold of winter, sends up a flower stalk the next spring, sets seed, and dies. People, however, step in partway through that life. They harvest before the flowers open, cover the base with soil to lengthen the white part, and separate the young plants that split off at the side and replant them. As a result, a biennial plant carries on in the field for several years. The Perenniality entry in the evaluation table, grade S with continuous harvest for 2-4 years when tillering is used, is the result of this intervention.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This puts Welsh onion in the same category as garlic chive and purslane, but its character is different. Garlic chive roots overwinter on their own, and purslane seeds remain in the soil on their own. Welsh onion can be an annual vegetable or a semi-permanent crop lasting several years, depending on what people do with it. If a cycle is set up in which tillered plants are separated and replanted, the stand continues without seed; if the cycle is broken and everything is pulled and eaten, nothing is left the following year. This choice is the heart of how Welsh onion is handled in disaster preparedness.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In the evaluation table, Welsh onion's overall grade is C, with a score of 44.2. That falls well short of garlic chive's S with 52.2. Almost all of the gap comes from a single criterion: Speed to harvest is D, with 200 days or more from sowing to harvest. Raising seedlings alone takes 60 days, and the plants then need four more months in the main field. It is a crop sown in spring and eaten only in autumn, and this long wait greatly reduces its usefulness in the early response to a disaster. When food is needed right away, Welsh onion is not the answer.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In return for that long wait, however, Welsh onion gains something other vegetables lack: storability. Leafy vegetables usually get a storage grade of E and do not last more than two or three days at room temperature. Welsh onion keeps for a month at room temperature without any power. It also has an even more important property: it can get through the winter standing in the field. It can overwinter in the open field at 10°C below zero, and even when frost dries out the outer leaves, the inside and the blanched white shaft stay sound. The fact that Welsh onion pulled from a snow-covered field is a vegetable that can be eaten as it is carries great significance where there are no storage facilities.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The nutritional side is also worth a close look. Micronutrients is graded A, with 15 nutrients supplied at more than 10% of the daily recommended amount. Compared with 8 for garlic chive, which belongs to the same genus Allium, that is nearly double. Protein density is graded SS: the 1.78g of protein in 100g gives a P:E of 31% on 23kcal of energy. It is easy to think of Welsh onion only as a seasoning, but by the numbers it is a proper vegetable with a high nutrient density per calorie.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Three weaknesses remain. Fertilizer demand is graded C: Welsh onion is a heavy feeder that needs 30g of fertilizer per 1m², and when feeding stops, the blanched white shaft thins and the leaves become shorter. It is also very vulnerable to waterlogging, and where water stands, a whole year's crop can be lost. And seed longevity is graded D, with seed not lasting beyond 1 year at room temperature, so the strategy of stockpiling seed does not work. The answer to this last weakness is the cycle of keeping tillered plants alive, the same solution used for garlic chive.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In summary, Welsh onion is a crop to plant in advance. Sowing seed after a disaster has begun yields nothing that year. In exchange, Welsh onion already standing in the field becomes a storehouse that stays up all winter without freezing and can be pulled and used whenever needed. Its taste and aroma make a meager table bearable, and its nutrition fills the slot that grains leave empty. The wait is long, but in return it is a crop that stands for a long time.
