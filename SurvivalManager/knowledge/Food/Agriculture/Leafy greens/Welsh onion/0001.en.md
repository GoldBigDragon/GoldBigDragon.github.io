# Welsh onion

## Introduction
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Welsh onion (Allium fistulosum) is a biennial of the genus Allium in the family Amaryllidaceae, but through human intervention (harvesting before flowering, mounding soil to lengthen the white part, and dividing off side shoots to replant them) it carries on in the field for 2-4 years (perenniality S). Its overall grade is C (44.2 points, 68th of 183 valid species); long term B (44.4) is higher than short term C (44.0), and its supplementary-food grade is SS (61.0 points). Its biggest weakness is harvest speed D: it takes over 200 days from sowing to harvest, so planted after a disaster begins it yields nothing that year. In return it overwinters standing in the field down to -10℃ and can be pulled one stalk at a time whenever needed, and it is a respectable vegetable with protein density SS and micronutrients A (15 nutrients). Its other weaknesses are fertilization C (30 g per ㎡), waterlogging damage, and seed longevity D (under 1 year at room temperature). Welsh onion is a crop to plant ahead of time and to carry on by dividing and replanting tillered clumps.

> **Welsh onion is not a crop you can plant after a disaster begins and eat that year. In disaster preparedness its value holds only when it is already standing in the field.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;It scores 8 points below garlic chive overall almost entirely because of harvest speed D (200 days), seven times the 30 days of purslane and water spinach. Taste D is a judgment that the smell and aroma are off-putting, so its use as an aroma added to other ingredients is not captured. The grade for each item is in the Crop Evaluation Results document.

## 2. Plant Overview
| Item | Content |
|:---|:---|
| Classification | Biennial of the genus Allium in the family Amaryllidaceae. The species name fistulosum means hollow tube, which sets it apart from garlic chive with its flat, solid leaves. Native to cold inland areas from western China to Siberia |
| Leaves, blanched part | Leaves are hollow cylinders, and the white wax layer on the surface makes water roll off. The blanched part is the white part made of leaf sheaths stacked in layers and is not a true stem; the true stem is the flat disc at the very bottom. Unlike onion it does not grow a bulb but stacks leaf sheaths upward |
| Roots, flowers | White fibrous roots concentrated at a depth of 20-30 cm. Flowers are ball-shaped, white to pale purple, and bloom in the spring of the second year |

> **The part covered with soil gets no light, makes no chlorophyll, and stays white. The length of the blanched part is decided not by the plant itself but by how much soil a person has piled on.**

| Stage | Period | Key points |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Seedling stage | 40-60 days after sowing | A single thread-like leaf rises bent, and true leaves increase |
| Vegetative growth, harvest | 1-6 months after transplanting | Leaves increase and the blanched part lengthens, thickens, and firms |
| Bolting, seed set | Spring of the second year | The flower stalk rises and seed forms |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Bolting occurs when a seedling grown to pencil thickness (6-7 mm in diameter) or more goes through a period of cold at 13℃ or below, and the flower stalk pierces the center of the blanched part, making it inedible. Per 100 g: 23-34 kcal, protein 1.78 g (31.0% of calories), carbohydrate 5-7 g, sugars 2.63 g, and potassium 181 mg; vitamins A and K are concentrated in the green leaves, so discarding the leaves is a nutritional loss. Sulfur compounds form when tissue is wounded and are the source of the smell, antibacterial action, and pungency; they are weak to heat, so cooking brings out sweetness.

## 3. Growing Environment and Varieties
| Condition | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Temperature | Optimum germination around 20℃; optimum growth around 20℃, and above 25℃ growth slows and disease increases. It overwinters in the open field down to -10℃, and the dried outer leaves protect the inside, so do not strip them in winter. In the central region and north, or in cold years, cover with hilled soil and straw |
| Light | Yield stays at 86% under 40% shading. If light is short, leaves droop and tissue softens, so plants fall over easily |
| Water, soil | Deep, well-drained soil, pH 5.7-7.4. Total evapotranspiration about 550 mm, and it is relatively strong in drought |

> **The cause of losing an entire Welsh onion crop is almost always standing water. In a field submerged for a few days in the rainy season, fully grown plants rot from the base and fall over.**

| Type | Characteristics |
|:---|:---|
| Single-stalk type (stem onion) | Does not tiller; the blanched part is long and thick, and hilling is essential. One stalk is bulky, so storage efficiency is good, but once pulled it is finished, so seed is needed every year |
| Leaf type (tillering onion) | Tillers into 8-10 or more; the blanched part is short and thin, and hilling is not needed. It is continued by dividing clumps without seed, and suits shallow or gravelly soil |
| Joseon type (landrace) | Tillering and blanched part are intermediate, overwintering is reliable, and it is adapted to local climate, so it suits the central region and north |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;If a seed supply cannot be expected, choose the leaf type and the Joseon type; if the aim is a winter storage resource, choose the single-stalk type; realistically, keep both types together. The practical means of self-reliance is not seed but tillered clumps, so the degree of tillering matters more than whether it is F1 or a fixed variety.

## 4. Field Preparation, Sowing, and Transplanting
| Item | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Making the field | Dig 20-30 cm deep and pick out stones. Set wide row spacing, 75-85 cm for the single-stalk type and 1.5 m for the leaf type, to secure soil for hilling, and dig a furrow 15-20 cm deep and plant at its bottom |
| Fertilization (1,000㎡) | Nitrogen equivalent to 25 kg of urea, phosphorus equivalent to 6.6 kg of fused phosphate (all as basal fertilizer), potassium equivalent to 14 kg of potassium chloride, and fully matured compost 1,500 kg. Top-dress three times, once a month after transplanting, together with hilling: put the fertilizer in the furrow first and pile the soil on afterward |
| Substitute resources | Urine diluted 8-10 times (poured into the furrow before hilling and covered with soil), fully matured compost (poorly rotted compost attracts onion maggots), wood ash (a handful or two in the furrow before hilling), fermented oil cake |

| Cropping type | Sowing | Transplanting | Harvest |
|:---|:---|:---|:---|
| Spring crop | Early March-end of April | Early May-end of June | Mid September-end of April next year |
| Autumn crop | End of Aug-end of Sep | Early Oct-end of Nov | Mid April-end of May next year |

> **Keeping to the sowing time matters especially in autumn cropping. If sown too early and seedlings pass pencil thickness before winter, they go through cold, flower buds differentiate, and only a flower stalk rises the next spring.**

| Step | Method |
|:---|:---|
| Raising seedlings | Broadcast on a seedbed or sow in rows 10 cm apart, depth 5-6 mm. Germination in 7-10 days at 20℃, raise for 40-60 days, and keep the topsoil from drying. Skim off floating seeds and soak in warm water near 50℃, but above 60℃ the seeds die |
| Transplanting | When there are 4-5 true leaves and a stem thickness of 5-7 mm (thinner seedlings cannot withstand transplanting, thicker ones risk bolting). Plant spacing 7-12 cm for the single-stalk type and 12-15 cm for the leaf type, 2-3 plants at a time, laid on a slant at the bottom of the furrow with only the roots covered (about 3 cm); press the soil and water |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Plants are not buried deep because the growing point sits just above the bottom disc, and burying it deep makes new leaves struggle or rot; the blanched part is made by covering a little at a time while it grows.

## 5. Water Management and Cultivation Management
| Period | Water management |
|:---|:---|
| Sowing to seedling stage | Daily so the topsoil does not dry until germination, then once every 2-3 days in the seedling stage |
| Right after transplanting | Plenty, twice a week |
| Growth after establishment | Once a week, deeply in one go (shallow, frequent watering makes roots shallow), and once right after hilling |
| Winter | None (wet soil that freezes damages the blanched part) |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Welsh onion only slows its growth in drought and does not die easily, so when water must be saved its irrigation priority can be pushed back.

| Hilling | Timing | Height | Notes |
|:---|:---|:---|:---|
| 1st | 1 month after transplanting | 5-6 cm | Put fertilizer in the furrow and pile soil on top |
| 2nd | 1 month after the 1st | 10 cm | Same |
| 3rd | 1 month after the 2nd | Enough to cover about 10 cm of the part where the leaves split | Same |
| Finishing | 3-4 weeks before harvest, before winter (late October) | As needed | Also insulates for winter |

> **The length and thickness of the blanched part are decided not by the seed but by how many times and how much soil a person piles up. A single-stalk onion never hilled is close to a scallion.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The most important reference line is the point where the leaves split: if it is covered, soil and water seep between the leaf sheaths and soft rot begins. So pile the soil in three steps matched to the speed of growth, using dry, crumbly soil after several clear days (wet soil hardens and air cannot pass). Hilling makes the blanched part, buries fertilizer, and removes weeds all at once, and because Welsh onion has narrow leaves that do not cover the ground and is weak against weeds, do not skip weeding in the first month or two.

| Management | Method |
|:---|:---|
| Dividing tillered clumps | In early spring (March-April) or autumn (September-October), dig up a whole clump with several stalks split from the base and divide it into one stalk or two or three at a time. Cut the leaves by about half and trim long root tips (water balance), and replant in a new furrow the same way as transplanting. If one clump splits into eight stalks, eat half and replant half, so it continues without seed |
| Overwinter care | Finish the last hilling in late October and cover with 5-10 cm of straw or fallen leaves from December to February (especially in the central region and north) |

## 6. Pests, Diseases, and Nutrient Problems
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Pest-control self-reliance B rests on six German organic onion field studies, in which downy mildew was the greatest constraint on the genus Allium and disease severity in the untreated control was 96%. The responses were resistant lines, wide plant spacing, and securing drainage, and since the blanched part, the main harvest, remains even when leaves are damaged, it acts as a buffer. Because the wax layer on the leaves makes liquid control agents run off, mix in a very little soap (20 g of unscented soap per 1 L of water) as a spreader.

| Pest or disease | Symptoms and conditions | Control without chemicals |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Downy mildew, rust, leaf blight, black spot | Gray mold and yellow streaks (cold and humid) / orange powdery spots (rainy season, excess nitrogen) / black spots (poor airflow, hot and humid) | Secure drainage, airflow, and spacing; carry affected leaves out of the field and burn or bury them; supplement potassium |
| Soft rot | The inside of the blanched part turns mushy with a foul smell. Excess water, wounds, soil getting between leaves | Cannot be treated. Prevent with drainage and hilling height |
| Black sclerotial rot | The base rots and black granules form. Cold and humid, continuous cropping | Do not plant the genus Allium on that spot for several years |
| Onion maggot | Larvae eat the base and underground part and the plant dies. Caused by poorly rotted compost | Use only fully matured compost, sprinkle wood ash at the base, insect netting |
| Armyworm, thrips | Chew the leaves / white spots on leaves (hot and dry) | Catch egg masses and young larvae by hand / keep from drying out |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Soft rot progresses inside the blanched part and cannot be seen from outside, so by the time the outer leaves yellow and the stalks lean, it is already too late.

| Symptom | Cause and remedy |
|:---|:---|
| Whole leaf pale green and short, thin blanched part | Nitrogen deficiency. Pour urine diluted 8-10 times into the furrow before hilling and cover with soil |
| Leaves unusually large and soft, falling over, and severe rust | Nitrogen excess, potassium deficiency. Stop fertilizing and add wood ash |
| Blanched part does not thicken and only the leaves lengthen | Too little hilling or too little light from dense planting. Not a nutrition problem, so check hilling and site |

## 7. Intercropping and Crop Rotation
| Compatibility | Companions |
|:---|:---|
| Good | Carrot (each other's smell drives off flies, so plant rows alternately), tomato, eggplant, and pepper (root exudates suppress wilt and blight), lettuce and spinach (use the space between rows) |
| Poor | Legumes (soybean, pea, kidney bean: Allium exudates suppress nodule bacteria and reduce nitrogen fixation), the same genus Allium (garlic chive, garlic, onion: pathogens and pests build up together) |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The problem is planting legumes and Welsh onion side by side at the same time; planting Welsh onion in a spot where soy was harvested and the residue buried is in fact beneficial. Continuous-cropping tolerance S is because the genus Allium is a group harmless to continuous cropping, so it can be planted again and again in the same spot and soil disease density falls as well. But a spot where black sclerotial rot has appeared is rested for several years, and a spot with heavy onion maggot damage is rested a year while compost management is corrected. Since it is pulled and used in winter too, a place with deep soil and few stones, where snow lies less and access is easy, suits it.

## 8. Harvest and Seed Saving
| Harvest | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Timing | Scallion stage 40-50 days after transplanting (thin but eaten as is), medium stage 2-3 months, full Welsh onion 3-6 months (when the blanched part is thick and firm). Winter harvest from after frost until spring as needed; the deadline is before the flower stalk rises in spring |
| Method | Pulling up by the roots (the blanched part is obtained whole and that plant is finished) / cutting leaves only (leave 3-4 cm above ground; new leaves come but no blanched part) / detaching tillered clumps (dig up a clump, divide it, eat only half, and replant) |
| Winter use | Leave fully grown plants unpulled in autumn and pull one stalk at a time as needed. Loosen frozen ground to the side to break ice and soil before lifting. If signs of a flower stalk appear in late March-April, pull what is left all at once and turn it to drying or pickling |
| Handling | Shake off soil and strip only one or two dried or damaged outer leaves. Wash only what will be used right away (washed ones spoil fast), and do not cut the roots of what will be stored. Do not discard green leaves; dry them |

> **For Welsh onion the question is not when to harvest but how long it may be left. Even after fully growing, it stands in the field through winter, and that state is itself storage.**

| Seed saving | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Barriers | As a biennial it must overwinter and wait for bolting in the spring of the second year, and the blanched part of that plant cannot be eaten. It is cross-pollinated and mixes with other Allium nearby, and seed longevity is under 1 year |
| Preventing crossing | Keep seed plants 500 m or more from other Allium, or cover them with netting and move the pollen by brush. In a small field, let only one kind flower that year and cut off the flower stalks of the rest |
| How to collect | In autumn mark a few of the thickest, straightest, disease-free plants and overwinter them unpulled (cover generously with soil). Bolting April-May, white ball-shaped flowers May-June. When pods dry brown and some begin to split, cut the whole stalk, hang it upside down, and catch the seed on cloth spread below. Skim off floating empty seeds in water and dry the sunken seeds completely at once |
| Storage | Under 1 year sealed at room temperature. In a cool, dry place in a sealed container with ash or charcoal. Collect only the seed that will be used that year, and stockpile by planting tillered clumps in several places |

## 9. Storage
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Storability is D, but it lasts one month at room temperature without power, and overwintering storage standing in the field is possible.

| Method | Procedure and period |
|:---|:---|
| Leave in the field | From autumn until before bolting next year. Fine as is down to -10℃, and cover with straw if colder |
| Reburying in soil | Two to three months. Pull up with roots and lay on a slant in cool, shaded soil so the blanched part is covered (the roots stay attached so it stays alive) |
| Stand upright in a cool place | 2-4 weeks. If laid down, the leaves bend and it spoils fast |
| Drying | Shake off soil, wash, and drain completely; cut leaves into 3-5 cm pieces and the blanched part into thin diagonal slices or rings, and dry. Ground to powder and mixed with salt it becomes a seasoning. Much of the aroma escapes, so dry slowly in shade and seal at once when fully dry |
| Pickling | Packed in doenjang (a few months), green onion kimchi (1-2 months in a cool place). Sulfur compounds are antibacterial, so it does not spoil even with little salt |

## 10. Cooking and Precautions
| Method | Details |
|:---|:---|
| Raw | Wash, shred, and dress or use as a garnish. Pungency and vitamin C stay intact |
| Scallion oil | Slowly stir-fry sliced Welsh onion in oil over low heat to transfer the aroma. One spoonful gives flavor to several meals |
| Roasting | Roasted whole, the pungent compounds break down and the sugar comes out, so it becomes much sweeter. Throw it in embers and peel off the charred skin |
| Pancakes, dried powder | Mix into batter and fry / serves as both storage and seasoning |

> **The key to cooking Welsh onion divides in two. For aroma, add it last; for sweetness, cook it long from the start. The same ingredient does two entirely different jobs.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Vitamins A and K are only in the green leaves, so chop the leaves and put them in soup, or dry them and use them as powder.

| Hazard | Details |
|:---|:---|
| Excess intake, raw on an empty stomach | Sulfur compounds irritate the stomach lining and cause heartburn |
| Dogs, cats, livestock | The whole genus Allium destroys red blood cells and causes anemia. Cattle and horses are the same if they eat a lot, so do not give them leftover food containing Welsh onion |
| Look-alike poisonous plants | When looking for Allium in the wild, it can be confused with daffodil and spider lily types (underground part resembles an onion; vomiting, abdominal pain) or lily of the valley (vomiting, abnormal heartbeat). A cut leaf has no smell, and the leaves are flat and solid |

> **If you cut a leaf, rub it, and it does not smell of onion or garlic, it is not Allium. This one check filters out nearly all the poisonous plants that look like Allium.**

## 11. Annual Work Schedule
| Month | Work |
|:---|:---|
| Jan-Feb | Pull Welsh onion standing in the field as needed and check the covering |
| Mar-Apr | In March, spring sowing and dividing tillered clumps. In April, seedling care, and harvest all the overwintered plants before the flower stalk |
| May-June | In May, spring transplanting, and leave the flower stalks of the seed plants. In June, 1st hilling (put fertilizer in the furrow and pile soil on), and tidy drainage ditches before the rainy season |
| July-Aug | In July, 2nd hilling and watch for downy mildew and rust. In August, 3rd hilling, autumn sowing from the end of the month, and check for armyworm egg masses |
| Sep-Oct | In September, start harvesting the spring-planted onions and divide tillered clumps. In October, autumn transplanting and the last hilling |
| Nov-Dec | In November, apply overwintering fertilizer centered on potassium. In December, cover with straw or fallen leaves and secure access to the winter harvest area |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The two most important rows are April and August. If the overwintered plants are not finished in April the flower stalks rise and they all become unusable, and an autumn sowing after the end of August that misses its timing loses next year's crop to early bolting. Dividing tillered clumps in March and September is this crop's self-reliance, that is, its seed storage.

> **There is nothing to gain from planting after a disaster begins, but Welsh onion already standing in the field becomes a storehouse that does not freeze all winter. Welsh onion is a crop to plant ahead of time and to carry on by dividing and replanting.**
