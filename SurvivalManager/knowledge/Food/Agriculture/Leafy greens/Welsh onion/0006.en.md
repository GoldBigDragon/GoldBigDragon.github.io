## 12. Harvest - The Field Is the Storehouse

### 12-1. Judging the Timing

| Stage | Criterion |
|---|---|
| Silpa (thin young green onion) | 40-50 days after transplanting. Thin, but can be eaten as it is |
| Jungpa (medium-sized green onion) | 2-3 months after transplanting |
| Daepa (full-sized Welsh onion) | 3-6 months after transplanting, when the blanched white shaft is thick and firm |
| Winter harvest | From after the frost until the following spring, whenever needed |
| Cutoff point | Before the flower stalks rise the following spring |

> **For Welsh onion, the key is not when to harvest but how long it may be left. Even after it is fully grown it stands in the field through the winter, and that state is itself storage.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The cutoff point in the last row is important. When the flower stalk rises in the spring of the second year, a hard stalk runs through the very middle of the blanched white shaft and makes it inedible. The outside looks sound, but cut open, it has a woody core lodged inside. The strategy of leaving it standing in the field and using it comes with this spring deadline, and once that line is passed, all the Welsh onion remaining at that point becomes unusable.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;So the handling of winter Welsh onion goes like this. The fully grown Welsh onion is not pulled in autumn but left as it is. All winter, it is pulled one stalk at a time as needed. If, in South Korea, signs of rising flower stalks appear in late March or April of the following year, whatever remains is pulled all at once and turned to storage by drying or salting. This point is the boundary where field storage passes over to processed storage.

### 12-2. Harvesting Technique

| Approach | Method | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Pulling up by the roots | Loosen the soil around the base with a homi (small hand hoe) and lift it | That plant is finished. The whole blanched white shaft is obtained |
| Cutting leaves only | Cut, leaving 3-4cm above the ground | New leaves grow back. The blanched white shaft is not obtained |
| Taking tillered plants | Dig up a clump, divide it, and eat only half | The rest is replanted to carry on |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;These three diverge according to purpose. To get a thick blanched white shaft, the plant must be pulled up by the roots, and then that plant is finished. To keep harvesting, only the leaves are cut, giving up the blanched white shaft. For a tillering strain, the third approach gets half of each.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In the leaf-only method, the rule to leave 3-4cm above the ground is for the same reason as in garlic chive. Welsh onion's growing point sits just above the basal disc at the bottom, and if it is cut off, the place where new leaves are made is gone. The 3-4cm is a margin left safely above it.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Pulling in winter, when the soil is frozen, takes some know-how. If it is simply pulled, the blanched white shaft snaps in the middle and stays behind in the soil. The ice and soil have to be broken first by loosening the side with a homi or spade, and only then lifted. For the Welsh onion to be used in winter, burying about one row shallowly in advance in autumn, or covering it thickly with straw, makes this work much easier.

### 12-3. Post-Harvest Handling

| Item | Details |
|---|---|
| Shaking off soil | Shake it where it was pulled to knock the soil off the roots |
| Outer leaves | Peel off only one or two dried or damaged outer leaves |
| Washing | Wash only what will be used right away. Washed onions spoil quickly |
| Roots | Do not cut the roots of what will be stored |
| Leaf part | Do not throw it away; collect it separately and dry it |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There is a reason the roots of Welsh onion to be stored are not cut. With the roots attached, the Welsh onion remains alive and keeps its moisture, and with no cut surface, there are also fewer entry points for microbes. The storage method of reburying it in soil with the roots on works for the same reason.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The last row is also an item not to be missed in a disaster situation. The green leaf part of Welsh onion is often thrown away in normal times, but a good many of the 15 nutrients behind the Micronutrients grade of A are in this part. Vitamin A and vitamin K are almost absent from the blanched white shaft and concentrated in the green leaves. Sliced and dried, they become soup stock material and a powdered seasoning for winter.

## 13. Seed Saving - Waiting for Bolting in the Second Year

### 13-1. Two Barriers

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In the evaluation table, Seed saving is A and Seed longevity is D. Seed can be collected, but there are two barriers.

| Barrier | Details |
|---|---|
| Biennial habit | To collect seed, the plant must get through the winter and wait for bolting in the spring of the second year |
| Cross-pollination | Bees carry the pollen, so it mixes with other nearby Allium |
| Short-lived seed | Seed collected must be used within 1 year |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The first barrier is a matter of time. Harvesting Welsh onion to eat and collecting seed are not done from the same plant. The plant for seed must be left unpulled in autumn to get through the winter and then be allowed to send up its flower stalk the following spring, and that plant's blanched white shaft becomes inedible. Seed saving is obtained by giving up something to eat.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The second barrier is trickier. Welsh onion does not fertilize well with its own pollen and instead receives pollen from other plants. So if another Allium is flowering nearby, bees bring in that pollen. It does not flower at the same time as garlic chive, but if jjokpa (small shallot-like onion), onion, and another strain of Welsh onion are present together, they mix.

| Isolation method | Details | Reliability |
|---|---|---|
| Distance isolation | Keep at least 500m from seed plants of other Allium | High, but difficult in practice |
| Timing isolation | Adjust planting time so that flowering periods do not overlap | Medium |
| Netting | Cover with netting before the flower buds open | High. In exchange, a person must carry the pollen with a brush |
| Saving seed from one strain only | Let only one kind flower that year | The most realistic |

> **In a small field, the most realistic method is to settle on a single Allium to collect seed from that year and cut off and remove the flower stalks of all the rest.**

### 13-2. How to Collect Seed

| Step | Details |
|---|---|
| Selecting seed plants | In autumn, choose and mark a few of the thickest, straightest, disease-free plants |
| Overwintering | Leave them unpulled to get through the winter. Cover generously with soil |
| Bolting | In South Korea, April to May of the following year, the flower stalks rise |
| Flowering | May to June; white flowers bloom in ball shapes |
| Judging maturity | When the flowers have faded, the capsules dry to brown, and black seeds are visible |
| Harvest | Cut the whole flower stalk and hang it upside down over paper or cloth |
| Separation | Rub the dried flower stalk by hand to shake out the seed |
| Cleaning and sorting | Soak in water and skim off what floats. What sinks is plump seed |
| Drying | Dry completely in the shade |
| Storage | Put in an airtight container together with ash or charcoal and keep in a cool, dark place |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Sorting by soaking in water is especially useful for Welsh onion seed. Hollow chaff floats, and seed with an intact embryo sinks. Because this is a crop with short seed longevity, raising the germination rate even slightly is valuable. However, seed that has been soaked in water must be dried completely right away, and if left wet it becomes unusable.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;When the capsules dry they split open on their own and the seed falls, so if the time is missed, the seed spills onto the ground. It is safe to cut the whole flower stalk and hang it upside down when the capsules have turned brown and a few have begun to split open, and if cloth is spread underneath, the seed that falls as it dries is caught as it is.

### 13-3. Storage and Longevity

| Condition | Germination ability retained |
|---|---|
| Airtight at room temperature | Under 1 year |
| A cool, dry place | 1-2 years |
| A damp or hot place | Drops sharply within a few months |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The reason Allium seed is so short-lived is known to be that the fats in the seed oxidize easily. Low temperature and low humidity slow oxidation, so the seed is kept in a cool, dry place, and ash or charcoal is put in the airtight container to absorb moisture.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The practical conclusion is the same as for garlic chive. Welsh onion's seed store is not a packet but the field. Rather than stockpiling seed, keeping tillered plants alive and planting them divided among several places is the stockpile for this crop. Seed is collected only in the amount to be used within that year.

## 14. Toxicity and Compounds of Concern

### 14-1. Safety of Welsh Onion Itself

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Welsh onion is a safe food at ordinary intake levels. It can be eaten raw or cooked. Three points are worth knowing, though.

| Item | Details |
|---|---|
| Excessive intake | Sulfur compounds irritate the stomach lining and can cause heartburn |
| Raw on an empty stomach | Eating a lot of raw Welsh onion on an empty stomach irritates more |
| Dogs and cats | All Allium is toxic to pets and some livestock |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The third row is the practical caution in a disaster situation. Allium plants, including Welsh onion, garlic chive, garlic and onion, destroy the red blood cells of dogs and cats and cause anemia. A compound that causes no problem for people is a poison to these animals. In a situation where giving leftover food to livestock or dogs becomes common, food containing Welsh onion must be left out of what is given. Cattle and horses can also develop the same kind of anemia if they eat large amounts.

### 14-2. Look-Alike Poisonous Plants - Telling Them Apart by Smell

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There are dangerous cases when looking for Allium plants in the wild rather than in the field. Poisonous plants that resemble green onions really exist, and poisoning accidents are reported every year.

| Feature | Allium (Welsh onion, garlic chive, dallae (wild chive)) | Daffodils and magic lilies | Lily of the valley |
|---|---|---|---|
| Smell of a cut leaf | Always smells of green onion or garlic | No smell | No smell |
| Cross-section of the leaf | Welsh onion is a hollow cylinder | Flat and solid | Flat and solid |
| Leaf texture | Soft and juicy | Thick and smooth | Thick, stiff, and glossy |
| Underground part | Bulb or flat basal disc | Round bulb (onion-shaped) | Slender rhizome |
| If eaten | Edible | Severe vomiting, abdominal pain, diarrhea | Vomiting, dizziness, abnormal heartbeat |

> **If a leaf is cut and rubbed and does not smell of green onion or garlic, it is not an Allium. This one check screens out nearly all poisonous plants that resemble Allium.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Daffodils and magic lilies are especially dangerous because their underground part resembles an onion. Accidents have repeatedly occurred in which bulbs dug up in a yard or an old flower bed were mistaken for onions or green onion roots. The leaves alone are enough to tell them apart. Welsh onion leaves are hollow cylinders and cave in when pressed with a finger, whereas daffodil leaves are flat bands and stay as they are when pressed.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Lily of the valley contains compounds that act on the heart and is dangerous even in small amounts. Its leaves are broad, so it is rarely confused with Welsh onion, but accidents in which it gets mixed in while picking sanmaneul (wild mountain garlic) are frequent. When looking for Allium plants in the wild, always check the smell first.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Welsh onion grown in the field carries no such risk. However, if daffodils have been planted near the Welsh onion field, their young leaves can get mixed in in spring, so not keeping such plants near the Allium beds is a simple preventive measure.

## 15. Storage Without Refrigeration

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The Storability in the evaluation table is D, but it comes with the value of one month at room temperature without power and the proviso that overwintering storage standing in the field is possible. This is a very good condition for a leafy vegetable, and of the four crops this document covers, Welsh onion is the best in terms of storage.

### 15-1. The Field as Storehouse

| Method | Duration | Conditions |
|---|---|---|
| Left in the field unpulled | From autumn until before bolting the following spring | As it is down to 10°C below zero. Cover with straw if colder |
| Reburying in soil | Two or three months | Pull up with roots and bury at a slant in soil in a cool place |
| Pit storage | Two or three months | Dig a pit, stack the onions in layers with soil, and cover with straw |
| Standing upright in a cool place | 2-4 weeks | If laid down, the leaves bend and spoil quickly |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The reburying method in the second row is practical. When the remaining Welsh onion has to be pulled all at once before bolting begins in spring, it is laid at a slant in soil in a cool, shaded spot and buried so that the blanched white shaft is covered. With the roots attached, the Welsh onion remains alive, and the soil keeps temperature and humidity steady. It is the same principle as burying radishes in the ground for gimjang (making kimchi for the winter).

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The tip in the fourth row to keep it standing upright comes from a property common to Welsh onion and garlic chive. When laid down, the leaves try to bend upward, spending energy on that effort, and the tissue breaks down quickly. When kept standing, this reaction does not occur and several days to a week or two more can be gained.

### 15-2. Drying

| Step | Details |
|---|---|
| Preparation | Shake off the soil, wash, and drain completely |
| Cutting | Leaves into 3-5cm lengths; the blanched white shaft sliced thin on the diagonal or into rings |
| Spreading out | A shaded, well-ventilated place. Drying in the sun loses color and aroma |
| Judging completion | Until it crumbles when rubbed by hand |
| Making powder | Pound the completely dried material in a mortar |
| Storage | Put in an airtight container together with ash or charcoal |
| Duration | Well-dried, airtight material keeps from half a year to a year |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Making Welsh onion into powder is especially valuable in a disaster. The volume shrinks greatly so it takes up almost no storage space, and a spoonful in soup or juk (rice porridge) gives off aroma. Mixed with salt, it becomes a seasoning in itself. In a situation where seasonings have run out, a jar of dried Welsh onion powder is a resource that makes a meal bearable.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;It should be noted, however, that a large part of the aroma escapes during drying. This is because the sulfur compounds, the source of the smell, are volatile. Drying slowly in the shade reduces the loss somewhat, and sealing immediately after it is fully dry preserves the remaining aroma.

### 15-3. Salting and Fermenting

| Method | Procedure | Duration |
|---|---|---|
| Salting | Layer with salt at 5-10% of the weight | 3-6 months |
| Jjanji (heavily salted pickle) | Submerge in brine of 10% or stronger | Half a year or more. Soak in water before eating to remove the saltiness |
| Jangajji in soy sauce (pickled vegetables in soy sauce or paste) | Submerge in soy sauce | A few months |
| Doenjang embedding | Embed the blanched white shaft in doenjang (Korean soybean paste) | A few months |
| Pa-kimchi (Welsh onion kimchi) | Salt, then toss with seasoning | 1-2 months in a cool place |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Welsh onion suits pickling well because the sulfur compounds themselves have an antibacterial action. It does not spoil even with less salt than other vegetables, and in a situation where salt is scarce this difference is not small. Moreover, pickled Welsh onion loses its pungency and gains sweetness and savoriness, becoming an entirely different side dish from the raw one.

## 16. Cooking and Processing

| Method | Fuel | Procedure | Notes |
|---|:---:|---|---|
| Raw | 0 | Wash, cut into fine strips, and toss with seasoning or serve alongside | Pungency and vitamin C are fully intact |
| Geotjeori (fresh-salted side dish) | 0 | Salt briefly, then toss with seasoning | A side dish is made without fuel |
| Soups and stews (guk and jjigae) | Medium | Add just before turning off the heat | Long boiling loses the aroma |
| Welsh onion oil (pagireum) | Low | Stir-fry slowly in oil to transfer the aroma | A small amount of oil flavors many meals |
| Grilling | Low | Grill whole over fire | Sweetness rises greatly |
| Jeon (savory pancake) | Medium | Mix into batter and pan-fry | A small amount makes a meal |
| Dried powder | 0 | See Section 15 | Serves as both storage and seasoning |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In the evaluation table, Cooking & processing being SS means it can be washed and eaten right away. But Welsh onion's value shows not when it is eaten alone but when it adds aroma to other ingredients. The low grade of D for Taste is the result of evaluating Welsh onion itself and is not a value that includes its uses.

> **The core of cooking Welsh onion splits in two. For aroma, add it last; for sweetness, cook it long from the start. The same ingredient does two entirely different jobs.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The Welsh onion oil in the fourth row is especially useful where fuel is scarce. If a little oil is added to the pan and sliced Welsh onion is stir-fried slowly over low heat, the aroma compounds of the Welsh onion move into the oil. A spoonful of this oil can flavor many meals of bland grain dishes. It is a way of securing flavor for many meals with one use of fire, and the bits of Welsh onion left after frying are eaten as they are.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The grilling in the fifth row is also worth knowing. When the blanched white shaft of Welsh onion is grilled whole over fire, the compounds that give pungency break down and the sugars inside come out, making it considerably sweet. If it is tossed into embers and the charred outer skin is peeled off, the inside is found cooked soft. It is a method that can be used when there are no cooking tools and only fire.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The antibacterial action adds to this. Without refrigeration, cooked food begins to spoil within a few hours, but food containing Welsh onion spoils a little more slowly. When leftover food must be carried over to the next meal in summer, this difference has practical meaning.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Finally, the matter of not throwing away the green leaves is worth pointing out again. Vitamin A and vitamin K are found only in the green leaves and almost absent from the white blanched shaft. If the leaves are too tough to eat as they are, they can be chopped fine and put in soup, or dried and made into powder. Half of the Micronutrients grade of A lies in that part which used to be thrown away.

## 17. Annual Work Calendar

| Month (South Korea) | Work |
|---|---|
| January-February | Pull and use Welsh onion left standing in the field whenever needed. Check the cover |
| March | Spring sowing (early March to late April). Keep harvesting the Welsh onion planted in autumn. Divide tillered plants |
| April | Seedling care. Harvest all the Welsh onion that overwintered before its flower stalks rise |
| May | Spring transplanting (early May to late June). Leave the flower stalks on the plants kept for seed |
| June | 1st hilling (one month after transplanting). Put fertilizer in the furrow and pile soil. Maintain drainage channels before the monsoon |
| July | 2nd hilling. Growth slows in the hot season. Watch for downy mildew and rust |
| August | 3rd hilling. Autumn sowing (late August to late September). Look for beet armyworm egg masses |
| September | Harvest of the spring-planted Welsh onion begins. Divide tillered plants |
| October | Autumn transplanting (early October to late November). Finish the last hilling |
| November | Apply overwintering fertilizer, mainly potassium. Leave what will not be pulled as it is |
| December | Cover with straw or fallen leaves. Designate a winter harvest section and secure an access path |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The two most important rows in this table are April and August. If the Welsh onion that overwintered is not fully harvested in April, the flower stalks rise and all of it becomes unusable. For the autumn sowing in August, missing the timing causes premature bolting and the next year's crop is lost. In both cases, being a few days late shows no immediate sign and the result appears months later, so first-time growers miss them most often.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;And the three rows of June, July and August are the work that makes Welsh onion what it is. Welsh onion that has not been hilled three times is close to silpa, while Welsh onion hilled three times has a thick, white column. Three bouts of spade work, once a month, make that difference.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Lastly, dividing tillered plants in March and September determines the self-reliance of this crop. In a crop whose seed does not last 1 year, these two operations of splitting and replanting living plants in the field are the seed storage.
