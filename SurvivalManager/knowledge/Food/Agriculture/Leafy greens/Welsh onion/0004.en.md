## 4. Varieties

### 4-1. Characteristics by Strain

| Strain | Tillering | Blanched white shaft | Hilling | Suitability for disaster |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Single-stalk type (stem onion) | None | Long and thick | Essential | Each stalk is bulky, so storage is efficient |
| Leaf type (tillering onion) | 8-10 or more | Short and thin | Not needed | Carries on by clumps without seed |

| Variety | Features | Yield | Suitable environment |
|---|---|:---:|---|
| Joseon daepa (traditional landrace) | Reliably overwinters and can be harvested all year round | Medium to high | Across South Korea |
| Geummaek | Can be harvested early and has a long blanched white shaft | High | Across South Korea |
| Beullaek-dokkui | Grows thick and straight, with an even shape | High | Across South Korea |
| Ordinary single-stalk type | The most widespread strain | Medium | Across South Korea |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The most important point in these tables for disaster preparedness is where the two rows of the first table diverge. Single-stalk Welsh onion is a crop pulled and used one stalk at a time, and once pulled, that plant is finished. Replanting the next year requires seed, and Welsh onion seed does not last 1 year. Leaf-type onion is used by dividing clumps rather than pulling them up, so it carries on without seed. In exchange, its blanched white shaft is short, so the amount obtained at a time is small.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This is why a landrace such as Joseon daepa, which carries both traits in a middling way, suits disaster situations well. It tillers to a degree, and when hilled it produces a fair blanched white shaft too. It is the best at neither, but it gives up neither. Moreover, a landrace is a strain that has been filtered in its region over a long time, so it is already adapted to that climate.

### 4-2. Selection Criteria by Situation

| Situation | Choice | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| No seed supply can be expected | Leaf type, Joseon daepa | Tillered clumps are divided to carry on without seed |
| Planning to use it as a winter storage resource | Single-stalk type | The blanched white shaft is thick, so each stalk has great volume and weight |
| North of central South Korea, cold regions | Joseon daepa | Overwintering is reliable |
| Shallow or gravelly soil | Leaf type | Hilling is not needed |
| Wanting to harvest quickly | Any strain, as silpa (thin young green onions) | In 40-50 days it reaches the thickness of silpa |
| Planning to keep saving and using seed | Fixed varieties, landraces | Hybrids' traits segregate the next year |

> **A seed packet marked F1 is a hybrid. The first-year yield is good, but if seed is saved and planted, the traits segregate. If a disaster is the premise, whether a variety is fixed comes before how superior the variety is.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In Welsh onion, however, this principle is not as absolute as in other crops. Because seed longevity is under 1 year, the strategy of stockpiling seed for a long time does not hold in the first place. Welsh onion's real means of self-reliance is not seed but tillered plants, and in that respect, how much a strain tillers matters more than whether it is a fixed variety or a hybrid.

## 5. Field Preparation and Fertilizer

### 5-1. Preparing the Field

| Item | Standard | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Tilling depth | 20-30cm | This loosens the soil to be used for hilling |
| Ridge spacing | Single-stalk type 75-85cm, leaf type 1.5m | The space between ridges must be wide for hilling |
| Planting furrow depth | 15-20cm | Plant at the bottom of the furrow and push soil in progressively |
| Drainage channel | Must be secured | Waterlogging is the biggest cause of failure |
| Acidity correction | pH 5.7-7.4 | The range is wide, so little intervention is needed |
| Stone removal | Pick them out beforehand | Stones obstruct hilling and bend the blanched white shaft |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;What sets field preparation for Welsh onion apart from other crops is that the space between ridges is made wide. At a glance it looks like a layout that wastes land, but that empty space is in fact the storehouse of soil for hilling. Because soil has to be piled up three times, that much soil must be ready alongside, and if the ridges are narrow, there is no soil to pile up and the blanched white shaft stays short.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Digging deep furrows and planting at the bottom is the basic structure of Welsh onion cultivation. At first the seedlings sit low at the bottom of the furrow; as they grow, soil from both sides is pushed in little by little until the furrow becomes like a raised bed, and by autumn the place that was originally a furrow is the highest point. The high and low points of the field, in effect, are reversed over the course of a year.

### 5-2. Fertilizer Rates

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Welsh onion is a heavy-feeding crop. The Fertilizer demand grade of C in the evaluation table comes from the requirement of about 30g per 1m² for N, P and K combined, the largest amount among the four crops this document covers.

| Component | Per 1,000m² | Per 1m² | Notes |
|---|:---:|:---:|---|
| Nitrogen (N) | Equivalent to 25kg of urea | About 11g | Split between base fertilizer and top-dressing |
| Phosphate (P) | Equivalent to 6.6kg of fused phosphate | About 3g | All applied as base fertilizer |
| Potassium (K) | Equivalent to 14kg of potassium chloride | About 8g | Split between base fertilizer and top-dressing |
| Compost | 1,500kg | 1.5kg | Use only well-rotted compost |
| Lime | 100-150kg if needed | 100-150g | Only when the soil is strongly acidic |

| Application | Timing | Details |
|---|---|---|
| Base fertilizer | 1-2 weeks before transplanting | All the compost, all the phosphate, half of the nitrogen and potassium |
| Top-dressing, 1st | 1 month after transplanting | With the 1st hilling |
| Top-dressing, 2nd | 1 month after the 1st | With the 2nd hilling |
| Top-dressing, 3rd | 1 month after the 2nd | With the 3rd hilling |
| Before overwintering | November in South Korea | Mainly potassium |

> **Top-dressing Welsh onion is one body with hilling. When fertilizer is put in the furrow and soil is piled on top, the fertilizer is covered by the soil and does not escape, and newly extending roots reach it directly.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There is a reason this order matters. When hilling is done, Welsh onion sends out new roots into the newly covered soil. If fertilizer has been put in that spot beforehand, the new roots feed on it right away. If, conversely, soil is piled first and fertilizer is scattered on the surface afterward, it takes time for that fertilizer to move down, and a considerable part of the nitrogen escapes into the air. The order of fertilizer first, soil afterward gives a greater effect from the same amount of fertilizer.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The condition of using only well-rotted compost is especially strict for Welsh onion. Insufficiently rotted compost attracts onion flies, and this pest eats away the underground part of Welsh onion and kills the whole plant. Whether compost is fully rotted is judged by squeezing it in the hand: it gives off no heat, smells of earth, and the form of the original materials can no longer be recognized.

### 5-3. Substitute Fertilizers for a Disaster

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;When chemical fertilizer stops, the first change to appear in Welsh onion is a thinner blanched white shaft. This is the phenomenon that the value of Yield retention without synthetic inputs C, 40.8%, stands for. Welsh onion is a crop with a high requirement, so this loss shows up especially large.

| Substitute resource | Nutrients supplied | How to use | Caution |
|---|---|---|---|
| Human urine | Nitrogen (main), potassium, phosphate | Dilute 8-10 times with water and pour into the furrow before hilling | It scorches the leaves if it touches them. Cover with soil to prevent odor and loss |
| Well-rotted compost | Nitrogen, phosphate, potassium, organic matter | Generously as base fertilizer. As top-dressing, put in the furrow before hilling | Insufficiently rotted compost attracts onion flies |
| Wood ash | Potassium, calcium, phosphate | A handful or two in the furrow before hilling | Alkaline. Welsh onion tolerates up to pH 7.4 |
| Bone meal | Phosphate, calcium | Burn, crush, and mix into the base fertilizer | Takes effect slowly |
| Sesame/perilla oil cake | Nitrogen, phosphate | Use as a fermented liquid or mix into the base fertilizer | If spread as it is, fermentation heat damages the roots |
| Green-grass liquid fertilizer | Nitrogen, potassium | Soak cut grass in water for 2-3 weeks to ferment, then dilute 5 times | Strong smell |
| Legume preceding crop | Nitrogen | Grow soybean or pea the year before and bury the residue | Allium is not planted together with legumes |
| Seashells and eggshells | Calcium | Burn, crush, and use to correct acidity | A substitute for lime |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There is a reason diluted urine suits Welsh onion especially well. Top-dressing for Welsh onion is put in the furrow together with hilling and covered with soil; liquid fertilizer soaks straight into the soil, and the soil piled over it reduces both odor and loss. There is also little worry about it touching the leaves. A healthy person's urine contains 7-10g of nitrogen per 1L in the form of urea, so diluting it 8-10 times makes a weak nitrogen fertilizer.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Wood ash supplies potassium while raising the soil pH. Because Welsh onion tolerates up to pH 7.4, ash can be used more generously than for other crops. If ash is scattered in the furrow before hilling and soil is piled on top, potassium supply and disinfection of the underground part are achieved together. Since adult onion flies have a habit of laying eggs in damp soil, the effect of ash in making the surface dry and alkaline reaches here as well.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Section 11 explains why a caution is attached to the second-to-last row. Allium plants suppress the root-nodule bacteria of legume crops and so must not be planted together with them, but placing them before and after each other with time in between is actually advantageous.

## 6. Sowing, Raising Seedlings and Transplanting

### 6-1. Cropping Types

| Cropping type (South Korea) | Sowing | Transplanting | Harvest |
|---|---|---|---|
| Spring crop | Early March to late April | Early May to late June | Mid-September to late April of the following year |
| Autumn crop | Late August to late September | Early October to late November | Mid-April to late May of the following year |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The spring crop is harvested starting in autumn and carried through the winter, while the autumn crop passes the winter in a young state and is harvested early the following spring. From the standpoint of disaster preparedness, it is safer to overlap the two cropping types. That way, from April to April of the following year, Welsh onion is in effect standing in the field all year round.

> **Observing the sowing time matters especially in the autumn crop. If seed is sown too early and the seedlings grow beyond pencil thickness before winter, flower bud differentiation takes place under the cold, and only flower stalks rise the following spring.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This premature bolting is the failure that occurs most often with autumn sowing. Sowing early seems as if it would make the plants grow better, but it brings exactly the opposite result. The period from late August to late September is timed so that, before winter comes, the seedlings end up at just the in-between size, that is, grown enough to survive but only so much that they cannot fill the vernalization requirement.

### 6-2. Raising Seedlings

| Item | Standard |
|---|---|
| Sowing method | Broadcast on the seedbed or sow in rows 10cm apart |
| Sowing depth | 5-6mm, shallow |
| Days to germination | 7-10 days at 20°C |
| Seedling period | 40-50 days; up to 60 days at the longest |
| Best time to transplant | 4-5 true leaves, stem thickness 5-7mm |
| Seedbed care | Keep the surface soil from drying out |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;When Welsh onion seed first comes up, it emerges bent into a loop shape. This is because the tip of the cotyledon is still attached to the seed coat and the middle rises first. In this state it is very weak, so keeping the surface soil from drying out is nearly all there is to raising seedlings.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Two reasons overlap in setting the best transplanting time at a stem thickness of 5-7mm. One is that anything thinner cannot withstand being moved; the other is that anything thicker raises the bolting risk when it experiences cold. In spring transplanting the second reason is not a problem, but in autumn transplanting it is decisive.

### 6-3. Transplanting

| Item | Single-stalk type | Leaf type |
|---|---|---|
| Ridge spacing | 75-85cm | Ridge width 1.5m |
| Plant spacing | 2-3 plants every 7-12cm | 2-3 plants every 12-15cm |
| Planting depth | Just enough to cover the roots, about 3cm | Same |
| Planting posture | Lay at a slant on the bottom of the furrow | Same |
| After planting | Press the soil down lightly and water | Same |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The point that it is not planted deep looks odd at first. It seems that burying it deep would be needed to make a long blanched white shaft, but doing so fails. Welsh onion's growing point sits just above the basal disc at the bottom, and if it is buried deep, the new leaves spend their strength pushing up through the soil, or the plant simply rots. The blanched white shaft is made not by burying the plant deep from the start but by covering it little by little as it grows.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The trick of planting at a slant is in the same vein. When planted lying down, the root part is buried shallowly while the length of stem in contact with the soil increases. Welsh onion stands itself upright within a few days, so there is no need to worry about how it looks, and once it stands, a considerable length already lies close to the soil, which raises the starting line for hilling.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Planting two or three plants together in one spot also has a reason. The several stalks lean on one another and grow straight, and they shade one another, so the lower part thickens less and instead lengthens upward. Planted one at a time, each spreads sideways and tries to thicken, and the blanched white shaft stays short.

### 6-4. Seed Treatment

| Method | Procedure | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Hot-water soak | Soak in 50-55°C water for 10-15 minutes, then cool in cold water | Kills pathogens on the surface |
| Ash coating | Coat wet seed with sieved wood ash | Also disinfects the surface |
| Seed sorting | Soak in water and skim off what floats | Removes hollow seeds |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Where chemicals cannot be obtained, selecting only healthy seed and giving it a hot-water soak is the realistic best. Without a thermometer, mixing boiling water and cold water in roughly equal parts gives water that is hot but bearable to the hand, which is around 50°C. Above 60°C the seed dies, so it is safer to treat only part of the seed on hand and sow it in separate lots.

## 7. Water Management

### 7-1. Water by Growth Stage

| Period | Management | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| From sowing to germination | Daily, so the surface soil does not dry out | The core of seedbed care |
| Seedling stage | Once every 2-3 days | Too much makes the seedlings leggy |
| Right after transplanting | Twice a week, thoroughly | This is when severed roots grow back |
| Growing period after establishment | Once a week, deeply | Shallow, frequent watering makes the roots shallow |
| Right after hilling | Once | The new soil settles tightly around the roots |
| Monsoon season (jangma, late June to July in South Korea) | Do not water | Maintaining the drainage channels is the water management of this period |
| Overwintering period | Do not water | If wet soil freezes, the blanched white shaft is damaged |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Welsh onion is relatively tolerant of drought. The wax layer on the leaf surface prevents water loss, and because the leaf is cylindrical, its volume is small relative to its surface area. In a drought it only grows more slowly and does not die easily. So in a situation where water must be conserved, Welsh onion is a crop whose irrigation priority can be pushed back.

### 7-2. Symptoms of Too Little and Too Much Water

| Symptom | Cause | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| Leaf tips dry out and turn brown | Water shortage or excess salts | That leaf does not recover, but the plant survives |
| Leaves are thin and short and the blanched white shaft does not thicken | Persistent water shortage or fertilizer shortage | Yield falls |
| Leaves turn yellow and the plant falls over at the base | Excess moisture, soft rot | The inside of the blanched white shaft has already turned mushy |
| Foul smell from the base when pulled | Soft rot | It is bacterial. That spot is avoided for several years |
| Maggots near the roots | Onion fly | Caused by insufficiently rotted compost and excess moisture |
| Gray mold and yellow streaks on the leaves | Downy mildew | Cool, humid conditions favor it |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The third and fourth rows are the most frightening symptoms in Welsh onion. Soft rot is a disease caused by bacteria, and because it progresses between the leaf sheaths inside the blanched white shaft, it cannot be seen from outside. By the time the outer leaves turn yellow and the stalk starts to lean, the inside has already turned to mush. When pulled, it gives off a characteristic stench, and once that smell is known, the disease can be recognized by smell alone from then on.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;It should be made clear that there is no cure for this disease. Even with chemicals, rot that has started inside cannot be reversed. Prevention is the only countermeasure, and prevention means keeping water from standing and, when hilling, keeping the soil from covering above the point where the leaves split.

### 7-3. Mulching

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Welsh onion is a crop that has to be hilled repeatedly, so mulching that covers the raised bed does not suit it, because the cover would have to be removed each time soil is piled up. Instead, if straw and dry grass are laid only on the furrows between ridges, that is, the places from which hilling soil is not taken, weeds are suppressed without getting in the way of the work.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Winter is a different matter. After the final hilling has covered the blanched white shaft sufficiently, a 5-10cm layer of straw or fallen leaves laid over it provides insulation. Welsh onion withstands 10°C below zero as it is, but in regions colder than that or in an unusually cold year, this cover protects the Welsh onion left standing in the field. If the soil under the cover does not freeze, the plants can be pulled and used whenever needed all winter, and this is Welsh onion's greatest practical value.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;However, the underside of the cover is a good hiding place for slugs and rats, so lift it now and then to check. In a winter field, Welsh onion is one of the few conspicuous things to eat and can become a target for wild animals.
