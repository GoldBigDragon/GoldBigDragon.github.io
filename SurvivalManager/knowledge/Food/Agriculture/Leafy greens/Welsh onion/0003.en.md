## 2. What Kind of Plant Is This?

### 2-1. Taxonomy and Origin

| Item | Details |
|---|---|
| Scientific name | Allium fistulosum |
| Taxonomy | Order Asparagales, family Amaryllidaceae, genus Allium |
| Other names | Daepa (large green onion), as distinguished from pa (green onion), umpa (blanched shoots), silpa (thin young green onion) and jjokpa (small shallot-like onion) |
| Origin | Presumed to be the region from western China to Siberia |
| Life form | Biennial. With tillering it can be kept going for 2-4 years |
| Propagation | Seed and division of tillered plants |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The fistulosum in the scientific name means a hollow tube. The name points directly to the fact that Welsh onion leaves are hollow cylinders, and this is the first criterion that sets Welsh onion apart from garlic chive. Garlic chive leaves are flat and solid, whereas Welsh onion leaves are round and hollow. Press a leaf, and the Welsh onion caves in while the garlic chive holds its shape.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The fact that its origin is a cold inland region stretching from western China to Siberia explains Welsh onion's character. To survive where summers are short and winters long, two things are needed. One is the ability to withstand cold; the other is a timetable of building up the plant during the brief summer, getting through the winter, and setting seed early the following spring. Welsh onion's Cold hardiness S and its biennial life cycle both come from here.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Unlike the onion, Welsh onion does not grow a thick bulb. Instead of storing nutrients in a round bulb, it stacks leaf sheaths layer on layer and raises them upward. Those stacked leaf sheaths are the blanched white shaft, the white part. This difference splits the growing methods completely. Growing an onion is farming for the bulb, whereas growing Welsh onion is farming to lengthen the leaf sheaths, and the means of lengthening them is hilling.

### 2-2. Appearance

| Organ | Features |
|---|---|
| Leaf | Hollow cylinder. A white wax layer on the surface makes water roll off |
| Blanched white shaft (pseudostem) | The white part made of leaf sheaths stacked layer on layer. It is not a true stem |
| Stem (basal plate) | The flat disc at the very bottom of the blanched white shaft. Roots and leaves grow from here |
| Roots | White fibrous roots. Concentrated at a depth of 20-30cm |
| Flowers | White to pale purple. They bloom clustered in a ball. Second-year spring |
| Seed | Small black seeds with a wrinkled surface |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;It matters that the blanched white shaft is not a true stem. Welsh onion's only true stem is the single flat disc at the very bottom, and the white column visible above it is made of leaf sheaths, the lower parts of the leaves, stacked on one another. It is, in effect, the same structure that shows up as layers when an onion is peeled, stretched out long and upright.

> **The part covered with soil receives no light, makes no chlorophyll, and stays white. The length of the blanched white shaft is not decided by the plant itself but by how much soil a person has piled over it.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This fact explains almost everything about growing Welsh onion. It is not the seed or the variety that makes the blanched white shaft. The work called hilling makes it. Even when the same seed is sown, a Welsh onion hilled three times and one never hilled become entirely different products. Section 8 covers this work in detail.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The wax layer on the leaf surface is also worth noting. That layer, which rubs off like a white powder when touched, is a protective film the plant makes to prevent water loss and to keep pathogens out. That is why spray solutions or soapy water do not stick well to Welsh onion leaves and run off. In a situation where pest control has to be done without chemicals, this property is a disadvantage; Section 9 covers it.

### 2-3. Tillering - Using a Biennial Like a Perennial

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;From the flat disc at the base of Welsh onion, not only leaves but also new plants grow. These new plants that split off to the side are called tillers, and the degree of tillering differs greatly by strain.

| Type | Tillering | Blanched white shaft | Hilling | Use |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Single-stalk type (stem onion) | Almost none | Long and thick | Essential | Pulled and used one stalk at a time |
| Leaf type (tillering onion) | 8-10 or more | Short and thin | Not needed | Clumps are divided to keep the planting going |
| Joseon daepa (traditional landrace) | Moderate | Medium | Beneficial | Overwinters and carries on for several years |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;What the evaluation table's entry for Perenniality S, continuous harvest for 2-4 years when tillering is used, refers to is the second and third rows. When tillered plants are separated and replanted in spring or autumn, the stand carries on without seed. One plant becomes eight, and a cycle is created in which half is eaten and half is replanted.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Here lies the practical judgment for disaster preparedness. To get a thick, white Welsh onion, the single-stalk strain has to be hilled, but the single-stalk type does not tiller, so once it is pulled, that is the end of it. Seed is needed every year, and that seed does not last 1 year. Conversely, the tillering strains have a short blanched white shaft and offer little to eat, but they carry on without seed. There is no need to pick one of the two; keeping both strains together is the realistic answer.

### 2-4. Growth Stages and Bolting

| Stage | Timing | What happens | Main needs |
|---|---|---|---|
| Germination and seedling stage | 30 days from sowing | A single thread-like leaf comes up, still bent over | Phosphorus, nitrogen |
| Seedling-raising stage | 30-60 days | True leaves increase and the stem thickens | Nitrogen |
| Transplanting and establishment stage | 1-2 weeks after transplanting | Severed roots grow back | Phosphorus |
| Vegetative growth stage | 1-3 months after transplanting | Leaves increase and the blanched white shaft lengthens | Nitrogen, potassium |
| Best harvest time | 3-6 months after transplanting | The blanched white shaft thickens and firms up | Balance of nitrogen and potassium |
| Overwintering | In South Korea, November to March | Outer leaves dry up while the inside and the blanched white shaft stay alive | Potassium |
| Bolting and seed set | Spring of the second year | The flower stalk rises and seed forms | Phosphorus, potassium |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Bolting, in the last row, is the point needing the most care in growing Welsh onion. Once the flower stalk rises, a hard stalk runs right through the middle of the blanched white shaft and makes it inedible. The outside looks perfectly normal, but cut open, it has a woody core lodged inside.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Two conditions have to coincide for bolting to occur. First, the seedling must have grown to a certain thickness or more. Roughly pencil thickness, 6-7mm in diameter, is the benchmark. Second, in that state it must go through a period of low temperature, 13°C or below. When the two conditions meet, the plant judges that it has been through a winter and prepares to flower. A young seedling will not bolt no matter how much cold it experiences.

> **If seed is sown too early in autumn and the seedlings thicken before winter, the next spring brings only flower stalks and nothing to eat. This is why the autumn sowing time is observed.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Turning this principle around gives the method for seed saving. To collect seed, simply leave a thickly grown Welsh onion to go through the winter. Section 13 covers it.

### 2-5. Nutrition per 100g

| Nutrient | Content | Notes |
|---|:---:|---|
| Energy | 23-34kcal | Varies with the source and the part |
| Water | About 90g | |
| Protein | 1.78g | Share of calories (P:E) is 31.0% |
| Carbohydrates | 5-7g | |
| Sugars | 2.63g | Sweetness becomes prominent when cooked |
| Potassium | 181mg | |
| Calcium | Present | |
| Iron | Present | |
| Vitamin A | Abundant in the green leaves | Almost none in the blanched white shaft |
| Vitamin C | High | A considerable amount is present in the blanched white shaft too |
| Vitamin K | High | Concentrated in the green leaves |
| Folate, thiamin, riboflavin, pantothenic acid | Present | |
| Sulfur compounds (allicin family) | Present | Source of the odor, antibacterial action and pungency |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;What is worth noticing in this table is that the green leaves and the white blanched shaft differ in composition. Vitamin A and vitamin K are made together with chlorophyll, so they are concentrated in the green leaves and almost absent from the blanched white shaft. Conversely, sugars are more abundant in the blanched white shaft, so it turns sweet when cooked.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;So the common practice of throwing away the green leaves and using only the white part is a loss nutritionally. Of the 15 nutrients behind the Micronutrients grade of A, a good many come from the green leaves. In a disaster situation, it makes sense to slice the leaf part separately, dry it, and then add it to broth or grind it into powder.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Sulfur compounds are produced by enzyme action when the tissue is damaged. The stinging of the eyes when Welsh onion is cut is due to this reaction, and these compounds have an antibacterial action. They are sensitive to heat and escape when cooked for a long time; in exchange, cooking removes the pungency and brings out sweetness. This is why the same Welsh onion becomes an entirely different ingredient when used raw and when cooked.

## 3. Growing Conditions

### 3-1. Temperature

| Category | Temperature | Result |
|---|:---:|---|
| Germination possible | 15-25°C | Outside this range the germination rate drops |
| Optimal germination temperature | Around 20°C | Sprouts in 7-10 days |
| Optimal growth temperature | About 20°C | Prefers a cool climate |
| Growth range | 5-30°C | Range 25°C. Basis for the SS in Temperature range |
| Heat injury | 25°C and above | Growth slows and disease increases |
| Growth stops in cold | 5°C and below | |
| Outer leaves die | Colder than 10°C below zero | The inside and the blanched white shaft stay alive |
| Open-field overwintering limit | 10°C below zero | Basis for the S in Cold hardiness |
| Conditions for flower bud differentiation | 13°C or below for a certain period | Only when the seedling is pencil thickness or more |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;An optimal growth temperature of about 20°C means that Welsh onion dislikes high summer. In South Korea, Welsh onion in July and August does grow, but slowly, and it catches diseases easily. This is also why downy mildew and rust cluster in the monsoon season (jangma, late June to July in South Korea). It is here that the reason lies for the actual growing rhythm splitting in two: planted in spring and harvested in autumn, or planted in late summer and harvested the following spring.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Cold hardiness S and open-field overwintering are the most practical properties of Welsh onion. It can stand in the field as it is down to 10°C below zero, and in central and southern South Korea it gets through the winter without special protection. In areas north of central South Korea, or in an unusually cold year, generous hilling to cover the blanched white shaft with soil lets that soil act as insulation so the plants hold up.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;It is easy to mistake outer leaves dried by frost for dead plants. In fact only the few outermost leaves dry, and the inner leaves and the blanched white shaft are intact. Peel off the dried outer leaves and a sound white stalk emerges underneath. In fact, the dried outer leaves wrap and protect the inside, so in winter it is better to leave them on.

### 3-2. Light

| Condition | Relative yield | Interpretation |
|---|:---:|---|
| Full direct sun all day | 100% | This is the baseline |
| 6 hours or more a day | Nearly 100% | The practical minimum |
| 40% shade | About 86% | Confidence interval 61-120%. Basis for the B in Sunlight |
| Partial shade | It does grow | The blanched white shaft gets thinner and the leaves get longer |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Welsh onion is relatively tolerant of shade. Even with 40% blocked, yield holds at 86%. However, a lower yield is not the only symptom of too little light in Welsh onion. When light is short, the plant tries to stretch upward in search of it, and as a result the leaves droop and the tissue turns soft. Softened tissue falls over easily and is vulnerable to disease.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;It is interesting, though, that deliberately blocking light is the key technique of growing Welsh onion. Hilling is exactly that work. Letting the aboveground part receive plenty of sun while covering the underground part completely with soil, accomplishing both at once, is the structure of a Welsh onion field.

### 3-3. Water and Soil

| Item | Standard | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Soil type | Land with deep soil and good drainage | Plenty of soil is needed for hilling |
| Soil acidity | pH 5.7-7.4 | A relatively wide range |
| Organic matter | Plenty of well-rotted compost | A heavy-feeding crop |
| Drainage | Very important | One of the crops most vulnerable to waterlogging |
| Irrigation | Total ET about 550mm | Relatively tolerant of drought |
| Raised bed height | 15-20cm or more | 25cm on poorly drained land |
| Ridge structure | Dig the furrows deep | This secures in advance the soil to be used for hilling |

> **The cause of losing a whole Welsh onion crop is almost always standing water. In a field that stays flooded for a few days in the monsoon season, fully grown Welsh onions turn soft from the base and fall over.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The reason Welsh onion is especially vulnerable to waterlogging lies in its structure. The blanched white shaft is a column of leaf sheaths stacked layer upon layer, and when water seeps between the layers, a sealed, moist space with no air movement forms. These are perfect conditions for soft-rot bacteria to grow. Moreover, since that part has been buried in soil by hilling, there is no telling from the outside whether it is rotting within. One finds out only on seeing the aboveground part turn yellow and fall over, and by then it is already too late.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The last row, digging the furrows deep, is the hallmark of a Welsh onion field. Other crops are planted on top of a raised bed, but Welsh onion is planted at the bottom of a deep furrow and grown while soil from both sides is pushed in little by little. This way there is no need to bring in soil separately for hilling, and at the same time the furrow serves as a drainage channel. One operation solves two problems.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The requirement for deep soil also comes from this structure. To build a blanched white shaft of close to 30cm, that much soil has to be available, and on gravelly or shallow land hilling cannot be done. On such land it makes sense to give up on the blanched white shaft and change direction toward planting a tillering strain and using the leaves.
