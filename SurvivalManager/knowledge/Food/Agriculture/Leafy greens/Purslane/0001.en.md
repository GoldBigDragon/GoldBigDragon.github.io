## Introduction

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Purslane (Portulaca oleracea) is a succulent herb of the purslane family (Portulacaceae). It comes up by itself on ground that nobody looks after, such as field banks, cracks in roadside concrete, gravel patches and empty construction lots, and in South Korea it has long been treated as nothing more than a weed to be pulled out. Even its Korean name, soebireum, means "the amaranth that cows eat," and its Chinese-character name, machihyeon (馬齒莧), comes from the leaves' resemblance to a horse's teeth. In other words, this is less a crop that people plant and harvest than a plant that is simply there, whatever people do.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;What groups purslane with the other three crops covered in this document, namely garlic chive, Welsh onion and water spinach, is that once it takes hold in a spot, it comes back into your hands every year. The way it does so is different, though. Garlic chive roots survive the winter and the same clump comes up again the next year, and Welsh onion clumps multiply by tillering. Purslane carries on through seed, not roots. A single plant scatters tens of thousands of seeds, and those seeds survive in the soil for decades before germinating in a year when conditions are right. The individual plant is an annual, but the patch is effectively permanent. This is why, in the evaluation table, Continuous-cropping tolerance and Seed longevity both reach SS, the top of the entire table.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Because of this trait, it is more accurate to call purslane managed neglect than cultivation. The whole sequence of plowing the field, putting in seed and watering is hardly needed for purslane. All that people do is decide where purslane may grow, keep its boundary only as tight as needed so that it does not push out other crops, and leave a few plants standing until the seed has dropped. The grades of SS for Fertilizer demand, SS for Water use and SS for Infrastructure needs are all figures that point to this suitability for being left alone. Since no fertilizer is applied, there is no yield to lose in the first place, so zero-input yield retention comes out at 100%. In a world where the chemical-fertilizer factories have stopped, few plants have a trait this useful.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;On the nutrition side, one thing stands out in particular. For a leafy vegetable, purslane contains an unusually large amount of omega-3 fatty acids, more precisely alpha-linolenic acid (ALA). Fatty acids are seldom even discussed for vegetables, yet purslane carries ALA in the range of hundreds of mg per 100g. In a disaster, when fish and cooking oil disappear, the omega-3 family of fatty acids is the first thing to be cut off. A diet built only from grains, tuber crops (tubers/storage roots) and leafy vegetables has almost no channel to supply this nutrient. Purslane is one of those rare channels. This composition is the background to the Supplementary score: SS at 69.2 points, a full 27 points higher than the Overall score of 42.1 points.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The weaknesses are just as clear. At around 20kcal per 100g, its Calories per area grade is D. It does nothing to fill the stomach. A single frost kills it outright, so Cold hardiness is E, and it vanishes from the field in winter. Cut stems do not last three days at room temperature, so Storability is also E. And, contrary to appearances, it is weak in shade. Relative yield drops to 45% at 40% shade, which makes the Sunlight criterion E; this is because purslane is a plant that uses C4 photosynthesis. Its body is designed on the assumption of strong light, so it loses a lot when light is reduced.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There is one more thing: there is a limit on how much of it can be eaten. Purslane contains a fair amount of oxalic acid, at a level similar to or higher than spinach. Treated as a daily staple eaten in big bowls, it interferes with calcium absorption and puts a strain on the kidneys. Also, its habit of growing flat against the ground resembles the prostrate spurges (Euphorbia) of the spurge family (Euphorbiaceae), so anyone who picks it without knowing the difference ends up eating a poisonous plant. Section 14 deals with these two points separately.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In short, purslane is not the star of the field but a crop that takes charge of the field's edges and its empty ground. The labor of planting it is effectively zero, there is almost no room for failure, and it puts on the table a line of fatty acids that no other crop provides. In return, it does not fill the stomach, and it must not be eaten in large amounts. Used with that limit in mind, it is a highly efficient slot in a disaster diet.
