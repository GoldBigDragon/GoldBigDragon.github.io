## Introduction
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Purslane (Portulaca oleracea) is a succulent annual herb of the purslane family that comes up by itself on land nobody looks after, such as field ridges, cracks in roadside cement, and gravel. Its Korean name machihyeon, written with Chinese characters meaning horse-tooth amaranth, comes from the leaves resembling horse teeth. Its overall grade is C (42.1 points, 85th of 183 valid species), but its supplementary-food grade is SS (69.2 points). A single plant scatters tens of thousands of seeds, and those seeds survive in the soil for decades, so the individual is an annual but the place is practically permanent. That is why continuous-cropping tolerance, seed longevity, and seed saving are SS, and fertilization, water consumption, and facility burden are SS too. It is closer to managed neglect than to cultivation: what people do is decide where to let it grow, guard the boundary so it does not push out other crops, and leave a few plants until the seed drops. In nutrition, what stands out is that, unusually for a leafy vegetable, it carries 300-400 mg of alpha-linolenic acid (ALA), an omega-3 fatty acid, per 100 g. This is the nutrient that is first cut off in a diet where fish and oil have disappeared, and grains, tubers, and leafy vegetables alone have almost no way to supply it; purslane is one of those rare channels. Its weaknesses are that at 16-20 kcal per 100 g calories per area is D, it dies with a single frost so cold tolerance is E, it spoils in 2-3 days at room temperature so storability is E, and being a C4 plant its relative yield falls to about 45% under 40% shade, so sunlight is E. Its oxalic acid is as high as spinach's, so it cannot be eaten in quantity, and it must be told apart from a look-alike poisonous plant (spurge). Purslane is not the star of the field but a crop that takes care of the field's edges and empty land.

## 2. Plant Overview
| Item | Content |
|:---|:---|
| Classification | Annual of the order Caryophyllales, purslane family (Portulacaceae), genus Portulaca. Other names: machihyeon, dwaejipul, ohaengcho, jangmyeongchae. Presumed native to India or the eastern Mediterranean and naturalized worldwide. By natural self-seeding it reappears in the same place every year |
| Leaves, stem | Leaves are thick, glossy, succulent, and spoon-shaped (smooth edges), clustered at the stem tips. The stem is reddish to purplish and cylindrical, creeping along the ground and spreading 20-30 cm. Cutting it gives a clear sap; a white sap never comes out |
| Roots, flowers, fruit | One thick white root with shallow side roots, and new roots form from the nodes even when it is cut. Yellow flowers about 5 mm across (5 petals) open only on the mornings of clear days from June to September. The fruit is a capsule that splits crosswise like a lid, holding dozens of small black seeds |

| Stage | Period (after sowing) | Key points |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Germination, seedling stage | Up to 2 weeks | Two very small cotyledons. Only in this period is it weak |
| Vegetative growth | 2-6 weeks | The stem creeps and spreads over the ground and leaves thicken |
| Best harvest time | 6-8 weeks | Stems 15-20 cm, with leaves and stems at their most tender |
| Flowering, fruiting | 8-12 weeks | Flowers open at every node and fruit forms, and stems turn tough |

> **When water is available purslane grows fast as a C4 plant, and when water is cut off it switches to CAM, stops growing, and holds on. Where fields cannot be irrigated, this switch is survival itself.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;CAM opens the stomata at night to take in carbon dioxide, so water loss is very small. C4 is efficient in strong light and high heat but loses a lot in weak light, so purslane likes summer best and is weak in shade.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Per 100 g: 16-20 kcal (7-20 kcal depending on the source), water about 93 g, protein about 2.0 g (40.6% of calories), fat about 0.4 g, alpha-linolenic acid 300-400 mg, potassium 494 mg, magnesium 68 mg, calcium 65 mg (absorption is reduced by oxalic acid), iron 0.9-2.0 mg, and vitamin C 21 mg (more than half is lost on blanching). Vitamin E is high among greens, and it also has thiamin, niacin, vitamin B6, and folate.

## 3. Growing Environment and Varieties
| Condition | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Temperature | Minimum for germination about 15℃, optimum 20-30℃ (3-7 days). Optimum growth 20-35℃, possible range 10-35℃ (a width of 25℃). It does not die even above 40℃, growth stops at 5℃ or below, and 0℃ or a single frost softens and kills the aboveground part |
| Light | A place with sun all day. Relative yield about 45% under 40% shade. In partial shade the stems become thin and long, and in deep shade it hardly germinates |
| Water, soil | Almost any soil (gravel, sandy ground, hardened roadsides). pH 5.6-7.8, no organic matter needed, quite salt tolerant. Irrigation is unnecessary outside germination (it grows on under 250 L/㎡ in total). Drainage is the only thing to attend to; what kills it is almost always not too little water but water that does not drain |

| Strain | Characteristics |
|:---|:---|
| Wild type (deulsoebireum) | Small leaves, stems flat on the ground, low yield. It comes up by itself anywhere, so securing it takes no effort |
| Large-leaf type (keunnip soebireum) | A large plant with broad, thick leaves that yields nearly twice as much as the wild type from the same area |
| Small-leaf type, upright type | The small-leaf type has stronger constituents and is good for medicine and tea; the upright type (golden purslane and others) has upright stems, is easy to cut, and spreads less sideways, so its boundary is easy to hold |

> **Far more important than choosing a variety is where you pick it. Purslane that grew beside roads and on vacant lots is exposed to exhaust, herbicides, and heavy metals, and this plant has the habit of accumulating such substances in its tissue.**

## 4. Field Preparation and Sowing
| Item | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Preparing the soil | Roots are shallow, so dig only 10-15 cm and build low beds at least for drainage. No pH correction or organic matter (with much fertilizer only the leaves go soft and leggy). A spot with sun all day |
| Fertilization | Nothing to add. Only a very small amount of nitrogen after cutting (it only helps new shoots regrow, and it regrows without it). No phosphorus, potassium, compost, or lime |
| Substitute resources | Urine diluted 10-15 times (poured in the furrows right after cutting, weaker than for other crops), a handful of fully matured compost (a thick cover touches the stems and makes them soft), a handful of wood ash, grass liquid fertilizer diluted 10 times, and leaf mold (thinly on the germination spot). When fertilizer is scarce, sending it to kale, Chinese cabbage, and Welsh onion instead increases the total harvest |

| Period | Sowing | Harvest | Notes |
|:---|:---|:---|:---|
| Open-field spring | Mid April-May | Late May-Oct | After the risk of frost has completely passed |
| Open-field summer | June-early July | July-Oct | Soil temperature is high so germination is fastest |
| Natural germination | Not sown | June-Oct | Comes up by itself where seed fell the previous year. The center of actual operation |

| Sowing | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Method | Depth 0.2-0.5 cm, barely covered, pressed with the palm or a board so seed and soil make contact (it germinates well only with light, so it must not be buried deep). The seed is very small, so mix it 1 to 10 with dry sand or fine soil and broadcast or sow in rows. Row spacing 20-30 cm, germination in 3-7 days at soil temperature of 20-30℃ |
| Seed treatment | Hot-water soaking and chemical disinfection are not needed. Lightly coating the seed with sifted wood ash disinfects the surface and makes the sown spot visible |

## 5. Water Management and Cultivation Management
| Period | Water management |
|:---|:---|
| Sowing to germination | Keep the topsoil from drying (only these 2 weeks need human hands) |
| Seedling stage | Once every 2-3 days. After that it grows rapidly stronger |
| Vegetative growth | Not needed (if dry, leaves just thicken) |
| Harvest stage | Once right after cutting (new shoots regrow faster) |
| Flowering, fruiting | Cut it off instead (the drier it is, the better the seed ripens) |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Leaves that become small, thick, and red are a normal adaptation to water shortage, and when it switches to CAM in drought, leaves picked at dawn are noticeably sour. If stems are thin, long, and pale, it lacks light, so move the spot; if it softens brown from the base, it is rotting from excess water and the whole plant collapses within days. Mulch is unnecessary and even a nuisance, and purslane itself acts as a mulch covering the ground.

| Management | Content |
|:---|:---|
| Boundary | The most important task. Set the area to be used narrow from the start and pull up everything outside it before it flowers |
| Thinning | First to 5 cm at 2-3 true leaves, second to 10-15 cm (final) at 4-5. Eat what is thinned. The seed is small, so broadcast sowing comes up thick, which makes this the only task that needs hands |
| Pinching, regrowth | First cutting 6-8 weeks after sowing at stems 15-20 cm. Cut 2-3 nodes above the base (new shoots come only from nodes) and it regrows after 10-20 days, 3-4 times before frost. With repetition the stems become thinner and the leaves smaller |
| Overwintering | It cannot get through winter; the seed does. To have it in winter, storage is the only way |

> **For purslane, the problem is not the moment of planting but after it starts to spread. Setting the area to use narrow from the start and pulling up everything outside it before it flowers is the whole of managing this crop.**

## 6. Pests, Diseases, and Nutrient Problems
| Problem | Symptoms and conditions | Control without chemicals |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Root rot | The base turns brown and soft and the plant collapses. Spots where water pools, rainy season | Beds and drainage ditches; remove rotted plants |
| Slugs | Irregular holes and slime trails on leaves. Damp, shaded spots | Sprinkle ash or diatomaceous earth around the plants; collect by hand at night |
| Aphids, gray mold | Cluster at the shoot tips (excess nitrogen, drought) / gray mold inside crowded plants (poor airflow) | Wash off with water or use soapy water / thin to widen spacing and clear dry leaves |
| Stink bugs | Needle marks on stems, growth stops. When surrounding weeds are rampant | Clear old grass around the field |

> **Purslane's pest and disease grade is C not because the crop is weak but because no one has ever watched over it.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Only root rot really ruins the harvest; the rest is handled by drainage, thinning for airflow, picking by hand, soapy water (little damage because the leaves are thick), bands of wood ash, and wood vinegar at 300-500 times dilution. Purslane's being in the field is itself a kind of control. It becomes a trap crop that takes pests in place of the main crop, and it covers bare ground to prevent soil splash and evaporation.

| Symptom | Cause and remedy |
|:---|:---|
| Pale green leaves, small plant | Nitrogen shortage or extreme poverty of soil. Urine diluted 15 times once (a harvest is still possible if left alone) |
| Unusually large, soft leaves that lie down easily | Nitrogen excess (nitrate has built up). Stop fertilizer |
| Leaves red or deep purple | Cold, phosphorus shortage, strong light and dryness. Mostly a normal reaction |
| Edges of old leaves dry and scorch | Potassium shortage or salt excess. A little wood ash; wash out salts with water |
| Clumped, deformed new shoots | Calcium or boron shortage. Burn and crush shells and eggshells and mix them in |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Thin, long stems mean lack of light, not a nutrient problem. Purslane meets excess more often than shortage, so when in doubt, stop fertilizer first.

## 7. Intercropping and Crop Rotation
| Compatibility | Companions |
|:---|:---|
| Good | Tomatoes, peppers, eggplants (cover the bed to prevent soil splash and evaporation; the heights differ so they do not compete for light), corn (both are C4 and grow well in summer heat, but avoid the shade of corn), pumpkins and oriental melons (both creep but at different layers, so they coexist) |
| Bad | Carrots, spinach, radish (slow when young and get covered by purslane), freshly transplanted seedlings (they wilt if covered before they take root), crops watered often (purslane rots from excess water) |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Continuous-cropping tolerance is SS, so rotation is unnecessary, but within other crops' rotations it can serve as a crop covering the empty summer field after potatoes or garlic are dug, in the bed shoulders and furrows the main crop does not use, and as a border strip that doubles as a trap crop and a picking source. On newly broken poor land where other crops cannot establish, it is the only crop that yields in the first year.

## 8. Harvest and Seed Saving
| Harvest | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Timing | Baby leaves at 4-5 weeks (combined with thinning, the most tender), regular harvest at 6-8 weeks (stems 15-20 cm, before flowering), regrowth harvest 10-20 days after cutting (when new shoots reach 15 cm), and medicinal or tea use around flowering (constituents are strongest) |
| Technique | Cut with scissors or a sickle 2-3 nodes above the base, taking the top 10-15 cm of stem each time (roots are shallow, so pulling by hand uproots it). Plenty of soil sticks to the underside of leaves, so soak and shake them in water and then rinse under running water |
| Yield by cutting | The first cutting is the largest, the second is 70%, the third is 50%, and the fourth (more than 20 days after the third) is small and tough, so it usually ends there |

| Seed saving | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Characteristics | It self-pollinates so there is no worry of mixing with other plants, one plant sets thousands to tens of thousands of seeds, and the seed keeps its germinating power for decades in the soil. Most of it happens by itself, so seed saving is optional |
| Procedure | Stop cutting and decide on a few plants to let flower. When the fruit dries brown and its upper part is about to open like a lid (Sep-Oct), cut the whole stem and dry it upside down on paper or cloth, rub the dry stems by hand to shake out the seed, and keep it in a sealed container in a cool, dark place |
| Storage life | Decades naturally in the soil, over 5 years sealed at room temperature, and over 10 years if cool and dry. In damp conditions mold makes it unusable |

> **When purslane fruit ripens it splits crosswise like a lid and spills its seed. If you wait until it is completely dry, the seed has already fallen to the ground.**

## 9. Storage
| Method | Procedure and period |
|:---|:---|
| Leaving it in the field | Leave it uncut until frost and cut only what is needed. Practically the only real answer. Pulled up with the roots and left in shade, 3-5 days; with the base in water, 2-3 days |
| Drying | Wash, shake off the soil, blanch in boiling water for 30 seconds to 1 minute (color stays and oxalic acid decreases), and squeeze out as much water as possible. Spread in a shaded, well-ventilated place (direct sun destroys constituents) and keep in a sealed container with ash or charcoal |
| Pickling, fermentation | Layer with 3-5% salt and keep in a cool place for 2-3 months / press under 2-3% brine for 1-2 weeks of fermentation, then 1-2 months in a cool place / pack in soy sauce or soybean paste for several months |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Storability E, 2-3 days at room temperature, is purslane's greatest weakness, so the field itself is the storehouse. Dried purslane changes character from the fresh and is used as seasoned greens and tea in winter. Lactic fermentation is a way to gain storage time while saving salt, and purslane has a mucilage, so pickling strengthens the slimy texture.

## 10. Cooking and Precautions
| Method | Tips |
|:---|:---|
| Raw | Washed and eaten as is. Needs no fuel; sour and mucilaginous, and vitamin C stays intact |
| Blanched greens | Boiling water for 30 seconds to 1 minute (no longer), rinsed in cold water and tossed with seasoning. Oxalic acid decreases and volume shrinks so you eat more |
| Stir-fry, soup, porridge | Stir-frying in oil for 2-3 minutes improves beta-carotene absorption / in soups and stews, add at the end (long boiling makes it dissolve) / chopped into grain porridge, the mucilage thickens it |
| Tea | Boil the dried leaves. A winter way of using it |

| Component | Problem and response |
|:---|:---|
| Oxalic acid | Roughly 1,000-1,700 mg per 100 g raw, similar to or higher than spinach. It binds calcium, iron, and magnesium and blocks their absorption, and eating too much carries a risk of kidney stones. Be especially careful with a history of kidney stones or poor kidney function. Blanching and discarding the water removes 30-50%, so eat about 100 g a day raw and blanch anything more |
| Look-alike poisonous plant (spurge) | Spurges of the Euphorbiaceae family release a white milky sap where cut, have thin, paper-like leaves that are always opposite, with fine teeth on the edges and often a purplish spot in the center. Purslane has a clear sap, thick succulent leaves that are alternate, and smooth edges. Eating spurge burns the mouth and throat and causes vomiting and diarrhea |
| Contamination | Your own field or garden is safe. Roadsides carry high risk from lead and other heavy metals and exhaust deposits, field edges may have been sprayed with herbicide, and long-abandoned vacant lots should be avoided if the former use is unknown. Do not pick on factory or waste sites |

> **If white sap comes out when you cut the stem or tear a leaf, it is not purslane. Checking this one thing screens out nearly all the spurge-family poisonous plants.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Until you are sure of the distinction, always snap the stem of each plant. The similarly named wild amaranths belong to a different group from purslane but are all edible, so mixing them up is not dangerous. Traditionally purslane was regarded as a cold-natured plant and was not recommended to people who feel cold.

## 11. Annual Work Schedule
| Month | Work |
|:---|:---|
| Jan-Feb | Keep seed in a cool, dry place; dried purslane for seasoned greens and tea |
| Mar-Apr | Choose a sunny spot where water does not pool in March. In April, after the frost has completely passed, sow, barely covering the seed and pressing it in |
| May-June | Water management during germination and first thinning (spacing 5 cm, eat what is thinned) in May. Second thinning (10-15 cm) in June, with natural germination beginning where seed fell the previous year |
| July-Aug | First cutting in July (leave 2-3 nodes at the base), the peak of the hot season. Repeat second and third cuttings in August |
| Sep-Oct | Finish cutting in September and leave seed plants to flower. In October when fruit turns brown, cut the whole stem, dry in shade to collect seed, and blanch and dry what remains |
| Nov-Dec | The aboveground part dies with the first frost in November, and the spot is left unplowed. Use dried and pickled stock and check seed in December |

> **Purslane guards the table in July and August, when other vegetables collapse in the heat. These two months are the real reason to plant this crop.**
