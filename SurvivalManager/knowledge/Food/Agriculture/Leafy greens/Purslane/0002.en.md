## 1. Disaster-Preparedness Evaluation

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Purslane's Overall grade is C, with a score of 42.1 points. Broken down by scenario, its Short-term ability to endure the early stage of a disaster is C (41.3), its Long-term ability to be self-reliant over many years is C (43.0), and its Supplementary value, seen as a side-dish resource apart from the staple, is SS (69.2). There is a 27-point gap between Overall and Supplementary, and this gap is the key to reading purslane. It means that its overall performance as a crop is in the lower-middle range, while its worth as a side dish is at the very top.

### 1-3. Why a Crop with Zero Inputs Scores Low

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This table is a little bewildering at first sight. Nine SS grades are attached, yet the Overall grade is C. Fertilizer demand SS, Water use SS, Infrastructure needs SS, Cooking & processing SS, Temperature range SS, Continuous-cropping tolerance SS, Prevalence in South Korea SS, Seed saving SS and Seed longevity SS make nine, and adding Speed to harvest SS and Protein density SS brings it to eleven. Even so, the score stops at 42.1 points.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The reason is simple. Almost every item that carries an SS is on the input side or the reproduction side, and the output side is empty. The D for Calories per area means only 400kcal from 1m², no more than 0.15 times the self-sufficiency line. For one person to cover 2,100kcal a day from purslane alone, they would have to eat 10.5kg. Eating that much of a plant that is 93% water is physically impossible. The Overall score in the disaster-preparedness evaluation is, in the end, a question of how many days a crop can keep that person alive, and purslane has almost no answer to that question.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The SS for Protein density has to be read in the same light. The P:E value of 40.6% comes not from purslane being rich in protein but from its having almost no calories. The protein in 100g is a little over 2g, about 5% of what 100g of soybeans contain. When the denominator is small, the ratio can become as large as you like.

> **In purslane's evaluation table, SS does not mean that this crop is excellent; it means that people have to pay almost no price to obtain it.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;And it is for precisely that reason that the Supplementary grade climbs to SS, 69.2 points. The Supplementary score is calculated separately, leaving out the contribution of calories and counting only worth as a side dish. It is obtained without inputs, is nutrient-dense and has no off-putting taste, so if the role is limited to that of a side dish, the conditions are almost perfect. The 27-point difference between Overall C and Supplementary SS is not a contradiction; it is two ways of expressing a single sentence: seen as a staple meal, this plant is disqualified, and seen as a side dish, it is top-grade.

### 1-4. How to Read the Strengths

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Four of the five Input resources criteria are SS. They are Fertilizer demand, Water use, Infrastructure needs and Cooking & processing, and the picture these four paint together is the real value of purslane.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The SS for Fertilizer demand does not mean that less fertilizer is needed; it means that none is needed at all. And from this comes the unusual value for Yield retention without synthetic inputs: grade B, with a retention of 100%. For other crops this criterion is usually around 40%, which means that yields collapse to less than half when chemical fertilizer is cut off. Purslane has never grown on chemical fertilizer in the first place, so there is nothing to cut off and therefore nothing to lose. In the year after the fertilizer factories stop, when every other crop in the field is cut to a half harvest, only purslane grows exactly as usual. This criterion stopped at grade B because the output itself is small, not because the plant's nature is weak.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The SS for Water use comes from succulence and the mode of photosynthesis. Purslane's leaves and stems are water-storing tissue, and when drought becomes severe it switches its photosynthetic pathway altogether. Section 2 explains just how unusual this switch is. To state only the result: for more than a month with no rain, while every other vegetable wilts and lies flat, purslane holds on by thickening its leaves. It is also common for a plant that has been pulled up and tossed on the field to root again a few days later.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The SS for Speed to harvest, within 30 days of germination, is among the fastest in this table. It means that the time from the start of a disaster until the first thing from the field goes into your mouth is one month. Compared with potato at 90 days or Welsh onion at more than 200 days, it is in another league. For filling the gap of the first month or two, there is nothing like it.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The SS for Seed longevity is the top grade in this entire table. Purslane seeds buried in the soil stay alive for decades. In fact, when long-neglected ground is turned over, purslane that had not been seen for several years comes up all at once the following year. This means that seed does not even need to be kept in envelopes, and in a situation where storing seed is survival, that is a great safeguard. Read together with the SS for Seed saving, it means that once you have given the plant a place, there is nothing left for people to do about seed.

### 1-5. How to Read the Weaknesses

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Three criteria carry an E: Sunlight, Cold hardiness and Storability. D went to Calories per area, Tillage & digging energy, Yield stability and Non-food value.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The E for Sunlight is the criterion most easily misunderstood. Because purslane grows anywhere along a roadside, it seems it should also do well in shade, but the opposite is true. Purslane is a C4 photosynthesizer, and the C4 pathway is designed to concentrate carbon dioxide under strong light and high temperatures and so raise efficiency. In exchange, running the pathway costs extra energy, so when light weakens, that extra cost becomes a straight loss. Relative yield falling to 45% at 40% shade means losing more than half. Purslane is in the same boat as maize, and in practice the places where it flourishes are always bare ground with the sun beating straight down. Do not count on purslane under trees, in the shade of a building, or in shaded furrows between other crops.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The E for Cold hardiness is a wall set by the seasons. A single frost turns the above-ground parts to mush and kills them. Purslane is not a crop like kale or garlic chive that stays standing in the winter field; it appears in late spring and disappears with the first frost. There is a caveat, however. Even though the above-ground parts die, the seeds dropped that year pass the winter in the soil and come up on their own in late spring of the following year. At the level of the individual plant it is an annual, but at the level of the patch it behaves like a perennial. The SS for Continuous-cropping tolerance points to this trait.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The E for Storability is the price of succulence. Tissue that is 93% water begins to soften the moment it is cut. At room temperature it goes limp within 2-3 days. Without refrigeration there is no way to solve this weakness head-on, but there are two detours. One is to leave the plants standing in the field and cut only as much as will be eaten, when it is needed; the other is to change its nature by blanching and drying it or salting it. Section 15 covers this.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The D for Yield stability, with a year-to-year variation of 24.8%, means that the harvest swings widely from year to year. This is the fate of a crop that depends on natural germination. The timing and density of germination change with how the spring rain fell, when the soil warmed, and how much earlier-growing plants covered the ground. Fixing purslane at a planned yield is impossible; it has to be counted as a resource that you eat if it is there and do without if it is not.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The C grades for Pest & disease pressure and Pest-control self-reliance look bad if you go by the letters alone, but the substance is different. These two grades were not assigned by observing purslane itself; they are estimates borrowed from the average of a higher taxonomic group, the dicot class Magnoliopsida. The notation Reliability L6 reflects that circumstance. In actual fields, almost no pest or disease that is fatal to purslane has been reported. What the Basis column for the C in Pest-control self-reliance says, "No pest control needed," is closer to the reality. This is a rare case where the numbers in the table and experience in the field diverge, and Section 9 covers the difference in detail.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The D for Tillage & digging energy comes from a calculation in which the calories obtained from tilled ground come to only 3.7 times the calories spent on digging. For other crops this multiple reaches 10-40 times. But this criterion is effectively meaningless for purslane, because there is no need to till the ground for it. If the ground is not tilled, the digging energy is 0 and the multiple becomes infinite. This grade should be read as a figure showing the inefficiency of growing purslane properly, as a regular crop like the others, and it is in itself a signal not to treat purslane as a field crop.
