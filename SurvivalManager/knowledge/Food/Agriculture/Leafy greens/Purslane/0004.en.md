## 4. Varieties

### 4-1. Characteristics by Type

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There is almost nothing in purslane that could be called a bred cultivar. Its history as a commercial crop is short, and in most regions it has been classified as a weed. The distinctions stop at wild types versus cultivated types, and differences in leaf size.

| Type | Leaves and habit | Yield | Suitability for disasters |
|---|---|:---:|---|
| Wild type (field purslane) | Small leaves; stems lie completely flat on the ground | Low | Comes up by itself anywhere, so securing it takes no effort |
| Large-leaved type (big-leaf purslane) | Broad, thick leaves and large plants | High | Yields nearly twice as much as the wild type from the same area |
| Small-leaved type | Small leaves with concentrated constituents | Low | Good for medicinal use and tea, with a strong flavor |
| Upright type (golden purslane, etc.) | Stems grow upright | Medium | Easy to cut and less soil sticks to it, so it is easy to clean |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In Europe and the Middle East purslane has been grown as a vegetable for a long time, and the lines from there are the cultivated types with large leaves and upright stems. In South Korea, too, the seed that can be obtained is mostly of this line. However, with disaster conditions in mind there is no great reason to cling to this distinction. Wild types that come up by themselves in the field are perfectly fine to eat, and when seed of the cultivated type falls and a few years pass, it mixes with the wild type and the distinction blurs anyway.

### 4-2. Selection Criteria by Situation

| Situation | Choice | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Purslane is already growing in the field | Use it as it is | It is a type already adapted to the conditions of that spot |
| Want to raise the yield | Large-leaved type | Large leaves and thick stems give a larger amount per harvest |
| Want to manage it in a small space | Upright type | It does not spread sideways, so its boundary is easy to hold |
| For medicinal use and tea | Small-leaved type | Its constituents are concentrated |
| Plan to store seed for a long time | Any type | Purslane seed has a very long lifespan |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;With other crops it is important to distinguish fixed (true-breeding) varieties from hybrids, but not with purslane. Purslane pollinates itself (it is self-pollinating), and if you collect seed and plant it, what comes up is almost identical to the parent. The SS for Seed saving in the evaluation table comes from this. It is one of the few crops for which you need not worry about securing seed, and in practice, even without any worry, the seed falls by itself and comes up the next year.

> **For purslane, far more important than choosing a variety is where you pick it. Purslane that has grown along roadsides and on vacant lots has been exposed to exhaust fumes, herbicides and heavy metals, and this plant has a tendency to accumulate such substances in its tissues.**

## 5. Field Preparation and Fertilizer

### 5-1. Preparing the Field

| Item | Standard | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Tillage depth | Shallow, 10-15cm | The roots are shallow, so there is no need to till deeply |
| Raised bed | Make one, even a low one | Securing drainage is its only purpose |
| Acidity correction | Not done | It tolerates pH 5.6-7.8 as it is |
| Organic matter | Not added | Too much fertilizer makes the leaves soft and leggy |
| Site | A spot with sun all day | Shade cuts the yield in half |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Where the field-preparation items for other crops deal with what more to add, for purslane they deal with what not to add. If purslane is planted in a field loaded with compost and tilled deeply to make it fluffy, the stems go soft and the leaves get bigger, but the plants lodge easily and rot. Its body was built for poor ground, so when nutrients are in excess the balance collapses instead.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The D for Tillage & digging energy should also be read in this context. If you till the ground for purslane alone, you get little in return for the labor put in. So in actual practice, people use the edges of fields already tilled for other crops, or use untilled idle ground as it is. On hard ground, it is enough to scratch only the surface with a rake so that the seed touches the soil.

### 5-2. Fertilizer Rates

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The SS for Fertilizer demand in the evaluation table means there is nothing to add. No separate standard that could be called a standard fertilizer rate has been set up; the following table lists the minimum that helps regrowth when harvesting is repeated several times.

| Nutrient | Standard | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Nitrogen (N) | A very small amount after cutting | Helps new shoots regrow. Regrowth happens without it too |
| Phosphate (P) | Not applied | Adapted to poor ground |
| Potassium (K) | Not applied | Adapted to poor ground |
| Compost | Not applied | Too much makes the plants leggy and soft |
| Lime | Not applied | Adapted to a wide range of acidity |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There is a reason nitrogen is listed as a very small amount. Purslane is a plant that tends to accumulate nitrates in its tissues. Purslane grown in a field given plenty of nitrogen fertilizer builds up high nitrates in the leaves. A plate or two of namul (seasoned vegetable side dish) is not an amount that would cause a problem, but there is all the less reason to raise that concentration by deliberately adding fertilizer. Holding back on fertilizer is, for this crop, not a yield loss but a gain in quality.

### 5-3. Substitute Fertilizers for Disasters

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The reason purslane is special when chemical fertilizer has stopped lies in the very fact that there is almost nothing to cover in this section. Still, when you want to keep harvesting from the same spot through three or four repeated cuttings, a little supplementation helps.

| Substitute resource | Nutrients supplied | How to use | Cautions |
|---|---|---|---|
| Human urine | Nitrogen, potassium | Dilute 10-15 times with water and pour into the furrow right after cutting | Use it more dilute than for other crops. It leads to nitrate accumulation |
| Well-rotted compost | All-round | Lay on only about a handful, very thinly | If covered thickly, the stems touch it and go soft |
| Wood ash | Potassium, calcium | Scatter a handful after cutting | Alkaline. Purslane tolerates it, but neighboring crops are affected |
| Plant liquid fertilizer | Nitrogen, potassium | Dilute 10 times and apply to the soil | Leaves a mark if it touches the leaves |
| Leaf mold | Organic matter, trace elements | Thinly on the germination spot | Doubles as moisture retention during germination |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;It is worth noting here that the role of fertilizer differs from that for other crops. For kale or Welsh onion, fertilizer is the raw material that makes the yield. For purslane, fertilizer is closer to a switch that regulates how quickly new shoots come up from the cut. With fertilizer, regrowth is faster; without it, shoots come up slowly. Either way, they do come up in the end.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;If fertilizer is scarce, it increases the overall harvest to divert that fertilizer to kale, napa cabbage or Welsh onion instead of purslane. The Yield retention without synthetic inputs value of 100% is precisely the basis for that judgment. Purslane loses nothing when it gets no fertilizer, whereas other crops lose half if they get none.

## 6. Sowing

### 6-1. Sowing Time

| Cropping type | Sowing (South Korea) | Harvest (South Korea) | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Open-field spring sowing | Mid-April to May | Late May to October | The guide is after the risk of frost has completely passed |
| Open-field summer sowing | June to early July | July to October | High soil temperature gives the fastest germination |
| Natural germination | Not sown | June to October | Comes up by itself where seed fell the previous year |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There is no constraint other than the condition that frost risk has passed. On the contrary, if sown too early, the soil temperature falls short of 15°C and the seed simply waits in the soil. It does not die; it waits until it warms up and then germinates. This waiting is itself a characteristic of purslane seed, an everyday version of the trait of holding out in the soil for decades.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The natural germination in the third row is the center of actual practice. A spot where purslane was grown for a year and allowed to drop its seed needs no sowing from the next year on. In a disaster, there is nothing like it for saving seed packets and eliminating sowing labor, and the price is that people cannot decide where or how many plants will come up. This is the substance of the D for Yield stability.

### 6-2. Sowing Method

| Item | Standard | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Sowing depth | 0.2-0.5cm, hardly covered | The seeds are very tiny and need light to germinate well |
| How to sow | Broadcast or in rows | The seeds are tiny, so it is hard to sow them evenly |
| Row spacing | 20-30cm | When sown in rows |
| Final plant spacing | 10-15cm | Adjusted by thinning |
| Days to germination | 3-7 days at a soil temperature of 20-30°C | |
| After sowing | Press lightly with the palm of the hand or a board | The seed must be in close contact with the soil to take up moisture |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Not burying the seed deeply is nearly all there is to sowing purslane. The seed is a little over 0.5mm across, smaller than ground pepper, and the distance it can reach to come up on the nutrients inside is no more than a few mm. A seed buried 1cm down sprouts, tires out and dies. In addition, purslane seed germination is promoted by light, so sowing without covering the seed with soil and then pressing it down actually gives a higher germination rate.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;When the seed is too tiny to sow evenly by hand, mix it into dry sand or fine soil to increase the volume and then sow. About ten spoonfuls of sand to one spoonful of seed gives a feel that the hand can grasp, and the density becomes visible. If ash is mixed in, the sown spot looks white, so you can check where you have already passed.

### 6-3. Seed Treatment

| Method | Procedure | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Dusting with ash | Lightly coat the seed with sifted wood ash | Disinfects the surface and makes the sown spot visible |
| Mixing with sand | Dilute 10 times with sand and sow | Lets you adjust the density by eye |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The hot-water soaking or chemical disinfection done with other crops is not needed for purslane. No serious seed-borne disease is known, and in the first place the seed is so tiny that once it is soaked in water it is hard to fish out. Soaking in water has no clear effect either. For this crop, seed treatment is in practice a question of how to sow evenly.

## 7. Water Management

### 7-1. Water by Growth Stage

| Period | Management | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Sowing to germination | Keep the surface soil from drying out | Only these 2 weeks need human hands |
| Seedling stage | Once every 2-3 days | After this it becomes very much stronger |
| Vegetative growth stage | No watering needed | In a drought the leaves just get thicker |
| Harvest period | Once right after cutting | Speeds up regrowth of new shoots |
| Flowering and fruiting stage | Stop watering instead | The drier it is, the better the seed ripens |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Where the water-management tables of other crops deal with when and how much to give, the table for purslane deals with when to take one's hands off. Total consumption is under 250L per 1m², and compared with kale's 400mm or water spinach's 1,500L, it is on a different order of magnitude. This is the substance of the SS for Water use in the evaluation table.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In a disaster where water is scarce, this trait means a great deal. Once the wells dry up and people are reduced to hauling water on a muljige (a shoulder frame for carrying water buckets), the total amount of water that can be given to the field becomes the total amount of crops that can be grown. In that calculation, purslane does not enter the denominator. It is a crop that gives edible leaves without taking up a line of the water-allocation table, and such crops are not common.

### 7-2. Symptoms of Too Little and Too Much Water

| Symptom | Cause | What is happening |
|---|---|---|
| Leaves become small, thick and reddish | Water shortage | A normal adaptive response. The plant does not die |
| Leaves picked at dawn are unusually sour | Switch to CAM due to drought | Organic acids collected overnight have built up |
| Stems become thin and long, and the color is pale | Light shortage | Not a water problem. The plant must be moved |
| Turns brown and soft from the base | Excess moisture, root rot | The whole plant collapses within a few days |
| Leaves turn yellow and drop | Lack of oxygen at the roots | A spot where water pools |
| Leaves are soft and large and the plant lodges easily | Too much water and fertilizer | Storability and texture worsen |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The point of this table is to tell the difference between the first row and the fourth row. When purslane turns red and shrivels, it is not a problem but a coping response. When water is short, it thickens the wax layer on the leaves and increases red pigment to protect its tissue from strong light. Purslane in this state gets a stronger flavor and a higher concentration of constituents. By contrast, once the base starts to turn brown and soft, it is already too late. Roots rot much faster than the above-ground parts wilt.

### 7-3. Mulching

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Purslane not only needs no mulching but is one of the few crops that treats mulch as an obstacle. The seed germinates well only when it receives light, and covering it with straw or fallen leaves blocks that light; and because the stems creep along the ground, they end up lying on top of the cover, which actually makes ventilation worse. In a spot where you expect natural germination, it is better to leave the soil bare.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Turn the direction around, however, and it becomes useful. Purslane itself acts as mulch. Because it creeps along the ground and covers it densely, the soil underneath is not heated directly by the sun and water evaporation is reduced. When purslane carpets the empty ground on a raised bed of tomatoes or chili peppers, it becomes a living cover, and because its roots are shallow, it does not compete much with the crop for water. Section 11 covers this relationship again.
