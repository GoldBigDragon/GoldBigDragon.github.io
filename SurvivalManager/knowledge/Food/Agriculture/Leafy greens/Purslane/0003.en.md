## 2. What Kind of Plant Is This?

### 2-1. Classification and Origin

| Item | Details |
|---|---|
| Scientific name | Portulaca oleracea |
| Taxonomy | Order Caryophyllales, family Portulacaceae, genus Portulaca |
| Other names | machihyeon, dwaejipul ("pig grass"), ohaengcho ("five-elements herb"), jangmyeongchae ("long-life vegetable") |
| Origin | Presumed to be India or the eastern Mediterranean; now naturalized worldwide |
| Life form | Annual. Reappears in the same place every year through natural self-sowing |
| Photosynthetic pathway | C4. Switches to CAM under drought stress |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There are several theories about where purslane originated, but the debate itself has lost its meaning. It grows on every continent except Antarctica and turns up almost without exception wherever people live. A plant that has spread this widely usually meets two conditions: its seeds are small and numerous, and it is not choosy about where it lives. Purslane meets both conditions to an extreme degree. A single plant produces tens of thousands of seeds, and those seeds travel mixed into soil, on shoe soles, on cart wheels and inside the bellies of livestock.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There is one more piece of history that shaped this plant's character. The places where purslane mainly grows are the fringes of deserts, gravel beds along rivers, sandy ground by the sea, and roadsides packed hard by foot traffic: ground where water and nutrients are both short and only sunshine is plentiful. Other plants cannot hold on there, so the space is open, but to survive there a plant has to save water and withstand strong light. Its succulent tissue, its C4 photosynthesis, and its vitality to take root again even after being cut all came out of those places.

### 2-2. Appearance

| Organ | Features |
|---|---|
| Leaf | Thick, glossy, succulent, egg-shaped. Spatula-shaped with a rounded tip and smooth margins |
| Leaf arrangement | Alternate or almost opposite. Several leaves cluster together at the stem tips |
| Stem | Cylindrical, reddish to purplish. Creeps along the ground and spreads in all directions, 20-30cm long |
| Root | One thick white main root with shallow side roots. Even when cut, roots form again at the nodes |
| Flower | Yellow flower about 5mm across. 5 petals. June to September in South Korea; opens only on sunny mornings |
| Fruit | A capsule that splits crosswise like a lid (pyxis). Dozens of tiny black seeds per fruit |
| Sap | A clear juice comes out when it is cut. A white juice never comes out |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The last row is the most important in this table. When a purslane stem is cut, a transparent, slightly sticky clear juice comes out. If a juice like white milk comes out, it is not purslane but another plant of the spurge family (Euphorbiaceae), and it must not be eaten. Section 14 explains this distinction again in detail.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;It is also worth knowing that the flowers open only on sunny mornings. If you inspect the field on a cloudy day or in the afternoon, you may pass by without seeing a single flower. For seed saving, rather than noting when the flowers bloomed, it is more reliable to check whether the small jar-shaped fruits with lids have formed at the stem nodes.

### 2-3. Photosynthesis That Switches Between C4 and CAM

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This is where purslane differs fundamentally from other leafy vegetables. Most plants use ordinary C3 photosynthesis, some, such as maize and sorghum, use C4 photosynthesis, and desert plants such as cacti and agaves use CAM photosynthesis. Purslane runs on C4 as a rule and switches to CAM when drought becomes severe. Plants equipped with both modes are very rare, and this switch is what gives purslane its extreme drought tolerance.

| Pathway | When carbon dioxide is taken in | When stomata open | Water loss | Pros and cons |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| C3 | Day | Day | High | Most vegetables. Little loss when light is weak |
| C4 | Day | Day | Medium | High efficiency in strong light and high temperatures. Large loss when light is weak |
| CAM | Night | Night | Very low | Slow growth, but survives extreme drought |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The route by which a plant loses water is the stomata. To take in carbon dioxide it must open its stomata, and when it opens them, water vapor escapes. The day is hot and dry, so opening by day is the costlier choice. CAM plants resolve this dilemma by dividing up time. They open their stomata only at night, when it is cool, take in carbon dioxide and store it in the form of organic acids, and by day they keep the stomata tightly shut and photosynthesize using the stored carbon dioxide.

> **When water is available, purslane grows quickly on C4; when the water stops, it switches to CAM, stops growing and holds on. In a situation where the field cannot be irrigated, this switch is survival itself.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This switch comes at a price. Purslane that has turned to CAM hardly grows at all. It means the plant is alive, but there is nothing more to harvest. And because organic acids accumulate in the leaves, purslane picked at dawn tastes strongly sour. In fact, purslane picked early in the morning after a long dry spell is tart. This is not spoilage but normal, and as the day passes the acids are used up, so the sourness fades in the afternoon.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Another consequence of being C4 is the Sunlight E seen earlier. C4 plants run an extra process that concentrates carbon dioxide once more inside the cell, and that process costs energy. When light is plentiful it is a profitable trade, but when light is short, that cost goes straight into the red. Plant purslane in the shade and it will not die, but the harvest is cut in half.

### 2-4. Growth Stages

| Stage | Timing | What happens | Main needs |
|---|---|---|---|
| Germination and seedling stage | 2 weeks from sowing | Two tiny seed leaves emerge. This is the only stage at which the plant is weak | Moisture in the topsoil, soil temperature of 20°C or higher |
| Vegetative growth stage | Weeks 2-6 | Stems creep along the ground and spread in all directions, and the leaves thicken | Sunlight, a little nitrogen |
| Best time to harvest | Weeks 6-8 | Stems are 15-20cm, and leaves and stems are at their most tender | Balance |
| Flowering and fruiting stage | Weeks 8-12 | Flowers bloom at every node and fruits set. Stems become tough | Phosphorus, potassium |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;It is worth pointing out that the seedling stage is the only period of weakness. Fully grown purslane revives even if it is trampled underfoot or pulled up and thrown aside, but newly opened seed leaves die if the surface soil dries out completely for even a single day. This is why, in nature, purslane comes up all at once right after rain. If people have to lend a hand, these 2 weeks are all there is, and after that there is nothing to do.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Once flowering begins, fiber increases in the stems and they become tough, and the leaves also get smaller. For a harvest to eat, it is best to cut before flowering, and if the aim is to collect seed, a few plants must be set aside and left until the end. These two aims cannot both be met from the same plant.

### 2-5. Nutrition per 100g

| Nutrient | Content | Notes |
|---|:---:|---|
| Energy | 16-20kcal | Varies widely by source, from 7-20kcal |
| Water | about 93g | The main reason the energy content is low |
| Protein | about 2.0g | Protein share of calories (P:E) is 40.6% |
| Fat | about 0.4g | A considerable part of it is alpha-linolenic acid |
| Alpha-linolenic acid (ALA) | 300-400mg | An exceptional figure for a leafy vegetable |
| Potassium | 494mg | Involved in sodium excretion and blood pressure regulation |
| Magnesium | 68mg | Near the top among leafy vegetables |
| Calcium | 65mg | Absorption falls because of oxalic acid |
| Iron | 0.9-2.0mg | Differs widely by source |
| Vitamin C | 21mg | More than half is lost on blanching |
| Vitamin A | Large amount in the form of beta-carotene | Absorption improves when eaten with oil |
| Vitamin E | High | Among the best of all namul (seasoned vegetable side dish) greens |
| B vitamins | Thiamin, niacin, vitamin B6, folate | |
| Oxalic acid | High | Covered separately in Section 14 |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;It is not strange that the energy figure ranges from 7 to 20kcal depending on the source. Because purslane is a succulent, its water content varies greatly with the environment it grew in. Purslane grown on wet ground in a rainy year is almost all water, while purslane grown on dry ground in a dry year has twice the dry matter in the same weight. The 20kcal used in the evaluation is the higher of these values. Whichever value is used, the conclusion is the same: calculating calories from this plant is meaningless.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The alpha-linolenic acid row is the heart of this table. Omega-3 fatty acids are essential fatty acids that the body cannot make and must get by eating, and the main sources are oily fish, perilla seeds, flaxseed and walnuts. In a disaster most of this list disappears. Fishing stops, and pressing oil becomes difficult too. Leafy vegetables normally cannot fill this gap, but purslane, exceptionally, holds 300-400mg per 100g. The recommended daily intake is roughly 1,600-2,000mg, so it is hard to meet that with purslane alone, but a diet with absolutely none and a diet that takes in some are entirely different stories.

## 3. Growing Conditions

### 3-1. Temperature

| Category | Temperature | Result |
|---|:---:|---|
| Germination minimum | about 15°C | Almost no germination below this |
| Optimum for germination | 20-30°C | Germinates evenly in 3-7 days |
| Optimum for growth | 20-35°C | The middle of summer is when it grows best |
| Growth range | 10-35°C | Range 25°C. Basis for the SS in Temperature range |
| High-temperature limit | Survives even at 40°C or above | Growth slows but the plant does not die |
| Low-temperature limit | Growth stops at 5°C or below | |
| Lethal | 0°C, a single frost | The above-ground parts turn to mush and die |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Among the vegetables covered in this document, purslane is one of those that like summer best. July and August in South Korea, when lettuce, napa cabbage and spinach melt away in the heat and bolt, are purslane's prime. In a disaster-time field it is common for one month in the middle of summer to become a gap in vegetables, and purslane fills exactly that gap. This is one of the reasons to keep this plant around even though it has no calories and cannot be stored.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The other end is merciless. One dip below freezing and it is finished. It is the exact opposite of crops like kale that actually turn sweeter after a frost, and on the morning after the first frost every purslane plant in the field is blackened and limp. There is no need to feel sorry at that point, because the seeds have already fallen on the soil before then.

### 3-2. Light

| Condition | Relative yield | Interpretation |
|---|:---:|---|
| Full sun all day | 100% | This is the baseline |
| 40% shade | about 45% | Confidence interval 37-56%. Basis for the E in Sunlight |
| Partial shade | Greatly reduced | It survives, but the stems become thin and long |
| Deep shade | Hardly grows | It does not even germinate well |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This is where the most common misunderstanding about purslane lies. Because it grows anywhere like a weed, people assume it must not care about light, but in reality it is more sensitive to light than any other crop in this document. This is because of the C4 photosynthesis seen earlier.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;What this means for cultivation is clear. Purslane is not a crop to put under maize, between tomatoes or in the shade of trees. Its proper place is a spot that gets sun all day long, such as the end of a raised bed, idle ground at the edge of a field, a sunny spot under a fence, or a roadside slope. It is no exaggeration to say that finding a sunny spot where it will not compete with other crops is almost all there is to managing purslane.

### 3-3. Water and Soil

| Item | Standard | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Soil texture | Almost any soil | Grows even on gravel, sandy ground and hard-packed roadsides |
| Soil pH | pH 5.6-7.8 | A very wide range |
| Organic matter | Not needed | A plant adapted to poor ground |
| Drainage | Important | The only item to watch |
| Irrigation | Not needed except at germination | Grows on less than 250L/m² in total |
| Salinity | Tolerates quite a lot | Grows wild even on sandy seashore ground |

> **The cause when purslane dies is almost always not too little water but water that fails to drain away.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The common weakness of succulents is excess moisture. Because the tissue itself holds water, once the area around the roots is also submerged, oxygen is cut off and rot sets in right away. Purslane in a spot where water stood for a few days during the monsoon season (jangma, late June to July in South Korea) rots away entirely. Conversely, even after a month of drought the leaves merely shrivel a little and the plant does not die. Knowing this asymmetry settles the direction of management. Rather than thinking about giving water, it is far more effective to choose a spot where water will not pool.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The wide range of soil pH is also of great value in a disaster. When lime runs out and a field tilts toward acidity, crops of the cabbage family (Brassicaceae) collapse to clubroot and legume crops cannot form root nodules, yet purslane is not affected at all. It is one of the few crops that can be used as it is when there are no resources to spend on correcting acidity.
