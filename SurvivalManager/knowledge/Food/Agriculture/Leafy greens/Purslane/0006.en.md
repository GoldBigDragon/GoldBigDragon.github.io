## 12. Harvest - Cut and Cut Again

### 12-1. Judging the Timing

| Use | After sowing | Condition |
|---|:---:|---|
| Baby leaves (salad) | 4-5 weeks | Doubles as thinning. The most tender |
| General harvest | 6-8 weeks | Stems 15-20cm, before flowering |
| Repeat harvest | 10-20 days after cutting | When the new shoots again reach 15cm |
| Medicinal and tea use | Around flowering | When its constituents are at their strongest |
| Seed saving | September-October (in South Korea) | When the fruits dry to brown |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;"Before flowering" is the most practical criterion. Once purslane begins to flower, fiber increases in the stems, which become tough, and the leaves get smaller. When yellow flowers start to catch the eye, the plant can be considered to have moved from the eating side to the seed-saving side.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The time of day of harvest also affects flavor. In plants that have switched to CAM under drought, the organic acids stored up overnight peak at dawn. That is why purslane picked at dawn is unusually sour; to reduce the sourness, it is better to pick it a few hours after sunrise. Conversely, to put the sourness to use in a salad, early morning is best. Purslane picked under the scorching midday sun, however, softens quickly in its tissues and stores poorly.

### 12-2. Harvesting Technique

| Item | Details |
|---|---|
| Cutting height | 2-3 nodes above the base |
| Tool | Scissors or a sickle. Tearing by hand pulls the plant out by the roots |
| Part taken at one time | The top 10-15cm of the stem |
| Soil after harvest | Because it creeps along the ground, a lot of soil clings to the undersides of the leaves |
| Washing | Soak in water and swish, then rinse under running water |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The roots are shallow and soft, so tearing by hand often pulls up the whole plant. That makes no difference if you plan to harvest once and be done, but if you expect regrowth, you must cut. Without a tool, press down on the base with one hand and snap the stem with the other.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The amount of soil that clings is a more bothersome problem than it sounds. The leaves are thick and concave, so grains of soil lodge in them easily, and because the stems grow along the ground, soil is smeared on every node. Where water is scarce, the water needed for this washing is considerable. The alternative is to shake the soil off in the field beforehand. If you cut, shake the stems a few times on the spot and then put them in the basket, the washing water is halved. Choosing an upright strain greatly reduces the problem.

### 12-3. How Much One Spot Yields

| Round | Timing | Amount |
|---|---|---|
| Thinning harvest | 4-5 weeks after sowing | Small. Tender |
| 1st cutting | 6-8 weeks | The largest |
| 2nd cutting | 10-20 days after the 1st | About 70% of the 1st |
| 3rd cutting | 15-20 days after the 2nd | About 50% of the 1st |
| 4th cutting | 20 days or more after the 3rd | Small and tough. Usually the end of the line |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;With each round the yield shrinks and the stems toughen. This is the natural result of constantly taking from the plot without returning anything. Rather than forcing a 4th cutting, it is better to stop at the 3rd and let that plant flower. Only then does the seed fall and the patch persist into the next year.

## 13. Seed Saving - Helping Along What Happens on Its Own

### 13-1. Why Seed Saving Is Easy

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Very few crops earn SS for both Seed saving and Seed longevity in the evaluation table. There are three reasons.

| Reason | Details |
|---|---|
| Self-fertilization | It pollinates itself. There is no worry of mixing with other plants |
| High seed output | A single plant sets several thousand to tens of thousands of seeds |
| Longevity | The seeds stay viable in the soil for decades |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The cross-pollination problem that Welsh onion and kale suffer from does not exist in purslane. Fertilization with its own pollen is already complete before the flower opens, so the seed does not get mixed no matter what strain grows nearby. This means there is no need to measure isolation distances or cover plants with netting, and from the standpoint of seed self-reliance it is one of the easiest crops.

### 13-2. How to Collect the Seed

| Step | Details |
|---|---|
| Choosing seed plants | Stop cutting and pick a few plants to let flower |
| Judging ripeness | When the fruits dry to brown and the upper part is about to split open like a lid |
| Harvest | Cut the whole stems and dry them upside down on paper or cloth |
| Separation | Rub the dry stems by hand to shake the seeds off |
| Cleaning | Sift through a sieve, or blow the chaff away in the wind |
| Drying | Dry completely in the shade |
| Storage | Put in an airtight container and keep in a cool, dark place |

> **When ripe, the purslane fruit splits crosswise like a lid and spills out its seeds. If you wait until it is completely dry, the seeds have already fallen to the ground.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Because of this trait, timing is the most important thing in seed saving. Cut the whole stems when the fruits have begun to turn brown but have not yet split open, and hang them upside down in the shade. If you spread cloth or paper underneath, the fruits split by themselves as they dry and the seeds drop. The seeds are so small and black that once they fall onto the soil they cannot be picked up again.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;It should be pointed out, though, that all of this work is in fact optional. If you do not save seed and simply leave the plants, the seed falls in the field and comes up by itself the next year. People collect the seed for only two reasons: to plant it in another spot, and because they plan to turn over that spot.

### 13-3. Storage and Longevity

| Condition | Viability retained |
|---|---|
| Natural state in the soil | Decades. The top of this table |
| Airtight storage at room temperature | 5 years or more without trouble |
| Cool, dry place | 10 years or more |
| Damp place | Once mold appears, the seeds become unusable |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;When storing the seeds of other crops, planning assumes that the germination rate falls every year, so seed is renewed every few years. Purslane does not need this. A single packet of seed, once collected, lasts a human generation. In a situation where no one knows how many years a disaster will last, this trait is close to insurance. The seeds also take up almost no space: a single spoonful holds enough to plant thousands of plants.

## 14. Toxicity and Compounds of Concern - Oxalic Acid and Poisonous Look-Alikes

### 14-1. Oxalic Acid

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Purslane is an edible plant and is safe as long as normal amounts are eaten. One compound, however, draws a line on how much to eat: oxalic acid.

| Item | Details |
|---|---|
| Content | Roughly 1,000-1,700mg per 100g raw. Similar to or higher than spinach |
| What it does in the body | Binds calcium, iron and magnesium and blocks their absorption |
| With excess intake | Calcium oxalate stones can form in the kidneys |
| Who should take particular care | People with a history of kidney stones, people with poor kidney function |
| How to reduce it | Blanching in boiling water and discarding the water removes 30-50% |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;A circumstance specific to disaster conditions overlaps with this. Ordinarily a person eats a bite or two of purslane as a side dish and that is the end of it, but when food runs short, this free weed from the field comes to be eaten in large quantities, like a staple. That is when the oxalic acid problem actually surfaces. And such a situation is usually one in which calcium intake is also short. In a diet where milk and fish eaten with their bones are gone, losing even the little calcium there is to oxalic acid is a heavy loss.

> **Purslane can be eaten every day, but it is not a crop to eat one's fill of at every meal. The safe line is around 100g a day when eaten raw, and blanching it and discarding the water when eating more than that.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;It is also worth knowing why blanching works. Oxalic acid dissolves readily in water, so when purslane is blanched in boiling water a considerable amount passes into the water. If the blanching water is used as broth, the effort is pointless, so it must be discarded. Vitamin C is lost along with it, however, so if vitamin C is the aim, it is reasonable to split the use: eat a little raw, and blanch the portion that makes up the quantity. Fermentation also reduces some of the oxalic acid.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Records also survive showing that, traditionally, purslane was regarded as a plant cold in nature and was not recommended to people whose bodies run cold. In fact, it is rich in potassium, has a diuretic effect and acts in the direction of lowering blood pressure, so people with low blood pressure or frequent diarrhea are better off reducing the amount.

### 14-2. Poisonous Look-Alikes - Spurges and Ground Spurges

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This section is the most important part of this document. When gathering purslane in fields or along roadsides, there is a poisonous plant that grows in almost the same places and looks almost the same. It is the ground spurges (the ttangbindae group) of the spurge family (Euphorbiaceae), that is, plants such as aegi-ttangbindae and keun-ttangbindae.

| Feature | Purslane | Ground spurges (Euphorbiaceae) |
|---|---|---|
| Sap at a cut | Clear and transparent | Produces a white, milk-like sap |
| Leaf thickness | Thick and fleshy | Thin and paper-like |
| Leaf arrangement | Alternate, clustered at the stem tips | Always opposite |
| Leaf margin | Smooth | Finely toothed |
| Leaf marking | None | Often a purplish spot in the center |
| Stem | Plump and juicy | Thin and tough, with fine hairs |
| Flower | 5 yellow petals, 5mm across | What look like petals are white or red and very small |
| If eaten | Edible | Burns the mouth and throat and causes vomiting and diarrhea |

> **If white sap comes out when you cut a stem or tear off a leaf, it is not purslane. This one check alone can screen out almost all of the spurge-family poisonous plants.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The compounds in the white sap of spurge-family plants irritate the skin and mucous membranes. If the sap gets into the eyes it causes severe inflammation, and if eaten it brings a burning pain in the mouth and throat followed by vomiting and diarrhea. Fatal cases are rare, but in a disaster, being unable to eat or move for several days is dangerous in itself. White sap should be washed off even when it is only on the hands, and if it has been touched while gathering, do not rub the eyes with that hand.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The surest way to learn to tell them apart is to snap the stem of each plant, one at a time, until you are confident. Purslane's plump stem breaks off with a juicy snap and a little clear sap seeps out. A ground spurge's thin stem tears toughly, and a white droplet forms where it is broken. It is a difference that is hard to forget once seen.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;For reference, gaebireum and chambireum (amaranths with similar-sounding Korean names) are plants of the amaranth family, a different group from purslane, but both are edible, so confusing them is not dangerous. The only dangerous ones are the group that gives off white sap.

### 14-3. Contamination Depending on Where It Grew

| Gathering location | Risk | Judgment |
|---|---|---|
| Own field or kitchen garden | Low | Use with confidence |
| Roadside | High | Lead and other heavy metals, and deposited exhaust fumes |
| Edges of paddies and fields | Medium | Herbicide may have been sprayed |
| Factory and waste sites | Very high | Do not gather |
| Long-abandoned vacant lots | Medium | Avoid if the former use is unknown |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Purslane is known as a plant that accumulates heavy metals from the soil in its tissues. It has even been studied for cleaning up contaminated land. This trait is a survival strategy for the plant but a danger to the person eating it. The fact that it is growing well is no proof that the spot is clean. On the contrary, because it thrives on land that no one tends, it can happen that it grows most lushly on the very land people should touch least.

## 15. Storage Without Refrigeration

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Storability E, with 2-3 days at room temperature, is purslane's greatest weakness. Succulent tissue that is 93% water begins to collapse the moment it is cut. Without electricity there is no way to solve this weakness head-on; there are only detours: changing its nature, or using the field as the storehouse.

### 15-1. The Field as Storehouse

| Method | Duration | Conditions |
|---|---|---|
| Leave uncut in the field | Until frost | Cut only what is needed, when it is needed |
| Pull up with the roots and leave in the shade | 3-5 days | Lasts longer if left with the soil still on |
| Wrap in a damp cloth and keep in a cool place | 3-5 days | Lasts longer if a root-cellar pit or earth cellar is available |
| Stand the stem bases in water | 2-3 days | Only until the leaves soften |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The first row is in effect the only real answer. The solution for a crop that stores poorly is not to store it, and in South Korea purslane stands in the field for more than four months, from June to October, and plays this role. During that period there is no reason to store purslane, and once the period ends, what has been stored does not last long either. So in practice the operation takes the form of continuing to pick and eat through the summer and drying only the winter portion in autumn.

### 15-2. Drying

| Step | Details |
|---|---|
| Preparing | Wash, shake off the soil and drain |
| Blanching | 30 seconds to 1 minute in boiling water. It can be skipped, but blanching keeps the color and reduces oxalic acid |
| Removing water | Squeeze out as much as possible. If water remains, mold appears during drying |
| Spreading out | A shady, well-ventilated place. Drying in direct sun destroys its constituents |
| Judging completion | Until it crumbles when rubbed by hand |
| Storage | In an airtight container with wood ash or charcoal to absorb moisture |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There is a reason drying suits purslane well. With so much water, its volume falls below 10% when dried, so a whole basket becomes a handful. This means it takes up almost no storage space, which is a great advantage where storage resources are scarce. Well-dried, airtight purslane keeps for six months to a year.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;However, dried purslane takes on an entirely different character from fresh. Almost no vitamin C remains, and the texture becomes chewy. In exchange, the minerals, fatty acids and fiber remain and are in fact concentrated. So dried purslane settles into a role not as a winter vitamin source but as a supplement of minerals and fatty acids and as a soup ingredient. It is soaked in water and dressed as namul (seasoned vegetable side dish), added to soups, or boiled and drunk as tea.

### 15-3. Pickling and Fermentation

| Method | Procedure | Duration |
|---|---|---|
| Salt pickling | Layer with salt at 3-5% of the weight | 2-3 months in a cool place |
| Jjanji (heavily salted pickle) | Submerge in a strong brine of 10% or more | 6 months or more. Soak in water before eating to remove the saltiness |
| Lactic acid fermentation | Weigh down so it stays submerged in a 2-3% brine | Ferment for 1-2 weeks, then 1-2 months in a cool place |
| Jangajji (pickled vegetables in soy sauce or paste) | Pack into soy sauce or doenjang (Korean soybean paste) | A few months |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Lactic acid fermentation is a way to gain storage time while saving salt. A 2-3% brine is a concentration that suppresses stray microbes while keeping lactic acid bacteria alive, and the acid the bacteria produce lowers the pH so that spoilage bacteria cannot grow. Whether it succeeds depends on pressing the material down so that it stays submerged and out of contact with air. White mold floating on the surface can simply be skimmed off, but if black or blue mold appears, the batch must be discarded.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Purslane is naturally a mucilaginous plant, so pickling makes the slippery texture stronger. Some people dislike this, and in some regions it is eaten with relish precisely because of this texture. A brief blanching before putting it in the brine reduces the mucilage.

## 16. Cooking and Processing

| Method | Fuel | Procedure | Notes |
|---|:---:|---|---|
| Raw | 0 | Wash and eat as is | Sourness and mucilage are its character. Vitamin C is intact |
| Blanched namul | Low | 30 seconds to 1 minute in boiling water, rinse in cold water and dress | Oxalic acid and volume are reduced, so more can be eaten |
| Stir-fry | Low | 2-3 minutes in oil | Improves beta-carotene absorption |
| Soups and stews | Medium | Add at the end | Dissolves if boiled long |
| Porridge | Medium | Chop and add to grain porridge | The mucilage thickens it |
| Tea | Low | Boil the dried herb | A winter way of using it |
| Fermentation | 0 | See section 15 | Serves as both storage and cooking |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;That Cooking & processing is graded SS in the evaluation table means purslane can be washed and eaten right away, and where fuel is precious, the value of this criterion is great. However, because of the oxalic acid discussed in the previous section, eating large amounts raw is not advisable. In practice, use divides into a little raw every day, and blanching with the water discarded when making up the quantity.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The knack in blanching is not to go beyond 30 seconds to 1 minute. Being succulent tissue, it goes limp in an instant once heat gets in. The moment the water returns to a boil is the moment to lift it out. Rinsing in cold water after blanching keeps the color and prevents further softening from residual heat.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;How to handle the mucilage is the fork in the road for cooking purslane. Purslane contains a gummy substance called mucilage, which gives a slippery texture when blanched. This is why the Taste grade in the evaluation table is S and yet opinions on the texture are divided. If you dislike it, blanching and rinsing several times in cold water reduces it considerably; conversely, if you make use of this mucilage, it becomes an ingredient that thickens porridge and soup. Where starch is scarce, it can serve as a thickener for broth.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Cooking with oil is especially valuable. Purslane's beta-carotene and vitamin E are fat-soluble and need oil to be absorbed properly. And since the alpha-linolenic acid the plant holds is itself a fat, stir-frying in oil lets that component come out of the tissue more easily. If perilla oil is available, it is a good pairing, being itself a source of omega-3.

## 17. Annual Work Calendar

| Month (South Korea) | Work |
|---|---|
| January-February | Store seed in a cool, dry place. Use dried purslane for namul and tea |
| March | Choose the planting spot. A sunny place where water does not pool |
| April | Sow after the last frost has fully passed. Press the seed in, barely covering it with soil |
| May | Manage moisture during germination. 1st thinning (spacing 5cm). Eat what is thinned out |
| June | 2nd thinning (spacing 10-15cm). Natural germination begins where seed fell the previous year |
| July | 1st cutting begins. Cut, leaving 2-3 nodes at the base. Peak of the hot season |
| August | Repeat the 2nd and 3rd cuttings. The mainstay in the gap when other vegetables collapse in the heat |
| September | Wrap up cutting. Leave seed plants to flower |
| October | When the fruits turn brown, cut the whole stems and dry in the shade to collect seed. Blanch and dry what remains |
| November | First frost kills the aboveground part. Leave the spot unturned |
| December | Use dried and pickled purslane. Check the seed |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;What deserves attention in this table is the November row. Not turning over that spot is the whole of next year's farming. Seed that fell in autumn must be near the surface to receive light and germinate in the following spring. If the soil is plowed deeply, the seed is buried and will not come up that year.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;And the two rows for July and August are the practical reason to plant this crop. High summer is when lettuce, spinach and napa cabbage all collapse, and it is the stretch in which fresh leaves stop coming from a disaster-time field. That is exactly when purslane is at its peak. Even if you give it nothing, even if you do not water it, it is still there.
