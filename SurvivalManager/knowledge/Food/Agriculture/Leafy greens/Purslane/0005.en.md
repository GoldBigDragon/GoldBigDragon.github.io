## 8. Crop Management - Managed Neglect

### 8-1. Boundary Management, Not Cultivation

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The nature of purslane management is fundamentally different from that of other crops. With other crops, management means helping them grow. With purslane, management means deciding how far to let it grow. It is not a plant that needs help but a plant for which a line must be drawn.

| Management item | Other leafy vegetables | Purslane |
|---|---|---|
| Water | Regular irrigation | None, except during germination |
| Fertilizer | Base fertilizer and top-dressing | None, or a very small amount after cutting |
| Thinning | Needed | Needed. The only task that takes any effort |
| Weeds | Pulled | Purslane suppresses other grasses |
| Stakes | Needed in some cases | None |
| Pest control | Needed | Practically none |
| Boundaries | Not an issue | The most important task |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The last row is the key. From the moment purslane is brought into the field, this plant comes up by itself every year and does not stay within the area people have set for it. Seeds move with rainwater and travel on shoes. After a few years it spreads across the whole field and may smother young carrots or newly transplanted seedlings. There is good reason it has been treated as a weed.

> **With purslane, the trouble is not the moment of planting but what happens once it starts to spread. Limit the area where it is allowed in from the start, and pull and remove it outside that area before it flowers: that is all there is to managing this crop.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The condition "before it flowers" matters. Pulled purslane thrown on the field survives for days even without roots, and in the meantime ripens the fruit already set and drops its seed. Pulling and tossing it actually spreads the seed. If it was pulled after flowering, it is better to carry it out of the field, dry it and burn it, or simply eat it up.

### 8-2. Thinning

| Round | Timing | Spacing to leave | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1st | 2-3 true leaves | 5cm | The thinnings can be eaten |
| 2nd | 4-5 true leaves | 10-15cm | Final spacing |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The seed is so small that broadcast sowing always produces dense stands. Left as it is, the stems tangle, grow upward, and rot at the bottom. Unlike other crops, however, there is no reason to hurry the thinning. The thinned young purslane is itself at its softest and tastiest. Young purslane with two or three true leaves is best for eating as salad, and this stage is in practice the first harvest.

### 8-3. Pinching and Regrowth

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;When the stem tips are cut off, the dormant buds at each node wake up and several side shoots come up. This is the principle by which purslane can be harvested many times, and it is the basis for the Perenniality grade of A in the evaluation table.

| Item | Standard |
|---|---|
| First cutting | 6-8 weeks after sowing, stems 15-20cm |
| Where to cut | 2-3 nodes above the base |
| Regrowth period | 10-20 days |
| Number of repeats | 3-4 times before frost |
| After cutting | One watering; fertilizer a very small amount or none |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The reason 2-3 nodes must be left at the base is that new shoots come only from nodes. If the plant is cut close to the root, no buds remain, so regrowth fails or becomes very slow. Conversely, if only the top is cut lightly, the buds near the base do not wake up and the plant just grows longer upward. This is why it is cut at a middle height.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;With each regrowth, stems get thinner and leaves get smaller. The third and fourth cuttings are about half of the first harvest. This is a natural result of continually removing nutrients from the field while returning nothing, and a little fertilizer can ease it. But as noted earlier, fertilizer raises nitrate concentration, so at this point it is also a reasonable call to leave that plant alone and switch to letting it flower and setting seed.

### 8-4. It Does Not Overwinter

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;As the Cold hardiness grade of E indicates, purslane cannot survive the winter. At the first frost the aboveground part turns mushy and dies, and the roots do not survive either. Standing it in the field through winter, as with garlic chive or Welsh onion, is impossible.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Instead, the seed overwinters. Seeds that fall in autumn actually have their dormancy broken by repeated freezing and thawing in the soil, and they germinate the following year once soil temperature passes 15°C. There is only one thing for people to do: do not turn over that spot in autumn. Deep tilling buries the seed too deep to germinate, and then it does not come up the following year. Of course, that seed does not die; it comes up again a few years later when the ground is turned again. This is the vivid form of the saying that purslane seed lives for decades.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;If purslane is wanted in winter too, storage is the only way. Growing it in a heated facility is not viable in a disaster setting. What is dried or pickled in autumn is the winter share, and Section 15 covers those methods.

## 9. Pests and Diseases - What "Almost None" Means

### 9-1. Why the Grade and the Field Disagree

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;To read Pest & disease pressure C and Pest-control self-reliance C in the evaluation table as meaning that purslane is a crop plagued by pests and diseases is a misreading. These two grades are not the result of observing purslane directly. The Reliability L6 label tells the story. Because there is almost no research on pests and diseases of purslane, the figure was borrowed from the 50.6% average yield loss rate of the dicotyledon class as a whole.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In reality, almost no pests or diseases that cause economic damage to purslane have been reported. To begin with, this plant has been on the side of the weeds that are targets of control, not among the crops that are protected, and while herbicide resistance has been studied, disease has rarely been. The note "no control needed" in the Basis column of the Pest-control self-reliance row is much closer to what happens in the field.

> **Purslane's pest and disease grade is C not because the crop is weak but because no one has ever observed it closely.**

### 9-2. Problems That Still Appear

| Problem | Symptoms | Conditions | Response without chemicals |
|---|---|---|---|
| Root rot | The base turns brown and mushy and the plant collapses | Waterlogged spots, the monsoon season (jangma, late June to July in South Korea) | Build up raised beds and cut drainage ditches. Remove plants that have already rotted |
| Slugs | Irregular holes in the leaves, slime trails | Damp, shaded spots, under mulch | Sprinkle ash or diatomaceous earth around the plants to block their path. Pick them by hand at night |
| Aphids | Cluster on the tips of new shoots | Excess nitrogen, dryness | Wash off with water or spray soapy water |
| Gray mold | Gray fuzz appears inside dense plants | Crowded, poorly ventilated stands | Thin to widen spacing and remove dry lower leaves |
| Stink bugs | Needle marks on stems, growth stops | When weeds around are thick | Clear the old grass around the field edge |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Five are listed, but only the first row actually ruins a harvest. The rest amount to losing a few leaves and usually pass on their own. It is not far off the mark to sum it up this way: in growing purslane, failure is not catching a disease but being waterlogged.

### 9-3. Why Control Without Chemicals Is Easy

| Method | Where it stands for purslane |
|---|---|
| Drainage management | The only control that must be done |
| Thinning for ventilation | Solves most mold problems |
| Hand picking | Plants are low and leaves large, so insects are easy to spot |
| Soapy water | Used for aphids and mites. Leaves are thick, so little chemical injury |
| Wood ash | Ring it around the base to keep slugs out |
| Wood vinegar | Dilute 300-500 times and spray. For prevention |
| Bordeaux mixture | Almost no need to make it |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Take note of the last row. Bordeaux mixture is a traditional fungicide made from copper sulfate and quicklime, and in a disaster field it is the most valuable means of control. But the ingredients are hard to obtain and making it takes labor. There is no occasion to spend that precious resource on purslane. It is right to save it for diseases that wipe out an entire year's crop, such as potato late blight or napa cabbage soft rot.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The thick leaves and well-developed wax layer also make control easy. Even when an irritating liquid such as soapy water or wood vinegar is sprayed, the leaves do not scorch the way tender leafy vegetables do. It tolerates a slightly wrong concentration in most cases.

### 9-4. When Having Purslane in the Field Is Itself Control

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There is also a direction in which purslane itself takes part in the control of other crops. Two are known.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The first is the trap-crop effect. Purslane is a plant preferred by some pests, including aphids and thrips, so with purslane nearby the pests gather there. It takes the damage the main crop would have received. However, the opposite outcome is also possible, in which pests multiply on the purslane and eventually move to the main crop, so to use it as a trap crop the purslane that has attracted pests must be removed periodically.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The second is the ground-cover effect. Covering the ground to prevent soil splash reduces the route by which soil-borne pathogens are splashed by raindrops onto the leaves. Late blight and anthracnose of tomato and chili pepper often spread by this route, so a covered bed surface alone reduces outbreaks. A living plant takes over the job of plastic mulch, and where there is no plastic, this substitution has real value.

## 10. Nutrient Deficiency and Excess

| Symptom | Cause | Remedy with natural materials |
|---|---|---|
| Leaves pale green overall, plant small | Nitrogen shortage or extremely poor soil | Apply diluted urine (diluted 15 times) to the soil once. It can be harvested even if left alone |
| Leaves unusually large, soft, and prone to lodging | Excess nitrogen | Stop fertilizing. Nitrate has built up |
| Leaves turn red or deep purple | Cold, phosphorus shortage, or strong light and dryness | Mostly a normal reaction. Common in the cold spells of spring |
| Edges of older leaves dry and scorch | Potassium shortage or excess salts | Scatter a small amount of wood ash. If salts are suspected, water thoroughly to flush them out |
| New shoots bunch up and are deformed | Calcium or boron shortage | Burn and pound shells and eggshells and mix them in |
| Stems thin and long, nodes spread apart | Lack of light | Not a nutrient problem. Move the plants out of the shade |
| Yellow spots spread on leaves | Magnesium shortage | Rare in purslane. Suspect overwatering first |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;One premise must be kept in mind when reading this table. Purslane is a plant that rarely shows deficiency symptoms. Its body is built for poor ground, and most shortages merely slow its growth a little. A condition that would be diagnosed as a deficiency in another crop is often just the normal appearance in purslane.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;So the problem met more often is not shortage but excess. The second row, with leaves large, soft, and lodging, is that. It looks as if the plant is growing well, but the tissue is fragile and rots easily, storage becomes harder, and nitrate accumulates. This is a case where the hand that gave fertilizer instead lowered the quality.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The reddening in the third row is also an item easily misdiagnosed as a deficiency. Purslane stems are red by nature, and reddening that extends to the leaves is a normal pigment reaction to strong light, dryness, and cold. If anything, purslane in this state has higher concentrations of its compounds and a richer taste. There is no need to judge it as a phosphorus shortage and add fertilizer.

## 11. Intercropping and Crop Rotation

### 11-1. Compatibility

| Compatibility | Crop | Reason |
|:---:|---|---|
| Good | Tomato, chili pepper, eggplant | Covers the bed to stop soil splash and evaporation. Different heights, so they do not compete for light |
| Good | Maize | Both are C4 and grow well together in summer heat. But maize shade must be avoided |
| Good | Squash, chamoe (Korean melon) | Both creep along the ground, but at different layers, so they can coexist |
| Fair | Legumes | Little competition, but little benefit for each other either |
| Poor | Carrot, spinach, radish | Grow slowly when young and get smothered by purslane |
| Poor | Any freshly transplanted seedling | If covered before taking root, they simply wilt |
| Poor | Crops watered frequently | The purslane rots from excess moisture |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;What divides good from poor in this table is height and timing. Purslane clings to the ground and spreads sideways, so under crops that have already grown tall it does no harm and actually covers the soil. Beside seedlings just planted, or crops that grow slowly when young, it smothers them within days. This is why the same plant can be a help in the same field and a catastrophe.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;It is also worth knowing that it sits at the opposite end from water spinach. Water spinach takes the waterlogged spots, and purslane takes the well-drained spots. If a field divides into a wet side and a dry side, simply placing the two crops apart leaves almost no unusable ground.

### 11-2. Crop Rotation

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The Continuous-cropping tolerance in the evaluation table is SS. Coming up in the same spot every year is the normal state, and no damage from continuous cropping has been reported. No rotation plan is needed for purslane.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There is, however, reason to think separately about what role purslane can play within the rotation plans of other crops.

| Placement | Details | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Gap after the preceding crop | Summer fallow after digging potatoes or garlic | Covers bare ground and prevents topsoil loss and drying |
| Bed shoulders and furrows | Spots the main crop does not use | Gets food from idle area |
| Field edges | Border strip | Serves as both trap crop and foraging source |
| Newly cleared poor ground | First-year crop | The only harvest on land where other crops cannot establish |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The last row is especially valuable in a disaster. On gravelly ground without organic matter, ground left abandoned and hardened for a long time, or ground covered with construction fill, no vegetable grows properly in the first year. Purslane comes up even there. If purslane is grown on such ground for several years and cut down and laid flat, organic matter builds up in the topsoil, and meanwhile the ground changes into land where other crops can be introduced. It plays the role of producing food while building the soil at the same time, in effect doubling as a green manure crop.
