## Introduction

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Iceberg lettuce (Lactuca sativa var. capitata) is the same species as lettuce. The first two words of its scientific name are identical to lettuce's; only the capitata that follows differs. The word comes from the Latin caput, meaning head, and refers to the trait of leaves curling inward to form a round, head-like mass. Botanically, iceberg lettuce is merely a variety of lettuce, and the two cross-pollinate and their seeds mix freely. Yet by the yardstick of disaster preparedness, this single difference sets the fates of the two crops apart.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Iceberg lettuce's overall grade is E, 35.7 points, lower than lettuce's D, 37.7 points. Placing the criteria side by side makes clear where the difference comes from. Perenniality fell from lettuce's A to D, temperature range narrowed from C to D, water use dropped from A to B, speed to harvest fell from S to A, micronutrients fell from S to A, and prevalence in South Korea fell from S to A. All six criteria moved in the same direction, and the one trait behind that direction is heading.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This drop makes sense once you consider what heading actually involves. For the leaves to curl inward and form a ball, the outer leaves must first grow large enough; then the new leaves that follow must be pressed inward by the outer ones and curve in, and the head must fill out in that state. The whole process takes time, so the growing period lengthens. Harvesting that head means taking the entire growing point at the center, so repeat harvesting is impossible. Swelling a large head takes more water. And the temperature range in which heading occurs is narrower than the range in which leaves simply grow. One trait, heading, produces four disadvantages at once.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;When leaf lettuce bolts from heat, whatever leaves you have already picked remain yours. Iceberg lettuce, if it fails to head, leaves almost nothing usable in a field you tended for four months. The leaves grow apart without ever heading, then the plant bolts, and by then those leaves are already tough and bitter. In a disaster, the complete failure of one growing cycle is not a simple loss - it collapses that season's entire food plan. All the labor, fertilizer, and water invested in it disappear.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;That is not to say iceberg lettuce has no advantage at all. There is one thing it gains when heading succeeds: storability. Iceberg lettuce's storability grade is D, one week unpowered at room temperature. That is a low grade, but it is still two to three times lettuce's E, 2-3 days. The reason lies in its structure. In a headed lettuce, several layers of outer leaves wrap the inner leaves, so the inner leaves are never exposed to air. The outside dries out and spoils while the inside stays intact - a structural advantage that leaf lettuce, whose every leaf is fully exposed, can never match. Yield per area is also higher: 4,000kg per 1,000m², against lettuce's 3,000kg.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In short, iceberg lettuce is not a substitute for lettuce but a crop that does one thing lettuce cannot. If you plant it in fall, harvest a few heads in early winter, and put them in a root-cellar pit, you will still have something fresh for a few weeks after frost has finished off every other leafy vegetable. Giving up a small corner of the field for those few weeks is how this crop is meant to be used. It is not a crop to devote the whole field to, and even less a crop to rush into the ground early in a disaster.
