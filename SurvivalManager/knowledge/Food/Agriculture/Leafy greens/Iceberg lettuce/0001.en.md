## Introduction
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Iceberg lettuce (Lactuca sativa var. capitata) is the same species as lettuce, and heading, in which the leaves roll inward into a round, head-like mass, is what splits the fates of the two crops. Its overall grade is E (35.7 points, rank 161 of 183 valid species), lower than lettuce's D (37.7 points). Six items, including perenniality, temperature range, water consumption, and harvest speed, all moved in the same direction, and the cause is the single trait of heading. Heading needs the outer leaves to grow first, which lengthens the growing period; the whole growing point must be taken, so regrowth harvesting is impossible; inflating a large mass takes more water; and the temperature range for heading is narrower than for leaf growth. If heading fails, almost nothing usable is left from a bed raised for four months, and a season's food plan collapses. It has one advantage, storability (D, 1 week at room temperature; lettuce is E, 2-3 days), because several layers of outer leaves wrap the inner leaves so they are not exposed to air. The way to use this crop is to plant it in autumn, harvest a few heads in early winter, and put them in a storage pit so that fresh vegetables remain for a few weeks after frost has finished all the other leafy greens. It is not a crop to entrust a whole field to, and even less one to plant in a hurry early in a disaster.

## 2. Plant Overview
| Item | Details |
|:---|:---|
| Classification | Family Asteraceae, genus Lactuca. An annual (a biennial depending on conditions). Other names: head lettuce, crisphead, iceberg |
| Leaves | Round and broad, with deeply cut edges forming waves. The outer leaves are dark green, thick, and tough, a skin that protects the inside; the inner leaves get no light, so they are pale yellow to white, crisp, watery, and mild in taste |
| Stem, roots, flowers | The stem is very short and compressed, at the center of the head. The taproot is shallow, 20-30 cm. Yellow flower heads form a panicle, and the fruit is an achene 3-4 mm long (with a pappus at the tip) |

| Stage | After transplanting | Key point |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Establishment | 1-2 weeks | Roots settle in the new soil. 15-20℃ |
| Outer leaf formation | 2-6 weeks | 10-15 outer leaves unfold. 15-20℃ |
| Start of heading, filling | 6-7 weeks, 7-10 weeks | New leaves begin to curl inward (15-18℃ is best), then the head fills and firms (10-18℃) |
| Harvest period | After 10 weeks | Ready when it feels firm to a press |

> **Heading begins only after the outer leaves have grown enough. If growth is poor in the outer-leaf stage, that plant cannot even enter heading. A large share of heading failures is already decided not in the heading period but in the stage before it.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Per 100 g: 14 kcal, 94-96% water, 0.90 g of protein (a high ratio to calories), 2.3 g of carbohydrate, 1.2 g of dietary fiber, 1.97 g of sugars, 7 mg of vitamin C, 96 ㎍ of beta-carotene (concentrated in the outer leaves, as is vitamin K), 66.7 ㎍ of folate, 167 mg of potassium, and 32 mg of calcium.

## 3. Growing Environment and Varieties
| Condition | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Temperature | Best for germination 18-21℃ (6-8 days); at 26℃ or above it does not germinate because of heat dormancy. Best for growth 15-20℃, best for heading 15-18℃, heading possible at 10-20℃, and at 25℃ or above the leaves open out and it bolts. At -5℃ the outer leaves are damaged and, if severe, the head breaks |
| Light | 8-10 hours a day. It tolerates partial shade, but heading becomes loose. 86% relative yield under 40% shading. It is a light-germinating seed, so seed buried deep does not germinate |
| Water, soil | Total evapotranspiration about 500 mm (lettuce 350 mm). Keep it from drying out during heading (drought at this stage is the main cause of failed heading). Excess water invites root rot and soft rot, so raise the beds. Well-drained sandy loam to loam, pH 6.0-6.8, the more organic matter the better |

> **There is one key question in growing iceberg lettuce: is the air temperature 15-18℃ when heading starts? Working the sowing date backward to answer that question is hardly an exaggeration to call the whole technique of this crop.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Autumn growing is favorable, and a spring crop easily fails because the temperature rises during heading. Varieties divide into crisphead (deeply cut leaf edges and a firm head, with the best storability, which suits the purpose of planting this crop) and butterhead (semi-heading, with soft leaves and a loose head, so the risk of failure is low but the storage advantage is also small). There are imported varieties resistant to aphids and downy mildew, but as a disaster lengthens their seed is not replenished. For storage, choose an early crisphead (which finishes heading before frost); if you worry about failed heading, choose butterhead or leaf lettuce (the leaves can be eaten even if it fails); if you plan to carry the seed on, choose a fixed variety.

## 4. Field Preparation, Sowing, and Transplanting
| Item | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Preparing the soil | Dig 25-30 cm deep; bed height 20 cm or more (prevents excess water and soft rot), width 100-120 cm (two rows), a drainage ditch is a must (if water pools during heading, the whole plant rots), and level the surface |
| Fertilization (1,000㎡) | Nitrogen 12-15 kg, phosphorus 8-10 kg (all basal; it takes direct part in heading), potassium 8-10 kg, lime 150 kg. Basal fertilizer 1-2 weeks before transplanting: all the compost, all the phosphorus, and half of the nitrogen and potassium. First top dressing about 3 weeks after transplanting, nitrogen and potassium (builds the outer leaves); second around the start of heading, potassium and phosphorus (fills and firms the inside) |
| Substitute resources | Wood ash, a handful per plant (alkaline, so too much raises pH), well-rotted compost (half-rotted compost damages roots), urine diluted 10-20 times (between plants; not after heading starts), bone meal, eggshell and shell powder (calcium), fermented oil cake (raw it burns roots with fermentation heat) |

> **Too much nitrogen during heading makes the leaves keep trying to grow larger and not roll inward. The most common cause of plants that look lush but are hollow inside is excess nitrogen at heading.**

| Type | Sowing | Transplanting | Harvest |
|:---|:---|:---|:---|
| Autumn crop | August-September | September-October | November-December. In practice the only choice in a disaster |
| Spring crop | February-March | March-April | June-July. The temperature rises during heading and the risk of failure is high |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;A summer crop is not recommended because both germination and heading are blocked. The difficulty of autumn sowing is August heat (heat dormancy at 26℃ or above), and it is handled by cold priming (soak the seed for 4-6 hours, wrap it in wet cloth, and leave it in a cool place for a day), sowing pre-sprouted seed, sowing after sunset, making light shade, and raising seedlings in a cool spot.

| Planting | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Seedlings | Depth 0.5-1 cm (it germinates in light, so buried deep it does not come up), germination in 6-8 days at 18-21℃, raise 20-30 days and transplant at 4-5 true leaves. Cutting back water and putting seedlings out in the open air a few days before transplanting to firm them speeds establishment |
| Transplanting | Plant spacing 30-35 cm, row spacing 50-60 cm (3,000-3,500 plants per 1,000㎡). Plant on a cloudy day or at dusk, without burying the base of the true leaves (buried, the base rots), and water thoroughly at once. Narrowing the spacing is usually a loss |

> **If transplanting is late, heading fails. Seedlings past 6 true leaves already have roots tangled in a cramped space, and after moving they spend time recovering and cannot grow the outer leaves in time.**

## 5. Water Management and Cultivation Management
| Period | Water management |
|:---|:---|
| Right after transplanting to establishment | Soak thoroughly (the roots must attach to the new soil) |
| After establishment to outer-leaf formation | When the topsoil dries (draws the roots downward) |
| Heading | Keep it from drying (the water management of iceberg lettuce hinges on the heading period alone) |
| 1 week before harvest | Reduce or stop (the tissue firms and storability rises) |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Wilting by day and recovering by evening is a temporary shortage; if it does not recover by evening, water thoroughly at once. A loose, light head means water shortage during heading, which cannot be undone; tipburn, brown dry tips of the inner leaves, is a calcium transport disorder caused by uneven moisture, so water evenly; a split head comes from watering heavily and suddenly after drying out; and if the outer leaves turn yellow and the base softens, it is excess water, so open the ditch and stop watering. To save water, use straw and dry grass mulch (the cheapest water-saving measure), furrow watering in the early morning, and windbreaks.

| Conditions for failed heading | What happens |
|:---|:---|
| Heat | The leaves grow open and then a flower stalk rises (the difference in growth rate between the lower and upper sides of the leaf disappears) |
| Excess nitrogen | Large, lush leaves but hollow inside (vegetative growth does not stop) |
| Late transplanting | The outer leaves are poor and heading cannot start |
| Lack of phosphorus, lack of water during heading, lack of light, crowding | Head loose and light / the inside does not fill / head loose / plants shade each other and compete for light and nutrients |

> **Failed heading is not an accident of the heading period but the moment the results of the preceding two months show. Once the leaves begin to open, almost nothing can be done. It is already decided at the point of fixing the sowing date, the point of moving the seedlings, and the point of cutting off nitrogen.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Besides these, fill gaps 1 week after transplanting, weed while the weeds are small (the roots are shallow and deep hoeing damages them), hill once in the middle of growth, and remove the yellowed, dried lower leaves.

## 6. Pests, Diseases, and Nutrient Problems
| Disease | Symptoms and conditions | Response without chemicals |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Downy mildew | Yellow-brown spots on the upper face of leaves and white mold underneath. Cool and damp (the most common in iceberg lettuce) | Ventilation, morning watering, resistant varieties, remove diseased leaves |
| Soft rot | Base and inside burst soft and stink. Hot and humid, poor drainage, wounds | Raise beds, drain, avoid wounds |
| Sclerotinia rot | Base softens, white mycelium and black grains. Cold and damp, continuous Asteraceae | Deep plowing, mulch, 2-3 year rotation, complete removal of diseased plants |
| Gray mold, powdery mildew, crown rot | Gray mold / white powder on the leaf surface / browning at the crown and collapse | Humidity control, securing spacing / ventilation, sun, milk dilution / shallow planting, drainage |

> **Soft rot is a matter not of cure but of managing conditions. Do not let water pool, do not bury the base in soil, do not wound the plant when harvesting, and do not harvest right after rain. These four are all that can be done without chemicals and all that actually works.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;A plant with sclerotinia rot is dug up together with the soil around its roots and carried out of the field. Do not put it on the compost heap (sclerotia survive the fermentation heat of compost and spread over the whole field). The pests are aphids (soapy water, protecting ladybugs and aphid parasitoid wasps, resistant varieties), thrips (yellow sticky traps, removing weeds nearby), moth larvae (they bore into the head: hand-picking in the early morning, insect net), slugs (catching at night, a ring of ash or rice hulls), and root mites (rotation, better drainage). The control agents are soapy water (20 g of unscented soap in 1 L of water), milk dilution (milk and water 1 to 9, for powdery mildew), wood-ash infusion (wood ash soaked in water for 24 hours and strained), and wood vinegar diluted 300-500 times. For cultural control, rest Asteraceae for 2-3 years, do not narrow the spacing, use mulch to stop rain splash, reduce nitrogen, and avoid growing in hot weather.

| Symptom | Cause and action |
|:---|:---|
| All leaves yellow and plant small | Nitrogen deficiency. Well-rotted compost, urine diluted 15-20 times (only before heading) |
| Leaf edges dry brown | Potassium deficiency. A little wood ash |
| Poor growth, purplish leaves | Phosphorus deficiency (the same symptom appears when the soil is cold). Bone meal, wood ash |
| Head loose and light | Shortage of phosphorus and potassium (decided at the basal fertilizer stage) |
| Leaves large and deep green but hollow inside | Excess nitrogen (often more dangerous than deficiency in iceberg lettuce). Cut off the nitrogen supply |

## 7. Intercropping and Crop Rotation
| Compatibility | Companions |
|:---|:---|
| Good | Legumes (peas, kidney beans, cowpeas: fix nitrogen), scallion, Welsh onion, garlic (the smell confuses pests), radish, napa cabbage and other brassicas (do not share pests and diseases and root at different depths), dill and coriander (call the natural enemies of aphids) |
| Poor | Lettuce, garland chrysanthemum, chicory, burdock, and other Asteraceae (share sclerotinia rot and downy mildew), iceberg lettuce in succession (sclerotia build up) |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;It is better not to plant iceberg lettuce and lettuce side by side in the same field, and since the planting spacing is wide and there is much empty ground early on, the gaps can be filled with scallion or dill. On a 4-year cycle of iceberg lettuce and other Asteraceae, legumes (fix nitrogen), grasses such as maize and sorghum (starve Asteraceae pathogens), and brassicas such as radish and napa cabbage, you start iceberg lettuce again with the sclerotia density lowered.

## 8. Harvest and Seed Saving
| Harvest | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Timing | After transplanting: autumn 50-60 days, spring about 60 days, winter 90 days. It is ready when the head holds firm when pressed from above. Harvest early in the morning (when it holds water and is heaviest) and avoid right after rain or when leaves are wet (water left between leaves becomes the starting point of soft rot in storage). Harvest a day or two after the rain stops, once the leaves are dry |
| Method | Cut the base in one stroke with a knife (cutting several times roughens the cut face and spoils it), leave 2-3 outer leaves (a skin that protects the inside in storage), dry the cut face in shade, and keep it unwashed. White latex comes out of the cut face, as with lettuce |
| Only once | Perenniality D means it is over once harvested, so spreading out the timing comes before storage as a problem. Side buds sometimes sprout from the cut base and grow a few small leaves |

| Seed saving | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Difficulty | The head is firm, so the flower stalk cannot break through it and bends and rots inside. Cut a shallow cross on top of the head to make a path, avoid rain so water does not pool inside, and secure drainage. Choose seed plants from those that headed properly and whose flower stalk came out safely (seed from a plant that failed to head carries that trait on) |
| Timetable | If seed plants are left at the harvest stage (50-60 days after transplanting), they bolt 3-5 weeks later, flower 3-4 weeks after that, and the seed matures 12-24 days later. 150-180 days after sowing, about a month longer than lettuce |
| Collecting seed | Cover the flower stalk with a breathable paper bag or cloth bag, strip only the puffed flowers every 2-3 days (the highest recovery), or cut the whole stalk when more than half have puffed and let it after-ripen (some immature seed is mixed in) |
| Longevity | 2-3 years sealed at room temperature (longer if cool and dry). Keep in a sealed container in a cool, dark, dry place. Lay 20 seeds on wet cloth every year and count the germination. Freshly harvested seed has stronger heat dormancy |

## 9. Storage
| Conditions | Storage period |
|:---|:---|
| As it is at room temperature | 5-7 days |
| Cool shade (around 15℃) | 1-2 weeks |
| Ground cellar or pit (around 10℃) | 2-3 weeks |
| In a pit with wet sand or straw | 3-4 weeks |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Leave 2-3 outer leaves attached, dry the cut face in shade for 1-2 hours, do not wash it, and either wrap it loosely in breathable wet cloth, or in a pit set it between wet sand or straw root side down with gaps so heads do not touch, or tie a string to the base and hang it upside down in a cool place; in a crock, lay wet cloth and cover loosely (fully sealed, it goes soft).

> **Sort what will be stored from what will be eaten first at the time of harvest. Split heads, ones with pressure marks, ones with signs of insects, and ones harvested right after rain are not stored but eaten first. One spoiled head drags down the sound ones beside it.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Iceberg lettuce is poorly suited to processed storage. Outer leaves blanched and dried in shade last a few months (for using up the outer leaves), water kimchi in 2-3% brine lasts 1-2 weeks (a little crispness remains), and quick-salted salad lasts 2-3 days (more cooking than storage).

## 10. Cooking and Precautions
| Part | Character | Use |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Inner and middle leaves | Crisp and mild, with dilute nutrition | Raw, wraps, salad, quick-salted salad (it wilts, shrinks in volume, and you eat more) |
| Outer leaves | Dark green, tough, slightly bitter. The best nutrition | Blanching, soups, stir-fries, drying for keeping |
| Core | Hard and sweet | Sliced thin and eaten raw, or stir-fried |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Water kimchi keeps a good part of its vitamin C through fermentation. Cooking removes the crispness and makes it soft, so do not boil it long.

| Component | Details |
|:---|:---|
| Toxicity, lactucarium | Iceberg lettuce has no toxicity. Cut, it gives off white latex (lactucarium) as lettuce does, but the inner leaves get no light and make fewer defensive compounds, so there is less than in lettuce. Oxalic acid is also low |
| Nitrate | It can accumulate more in the inner leaves than in lettuce (without light it is not converted into protein). It rises with excess nitrogen fertilizer, cloudy days, and early-morning harvest, and is lower with a clear-day afternoon harvest. Eating normally grown iceberg lettuce in ordinary amounts is no problem, and cutting off nitrogen during heading is right for both heading and lowering nitrate |

## 11. Annual Work Schedule
| Month | Spring crop | Autumn crop |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Jan-Feb | Check seed, plan the field, manage compost. Sow and raise seedlings in February (warmth needed) | |
| Mar-Apr | Seedlings continue, transplant and basal fertilizer in late March. Establishment and outer-leaf formation in April, first top dressing | |
| May-June | Heading period (water management is the most important). Harvest in June, leave the seed plants | |
| July-Aug | Seed plants bolt and flower, seed harvest and cleaning in August | Sow in late August (deal with heat dormancy) |
| Sep-Oct | | Seedlings and transplanting in September (finish by early month). Outer-leaf formation and start of heading in October, top dressing |
| Nov-Dec | | Heads complete and are harvested in November, storage starts. Check stored goods in December, tidy the field and handle crop residue |

> **Iceberg lettuce is not a pillar of the field but a bridge into winter. A few heads left in the pit after frost has finished all the leafy greens, those few weeks, are all this crop does. Whether it is worth three months and 500 mm of water for that is the criterion for whether to plant it at all.**
