## 2. What Kind of Plant Is This?

### 2-1. Classification and History

| Item | Details |
|:---|:---|
| Scientific name | Lactuca sativa var. capitata |
| Family | Asteraceae (daisy family) |
| Genus | Lettuce genus (Lactuca) |
| Same species | Leaf lettuce, romaine, and stem lettuce are all Lactuca sativa |
| Life cycle | Annual (biennial depending on conditions) |
| Other names | Heading lettuce, crisphead, iceberg |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Iceberg lettuce is a lineage that people selected and gathered from among the lettuces that spread from the Mediterranean coast into Europe, choosing individuals whose leaves curled inward. Lettuce that headed appears in 16th-century European writings, and today's firm crisphead type was completed in the United States between the late 19th and early 20th centuries. The name iceberg, literally an ice mountain, comes not from any trait of the lettuce itself but from how it was shipped. In the early 20th century, when it was sent from the American West to the East by rail, it traveled in rail cars packed with ice, and the nickname came from that sight.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This origin tells you exactly what kind of vegetable iceberg lettuce is. It was refined not as a vegetable to eat straight from the field but as one bred to travel far. Packed tightly, it does not bruise under pressure; the outer leaves protect the inside; and it stays crisp even after sitting for a long time. These are traits selected on the premise of mass distribution, so nutrient density or depth of flavor never entered the selection criteria. When evaluating this crop in a situation where logistics have broken down, this fact needs to be kept in mind. Iceberg lettuce was created for distribution, and once that distribution disappears, it loses half of the purpose it was made for. Only the other half remains: the structure that lets it hold up in storage.

### 2-2. What Is Heading?

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Heading is the phenomenon in which leaves curve inward as they grow and stack layer upon layer. It does not happen on its own; it is a physiological shift that begins only when conditions are right.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The sequence goes like this. First, ten to fifteen outer leaves unfold and grow. These leaves act as the photosynthesis factory. Then the new leaves that follow begin growing inside the enclosure the outer leaves have formed, and at this point the growth rates of the leaf's lower and upper surfaces start to differ. When the lower surface grows faster, the leaf curls inward. As these curved leaves overlap, a ball shape forms, and as new leaves keep pushing in from inside, the head firms up.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;What matters here is that the difference in growth rate between the lower and upper surfaces depends heavily on temperature. When temperatures are high, this difference disappears or even reverses, and the leaves spread outward instead. This is why the optimal temperature for heading is lower and narrower than the optimal temperature for leaf growth.

| Stage | After Transplanting | What Happens | Temperature Condition |
|:---|:---:|:---|:---|
| Establishment | 1-2 weeks | Roots settle into the new soil | 15-20°C |
| Outer leaf formation | 2-6 weeks | 10-15 outer leaves unfold | 15-20°C |
| Heading onset | 6-7 weeks | New leaves begin curving inward | 15-18°C is best |
| Head filling | 7-10 weeks | The head fills out and firms up | 10-18°C |
| Harvest stage | After 10 weeks | The right time is when it feels firm to the touch | |

> **Heading begins only after the outer leaves have grown enough. If growth during the outer-leaf stage is poor, that plant never enters heading at all. Much of heading failure is decided not during the heading stage itself but in the stage before it.**

### 2-3. Appearance

| Organ | Characteristics |
|:---|:---|
| Leaf | Round and broad, with edges deeply notched into a wavy shape. Curls inward to form the head |
| Outer leaves | Deep green, thick and tough. Act as a shell protecting the inside |
| Inner leaves | Pale yellow to white from lack of light. Crisp, high in moisture, and mild in flavor |
| Stem | Very short and compressed, at the center of the head |
| Root | Taproot. Relatively shallow at 20-30cm |
| Flower | Yellow flower heads form a panicle |
| Fruit | Achene. 3-4mm long, 0.8-1mm wide, with a pappus at the tip |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The color difference between the outer and inner leaves shows this crop's nutrient structure directly. The outer leaves are deep green, and correspondingly rich in chlorophyll, beta-carotene, and vitamin K. The inner leaves are white, and correspondingly poor in them. Yet what people mainly eat is the crisp inside. The outer leaves are often discarded for being tough. In a disaster, this is a habit that needs to be reversed. The outer leaves hold most of the nutrition, and if they are tough, blanching them or putting them in soup solves that.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;At 14kcal, calories are among the lowest of the crops this document covers. Filling an adult's daily 2,100kcal from iceberg lettuce alone would require eating 15kg, an amount no person can swallow. Expecting calories from iceberg lettuce simply is not a calculation that works to begin with.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Still, the SS for protein density is worth noting. 0.90g of protein per 100g is small in absolute terms, but dividing it by the 14kcal of energy gives a protein share of calories of 25.7%, two and a half times the requirement line of 10%. That figure comes purely from how low the calories are, so it does not mean iceberg lettuce is a protein source; the accurate reading is that no matter how much iceberg lettuce you eat, the protein ratio never gets worse.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Moisture at 94% to 96% has practical significance. When water is scarce, a vegetable high in moisture becomes a source of water in itself. That water, however, is simply water carried from the field in a different form, so growing iceberg lettuce is not a way to save water when drinking water is in short supply. A total evapotranspiration of 500mm is by no means a small amount.

## 3. Growing Conditions

### 3-1. Temperature

| Category | Temperature | Result |
|:---|:---:|:---|
| Optimal germination temperature | 18-21°C | Sprouts in 6-8 days |
| Germination limit | 26°C or above | Does not germinate due to heat-induced dormancy |
| Optimal growth temperature | 15-20°C | Leaves grow well |
| Optimal heading temperature | 15-18°C | Heads form best |
| Heading possible | 10-20°C | Outside this range, heading is at risk |
| Heading failure | 25°C or above | Leaves spread open and the plant bolts |
| Cold tolerance limit | -5°C | Outer leaves are damaged, and in severe cases the head breaks apart |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;What this table shows is that the three temperature ranges differ from one another. Germination favors 18-21°C, leaf growth favors 15-20°C, and heading is best at 15-18°C. Within a single growing cycle, the plant must pass through these three ranges in order, but natural air temperature moves in only one direction: it rises in spring and falls in autumn.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This is where the advantage of fall cultivation comes from. Sow in late August to early September, and the germination period is still warm, around 18-21°C; leaf growth in September-October falls in the 15-20°C range; and heading in October-November drops to 10-18°C. The direction in which the temperature falls matches the direction in which the crop's required temperature falls.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Spring cultivation is the reverse. In the February-March sowing period, germination temperatures fall short and warming is needed; in the May-June heading period, temperatures exceed 20°C and fall outside heading conditions. This is why spring cultivation has a higher failure rate and lower yield than fall cultivation. Trials in South Korea sometimes show a spring yield higher than the fall yield for the same variety, but that figure comes under conditions backed by warming structures and irrigation. Without structures or even plastic film, fall is overwhelmingly the more favorable choice.

> **Growing iceberg lettuce comes down to a single core question: will the air temperature be 15-18°C at the moment heading begins? It is hardly an exaggeration to say that working the sowing date backward to answer this question is the entire technique for this crop.**

### 3-2. Light

| Item | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Sunlight needed | 8-10 hours a day |
| Partial shade | Tolerated, but heading becomes loose |
| 40% shade | Relative yield 86% (confidence interval 61-120) |
| Light-dependent germination | Present. Seeds buried too deep will not germinate |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;When light is insufficient, heading turns loose. If photosynthesis produces too little sugar, the leaves become thin and light, and even when they overlap, they never build up weight or density. A head that gives way when pressed usually signals that light or nutrients were lacking. That said, shading trials show that leafy vegetables in general retain 86% relative yield even under 40% shade, so half a day of shade is not fatal. Heading still occurs even in a spot that gets sun for only half the day, such as in a building's shadow or under a tree.

### 3-3. Water and Soil

| Item | Standard | Notes |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Total evapotranspiration | About 500mm | More than lettuce's 350mm |
| Moisture during heading | Keep from drying out | Drought at this stage is the main cause of heading failure |
| Waterlogging | Root rot and soft rot | Raise the bed |
| Soil | Well-drained sandy loam to loam | Water retention is also needed |
| Soil pH | pH 6.0-6.8 | Growth declines in acidic soil |
| Organic matter | More is better | Raises both water retention and nutrients |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;A total evapotranspiration of 500mm is the most realistic burden in growing iceberg lettuce. It is 43% more than lettuce's 350mm, and that difference converts directly into the labor of hauling water. If the tap water is cut off and water has to be carried in from a well or a stream, that 43% is added straight onto every day's labor time.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There are two reasons iceberg lettuce uses so much water. One is that the growing period is long, so the total accumulates. The other is that during heading, the cells of a large mass must be filled with water. If water is short during heading, the head fails to fill, and that failure cannot be recovered later just by watering. Once cell division has finished, all that remains is swelling those cells, nothing more.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;When soil pH drops below 6.0, growth declines noticeably. In acidic soil, phosphorus gets bound up by aluminum and iron into a form the roots cannot use, and phosphorus is an element directly involved in heading. Without lime, wood ash, powder from burnt shells, or ground eggshells serve as natural materials for raising the pH. Adding a large amount all at once, however, locks up micronutrients, so it should be added in small amounts, spread out over time.

## 4. Varieties

### 4-1. The Two Types

| Type | Characteristics | In a Disaster |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Crisphead | Deeply notched leaf edges and a firm head | Best storability, matching the whole point of planting this crop |
| Butterhead | Semi-heading. Soft leaves and a loose head | Lower risk of heading failure, but the storage advantage is also smaller |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Choosing between the two types is really a matter of where you place the risk. Crisphead, when heading succeeds, forms a firm mass that lasts more than a week. In exchange, its heading conditions are demanding, so the risk of failure is high. Butterhead heads loosely, so it manages to take some shape even when conditions are a little off, but because it is loose, its storability suffers and ends up not very different from lettuce.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;If the purpose of planting iceberg lettuce is storage, crisphead is the right choice. If the purpose is not storage, the reason to plant iceberg lettuce at all is weak to begin with. So the practical choice tilts toward crisphead, and in exchange, more care has to go into meeting its heading conditions.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;What is worth noting in this table is the existence of resistant varieties. In a situation without pesticides, resistance is a means of control already built into the seed. Securing a variety resistant to downy mildew means you do not have to do anything at all about that disease, and this is more reliable than any natural pesticide.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;If a disaster drags on, however, seed for imported varieties will not be replenished. What remains then is only the seed you have saved yourself, and whether resistance is heritable becomes the question. Because iceberg lettuce is self-pollinating, resistance determined by a single dominant gene carries through unchanged to the next generation. If the variety is sold as an F1 hybrid, though, saving its seed causes the traits to scatter. Checking whether the seed packet marks it as a hybrid has real, practical meaning for this crop.

### 4-3. Choosing by Situation

| Situation | Choice | Reason |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Storage is the goal | Early-maturing crisphead | A firm head is what creates storability |
| Worried about heading failure | Butterhead or leaf lettuce | Even on failure, the leaves are still edible |
| Fall cultivation | Early-maturing variety | Heading must finish before frost |
| Spring cultivation | Late-bolting variety | Must hold out while temperatures rise |
| Fields with heavy aphids | Resistant variety | The most reliable way to stop them without chemicals |
| Planning to keep reusing seed | Open-pollinated variety | Hybrids scatter their traits |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Choosing an early-maturing variety for fall cultivation comes down to timing. Transplanting to harvest takes 50-60 days, and counting backward from the frost date, that period has to be secured. In central South Korea, the first frost falls in late October to early November, so transplanting needs to be finished by early September. Choosing a late-maturing variety means frost arrives before heading is complete.
