## 5. Field Preparation and Fertilizer

### 5-1. Preparing the Field

| Item | Standard | Reason |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Tillage depth | 25-30cm | Roots go down to 20-30cm |
| Bed height | 20cm or more | Prevents waterlogging and soft rot |
| Bed width | 100-120cm (two rows) | |
| Drainage channel | Must be provided | If water pools during heading, the whole head rots |
| Leveling | Even out the surface | Keeps water from pooling to one side |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In an iceberg lettuce field, raising the bed is close to mandatory, not optional. A headed lettuce sits almost touching the ground, and if water pools at the base, soft rot starts at the stem base. Soft rot is bacterial, so once it starts, it burrows inward, and within a few days the whole head turns soft and bursts. Something grown for three months can vanish in three days, and poor drainage is behind most of these cases.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Without plastic film, mulching materials are straw, dried grass, fallen leaves, and rice hulls. Mulch keeps splashing rain from carrying soil-borne pathogens onto the leaves, slows how much the soil temperature rises, and reduces moisture evaporation. For a crop like iceberg lettuce that needs water during heading, that last effect matters especially. Straw mulch, however, becomes a hiding place for slugs, so the compromise is to leave a palm-width of bare ground around the base clear.

### 5-2. Fertilization Amounts

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The fertilizer demand grade in the evaluation table is B, based on N, P, and K combined at about 24g per m², a little more than lettuce's 22g. Converted to a 1,000m² basis, it looks like this.

| Fertilizer | Per 1,000m² | Per m² | Notes |
|:---|:---:|:---:|:---|
| Nitrogen (N) | 12-15kg | 12-15g | Split between base fertilizer and top-dressing |
| Phosphorus (P) | 8-10kg | 8-10g | Entirely as base fertilizer. Directly involved in heading |
| Potassium (K) | 8-10kg | 8-10g | Split between base fertilizer and top-dressing |
| Compost | 1,500-2,000kg | 1.5-2kg | Well-rotted compost |
| Lime | 150kg | 150g | For correcting acidic soil |

| Type | Timing | What | Purpose |
|:---|:---|:---|:---|
| Base fertilizer | 1-2 weeks before transplanting | All the compost, all the phosphorus, half the nitrogen and potassium | Must already be decomposed by the time roots reach it |
| First top-dressing | About 3 weeks after transplanting | Mainly nitrogen and potassium | Grows the outer leaves |
| Second top-dressing | Around the start of heading | Mainly potassium and phosphorus | Fills out the head and firms it up |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There is a reason for putting all the phosphorus in as base fertilizer. Phosphorus barely moves through soil. Scattered on the surface, it stays put and never reaches the roots. That is why it has to be mixed into the soil and placed in advance in the layer the roots will grow through, while tilling. When iceberg lettuce lacks phosphorus, heading turns loose and the head fails to fill. Phosphorus deficiency rarely shows on the surface early on and only appears as a result at heading time, so it is more accurate to say the outcome is decided at the base-fertilizer stage.

> **Nitrogen works the opposite way. If nitrogen is abundant during heading, the leaves keep trying to grow larger instead of curling inward. Excess nitrogen during heading is the most common reason a plant looks lush on the outside but is hollow inside.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There is a reason this principle matters especially in a disaster. Without chemical fertilizer, the nitrogen sources available are diluted urine and less-than-fully-rotted compost, and both release their nitrogen slowly, late into the season. What was put in as base fertilizer can end up taking effect right at heading time. So when using natural materials, the plan should be to apply the nitrogen source earlier than usual and cut it off completely by the time heading begins.

### 5-3. Disaster Substitute Fertilizers

| Material | Nutrients Supplied | How to Use | Caution |
|:---|:---|:---|:---|
| Wood ash | Potassium, phosphorus, calcium | Mix a handful into the soil per plant | Alkaline; too much raises the pH |
| Well-rotted compost | A broad mix | Generously as base fertilizer | Under-rotted compost damages roots |
| Diluted urine | Nitrogen, potassium | Dilute 10-20× with water, apply between plants | Stop once heading begins |
| Bone meal | Phosphorus, calcium | Mix into the soil as base fertilizer | Acts slowly |
| Ground eggshells | Calcium | Mix into base fertilizer | Must be finely ground to break down |
| Sesame or soybean oil cake | Nitrogen, phosphorus | Ferment first, then use as base fertilizer | Unfermented, the heat of fermentation burns the roots |
| Burnt, ground shells | Calcium | Substitute for lime | Add in small amounts, spread over time |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Wood ash is the easiest source of potassium to obtain in a disaster, since it is always produced whenever a fire is burned. However, alongside potassium it also carries a lot of calcium, making it strongly alkaline, and it easily pushes past the pH 6.0-6.8 that iceberg lettuce prefers. Limit it to about a handful per plant, and unless the field is strongly acidic, do not add it every year. It must be kept dry, or the potassium leaches out.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Urine is useful because it carries both nitrogen and potassium, but with iceberg lettuce the timing of use has to be respected. Use it only up through the first top-dressing around 3 weeks after transplanting, and stop once heading begins. The dilution ratio is 10-20×, and since it burns leaves on contact, pour it into the furrow between plants. Left standing for a few days, the urea converts to ammonia, the smell grows much stronger, and the concentration changes too, so using it the same day it is collected is the more reliable approach.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The yield retention without synthetic inputs in the evaluation table is C, 40.8%. That means growing without synthetic fertilizer drops yield to around the 40% level, and vegetables show the largest such gap among all crop groups. In iceberg lettuce, this drop does not show up only as reduced yield but also as heading failure. If nutrients are short, the outer leaves cannot grow enough, and if the outer leaves are weak, the plant never moves on to heading. So when growing with natural materials, the practical approach is to put in more compost than for other crops, while also widening the planting spacing a little to secure more resources per plant.

## 6. Sowing and Transplanting

### 6-1. Cropping Seasons

| Season | Sowing | Transplanting | Harvest | Notes |
|:---|:---|:---|:---|:---|
| Spring cultivation | February-March | March-April | June-July | Temperatures rise during heading, so failure risk is high |
| Fall cultivation | August-September | September-October | November-December | Effectively the only choice in a disaster |
| Summer cultivation | Not recommended | | | Both germination and heading are blocked |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The reason fall cultivation is advantageous is exactly what was covered earlier under temperature. One more point to add: heads from fall cultivation arrive exactly when they are needed most. November and December are when what a field can offer drops off sharply, and that is precisely when a storable leafy vegetable becomes available.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The difficulty with fall sowing is the heat of August. The germination limit is 26°C, but soil temperature in late August runs higher than that. It is the same heat-dormancy problem as lettuce, and the response is the same.

| Method | Procedure | Principle |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Cold priming | Soak seeds in water for 4-6 hours, wrap in a wet cloth, and leave in a cool spot for a day | The soaked seed experiences cold, which releases dormancy |
| Pre-germinating | Let seeds just begin to sprout between wet cloths, then sow them out | Finishes germination outside the field |
| Evening sowing | Sow after sunset and water generously | Takes advantage of soil temperature dropping overnight |
| Shading | Use a mat or branches to cast light shade over the sowing spot | Lowers soil temperature by a few degrees |
| Raising seedlings in a cool spot | Grow seedlings under a north-facing eave or in a tree's shade | A few degrees cooler than the field |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Unlike lettuce, iceberg lettuce is normally raised as seedlings before transplanting, and this actually works in its favor when dealing with heat dormancy. You cannot lower the soil temperature of an entire field, but moving a single seedling tray to a cool spot is not hard. Sprout it under a north-facing eave or in a tree's shade, and once true leaves appear, move it to the field - and August's heat is bypassed entirely.

### 6-2. Raising Seedlings

| Stage | Standard | Notes |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Sowing depth | 0.5-1cm | Light-dependent germination; buried too deep, it will not come up |
| Germination temperature | 18-21°C | |
| Germination period | 6-8 days | |
| Seedling-raising period | 20-30 days | |
| Right time to transplant | 4-5 true leaves | Must not go past this point |
| Seedling tray | Anything works: a tray, a wooden box, a container filled with soil | |

> **If transplanting is delayed, heading fails. A seedling with more than 6 true leaves already has roots tangled in a cramped space, and it spends time recovering after being moved instead of growing its outer leaves on schedule. Weak outer leaves mean it never advances to heading, and by then the season has passed.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This is one of the most common causes of failure in growing iceberg lettuce. The bigger a seedling looks, the sturdier it seems, but the reality is the opposite. A seedling with root-bound roots stops growing for close to 2 weeks after transplanting, and those 2 weeks push the heading date back far enough to throw off the temperature match. The right time is 4-5 true leaves, with roots just lightly holding the tray's soil together.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Reducing water a few days before transplanting and putting the seedlings out in open air to harden them off speeds up establishment. Seedlings raised only indoors or in shade have thin, soft leaves, and as soon as they are put out in the field they scorch in the sun or wilt in the wind. Exposing them gradually to sun and wind over four to five days is enough.

### 6-3. Transplanting

| Item | Standard | Notes |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Plant spacing | 30-35cm | Needs room to head |
| Row spacing | 50-60cm | Ensures airflow |
| Planting density | 3,000-3,500 plants per 1,000m² | 3-3.5 plants per m² |
| Planting depth | So the base of the true leaves is not buried in soil | If buried, the base rots |
| Time of transplanting | An overcast day or toward evening | Planted at midday, it wilts |
| Right after transplanting | Water thoroughly | Roots and soil must make close contact |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Planting depth matters. Iceberg lettuce's stem is so compressed it is almost nonexistent, and the leaves attach directly at the base. Plant it deep enough that the base gets buried in soil, and water and soil touching that spot start soft rot and basal rot. The rule is to plant it at the same depth it grew in the seedling tray, or a little shallower still.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Narrowing the planting spacing raises the number of plants per area, but for iceberg lettuce that is usually a loss. Crowding worsens airflow, letting downy mildew and gray mold spread; it starves the plant of light, loosening the head; and above all, under conditions of growing with natural materials only, it leaves too few nutrients per plant. Since the size of a single head is essentially the yield for this crop, a few properly formed heads beat many small ones.

## 7. Water Management

### 7-1. Water by Stage

| Timing | Water Management | Reason |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Right after transplanting - establishment | Water thoroughly | Roots need to bond with the new soil |
| After establishment - outer leaf formation | When the topsoil dries | Encourages roots downward |
| Heading | Steadily, never letting it dry out | The most critical period |
| 1 week before harvest | Reduce or stop | Tissue firms up, raising storability |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Water management for iceberg lettuce comes down to a single period: heading. If the soil dries out during this stage, the head fails to fill, and a head that has failed to fill once cannot be filled later just by watering. Heading is a process in which cells divide and then those cells swell with water, so if the window for cell division is missed, there are simply no cells left to swell.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In the opposite direction, it is good to cut back on water right before harvest. A head watered generously right up to harvest has cells so full of water that they burst easily and soften faster in storage. Cutting back on water starting about a week ahead firms the tissue up somewhat and extends the storage period. When you are aiming for one week at room temperature, those few days matter.

### 7-2. Water Shortage and Excess

| Symptom | Cause | Response |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Wilts by day, recovers by evening | Temporary shortage | Within normal range. Increase watering slightly |
| Does not recover even by evening | Severe shortage | Water thoroughly right away |
| Head is loose and light | Water shortage during heading | Cannot be reversed |
| Inner leaf tips dry brown | Impaired calcium movement from uneven moisture | Water evenly |
| Outer leaves yellow and the base softens | Waterlogging | Open the drainage channel and stop watering |
| Head splits | A lot of water suddenly after drying out | Keep watering intervals even |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Head splitting shows up when the water supply is uneven. After staying dry for a long stretch, if heavy rain comes or a lot of water is given at once, the cells inside swell rapidly and push apart the already-firmed outer layer until it splits. Pathogens enter through the split and start rot during storage, so a split head should not be stored - eat it first. Prevention is not about frequent small amounts but about evenness, and mulching contributes a great deal to that evenness.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Tipburn, where inner leaf tips dry brown, occurs the same way it does in lettuce: calcium fails to reach the tip of the new leaf. It shows up more often in iceberg lettuce, because the inner leaves are wrapped in layer after layer and transpiration barely happens there. Calcium is an element that travels along the flow of water, so without transpiration it never arrives. This is the case where the outside looks fine but cutting it open reveals browning inside. Rather than adding calcium to the soil, keeping moisture even and avoiding overcrowding to secure airflow has a much bigger effect.

### 7-3. Ways to Save Water

| Method | Effect |
|:---|:---|
| Straw and dried-grass mulch | Sharply reduces evaporation. The cheapest water-saving method for iceberg lettuce |
| Watering in early morning | Avoids midday evaporation, and leaves dry off during the day |
| Concave ring around each plant | Water gathers toward the roots |
| Furrow irrigation | Keeps leaves dry, reducing disease |
| Windbreak | Wind greatly increases evaporation |
| Reusing greywater | Use only water low in soap and grease |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Converting the total evapotranspiration of 500mm to a per-m² basis gives 500L for one growing cycle. That is 500L for the 1m² where three heads grow, so growing ten heads works out to needing close to 1.7 tons of water in one cycle. Rain, of course, covers a good deal of that, but in a year without rain, a person has to haul that amount. When deciding whether to plant iceberg lettuce at all, this is the number to work out first.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Mulching visibly reduces this burden. Evaporation from bare soil accounts for a substantial share of total evapotranspiration, and covering it with straw or dried grass cuts that share down, leaving only what the roots themselves use. Without plastic film, straw mulch serves as a water-saving measure, a weed-suppression measure, and a disease-prevention measure all at once. In growing iceberg lettuce, it is the task that delivers the most effects for the least cost.
