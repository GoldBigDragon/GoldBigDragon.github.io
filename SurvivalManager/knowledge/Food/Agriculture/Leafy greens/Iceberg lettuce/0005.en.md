## 8. Crop Management - Conditions for Head Formation

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In growing iceberg lettuce, every other task serves a single purpose: making heading happen. If heading fails, three months of labor disappear, so this section deals, in effect, with what makes or breaks this crop.

### 8-1. Conditions That Cause Heading to Fail

| Cause | What Happens | Why |
|:---|:---|:---|
| High heat | Leaves grow open and the plant bolts | The growth-rate difference between the leaf's lower and upper surfaces disappears |
| Excess nitrogen | Leaves are large and lush, but the inside is hollow | Vegetative growth never stops, so the plant cannot move into the curling stage |
| Delayed transplanting | Outer leaves are weak, so heading never starts | A root-bound seedling spends its time on establishment |
| Phosphorus shortage | Head is loose and light | Phosphorus is needed for cell division and energy metabolism |
| Water shortage during heading | The head fails to fill | There is no water to swell the cells |
| Insufficient light | Head is loose | Sugar is scarce, so leaves stay light |
| Overcrowding | Plants shade each other and compete for light and nutrients | |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The three most common of these are the first three: high heat, excess nitrogen, and delayed transplanting. All three cannot be fixed once heading is under way; they are decided before it.

> **Heading failure is not an accident that happens during heading; it is the moment the result of the previous two months becomes visible. Once the leaves start to spread open, there is almost nothing left to do. It is already decided at the moment the sowing date is set, the moment the seedling is transplanted, and the moment nitrogen is cut off.**

### 8-2. Getting the Three Right

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The only response to high heat is timing. Work the sowing date backward so that the average temperature around the start of heading lands at 15-18°C. Sowing to the start of heading takes roughly two months, so the reference date for fall sowing is two months before the point when temperatures in that region drop below 18°C. In central South Korea, that is around late August; in the south, around early September. Weather differs from year to year, so this reference date should be set with a margin of safety, while also keeping an eye on the deadline by which heading must finish before frost.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The response to nitrogen is to split the timing. Concentrate nitrogen during the 2-5 weeks after transplanting, while the outer leaves are growing, and cut it off completely by the start of heading. Natural materials release their effect late, so it is safer to treat the first top-dressing at 3 weeks after transplanting as the last time diluted urine is applied. If a large amount of under-rotted compost went in as base fertilizer, it can go on releasing nitrogen all the way to heading time by itself, so the compost used must always be fully rotted.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The response for transplanting timing depends on how well you can judge a seedling. The right moment is 4-5 true leaves, with roots just lightly holding the tray's soil together. If bad weather makes transplanting impossible, move the tray to a cool spot and cut back water to slow its growth, but once that delay passes a week, the heading date starts to slip out of alignment.

### 8-3. Other Care Tasks

| Task | Timing | Details |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Gap filling | 1 week after transplanting | Put a new seedling where one has died |
| Weeding | Early stage | While weeds are small. Roots are shallow, so deep weeding injures them |
| Hilling | Once, midway through growth | A light dusting of soil at the base. Keeps the head from toppling as it grows heavy |
| Trimming outer leaves | As needed | Remove lower leaves that have yellowed and dried |
| Checking the mulch | As needed | Re-cover spots the wind has exposed |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Overdoing hilling actually causes harm. If the base gets buried in soil, soft rot starts right there. Do it only lightly enough to keep the plant from toppling over.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Removing yellowed, dried lower leaves matters more than it seems. Dying tissue becomes a foothold for gray mold, and the fungus that grows there spreads to healthy leaves. If removing them wounds the base, though, that wound becomes an entry point for disease, so grasp the leaf stalk and snap it sideways to remove it cleanly. Do not leave the removed leaves in the field; carry them well away or bury them deep in the compost pile.

## 9. Pests and Diseases - Prevention Without Pesticides

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Iceberg lettuce's pest & disease pressure is C, with an estimated yield loss without pest control of 50.6%, the same as lettuce. That is only natural, since they are the same species. However, the head shape creates two differences. One is that the inside of the head is humid and airless, making it vulnerable to bacterial soft rot. The other is that when disease progresses inside the head, it stays invisible from outside, so it is caught late.

### 9-1. Major Diseases

| Disease | Symptoms | Conditions for Onset | Response Without Chemicals |
|:---|:---|:---|:---|
| Downy mildew | Yellow-brown spots on the upper leaf surface, white mold on the underside | Cool and humid conditions; leaves staying wet for a long time | Airflow, morning watering, resistant varieties, removing diseased leaves |
| Soft rot | The base and inside turn soft, burst, and smell foul | Hot and humid conditions, poor drainage, wounds | Raising the bed, drainage, avoiding wounds |
| Sclerotinia rot | Base softens, with white mycelium and black grains | Cool and humid conditions, continuous cropping of Asteraceae | Deep tillage, mulching, 2-3-year crop rotation, complete removal of diseased plants |
| Gray mold | Gray mold growing over brown spots | Cool and humid conditions, poor airflow | Humidity control, adequate spacing, removing dry leaves |
| Powdery mildew | White powder on the leaf surface | Dry conditions with large day-night temperature swings | Airflow and sunlight, diluted milk spray |
| Basal rot | The base at soil level browns and the plant topples | Waterlogging, deep planting | Shallow planting, drainage |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The most characteristic disease in iceberg lettuce is soft rot. It is caused by bacteria and starts wherever there is a wound and water. The inside of the head traps humidity easily and lacks airflow, an environment good for bacteria to grow in. This is the case where the outer leaves look fine but cutting it open reveals a soft interior, and by that point, nothing can be done.

> **Soft rot is not a matter of treatment but of managing conditions. Keep water from pooling, keep the base from being buried in soil, avoid wounding the plant at harvest, and do not harvest right after rain. These four things are the whole of what can be done without chemicals, and they are also the whole of what actually works.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Downy mildew is the most common disease in iceberg lettuce. The basis attached to the pest-control self-reliance grade of B in the evaluation table is precisely about this disease. Organic-cultivation trials tested mixing cultivars, but mulching alone could not achieve commercial-level control, and moderate incidence brought a loss of at least 10%. In other words, stopping downy mildew without chemicals is not easy.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;However, since the conditions for its onset are clear, the direction of response is clear too. It gets worse the longer leaves stay wet. So water in early morning so leaves dry out during the day, pour it into the furrow so the leaves stay dry, and leave generous spacing so air can move through. A resistant variety, if you have one, is the most reliable tool. Mixing varieties can slow how fast a pathogen that one lineage is vulnerable to spreads across the whole field.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Sclerotinia rot is a soil-borne disease shared with lettuce. The black, hard sclerotia that form at the base of a diseased plant fall into the soil, survive there for several years, and germinate again when conditions are right. The continuous-cropping tolerance grade of B, with its 2-to-3-year rotation break, exists because of these sclerotia.

> **A plant infected with sclerotinia rot should be dug up along with the soil around its roots and carried out of the field. Do not put it in the compost pile. The sclerotia survive the heat of composting and spread across the whole field.**

### 9-2. Major Pests

| Pest | Damage | Control Without Chemicals |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Aphids | Suck sap from the underside of leaves and transmit viruses | Soapy water spray, protecting ladybugs and aphid parasitoid wasps, resistant varieties |
| Thrips | Scrape leaves and suck sap, leaving silvery-white marks | Yellow sticky traps, clearing nearby weeds |
| Moth caterpillars | Chew on leaves and burrow into the head | Hand-picking in early morning, insect netting |
| Slugs | Make holes in leaves and leave slime trails | Hand-picking at night, a ring of ash or rice hulls |
| Root mites | Gnaw at roots, causing the plant to wilt | Crop rotation, improved drainage |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;What is especially tricky for a heading type is moth caterpillars. Once the leaves begin curling, a caterpillar gets inside them, and after that it is out of reach. It chews away at the inside unseen from outside and leaves droppings, and those droppings and the wounds invite soft rot. The response is to reduce their numbers before heading begins. Starting right after transplanting, check the underside of leaves for egg clusters and destroy them, and if there is time to spare, cover the plants with loose netting before heading starts to prevent moths from laying eggs at all. Once heading has begun, it is too late.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Slugs are as much a nuisance here as with lettuce. They hide under straw or stones by day and come out at night, so going out at night with a lamp to pick them off by hand is the most reliable method. Ringing each plant with a band of dry wood ash, rice hulls, or eggshells keeps them from crossing, since they avoid drying out their bodies, but the effect disappears once it rains. Mulch becoming a hiding place is the same issue as before, so the same compromise applies: leave a palm-width of bare ground clear around the base.

### 9-3. Natural Controls and Cultural Practices

| Preparation | How to Make It | Target |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Soapy water | Dissolve 20g of unscented soap in 1L of water | Aphids, mites |
| Diluted milk | Mix milk and water at 1 to 9 | Powdery mildew |
| Wood ash extract | Soak wood ash in water for 24 hours, then strain and use | Aphids |
| Wood ash ring | Scatter dry ash around each plant | Blocking slugs |
| Yellow sticky trap | Coat a yellow board with something sticky and set it up | Thrips, winged aphids |
| Wood vinegar | Dilute 300-500× and spray | Repellent effect, disease suppression |

| Principle | Details |
|:---|:---|
| Crop rotation | Rest Asteraceae for 2-3 years, longer still if sclerotinia rot has occurred |
| Airflow | Do not narrow the planting spacing. The cheapest control measure there is |
| Drainage | Raise the bed. The foundation of dealing with soft rot |
| Watering time and placement | Water in early morning, into the furrow |
| Mulching | Blocks rain splash, cutting off the soil-borne infection route |
| Nitrogen management | Too much softens tissue and draws in aphids |
| Residue disposal | Dig up diseased plants with their soil and carry them off or burn them |
| Avoiding the hot season | Avoiding summer cultivation is itself a form of control |
| Resistant varieties | A control measure already built into the seed |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;When using soapy water on iceberg lettuce, the concentration has to be respected. Its leaves are thin and tender, so even a slightly higher concentration damages them, and the damaged spot becomes an entry point for soft rot. Do not exceed 20g per 1L, and rinsing with clean water a few hours after spraying reduces the damage. Spray in early morning or after sunset, avoiding midday.

## 10. Nutrient Deficiency and Excess

| Symptom | Cause | Remedy with Natural Materials |
|:---|:---|:---|
| The whole leaf is yellow and the plant is small | Nitrogen deficiency | Well-rotted compost, urine diluted 15-20×. Only before heading |
| Leaf edges dry brown | Potassium deficiency | A small amount of wood ash |
| Growth is stunted and leaves take on a purplish tint | Phosphorus deficiency | Bone meal, wood ash. Low soil temperature produces the same symptom |
| Head is loose and light | Phosphorus and potassium shortage | Decided at the base-fertilizer stage |
| Inner leaf tips dry brown | Impaired calcium movement | Even watering, ensuring airflow. Eggshells are a long-term measure |
| Yellowing between the leaf veins | Magnesium deficiency | Increase compost |
| Leaves are large and deep green, but the inside is hollow | Nitrogen excess | Cut off the nitrogen supply |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Two deficiency symptoms are characteristic of iceberg lettuce. One is tipburn, and the other is poor heading. Both share the trait of being hard to see from outside.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Tipburn is a symptom in which the tips of inner leaves scorch brown. It happens because calcium fails to reach the tip of the newly growing leaf, and that is less because the soil lacks calcium than because the flow of water never reaches that far. Calcium travels along the flow created by transpiration, and the leaves inside the head are wrapped in layer after layer, so transpiration there barely happens. This is why tipburn is worse in iceberg lettuce than in lettuce. The response is not to add calcium to the soil but to keep moisture even, avoid excessively high humidity, and avoid overcrowding.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Poor heading appears when phosphorus and potassium are short, or when nitrogen is excessive. It looks like it grew well on the outside, but pressed at harvest, it gives way and lacks weight. Nothing can be done by this point, so the judgment has to be made at the base-fertilizer stage. Since phosphorus does not move through soil, it must be mixed in while tilling, and since natural materials release nitrogen late, it must be cut off early.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In iceberg lettuce, excess nitrogen is often more dangerous than deficiency. The leaves are large and deep green, so it looks well grown, but the tissue is soft and the cell walls are thin, making it vulnerable to soft rot and gray mold and drawing in aphids. Above all, vegetative growth never stops, so the plant cannot move on to heading. A lush plant that turns out hollow inside usually comes from this.

## 11. Intercropping and Crop Rotation

| Compatibility | Companion | Effect |
|:---:|:---|:---|
| Good | Legumes (peas, kidney beans, cowpeas) | Fix nitrogen and leave it behind |
| Good | Scallions, Welsh onions, garlic | Their smell confuses pests |
| Good | Brassicas such as radish and napa cabbage | Share no pests or diseases and root at a different depth |
| Good | Strongly scented herbs such as dill and coriander | Attract natural enemies of aphids |
| Bad | Asteraceae such as lettuce, crown daisy, chicory, and burdock | Share sclerotinia rot and downy mildew |
| Bad | Continuous cropping of iceberg lettuce itself | Sclerotia accumulate |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;It is best to avoid planting iceberg lettuce and lettuce right next to each other in the same field. Being the same species, they share pests and diseases completely, and if downy mildew starts on one, it spreads straight to the other. If you grow both, put them at opposite ends of the field.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Because iceberg lettuce is transplanted at wide spacing, there is a lot of empty ground early on. Using that space is what intercropping does. However, if the crop planted between the rows grows taller than the iceberg lettuce, it blocks light and interferes with heading, so it has to be something low-growing and quick to finish. Baby-leaf lettuce, scallions, or spinach fit the bill. Because of the shared Asteraceae diseases, putting lettuce in between calls for caution, and young brassica greens are a safer choice instead.

| Example Rotation Sequence | Year | Crop | What Happens to the Field |
|:---|:---:|:---|:---|
| 4-year cycle | Year 1 | Iceberg lettuce and other Asteraceae | Sclerotinia rot risk begins to build up |
| | Year 2 | Legumes (soybean, pea, cowpea) | Fixed nitrogen accumulates |
| | Year 3 | Grasses such as maize and sorghum | Asteraceae pathogens starve |
| | Year 4 | Brassicas such as radish and napa cabbage | Deep roots turn over the soil |
| | Back to year 1 | Iceberg lettuce | Starts again with sclerotia density lowered |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Placing grasses right after Asteraceae is especially effective. The sclerotinia pathogen has a wide host range but cannot infect grasses well. Rice, maize, sorghum, wheat, and barley all fall into this group.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Placing legumes right before iceberg lettuce needs caution because of nitrogen. If legumes leave behind a lot of nitrogen, iceberg lettuce may keep receiving nitrogen all the way to heading time and fail to stop vegetative growth. When planting iceberg lettuce the year after legumes, it is safer to skip the nitrogen top-dressing altogether or cut it back sharply.
