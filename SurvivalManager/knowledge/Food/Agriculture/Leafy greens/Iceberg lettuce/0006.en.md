## 12. Harvest - A One-Time Harvest

### 12-1. Judging the Right Time to Harvest

| Category | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Fall cultivation | 50-60 days after transplanting |
| Spring cultivation | Around 60 days after transplanting |
| Winter cultivation | 90 days after transplanting |
| Judging the head | Holds firm when pressed from above |
| Time to harvest | Early morning. Heaviest, holding the most moisture |
| Times to avoid | Right after rain, while leaves are wet |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Pressing on the head is the most reliable way to judge. If it gives way, the inside has not filled yet, or that plant never will; if it holds firm, it is the right time. If it feels excessively hard, it may already be too late, with a flower stalk rising inside. When a flower stalk rises inside the head, the interior pushes up into a cone shape unseen from outside and bitterness rises with it, so the trick is not to leave it long after the head is complete.

> **Do not harvest right after rain. If it is cut with water still sitting between the leaves, that water becomes the starting point for soft rot during storage. Harvest a day or two after the rain has stopped, once the leaves have dried.**

### 12-2. Harvesting Technique

| Step | Details | Reason |
|:---:|:---|:---|
| 1 | Harvest early morning on a clear day | Heavy with moisture and fresh |
| 2 | Cut the base with a knife in one stroke | Multiple cuts leave a rough surface that spoils |
| 3 | Leave 2-3 outer leaves on | They act as a shell protecting the inside during storage |
| 4 | Dry the cut surface in the shade | A wet cut surface is where decay starts |
| 5 | Store without washing | Any moisture left makes it soften right away |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Leaving the outer leaves on is the single most practical technique in harvesting iceberg lettuce. Commercially distributed iceberg lettuce has all its outer leaves stripped off, but if storage is the goal, you should do the opposite. Two or three outer leaves are packaging in themselves, and while they dry out and brown during storage, the inner leaves stay protected. Peel them off when it is time to eat, and the inside is untouched.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The cut surface exudes a white latex, the same as with lettuce. As this latex dries and hardens into brown, it actually acts as a plug sealing the wound. Leave it in the shade for one to two hours until the cut surface dries before moving it to storage, and the storage period lengthens; put it away wet right away, and bacteria grow right there.

### 12-3. What "Only Once" Means

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;What perenniality D in the evaluation table means is worth revisiting at the harvest stage. Harvesting the head leaves no choice but to cut the base, and that removes the growing point along with it. That plant is finished.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This is why, in harvesting iceberg lettuce, spreading out the timing becomes a problem that comes even before storage. Plant twenty heads on the same day, and twenty heads arrive all at once in the same week, more than one week at room temperature can handle. Stagger planting by ten days to two weeks, and the harvest is staggered by that much too. In fall cultivation, though, this staggering has a limit, because frost sets a deadline. If the first frost falls in late October, transplanting has to be finished by early September, 50-60 days earlier, so the window available for staggering is only 2-3 weeks.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Side shoots sometimes grow from the base left behind after cutting, producing a few small leaves. They do not head and stay small, but they are edible. Calling this a repeat harvest would be a stretch; it is more accurate to see it as simply not throwing anything away before the field is cleared.

## 13. Seed Saving - The Barrier Heading Creates

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The seed-saving grade in the evaluation table is S, with the basis being self-pollination. Like lettuce, iceberg lettuce completes fertilization with its own pollen before the flower even opens, so genes barely mix even with bees visiting, and a practical isolation distance of 1-2m is enough. But saving seed from iceberg lettuce has one problem lettuce does not: the flower stalk has to push its way out through the head.

### 13-1. The Problem of a Trapped Flower Stalk

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Iceberg lettuce's leaves wrap layer upon layer into a firm mass. The growing point sits inside it, and once bolting begins, the flower stalk has to push through those layers. The firmer the head, the harder this is, and cases arise where the stalk grows bent inside and rots, and if water then pools inside, soft rot follows.

| Problem | What Happens | Response |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Flower stalk fails to break through the head | Grows bent inside and rots | Cut a shallow cross on top of the head to open a path |
| Water pools inside | Soft rot follows | Shield from rain where possible and ensure drainage |
| Head loosens late | Flowering is delayed and seed fails to mature | Choose plants with a looser head as seed plants |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Cutting a shallow cross into the top of the head with a knife is the traditional response. The depth is 1-2cm, just enough to split a couple of layers of outer leaves; cut too deep and it injures the growing point, ending the plant right there. However, since the cut is a wound, disease can enter through it if rain is frequent. A good number of heads break through on their own even left untouched, so it is safer to leave several plants as seed plants and use whichever comes through well.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Here lies the structural contradiction of saving iceberg lettuce seed. What is good to eat is a firmly headed one, but what is good for saving seed is a loosely headed one. Keep selecting year after year only the individuals that are good for saving seed, and within a few generations the lineage drifts toward loose heading.

> **Blocking this drift means applying two criteria together when choosing seed plants. Take seed from a plant whose head formed properly and whose flower stalk came through safely. Take seed from a plant that failed to head, and that trait carries straight through.**

### 13-2. Timeline

| Stage | Timing | State |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Right time to harvest | 50-60 days after transplanting | Seed plants are left unharvested at this point |
| Bolting | 3-5 weeks later | The flower stalk pushes up through the head |
| Flowering | 3-4 weeks after bolting | Yellow flowers open in the morning |
| Seed maturation | 12-24 days after flowering | The pappus puffs out |
| Seed harvest | 150-180 days after sowing | About a month longer than lettuce |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;What this table reveals is the practical difficulty of saving iceberg lettuce seed. Sowing to seed takes five to six months, so planting in fall is impossible, since winter arrives first, and it has to be planted in spring instead. Sow in February-March, and seed comes in July-August, but the June-July in between is both hot and monsoon season (jangma, late June to July in South Korea). If the period when the flower stalk rises overlaps with the monsoon, water easily pools inside the head and it rots. The advantage of self-pollination remains intact, but the road to using that advantage is long and has many points of failure.

### 13-3. Collecting and Storing Seed

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Once the flower stalk has risen, it is the same as with lettuce. The seed carries a pappus and blows away in the wind once ripe, and since the lower flowers ripen first in sequence, ripening times within a single plant can spread out over more than 3 weeks.

| Method | Procedure | Characteristics |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Bagging | Cover the flower stalk with a breathable paper bag or cloth sack | A good balance between labor and recovery rate |
| Gathering in stages | Every 2-3 days, strip off and collect only the puffed flowers | The highest recovery rate |
| Cutting the whole stalk | Once more than half have puffed, cut the whole stalk and let it after-ripen | Finishes in one pass, but mixes in unripe seed |

| Item | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Seeds per plant | Several thousand |
| Seed longevity | 2-3 years sealed at room temperature; longer if cool and dry |
| Storage | Sealed container, in a cool, dark, dry place |
| Desiccant | Roasted and cooled rice, charcoal, dry wood ash |
| Checking | Every year, place 20 seeds on a wet cloth and count how many germinate |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In iceberg lettuce too, heat dormancy shows up more strongly in freshly collected seed. That is because after-ripening has not finished, and germination-inhibiting substances remain. Using seed collected that same July for August's fall sowing drops the germination rate sharply, so keep two years' worth on hand and use the older stock first. It is also worth remembering that saving seed from a hybrid scatters the traits in the next generation, so that some plants head while others do not.

## 14. Toxicity and Compounds of Concern

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Iceberg lettuce has no toxicity. Being the same species as lettuce, its composition is the same too, but the head shape changes the intensity of a few points.

| Compound | Compared to Lettuce | Reason |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Lactucarium (latex) | Lower | Inner leaves get no light, so they produce less defensive substance |
| Nitrate | Can build up more in the inner leaves | Without light, nitrate cannot convert into protein |
| Oxalic acid | Low | Not a practical concern |

### 14-1. Lactucarium

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Cutting iceberg lettuce releases a white latex, just as with lettuce, and this is lactucarium, which contains lactucerin and lactucin. It is the source of bitterness and a compound with a mild sedative effect. In iceberg lettuce's inner leaves, however, this substance is lower than in lettuce. Tissue that receives no light produces less of this defensive substance. This is why the inside of iceberg lettuce is mild with almost no bitterness, and at the same time why its nutrition is diluted. The outer leaves carry it at a level similar to lettuce, which is why they taste more bitter. There is talk that eating it makes you drowsy, but the amount eaten at a meal falls far short of a level where that effect would actually be felt. A plant whose flower stalk has begun rising inside the head shows a noticeable rise in bitterness, but this is not toxicity appearing; it is a signal that its time has passed.

### 14-2. Nitrate

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The nitrate issue needs closer attention in iceberg lettuce than in lettuce. Nitrogen absorbed by the roots is stored in the leaves as nitrate and, when light is available, converted into amino acids using photosynthetic energy, but the inner leaves of the head get no light, so that conversion never happens, and nitrate can remain as is.

| Condition | Nitrate Accumulation |
|:---|:---|
| Excess nitrogen fertilization | Rises sharply |
| Inner leaves of the head | Get no light, so conversion is incomplete |
| A run of overcast days | Rises |
| Harvest in early morning | Conversion halts overnight, so it is high |
| Harvest in the afternoon on a clear day | Converted during the day, so it is low |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Still, eating normally grown iceberg lettuce in ordinary amounts is not a problem. Nitrate itself is not a poison, and the current general understanding is that the benefits of eating vegetables outweigh this burden. The combination worth worrying about is eating, every day and in large amounts, lettuce grown under insufficient sunlight in a field given too much nitrogen, with no other vegetables alongside it.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;It is fortunate that cutting off nitrogen at heading time is the right call both for making heading succeed and for reducing nitrate. Since the two goals point in the same direction, the decision is not hard to make. A risk beyond the compounds themselves is hygiene. Since it is a vegetable eaten raw, whatever condition the field is in goes straight into the mouth, and iceberg lettuce in particular is hard to rinse clean once soil and water get inside the head. Washing it by peeling off the outer leaves one layer at a time is the reliable method, and lettuce grown in a field given manure that was not fully fermented, or in ground that flooding has passed over, is safer eaten after blanching.

## 15. Storage Without Refrigeration

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Storability D in the evaluation table, one week unpowered at room temperature, is the one reason to plant iceberg lettuce. This section is the core of this crop's document.

### 15-1. Why It Lasts Longer Than Lettuce

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The reason is purely structural. Unlike lettuce, where every leaf sits fully exposed to air, a headed iceberg lettuce has several layers of outer leaves wrapping the inside. During storage, moisture loss, bacterial contact, and oxygen getting in to keep respiration going all happen at the outside first. While the outer leaves dry out, brown, and soften, the inner leaves stay protected within. The volume-to-surface-area ratio also works in its favor. A widely spread leaf has a large surface area for its weight, and loses moisture that much faster, whereas a mass balled into a sphere is the shape with the smallest possible surface area.

| Condition | Storage Period |
|:---|:---:|
| Room temperature, as is | 5-7 days |
| Cool shade (around 15°C) | 1-2 weeks |
| Earth cellar or root-cellar pit (around 10°C) | 2-3 weeks |
| In a root-cellar pit with damp sand or straw | 3-4 weeks |

### 15-2. Storage Methods

| Method | Procedure | Caution |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Leaving outer leaves on | Keep 2-3 outer leaves attached | Acts as packaging |
| Drying the cut surface | 1-2 hours in the shade after cutting | A wet cut surface is where decay starts |
| Not washing | Just brush off soil, no water | Any moisture left softens it right away |
| Wrapping in damp cloth | Dampen a breathable cloth and wrap loosely | Wrapping tightly crushes it |
| Burying in a root-cellar pit | Root end down, between damp sand or straw | Keep them spaced so they do not touch each other |
| Hanging upside down | Tie a string around the base and hang it in a cool spot | No surface touches the ground, so it is not crushed |
| Earthenware crock | Line with damp cloth, place it inside, and cover loosely | Sealing it completely makes it soften |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;When storing in a root-cellar pit, keeping heads spaced apart so they do not touch matters a great deal. A spot where two heads touch gets no airflow and traps humidity, so softening starts right there, and once one head begins to soften, ethylene and bacteria spread to its neighbors. Checking every few days and pulling out anything that has started to spoil has a large effect on how long the storage lasts.

> **Separate what will be stored from what will be eaten first, right at harvest. Split heads, ones with bruise marks, ones showing signs of insects having gotten in, and ones harvested right after rain should not be stored - eat them first. One spoiled head drags down the sound ones next to it.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Without a root-cellar pit or an earth cellar, look for a spot that gets no sun but has airflow, such as under a north-facing eave, under the floor, by a well, or in the shade of a stone wall. What matters is not the temperature itself but how steady it stays. In a spot that is hot by day and cold at night, condensation forms, and that water invites decay.

### 15-3. Storage by Processing

| Method | Procedure | Duration | Assessment |
|:---|:---|:---:|:---|
| Blanch and dry | Blanch the outer leaves and dry them in the shade | Several months | Useful for using up outer leaves |
| Mul-kimchi (kimchi in a light brine) | Soak in 2-3% brine | 1-2 weeks | Keeps a little crispness |
| Geotjeori (freshly seasoned, unfermented kimchi) | Salt and season | 2-3 days | More a preparation than storage |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Iceberg lettuce does not suit processed storage well. Its moisture is close to 95%, so drying leaves almost nothing behind, and salting it away destroys its crispness. This crop's storage strategy is not processing but leaving the head intact as it is. That said, the outer leaves removed at harvest do not have to be wasted if they are dried. Being deep green, the outer leaves have better nutrition than the inside, and being tough enough to be hard to eat raw, drying them for use in soup is a fitting use. A single head yields no small amount of outer leaves, so this one habit alone increases the effective harvest.

## 16. Cooking and Processing

| Method | Fuel | Notes |
|:---|:---:|:---|
| Raw (wraps, salad) | None | Inner leaves are crisp and mild |
| Geotjeori | None | Wilts down in volume, so you end up eating more |
| Mul-kimchi | None | Fermentation keeps a good share of the vitamin C |
| Blanching | Little | A way to use up the outer leaves |
| Soup or stir-fry | Shared with other cooking | Uses the outer leaves and core |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Iceberg lettuce's cooking & processing grade of SS comes from the same reason as lettuce's. Washing it and eating it as is takes no fuel, and heat-sensitive vitamin C and folate come through intact. Using this advantage properly, though, requires changing one habit: not throwing away the outer leaves. The outer leaves are deep green and concentrated with beta-carotene and vitamin K, so eating only the white inside makes iceberg lettuce's micronutrients in practice even more diluted than the 15 nutrients listed in the table. If the outer leaves are tough, blanching them or chopping them finely into soup solves that.

| Part | Character | Use |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Inner leaves | Crisp, mild, and nutritionally diluted | Raw, wraps, salad |
| Middle leaves | Moderately crisp | Raw, geotjeori |
| Outer leaves | Deep green, tough, and slightly bitter. Best in nutrition | Blanching, soup, stir-fry, drying |
| Core | Firm and sweet-tasting | Sliced thin, eaten raw or stir-fried |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The core in the middle should not be thrown away either. It is the short, firm stem at the center of the head, and once you peel off its tough outer part and slice it thin, it has a crisp texture and a subtle sweetness, good eaten raw or added to a stir-fry or soup. Geotjeori is a way to reduce volume and increase how much you actually eat. In a crop with 14kcal, eating a larger amount makes a real difference in nutrition supplied.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Cooking iceberg lettuce makes its crispness disappear and turns it soft, so it should not be simmered long. Adding it at the very end of a soup already on the fire, just long enough to wilt it, gives you a cooked form without using any extra fuel. Mixing in a little oil raises the absorption of beta-carotene, vitamin K, and vitamin E, so grinding in some perilla or sesame seed helps.

## 17. Annual Work Calendar

| Month | Spring Cultivation | Fall Cultivation |
|:---:|:---|:---|
| January | Check seed, plan the field, manage compost | |
| February | Sowing and raising seedlings. Warming needed | |
| March | Continue raising seedlings, transplant late in the month. Base fertilizer | |
| April | Establishment and outer leaf formation. First top-dressing | |
| May | Heading. Water management is most critical | |
| June | Harvest. Seed plants are left standing | |
| July | Seed plants bolt and flower | |
| August | Seed harvest and cleaning | Sow late in the month. Managing heat dormancy |
| September | | Raising seedlings and transplanting. Finish by early in the month |
| October | | Outer leaf formation and heading onset. Top-dressing |
| November | | Heading completes and harvest. Storage begins |
| December | | Checking stored heads. Clearing the field and disposing of residue |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;What stands out in this table is how long iceberg lettuce occupies the field. Spring cultivation runs five months, from February to June, or eight months if you carry it through to seed, and fall cultivation takes three months too. Nothing else can be grown in that spot for that whole stretch, and if heading fails, that entire span of time is lost. So the practical arrangement becomes: plant it only for fall cultivation, in a small corner of the field, and keep just a few plants for spring cultivation, purely to carry the seed forward. The rest of the space goes to leafy vegetables you keep picking from continuously, like lettuce, Swiss chard, and kale.

| Category | Spring Cultivation | Fall Cultivation |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Heading success rate | Low. Temperature rises during heading | High. Temperature falls |
| Germination conditions | Cold, so warming is needed | Managing heat dormancy is needed |
| Storage value | Low. It is summer, and it spoils soon | High. It carries into winter |
| Disease pressure | Overlaps with the monsoon; risk of soft rot | Risk of downy mildew |
| Seed saving | Possible | Difficult |
| Recommendation for a disaster | For seed saving only | This is the main approach |

> **Iceberg lettuce is not a pillar of the field but a bridge into winter. Having a few heads left in the root-cellar pit after frost has finished off every other leafy vegetable, and the few weeks that provides - that is the whole of what this crop does. The standard for deciding whether to plant it at all is whether three months and 500mm of water are worth spending for that.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;One last principle to note is spreading risk. Iceberg lettuce is a crop that leaves almost nothing behind when it fails. So filling the whole field with iceberg lettuce is an arrangement to avoid in every case. Plant leaf lettuce alongside it in the same spot, and even if the iceberg lettuce fails, the leaf lettuce still produces something. A year when heading does not succeed is bound to come around once every several years, and whether the field has something else in it that year decides that season's table.
