## 2. What Kind of Plant Is This?

### 2-1. Classification and Relatives

| Item | Details |
|:---|:---|
| Scientific name | Beta vulgaris subsp. cicla |
| Family | Amaranthaceae (Chenopodiaceae has been merged into it) |
| Genus | Beta (the beet genus) |
| Same species | Beetroot (not the same as red chard), sugar beet, fodder beet, wild beet |
| Life cycle | Biennial |
| Other names | Swiss chard, geundae-namul, baek-geundae (white chard) |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The fact that Swiss chard, beetroot and sugar beet are the same species matters for both cultivation and seed saving. In cultivation it means they share pests, diseases and continuous-cropping problems; in seed saving it means their pollen passes between them. These three crops must be managed as a single group.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Spinach also belongs to the same amaranth family. Because it is in a different genus it does not cross with them, but it shares a considerable share of their diseases, so it is counted together with them in crop rotation.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The ancestor is the wild beet (Beta vulgaris subsp.maritima) that grew on the shores of the Mediterranean and the Atlantic coast. It was a plant of salty sandy ground and cliffs, and that origin explains almost everything about Swiss chard's character. The ability to tolerate sodium, the thick, deep root that holds on in poor soil, and the broad adaptability that withstands both heat and cold all came from that seashore.

### 2-2. How Salt Tolerance Works

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Most plants are vulnerable to salt for two reasons. First, when the soil holds a lot of salt, osmotic pressure keeps the roots from drawing in water. Even when the soil is wet, the plant wilts from thirst. Second, sodium that enters the cells interferes with the working of enzymes. The cells are damaged chemically.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Plants of the genus Beta solved each of these two problems. They lock the sodium they absorb inside the vacuole, a large water sac within the cell, isolating it from the cytoplasm. The cytoplasm, where the enzymes are, stays clean, and the sodium piled up in the vacuole actually raises the osmotic pressure of the whole cell, becoming the force that draws water out of the soil. What is poison to others, they have turned to their own use.

| Crop | Salt tolerance limit (by soil electrical conductivity) | Notes |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Swiss chard, beetroot, sugar beet | Very high | Top tier among leafy vegetables |
| Barley | High | The strongest of the cereals |
| Spinach | Medium | Same family but weaker than Swiss chard |
| Lettuce, napa cabbage, radish | Low | Cannot grow on salt-affected land |
| Legumes | Very low | The first to die |

> **On land salted by a storm surge or flooding, and in fields where salts have built up from groundwater irrigation, Swiss chard is almost the only leafy vegetable left. Whether vitamins and minerals can be supplied from that land is the reason this crop exists.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;It needs to be made clear, however, that salt tolerance is not unlimited. At very high salinity, even Swiss chard grows poorly and its leaves become small. To tolerate means to stay alive and still yield something, not to grow as well as usual. On salt-affected land, plan a larger area, allowing for the reduced yield. Rain also washes the salt in the soil downward, so if good drainage is established, conditions improve year by year. When raised beds are built, salt rises to the bed surface and collects there, so on salt-affected land it is advantageous to plant in the furrow and leave the shoulder of the bed empty.

### 2-3. Appearance and Growth Stages

| Organ | Characteristics |
|:---|:---|
| Leaf | Broad ovate, glossy, with thick, distinct veins. The leaf surface is bumpy and wrinkled |
| Leaf stalk | Thick and juicy. White, red, yellow or pink depending on the variety |
| Stem | Compressed and almost absent; leaves emerge radially from the base |
| Root | A straight taproot that goes deep. Unlike beetroot, it does not swell and stays thin |
| Flower | The flower stalk rises in the second year, bearing small green flowers in a spike |
| Fruit | A seed ball: a cluster of several seeds fused together |

| Stage | Timing | What happens |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Germination | 7-14 days after sowing | Several seedlings emerge from a single seed ball |
| Seedling stage | 2 weeks after germination | 2-3 true leaves. Time for the first thinning |
| Vegetative growth | 3-7 weeks after sowing | Leaves grow quickly |
| First harvest | 40-60 days after sowing | Outer leaves 20-30cm |
| Repeated harvest | Continuously thereafter | Picking from the outside, new leaves rise from the center |
| Bolting | Spring of the following year | The flower stalk rises after a spell of cold |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Swiss chard has a single growing point, at the top of the compressed stem, right in the middle of the plant. New leaves keep rising from it and are pushed outward. So when the fully grown outer leaves are picked, the plant merely loses leaves that are already old, and the growing point stays intact. This is the structural basis for the A in Perenniality, and it is covered in detail in Section 12.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The thick, juicy leaf stalk is a hallmark of Swiss chard. Unlike other leafy vegetables, of which only the leaf is eaten, Swiss chard's leaf stalk is an ingredient in its own right. It is crisp to chew and turns tender when cooked, and since it weighs more than the leaf, it contributes substantially to the yield.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The key point of Swiss chard's nutrition is that calcium and iron are abundant but poorly absorbed. The amounts listed in the composition table do not enter the body in full. Oxalic acid binds with calcium to form crystals that do not dissolve in water, and those crystals are not absorbed but excreted as they are. Iron is affected in the same way to some extent.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;So it is not accurate to count Swiss chard as a source of calcium. If calcium is what is needed, kale, which is low in oxalic acid, is a far better choice. Swiss chard's strengths lie in vitamin K, vitamin A and folate, and these are not affected by oxalic acid.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The composition value of 0.00g of sugars also stands out. It means there is no sweetness at all, yet the Taste grade is S because neither the smell nor the texture is off-putting. Blandness is, if anything, an advantage in a disaster. A strongly flavored vegetable palls in a few days, but a bland one never gets tiresome even when eaten every day.

## 3. Growing Conditions - Salt Tolerance as a Weapon

### 3-1. Temperature

| Category | Temperature | Notes |
|:---|:---:|:---|
| Growth range | 5-30°C | Range 25°C, top tier among leafy vegetables |
| Optimum growth temperature | 15-18°C | Day 23-25°C, night minimum 5-6°C |
| Optimum germination temperature | 20-25°C | Also germinates at 10°C and above |
| Germination limits | Below 10°C, above 35°C | |
| Heat tolerance | Grows up to 30°C | Leaves become a little tougher |
| Cold tolerance | Keeps growing down to minus 5°C | Tolerates more if covered with straw |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The most practical point of Swiss chard's temperature behavior is that it gets through the summer. Lettuce and iceberg lettuce collapse at 25°C, and leafy vegetables of the cabbage family also collapse in summer to pests and diseases, but Swiss chard grows up to 30°C. On the question of whether anything green is left in the field at midsummer, Swiss chard is one of the few crops that provide an answer, together with purslane and water spinach.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;However, leaves in the hot season become tougher and their oxalic acid increases. Summer Swiss chard is better blanched than eaten raw, and ample watering lessens the toughening. Providing partial shade keeps the leaves tender.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;On the cold side, it keeps growing down to minus 5°C. The leaves do not die when hit by frost, and in central South Korea and further south it overwinters if covered with straw and fallen leaves. In winter it stops growing, but the leaves already there can be picked one at a time, and it puts out leaves again when spring comes. However, a spell of cold triggers vernalization and the flower stalk rises the following spring, so plants that have been overwintered do not last long in spring.

### 3-2. Light and Shade

| Item | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Required sunlight | 6 hours or more a day |
| Partial shade | Tolerated. Growth slows, but the leaves are tender |
| 40% shade | Relative yield 86% (confidence interval 61-120) |
| Summer shade | Delays the leaves toughening |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Swiss chard's tolerance of partial shade has practical meaning in an urban setting or a small yard. Swiss chard grows even in places that get sun for only half a day, such as the shade of a building, the north side of a fence, or between tall crops. It grows more slowly and the leaves become a little thinner, but there is something to eat. Lettuce also tolerates shade but dies in summer, whereas Swiss chard stays alive in summer too.

### 3-3. Water and Soil

| Item | Standard | Notes |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Total evapotranspiration | About 500mm | More than lettuce's 350mm |
| Irrigation | Water thoroughly when the topsoil dries | Deep roots make it more drought-tolerant than lettuce |
| Excess moisture | Root rot | Build raised beds |
| Soil | Not fussy | Better with plenty of organic matter |
| Soil acidity | pH 6.0-6.8 | Best around 6.5 |
| Salt tolerance | Very high | Can be used on salt-affected land |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The total evapotranspiration of 500mm is more than lettuce, but two circumstances have to be considered together. First, Swiss chard's long growing period is what makes the total add up; its daily water requirement is not especially large. Second, the root of Swiss chard grows straight and deep. Where lettuce's shallow roots wilt as soon as the topsoil dries, Swiss chard draws on water from below. In a drought, Swiss chard has the better staying power.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Not being fussy about soil is also a practical advantage. It grows in sandy ground and in clay. It dislikes standing water, however, so build raised beds and dig drainage ditches. On salt-affected land, as seen above, it is advantageous to plant in the furrow so that the salt rises to the shoulder of the bed.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;It likes a pH of 6.0-6.8, best around 6.5. Growth declines in strongly acidic fields, so raise the pH with wood ash, burned and powdered seashells, or powdered eggshells. Swiss chard is a crop with a relatively high boron requirement, and if the pH is too high, boron is not absorbed and the leaves split and the heart turns black. This is why lime is not put in all at once in large amounts.

## 4. Varieties

### 4-1. Varieties Divided by Color

| Variety | Stalk color | Characteristics |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Cheonggeundae (green chard, also called white chard) | White to light green | The most common, with stable yield and a chewy texture |
| Red chard | Red to pink | High in anthocyanins. Leaves are tender |
| Yellow chard | Yellow to light green | High in beta-carotene. Leaves are tender |
| Fordhook | White | The representative white variety. Leaves are large and thick |
| Ruby | Red | The representative red variety. Good cold hardiness |
| Rainbow strain | Several colors mixed | Seed made by mixing varieties of several colors |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The thing to know when choosing a Swiss chard variety is that differences in color have little effect on difficulty of cultivation or yield. Any variety is easy to grow and the yields are similar. Color is related mainly to the makeup of nutritional components and to texture.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There is, however, one other meaning that color has in a disaster. Red and yellow leaf stalks contain the pigments anthocyanin and betaxanthin respectively, and these pigments are antioxidants. They are components that white varieties lack. Planting several colors mixed together increases the kinds of components obtained from the same area. It is valuable as a way to broaden nutrition without spending any more labor or resources.

### 4-2. Choosing by Situation

| Situation | Choice | Reason |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Yield comes first | Cheonggeundae, Fordhook | Large leaves and thick stalks |
| Wanting a broader range of nutrition | A mix of red and yellow chard | Adds pigment components |
| Aiming to overwinter | Ruby, Fordhook | Better cold hardiness |
| Salt-affected land | Any variety | The whole species has salt tolerance |
| Summer cultivation | Any variety | All tolerate up to 30°C |
| Planning to carry on with your own seed | Open-pollinated (fixed) varieties | Hybrid varieties do not breed true |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Swiss chard is one of the few crops for which the choice of variety does not decide success or failure. This contrasts with iceberg lettuce, where the choice of variety and timing determined whether heads formed. Whatever seed can be had, it grows when planted. That in itself is an advantage in a disaster. In a situation with no room to choose, a crop that needs no choosing has just as little room to fail.
