## 8. Crop Management

### 8-1. A Low-Maintenance Crop

| Task | Needed? | Details |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Thinning | Essential | Must be done because of the seed ball |
| Weeding | Only early on | Weeds are suppressed once the leaves cover the ground |
| Hilling | Generally unnecessary | When grown for a long time, lightly cover the base with soil |
| Pinching | Unnecessary | No side shoots form |
| Staking | Unnecessary | The plant is low |
| Flower removal | When bolting | When the flower stalk rises, cut it off to buy time |
| Clearing dead leaves | As needed | Remove the yellowed, dried leaves at the bottom |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The list of cultivation tasks for Swiss chard is short. Thinning, watering and picking leaves are nearly all of it, and the rest is done only when needed. Compared with cabbage-family crops, which have to be tied to insect netting and bug-catching, and iceberg lettuce, which races the calendar to meet its heading conditions, it takes far less work. In a disaster, labor is the most precious resource, so this difference is large in practice.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Weeding is needed only in the early stage. Once the Swiss chard has grown and its leaves cover the ground, no light reaches below them and weeds are suppressed. If the young plants are kept from being overrun by weeds, from then on they hold their own place. Since part of the root system is shallow, however, do not hoe deeply near the plants; pull the weeds by hand.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Removing the yellowed, dried leaves at the bottom is worth doing. Dying tissue becomes a foothold for gray mold, and the ground beneath turns damp and draws slugs. When removing them, hold the leaf stalk and snap it sideways so that it comes cleanly away from the stem.

### 8-2. Dealing with Bolting

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Swiss chard is a biennial, so normally the flower stalk rises in the spring of the following year. A spell of cold is the trigger, and this is called vernalization. The problem is that this vernalization can occur earlier than scheduled.

| Condition | Result |
|:---|:---|
| Young seedlings go through a long spell below 10°C | The flower stalk rises within the same year |
| A late cold snap after early-spring sowing | The same thing happens |
| Spring after overwintering | Normal bolting. That plant is finished |
| Extreme drought or heat stress | Bolting can be brought forward |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Once the flower stalk rises, the leaves toughen and turn bitter. The plant's priority shifts from leaves to seed, nutrients drain from the leaves into the flower stalk, and lignin builds up in the tissue. This switch cannot be reversed.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Cutting off the flower stalk does buy a little time, however. If it is cut close to the base just as it starts to rise, the plant tries to put out leaves again, and for some weeks more it can be harvested. Another flower stalk soon rises, so this is not a complete solution, but it is useful as a way to extend the season a little. Except for the plants kept for seed, handle the rest this way.

> **To keep flower stalks from rising in an early-spring sowing, do not let young seedlings go through a long spell of cold. Sow after the last frost has fully passed, or, if sowing early, cover with straw or dry grass to hold the soil temperature. A flower stalk that has already risen cannot be undone.**

### 8-3. Managing One Plant for a Long Time

| Item | Details |
|:---|:---|
| After picking leaves | Water. Making new leaves takes moisture |
| Replenishing nutrients | When new leaves get small, add dilute fertilizer |
| Leaves to leave | Always at least 6 center leaves |
| Summer | Providing partial shade keeps the leaves tender |
| Autumn | Cover with straw to prepare for overwintering |
| Winter | Pick the existing leaves one by one. Almost no new leaves come |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;To use Swiss chard for more than a year, the key is how to get through the winter. In central South Korea and further south, mounding soil around the base and covering with straw and fallen leaves usually lets it survive. North of central South Korea it can fail, and in that case the plan is changed to sowing in autumn and harvesting before winter.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Plants that overwinter successfully put out leaves again in spring, but they do not last long. The winter cold acts as vernalization, so the flower stalk soon rises in spring. The spring harvest from overwintered plants should therefore be regarded as a bonus of a few weeks, and the right course is to collect seed from those plants and then sow anew.

## 9. Pests and Diseases - Preventing Them Without Chemicals

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The phrase "pest control almost unnecessary," written in the basis for the Pest-control self-reliance grade in the evaluation table, is the summary of this section. Swiss chard is among the easiest leafy vegetables to grow without pesticides. The reason lies in the family it belongs to.

| Crop group | Main pests | Does it come to Swiss chard? |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Cabbage family | Cabbage white butterfly, diamondback moth, flea beetles, clubroot | It does not come |
| Daisy family | Sclerotinia rot, downy mildew | It does not come |
| Nightshade family | Late blight, tobacco budworm | It does not come |
| Amaranth family (Swiss chard itself) | Aphids, spider mites, leaf beetles | It comes |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The greatest pest and disease pressure in the field comes from the cabbage family and the daisy family, because gimjang (making kimchi for the winter) vegetables and leafy vegetables are concentrated in those two groups. Swiss chard belongs to neither, so it stands outside that pressure. Even in a year when napa cabbage is eaten by insects, Swiss chard is unharmed.

### 9-1. Major Diseases

| Disease | Symptoms | Conditions | Response without chemicals |
|:---|:---|:---|:---|
| Cercospora leaf spot (brown spot) | Brown round spots form on the leaves, with the centers turning gray | Hot and humid | Remove diseased leaves, ventilation, rotation |
| Downy mildew | White mold on the underside of the leaf, yellowing on the upper surface | Cool and damp | Ventilation, morning watering, remove diseased leaves |
| Powdery mildew | White powder on the leaf surface | Dry, with large day-night temperature swings | Ventilation and sunlight, diluted milk |
| Root rot | The base turns brown and the plant wilts | Excess moisture, poor drainage | Raise the beds, drainage |
| Damping-off | Young seedlings pinch in at the base and fall over | Excess moisture, dense sowing | Drainage, thinning, hot-water dip |
| Yellows | Leaves turn yellow and the veins stand out | Continuous cropping, virus | Aphid control, rotation, remove infected plants |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The most common disease of Swiss chard is Cercospora leaf spot. In the summer monsoon season (jangma, late June to July in South Korea), brown spots appear on the leaves, and in severe cases the whole leaf dries up. This disease only costs a few leaves for that year, however, and does not kill the plant. If diseased leaves are removed and carried out of the field, the spread slows, and the new leaves that come afterward are healthy. The continuous-picking way of harvesting thus doubles as control of this disease.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Yellows calls for more care. It is caused by a virus and spread by aphids. An infected plant does not recover, so it is pulled up and destroyed, and reducing the aphid population is the only response. Beetroot and sugar beet catch the same virus, so it can be carried over from them.

> **Most diseases of Swiss chard stop at costing it leaves and do not kill the plant. This is the fundamental reason Swiss chard is strong in farming without chemicals. When diseased leaves are removed, new leaves come up, and that repetition is itself pest control.**

### 9-2. Major Pests

| Pest | Damage | Control without chemicals |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Aphids | Suck sap from the underside of leaves and transmit viruses | Wash off with a strong jet of water, soapy water, protecting natural enemies |
| Spider mites | Suck sap from the underside of leaves and spin webs | Spraying water, raising humidity |
| Swiss chard leaf beetles | Gnaw the leaves | Hand-picking |
| Leaf miners | Burrow inside the leaf and leave white streaks | Remove damaged leaves |
| Slugs | Make holes in leaves | Catching at night, a band of ash or rice hulls |
| Cutworm larvae | Cut off the base of young seedlings | Finding them in the soil at night and catching them |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Aphids are the most common pest of Swiss chard. Like spider mites, they multiply in dry conditions, so watering well and occasionally spraying water hard on the underside of the leaves brings a good share of them under control. Spider mites especially like dryness, so spraying water is itself control.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Leaf miner larvae burrow into the leaf tissue and leave white streaks. They are inside the leaf, so nothing sprayed on the outside reaches them, which makes them a difficult pest even when chemicals are available. The only response is to pick the leaves with visible streaks and carry them out of the field. For Swiss chard this is no great burden. It is a crop whose leaves are picked and eaten anyway, and selecting and removing damaged leaves overlaps with harvesting.

### 9-3. Natural Control Agents and Cultural Control

| Agent | Preparation | Target |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Soapy water | Dissolve 20g of unscented soap in 1L of water | Aphids, spider mites |
| Diluted milk | Mix milk and water 1 to 9 | Powdery mildew |
| Wood ash dusting | Sift ash and scatter it over the leaves and field | Repels pests, supplies potassium |
| Wood ash band | Scatter dry ash around the plants | Blocks slugs |
| Wood vinegar | Dilute 300-500 times and spray | Repellent effect |
| Strong jet of water | Spray on the underside of leaves | Aphids, spider mites |

| Principle | Details |
|:---|:---|
| Rotation | Group it with spinach, beetroot and sugar beet and rest the plot for 2 years or more |
| Ventilation | Secure spacing by thinning |
| Drainage | Build raised beds. The fundamental remedy for root rot |
| Watering time | Water in the early morning so the leaves dry during the day |
| Mulching | Prevents rain splash and cuts the soil-borne route of infection |
| Residue disposal | Carry diseased leaves out of the field or burn them |
| Healthy seed | Hot-water dipping reduces seed-borne infection |
| Control combined with harvesting | Pick diseased leaves and damaged leaves first |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The last item is a control method unique to Swiss chard. Because it is a crop that is picked continuously, deciding the order in which leaves are picked is itself pest control. Leaves with disease spots, leaves with visible leaf miner streaks and leaves with aphids on them are picked and dealt with first. What can be eaten is washed and eaten, and what cannot is carried out of the field. Harvesting and pest control are finished in one go, without setting aside separate time.

## 10. Nutrient Deficiency and Excess

| Symptom | Cause | Remedy with natural materials |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Whole leaves yellow and plants small | Nitrogen deficiency | Well-rotted compost, urine diluted 10-20 times |
| Leaf edges dry brown | Potassium deficiency | A small amount of wood ash |
| Growth is poor and leaves take on a purplish tint | Phosphate deficiency | Bone meal, wood ash. The same symptom appears when the soil temperature is low |
| Yellowing between the veins | Magnesium deficiency | Increase compost |
| New leaves twist and split and the growing point turns black | Boron deficiency | Dried seaweed, well-rotted compost. Cut back on lime |
| Leaves small and thin | Shortage of water and nutrients | Increase watering and fertilization together |
| Leaves overly soft and aphids gather | Nitrogen excess | Stop supplying nitrogen |
| Leaf tips dry and growth stops | Excess salinity | Water generously to wash the salt down |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The deficiency to look at as characteristic of Swiss chard is boron deficiency. Plants of the genus Beta have a high boron requirement, so this symptom, which rarely appears in other crops, actually occurs in Swiss chard. Newly emerging leaves shrivel and twist, the tissue between the veins splits, and in severe cases the area near the growing point dies and turns black. When the growing point dies, that plant is finished, so this is the most fatal of the deficiencies.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Boron runs short under two conditions: when it is washed out by rain in sandy ground, and when heavy applications of lime or wood ash raise the pH. The latter is more common. Even when boron is in the soil, under alkaline conditions it changes into a form the plant cannot absorb. Wood ash is an easy source of potassium, but a large amount at one time invites this problem, so for Swiss chard in particular it is applied in small portions. Dried seaweed contains boron and serves as a supplement.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Excess salinity in the last row is a condition that would kill other crops. Swiss chard tolerates quite high salinity, but not without limit, and beyond that limit the leaf tips dry and growth stops. The response is to water generously and wash the salt around the roots downward. Left dry, the salt becomes concentrated near the roots, so things actually get worse. This is why saving water is not always right when growing Swiss chard on salt-affected land.

## 11. Intercropping and Crop Rotation

| Compatibility | Partner | Effect |
|:---:|:---|:---|
| Good | Legumes (pea, kidney bean, cowpea) | They fix nitrogen and leave it behind |
| Good | Lettuce, garland chrysanthemum and other daisy-family crops | They do not share pests and diseases |
| Good | Radish, napa cabbage and other cabbage-family crops | They do not share pests and diseases |
| Good | Welsh onion, scallion, garlic | The smell confuses pests |
| Good | Tall crops such as maize | They provide partial shade in summer |
| Bad | Spinach, beetroot, sugar beet | Same family or same species, so they share diseases |
| Bad | Continuous cropping of Swiss chard | Pathogens build up |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The reason Swiss chard intercrops so well is simple. Crops of the goosefoot family, that is, the amaranth family, are uncommon in the field, so it does not share pests and diseases with most crops. Planted beside the cabbage family or beside the daisy family, neither is the worse for it. When laying out a field, Swiss chard is a piece that fits safely anywhere.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The only things to avoid are spinach, beetroot and sugar beet. Beetroot and sugar beet in particular are the same species as Swiss chard, so they share pests and diseases completely, and the seed-saving problem seen later is added on top. Treat these three as one group: do not put them in the same spot in the same year, and count them together in rotation.

| Example rotation | Year | Crop | What happens in the field |
|:---|:---:|:---|:---|
| 3-year cycle | Year 1 | Amaranth family such as Swiss chard | Pathogens begin to build up |
| | Year 2 | Legumes (soybean, pea, cowpea) | Nitrogen is fixed and accumulates |
| | Year 3 | Cabbage family or grasses | The amaranth-family pathogens starve |
| | Year 1 again | Swiss chard | Starts with a lower pathogen density |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The standard of A for Continuous-cropping tolerance, a 2-year rotation break, is more lenient than the 2-3 years of lettuce and iceberg lettuce. If the field is small and strict rotation is difficult, merely moving the planting spot makes a difference at the very least.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Placing Swiss chard after a legume suits it especially well. Swiss chard likes nitrogen and uses it steadily as it keeps producing leaves, and the nitrogen left by the legume meets that demand. In iceberg lettuce, the nitrogen left by a legume interferes with heading and becomes a problem instead, but in Swiss chard, which does not form heads, there is no such side effect. With plenty of nitrogen the leaves grow large, and that is the yield.

## 12. Harvest - The Technique of Continuous Picking

| Use | After sowing | Condition |
|:---|:---:|:---|
| Baby leaves from 1st thinning | 25-30 days | 2-3 true leaves. Salad or geotjeori (fresh, lightly seasoned salad) |
| 2nd thinning | 35-40 days | 4-5 true leaves. Blanched, as namul (seasoned vegetable side dish) |
| First harvest | 40-60 days | Outer leaves 20-30cm long |
| Repeated harvest | Continuously thereafter | 1-2 times a week |
| Winter harvest | November to March of the following year (South Korea) | It does not grow, so pick the existing leaves one by one |

### 12-1. Why Pick from the Outside

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Swiss chard has just one growing point, at the top of the compressed short stem, in the very middle of the plant. New leaves keep rising from it and grow as they are pushed outward. So when the fully grown outer leaves are picked, all the plant loses is old leaves whose photosynthetic ability has already declined, and the growing point stays intact and keeps making new leaves.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Conversely, if the center is cut off, that plant is finished. Without the growing point, there is no longer any place to make leaves. Harvesting Swiss chard by pulling it up by the roots or cutting the whole base is done only when you have decided to give up on that plant.

| Item | Tip |
|:---|:---|
| Leaves to pick | Fully grown leaves, starting from the outside |
| Where to cut | Cut leaving 3-5cm where the leaf stalk meets the stem |
| Amount per picking | One-third to no more than half of all the leaves |
| Leaves to leave | Always at least 6 center leaves |
| Tool | Cut with a knife or scissors rather than tearing by hand |
| Time of day | Early morning. Having taken up moisture overnight, the leaves are at their freshest |
| Harvest interval | 1-2 times a week |
| Afterward | Water |

> **Always leave at least six center leaves. The leaves left are the photosynthesis factory, and that factory makes the next leaves. If you get greedy and pick them all, that plant simply stops.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There is a reason for cutting while leaving 3-5cm of leaf stalk. If the cut is made tight against the stem, a wound forms close to the growing point and pathogens enter through it. If a little is left, the remaining leaf stalk dries and drops off by itself and the wound heals. The advice not to tear by hand has the same reason: tearing rips the leaf stalk from the stem and leaves a long lengthwise wound.

### 12-2. How Much Comes from One Plant

| Item | Value |
|:---|:---|
| Monthly yield per plant | 1-1.5kg |
| Harvest period | From May to October for a spring sowing (in South Korea); through to the following spring if overwintered |
| Plants needed | 5-10 plants per adult |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The figure of 1-1.5kg a month from one plant shows Swiss chard's real productive capacity. The leaf stalks are thick and juicy, so they weigh a lot. Compared with lettuce, of which only the leaf is eaten, the weight is greater for the same volume.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Reading the A for Perenniality and the S for Speed to harvest in the evaluation table together reveals Swiss chard's timetable. The first harvest comes 50-60 days after sowing, and after that it continues until frost arrives. If spring-sown Swiss chard lasts until October (in South Korea), that amounts to five months of harvest, and if it overwinters successfully, longer still. The total obtained from a single sowing is on a different scale from lettuce.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Using the leaf stalks separately also contributes to the yield. Separating the leaves from the stalks gives two ingredients, and because they are cooked differently, it feels less like eating the same thing. In a situation where it has to be eaten every day, this difference really counts.
