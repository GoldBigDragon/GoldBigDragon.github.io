## 13. Seed Saving - A Biennial Crop and Wind Pollination

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In the evaluation table, Swiss chard's Seed saving grade is A, and the basis given is "outcrossing; beware of crossing with sugar beet and beet." This is where it parts ways with lettuce's S. Saving Swiss chard seed runs into two walls.

| Barrier | Details | Solution |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Biennial habit | It must go through cold before a flower stalk rises | Overwinter the plants, or lift and store them and replant in spring |
| Wind-borne outcrossing | Wind carries pollen far | Isolation distance, staggered cropping, bagging |

### 13-1. What Does It Cross With?

| Crop | Scientific name | Crosses with Swiss chard |
|:---|:---|:---:|
| Beet, sugar beet, fodder beet | Beta vulgaris | Crosses completely |
| Other Swiss chard varieties | Beta vulgaris subsp. cicla | Crosses completely |
| Wild beet | Beta vulgaris subsp.maritima | Crosses |
| Spinach | Spinacia oleracea | Different genus, so no crossing |
| Goosefoot, amaranth | | No crossing |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;If beet flowers next to Swiss chard, the seed saved that year becomes a hybrid that is neither chard nor beet. What comes up has a root that is only half swollen and thin leaf stalks, of little use either way. The same goes for sugar beet.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;What makes the problem harder is the pollination method. Swiss chard is wind-pollinated: the wind carries its pollen. Insect pollination, with bees carrying the pollen, can be blocked by covering plants with netting, but wind is not so easy to handle. The pollen is light and small, and it travels from several hundred m to more than 1km. That is similar to, or more than, the 500m that kale requires and the 1km that radish requires.

| Method | Details | Practicality |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Isolation distance | At least 1,000m from flowering plants of any other Beta vulgaris | Difficult, because neighboring fields cannot be controlled |
| Staggered cropping | Only one kind is allowed to flower in a given year | The most practical |
| Bagging | Cover the flower stalks with a cloth bag before flowering | Reliable on a small scale |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The most practical solution is staggered cropping. This year, seed is saved only from Swiss chard, and all the beets are eaten so that they never flower. Next year, it is done the other way around. Because Swiss chard seed keeps for 5 years in sealed storage at room temperature, taking seed from one species per year in rotation never leaves a gap in the seed supply. This is where the Seed longevity grade of S offsets the difficulty of seed saving.

> **Saving Swiss chard seed is not a yearly chore but something done once every 4-5 years, and in that year it is done with proper isolation. The fact that the seed lasts 5 years is what makes this approach possible.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Bagging is the method for when the field is small and the neighbors are close. Before the flower buds form, the flower stalks of three or four plants chosen for seed are covered together with a loosely woven cloth bag to keep out outside pollen. However, Swiss chard is self-incompatible, meaning it does not fertilize well with its own pollen, so the flower stalks of several plants must be inside the bag together. If only one plant is covered, hardly any seed sets.

> **Keep at least 5 plants for seed, and preferably 10 or more. If seed is taken from only one or two plants of an outcrossing crop, inbreeding depression appears within a few generations and vigor declines.**

### 13-2. Overwintering and the Seed-Saving Procedure

| Step | Timing (South Korea) | Details |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Selecting seed plants | Autumn | Choose plants with large leaves, no disease and a clear variety color |
| Overwintering | November-February | Mound soil around the base and cover with straw and fallen leaves |
| Bolting | March-April | Flower stalks rise to more than 1m. Set up supports |
| Flowering | May-June | Small green flowers open in a spike shape. Keep the isolation in place at this time |
| Seed set | June-July | Seed clusters form |
| Harvest | July-August | When the seed clusters dry to brown, cut the stalks whole |
| After-ripening and threshing | Right after harvest | Dry in the shade for 1-2 weeks, then strip or rub off the seed |
| Cleaning and storage | After fully drying | Winnow off the chaff, then seal in a container and keep in a cool place |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In regions with harsh winters, such as central and northern South Korea, overwintering in the open field can fail. In that case, the plants are dug up with their roots before the first frost, with a little soil left clinging, buried in a root-cellar pit or a cool storeroom, and replanted the following spring. Conditions are kept at 0-5°C, cold enough that the plants do not freeze yet not warm enough for the buds to sprout. The cold the plants experience during storage is itself the vernalization, so plants that have gone through this process send up a flower stalk as soon as they are planted.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Swiss chard seed does not carry a pappus and blow away the way lettuce seed does. The seed clusters are heavy and attached firmly to the stalk, so they seldom fall on their own. This is a point where seed saving is easier to manage than with lettuce. However, if the stalks are left until fully dry, the lower clusters drop, so the stalks are cut whole when two-thirds of the clusters have turned brown and are left to after-ripen in the shade.

| Item | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Seed clusters per plant | Hundreds to thousands |
| Seeds per seed cluster | 2-5 |
| Seed longevity | 5 years sealed at room temperature |
| Storage | Airtight container, in a cool, dark, dry place |
| Desiccant | Roasted and cooled rice, charcoal, dry wood ash |
| Checking | Each year, place 20 seed clusters on a wet cloth and count how many germinate |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;A single plant produces hundreds to thousands of seed clusters, and each cluster holds several seeds, so saving seed from ten plants secures several years' worth of seed at once. Add the 5-year longevity, and a single seed-saving session lasts a long time.

## 14. Toxicity and Compounds of Concern - Oxalic Acid

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Swiss chard has no acute toxicity. Its oxalic acid content is high, however, so understanding this compound and handling it properly is part of the skill of using Swiss chard.

| Crop | Oxalic acid (approx. per 100g) |
|:---|:---|
| Swiss chard, spinach | Several hundred mg |
| Purslane | Several hundred mg |
| Kale | 20-50mg |
| Lettuce | Several tens of mg |

### 14-1. What Oxalic Acid Is and Why It Is a Problem

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Oxalic acid is an organic acid that plants make themselves. It serves several roles, one of which is to capture excess calcium inside cells and store it as crystals. For the plant, it is a useful device.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;When people eat it, two things happen. First, oxalic acid binds with the calcium in food to form calcium oxalate, which does not dissolve in water and is excreted without being absorbed. This is why Swiss chard, despite being rich in calcium, is hard to count as a calcium source. Iron is partly affected in the same way. Second, absorbed oxalic acid can combine with calcium in the kidneys and become the raw material of stones. This is the origin of the advice that people with a history of kidney stones should be careful with vegetables high in oxalic acid.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;But the size of the risk has to be judged accurately. For an ordinary person eating Swiss chard as a side dish, it is not a problem. Spinach and Swiss chard are vegetables people have eaten for a very long time, and the benefit of the vitamins and minerals gained from eating them outweighs the burden of oxalic acid. The situations to worry about are a history of kidney stones, and eating large amounts of Swiss chard or spinach every day with no other vegetables. In a disaster, the second condition can actually arise, so the rule is to eat a mix of vegetables.

### 14-2. Why Blanching Reduces It

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Oxalic acid exists in a water-soluble form and an insoluble form. The soluble form is the one that matters, and fortunately it leaches out during blanching.

| Treatment | Oxalic acid reduction | Notes |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Raw | None | |
| Blanched, water discarded | Substantial reduction | The most reliable method |
| Blanching water used in soup | No reduction | The oxalic acid is in the broth |
| Steaming | Slight reduction | There is nowhere for it to dissolve and leave |
| Stir-frying | Almost no reduction | |

> **The point is not the blanching itself but discarding the blanching water. Oxalic acid is not destroyed; it moves into the water. If that water is used in a soup, the oxalic acid comes along with it.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The Cooking & processing grade of A in the evaluation table, with the basis "blanching 5 minutes," refers to this procedure. These 5 minutes are the reason Swiss chard gets an A while lettuce gets an SS. Five minutes is short, though. Put the greens into boiling water and leave them until they wilt: 1 to 2 minutes for leaves, 3-5 minutes for thick leaf stalks. If the pot goes on a fire that is already boiling something else, almost no extra fuel is needed.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Blanching also leaches some of the vitamin C and folate into the water. In effect, some water-soluble vitamins are lost in exchange for lowering oxalic acid. So a balanced approach is to split the use: eat young leaves raw and mature leaves blanched. Young leaves have relatively less oxalic acid and are tender, which suits eating them raw.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Eating it together with calcium-rich foods is another countermeasure. If tofu, anchovies or fish eaten whole with the bones are served alongside, the oxalic acid binds with that calcium in the gut first and is excreted, so less oxalic acid is absorbed into the body. This trades away some of the food's calcium to reduce the oxalic acid that reaches the kidneys.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Growing conditions also affect the amount of oxalic acid. Leaves given a lot of nitrogen, leaves grown in hot weather, and old large leaves contain more. Young leaves from the cool season contain less. This is where the distinction comes from: summer chard is eaten blanched, while the young leaves of autumn chard are eaten raw.

## 15. Storage Without Refrigeration

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In the evaluation table, Storability is D: 1 week at room temperature with no power. That is better than lettuce at 2-3 days and on par with iceberg lettuce. This is thanks to the thick leaf stalks, which hold on to moisture.

| Method | Procedure | Duration | Limitation |
|:---|:---|:---:|:---|
| Remove moisture, then keep in a cool place | Do not wash; just shake off the soil | 3-5 days | Wilts if it dries out |
| Wrap in paper or cloth | Wrap in newspaper or a dry cloth and stand upright | 5-7 days | Goes soft if the cloth gets too wet |
| Stems in water | Stand the bases of the leaf stalks in water | 5-7 days | The water must be changed daily |
| Earth cellar or root-cellar pit | Stand upright in a cool underground space | 1-2 weeks | Requires a root-cellar pit |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The trick is to store Swiss chard standing upright. The leaf stalks are straight and thick, and when laid down they try to bend, which damages the tissue. Standing upright, they are spared that strain. The instruction to remove moisture before storing refers to wash water, not the moisture inside the leaves. Because water left on the surface after washing becomes the starting point of rot, wash it just before eating.

### 15-1. Mungnamul (Blanched and Dried Greens) - How Swiss Chard Is Stored

| Step | Method |
|:---|:---|
| Preparation | Separate the leaves from the leaf stalks |
| Blanching | In boiling water: 1-2 minutes for leaves, 3-5 minutes for leaf stalks |
| Rinsing | Rinse in cold water to cool |
| Squeezing | Squeeze firmly by hand |
| Spreading out | Spread out in a shaded, airy place or hang on a line |
| Drying fully | Until it crumbles by hand |
| Storage | In an airtight container with charcoal or roasted rice |
| Duration | 6 months to over 1 year |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Storing Swiss chard effectively comes down to one method, mungnamul. The process of blanching and drying does two jobs at once: it lowers oxalic acid and extends storage time. Because oxalic acid leaches into the water during blanching, dried chard is stored with less oxalic acid than raw chard.

> **When made into mungnamul, most of the vitamin C is lost, but vitamin A, vitamin K, minerals and dietary fiber remain. Winter vitamin C comes not from the dried product but from raw leaves still standing in the field or from fermented foods. Storage plans are made with this distinction in mind.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Thoroughly dried Swiss chard can be rubbed by hand into a powder and kept. Its volume shrinks greatly, which makes it easy to store, and a spoonful added to a soup or porridge brings in nutrients. Chard dried whole is soaked in water and then dressed as namul (seasoned vegetable side dish) or stir-fried. To soak it, leave it in lukewarm water for a couple of hours, then boil it once, and it becomes tender.

| Other methods | Details | Assessment |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Salting | Cure the leaf stalks in salt | Leaf stalks keep their shape when cured |
| Lactic fermentation | With 2-3% salt, like kimchi | A good share of the vitamin C remains |
| Jangajji (pickled vegetables in soy sauce or paste) | Cure the leaf stalks, then pack them in doenjang (Korean soybean paste) or soy sauce | Keeps for several months |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The thickness of the leaf stalks works in its favor for storage. Leaves go limp when cured, but the stalks stay crisp, so jangajji and brined pickles work. Separating leaves from stalks and storing them by different methods is the knack of using Swiss chard. Leaves are blanched and dried; stalks are brined or made into jangajji.

## 16. Cooking and Processing

| Method | Fuel | Vitamin C retention | Oxalic acid |
|:---|:---:|:---:|:---|
| Raw young leaves | None | Maximum | Not reduced, but young leaves have little |
| Blanch and dress | Little | Half or less | Substantially reduced |
| Added to soup | Shared with other cooking | Retained if the broth is eaten too | Not reduced |
| Stir-frying | Little | Medium | Not reduced |
| Lactic fermentation | None | A good amount kept | Slightly reduced |
| Dry and powder | Sun and wind for drying | Almost none | Reduced if blanched before drying |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The basis of cooking Swiss chard is to separate the leaves from the leaf stalks. Their thickness differs, so they cook in different times. Put in together, the leaves turn mushy and the stalks stay underdone. Add the stalks first and the leaves a little later, and both cook just right.

| Part | Character | Use |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Young leaves | Tender and low in oxalic acid | Raw, geotjeori (fresh, unfermented seasoned salad), salad |
| Mature leaves | Thick and high in oxalic acid | Blanched and dressed, soup, mungnamul |
| Leaf stalks | Crisp and juicy | Stir-fry, jangajji, brined pickles |
| Thick midribs | Tough | Chopped fine into soup or porridge |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In South Korea, Swiss chard has long been used on the dinner table as an ingredient in doenjang soup. Putting it in doenjang soup also makes sense in a disaster. It goes into a pot that is already on the fire, so no extra fuel is needed; the saltiness and umami of doenjang make up for chard's blandness; and the protein in doenjang and the micronutrients of chard complement each other. However, eating it with the broth brings the oxalic acid along too, so rather than eating only soup every day, it is better balanced to alternate with the cooking method of blanching and discarding the water.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Mixing in a little oil is nutritionally advantageous. The vitamin A, vitamin K and vitamin E in Swiss chard are fat-soluble and need oil to be absorbed. Adding ground perilla or sesame seeds, or putting the chard in a broth made from ground soybeans, has the same effect. The long-standing practice of dressing chard namul with perilla seed powder is not only a matter of taste.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;As the composition value of 0.00g sugars indicates, Swiss chard has no sweetness and only a faint aroma. That means it is bland, but in a disaster this is actually a strength. Strongly flavored vegetables become tiresome in a few days, whereas a bland one serves as a base that accepts other flavors. It goes with doenjang, soy sauce, perilla, chili pepper or garlic alike, and does not grow tiresome even when eaten every day. This is where the Taste grade of S comes from.

## 17. Annual Work Calendar

| Month (South Korea) | Work |
|:---|:---|
| January | Harvest winter leaves (overwintered plants). Check seed and plan the field |
| February | Turn the compost. Prepare base fertilizer |
| March | Spring sowing from mid-month. Base fertilizer and forming raised beds. Seed plants begin to bolt |
| April | First thinning (harvest young leaves). Weeding. Supports for seed plants |
| May | Second thinning. First harvest begins. Seed plants flower; manage isolation |
| June | Full-scale repeated harvest. Summer sowing (optional). Seed plants set seed |
| July | Repeated harvest. Drainage management in the monsoon season (jangma, late June to July in South Korea). Seed harvest from seed plants |
| August | Repeated harvest. Autumn sowing from late in the month. Scout for brown spot |
| September | Thin the autumn sowing. Keep harvesting the spring plants |
| October | Peak harvest. Leaves become tender. Make mungnamul |
| November | Harvest continues. Select seed plants. Prepare for winter (hilling, straw cover) |
| December | Harvest remaining leaves one by one. Check stored goods |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;What this table shows is that there are no blanks. Of the twelve months, February is the only one with no harvest listed, and even that gets filled if overwintered plants have survived. Compared with lettuce, which reaches the table for only four months, and iceberg lettuce, for only one or two, Swiss chard's place becomes clear.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In South Korea, October is the month when the leaves are at their best. As temperatures fall, the leaves become tender, oxalic acid decreases and a little sweetness rises. After blanching tough leaves all summer, by autumn they become good enough to eat raw. This is also a good time to make mungnamul. There are many leaves, and the dry weather helps them dry well.

| Category | Spring sowing | Late-summer sowing |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Sowing | Mid-March to early May (in South Korea) | Late August to early September (in South Korea) |
| First harvest | Mid-May | Mid-October |
| Harvest period | May-October | October-November; the following spring if overwintered |
| Leaf quality | Turns tough in summer | Tender to the end |
| Bolting risk | Early sowing can bolt the same year from cold | Low |
| Seed saving | Overwinter and save the following year | Overwinter and save the following year |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Sowing twice, in spring and in late summer, means harvesting in effect all year round. By the time the spring sowing's leaves turn tough in the summer heat, the late-summer sowing is growing up, and those plants produce the tender leaves of autumn. A few of them are overwintered and used as seed plants the following year. Once this cycle is established, Swiss chard keeps running in a corner of the field with almost no attention.

> **The reason to plant Swiss chard is not any single showy criterion. It is that it does not collapse under heat, cold, pests and diseases, or salt; that once planted it is harvested for a long time; that its seed lasts 5 years; and that it does not become tiresome even when eaten every day. This quality of never being the worst under any condition shines more the longer a disaster drags on.**
