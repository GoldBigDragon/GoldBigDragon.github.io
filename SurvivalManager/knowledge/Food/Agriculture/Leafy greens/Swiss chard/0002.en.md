## 1. Disaster-Preparedness Evaluation

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Swiss chard's Overall grade is D, with a score of 39.4 points. Broken down by scenario, it is Short-term D (39.9), Long-term D (39.0) and Supplementary SS (64.4). The gap between Short-term and Long-term is less than 1 point. This small gap shows the character of Swiss chard as a crop exactly: it is a crop that performs at almost the same value whether the horizon is short or long.

### 1-3. What the SS for Temperature range Means

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The only SS among the environment criteria in this table is Temperature range. Growth 5-30°C, range 25°C. Placing these numbers beside other leafy vegetables shows their weight.

| Crop | Growth temperature | Range | Grade |
|:---|:---|:---:|:---:|
| Swiss chard | 5-30°C | 25°C | SS |
| Kale | 5-30°C | 25°C | SS |
| Lettuce | 15-22°C | 10°C | C |
| Iceberg lettuce | Heading 15-20°C | 8°C | D |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;A wide range means two things. First, the window in which it can be planted gets longer. While lettuce is confined to two stretches, spring and autumn, and is harvested for four months of the year, Swiss chard continues from March to October (in South Korea). Second, it holds up when that year's weather goes off course. Even if spring warms up early or autumn stays warm late, Swiss chard keeps growing. If farming has to be done in a disaster with neither weather forecasts nor facilities, it is a hugely important trait that something remains even when the weather goes off course.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The Cold hardiness evaluation of A, maintaining growth down to minus 5°C, points in the same direction. It does not die when hit by frost, and in central South Korea and further south it overwinters if covered with straw. It stops growing in winter but stays alive and puts out leaves again in spring. A field that is not empty in winter means less reliance on storage.

> **The D for Storability is a value for after the leaves are picked, and Swiss chard standing in the field does not enter that calculation. If only as much as needed is picked on the day, there is nothing to store. For Swiss chard, the D for Storability is in practice the least important criterion.**

### 1-4. How to Read the C for Pest-control self-reliance

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;One cell in the evaluation table that needs care in interpretation is the C for Pest-control self-reliance. The grade looks low, yet the basis column says "pest control almost unnecessary." It looks like a contradiction, but it is not. This grade is structured so that a high score requires documented evidence from organic cultivation, and since Swiss chard has few pest and disease problems to begin with, such literature has never accumulated. The reliability of the basis for the yield loss is low, so it was excluded along with it, and the result is C.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This C therefore does not mean it is hard to grow without pesticides; it means supporting data are lacking. In actual growing experience, Swiss chard is among the leafy vegetables whose pests and diseases are easiest to manage. The cabbage white butterfly and flea beetles, which devastate crops of the cabbage family (Brassicaceae), do not come to Swiss chard. Being in the goosefoot family (Chenopodiaceae), it does not share diseases with the daisy family (Asteraceae) or the cabbage family. Aphids and spider mites do settle on it, but a jet of water and soapy water deal with those.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The A for Continuous-cropping tolerance, with a 2-year rotation break, is also worth pointing out. It is more lenient than lettuce and iceberg lettuce, which get B with 2-3 years. However, rotation must be calculated together with spinach, beetroot and sugar beet, which belong to the same goosefoot family. Diseases are shared among them, so they are treated as one group and the planting site is moved.

### 1-5. Summary of Strengths and Weaknesses

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;SS went to three criteria — Protein density, Infrastructure needs and Temperature range — and S went to five: Micronutrients, Speed to harvest, Taste, Prevalence in South Korea and Seed longevity. Of these, the one of particular value in a disaster is the S for Seed longevity.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Five years sealed at room temperature is long for a leafy vegetable. Compared with the 2-3 years of lettuce and iceberg lettuce, it is nearly double. Swiss chard seed comes as a seed cluster with a thick, hard shell, so it resists oxidation and moisture. What this means is that seed need not be saved often. Swiss chard is a biennial, which makes seed saving a bother, but fortunately that bother needs to be gone through only once in 5 years. The difficulty of seed saving and the length of seed life offset each other.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Micronutrients is S: 17 of 21 nutrients, more than lettuce's 16, and the amount needed to fill 10% of the RDA, 11.7kg, is similar to lettuce's 10.5kg. It supplies vitamins A, C, E and K, thiamin, riboflavin, pantothenic acid and folate across the board. It stands in the same place as lettuce and kale in being a crop that fills the slot that is first to empty in a diet built on grains and tuber crops (tubers/storage roots) for calories.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The heaviest weakness is Calories per area, rated D. At 18kcal per 100g and 720kcal per m², it is 0.28 times the self-sufficiency line. This is slightly better than lettuce's 0.23 times, but it is the same in that it cannot serve as a source of calories. The reason for planting Swiss chard is not to fill the stomach.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The A for Seed saving is covered in detail in Section 13 later. It is cross-pollinated and wind-pollinated, and since beetroot and sugar beet are the same species, they cross. As a biennial, it flowers only after getting through a winter. Compared with lettuce's S for self-pollination, this is clearly a disadvantaged position.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The A for Cooking & processing, blanching 5 minutes, is also a point where it parts company with lettuce's SS. Because oxalic acid makes it hard to eat raw in large amounts, a blanching step is needed, and that takes fuel. Still, blanching takes only 5 minutes, which is of a different order from boiling soybeans for an hour, and if the pot is placed on a fire that is boiling something else, almost no extra fuel is needed.
