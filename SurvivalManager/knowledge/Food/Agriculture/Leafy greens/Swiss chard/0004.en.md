## 5. Field Preparation and Fertilizer

### 5-1. Preparing the Field

| Item | Standard | Reason |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Tillage depth | 20-30cm | The straight root goes deep |
| Bed height | 15-20cm | Prevents excess moisture |
| Bed width | 90-120cm | Fit it to the row spacing |
| Drainage ditch | Provide one | Prevents root rot in the monsoon season (jangma, late June to July in South Korea) |
| Organic matter | Plenty | Because the crop is harvested for a long time, soil fertility has to support it |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;What needs special attention in a Swiss chard field is depth. The straight root of Swiss chard goes down more than 30cm, and this deep root is the source of its strength in withstanding drought and of its ability to draw nutrients from poor soil. If there is a hard layer, the root bends sideways and that ability is reduced. Turning the soil deeply once with a spade makes the following years easier.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;On salt-affected land, the layout is different. As water evaporates, salt rises and collects on the bed surface and the shoulders. So if planting is done not on top of the bed but low on the side of the bed or close to the furrow, there is less salt where the roots reach. Because rain washes salt downward, keeping the soil well drained makes conditions better year by year. Covering thickly with rice straw or dry grass to reduce evaporation slows the very rate at which salt rises to the surface.

### 5-2. Fertilizer Amounts

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The Fertilizer demand grade in the evaluation table is B, with N, P and K together at about 22g per m². This is the same level as lettuce.

| Fertilizer | Per 1,000m² | Per m² | Notes |
|:---|:---:|:---:|:---|
| Nitrogen (N) | 8-10kg | 8-10g | Mostly as base fertilizer |
| Phosphate (P) | 6-8kg | 6-8g | All as base fertilizer |
| Potassium (K) | 6-8kg | 6-8g | All as base fertilizer |
| Compost | 1,000-1,500kg | 1-1.5kg | Well-rotted compost |

| Category | Timing | Details |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Base fertilizer | 10 days before sowing | All of the compost, all of the phosphate and potassium, nitrogen |
| Top-dressing | Mostly omitted | Only a small amount when growth is poor or when harvesting is carried on for a long time |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Swiss chard is classed as a crop that needs almost no top-dressing. If the base fertilizer is applied properly, that is generally enough. This rule comes with a caveat, however. If the crop is harvested over several months by continuous picking, nutrients are drawn out of the soil accordingly. Nitrogen leaves the field in proportion to the weight of the leaves picked.

> **When Swiss chard needs top-dressing is shown not by a fixed date but by the condition of the leaves. When the newly emerging leaves become small and pale, the nutrients have run out. Adding dilute fertilizer at that point makes them grow large again. If you can read this signal, Swiss chard can be harvested all year round.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The trace element to watch in Swiss chard is boron. Plants of the genus Beta have a relatively high boron requirement. When boron is short, the tissue between the leaf veins splits, new leaves become twisted, and in severe cases the area near the growing point dies and turns black. Boron runs short under two conditions: when it is washed out by rain in sandy ground, and when heavy liming raises the pH and converts boron to a form that cannot be absorbed. Among natural materials, well-rotted compost and materials from the sea, namely dried seaweed worked into the soil, contain boron. The prevention is not to apply a large amount of lime at one time.

### 5-3. Fertilizer Substitutes for a Disaster

| Material | Nutrients supplied | How to use | Caution |
|:---|:---|:---|:---|
| Wood ash | Potassium, phosphate, calcium | Mix a handful into the soil per plant | It is alkaline, so too much invites boron deficiency |
| Well-rotted compost | General | Plenty as base fertilizer | Poorly decomposed compost damages roots |
| Diluted urine | Nitrogen, potassium | Dilute 10-20 times with water and apply between plants | Scorches the leaves if it touches them |
| Bone meal | Phosphate, calcium | Mix into the soil as base fertilizer | Acts slowly |
| Dried seaweed | Potassium, boron, trace elements | Rinse in water to remove salt, then mix into compost | Swiss chard tolerates it even unrinsed |
| Sesame/perilla oil cake | Nitrogen, phosphate | Ferment first, then use as base fertilizer | Raw cake burns roots with fermentation heat |
| Powdered eggshell | Calcium | Mix into base fertilizer | Must be ground fine to decompose |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Dried seaweed is a material that suits Swiss chard especially well. It is rich in potassium and trace elements and also contains boron. For most crops it has to be rinsed in water because of its salt, but Swiss chard is salt-tolerant, so that burden is lighter. If seaweed washed ashore can be gathered and used in an area near the coast, it is the fertilizer best suited to Swiss chard.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The Yield retention without synthetic inputs in the evaluation table is C, 40.8%. It means that yield falls sharply when the crop is grown without chemical fertilizer. In Swiss chard this drop appears not as plants dying but as leaves becoming smaller and growth slowing. It differs from iceberg lettuce in that it does not lead to total loss. If only natural materials are used, allow for the reduced harvest by enlarging the area or increasing the number of plants.

## 6. Sowing - Seed Clusters and Thinning

### 6-1. Cropping Types

| Cropping type | Sowing (South Korea) | Harvest (South Korea) | Notes |
|:---|:---|:---|:---|
| Spring crop | Mid-March to early May | Continuously from mid-May | The most common |
| Summer crop | June-July | August-September | Relatively strong in high heat |
| Autumn crop | August-September | October-November | The leaves are tender |
| Overwintering crop | September-October | Following spring | Central South Korea and south. Bolts the following year |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;That the cropping table for Swiss chard has almost no blank cells is the character of this crop. Unlike lettuce, which is confined to two stretches, spring and autumn, and iceberg lettuce, whose window is narrower still, Swiss chard grows whenever it is sown from March to October (in South Korea). Sow once in spring and once in late summer, and you are in effect harvesting all year round.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Sowing very early in spring does call for caution, however. If young seedlings go through a long spell of cold below 10°C, vernalization occurs and the flower stalk rises within that same year. A biennial behaves like an annual, and once the flower stalk rises the leaves turn tough and the harvest ends. Sow after the last frost has fully passed, or, if sowing early, cover with straw to shield the plants from the cold.

### 6-2. The Seed Ball, an Unusual Seed

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;A Swiss chard "seed" is not a single seed. Several seeds are wrapped in calyx tissue and fused into one lump, and this is called a seed ball or seed cluster. From the outside it looks like one bumpy brown granule, but inside it are two to five seeds.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This structure has two consequences for cultivation.

| Consequence | Details |
|:---|:---|
| Several seedlings from one spot | Sowing one seed ball produces several seedlings. Thinning is essential |
| Germination-inhibiting substance | The hull contains a substance that inhibits germination, so soaking in water improves germination |

> **For Swiss chard, thinning is not optional but essential. Three or four seedlings come up from one seed ball, so if left alone, several plants in one spot crowd each other as they grow. All of them become small and weak, and none grows properly.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The germination-inhibiting substance in the hull is originally a device that keeps the seeds in the wild from all germinating at once. If every seed sprouted after a single rain, they would all be wiped out when drought followed, so germination is regulated to occur only after repeated washing by water. In cultivation this device becomes a cause of delayed germination, so the seeds are soaked in water beforehand to wash the inhibitor out.

| Pretreatment | Method | Effect |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Soaking in water | 2-3 hours, or overnight at most | The inhibitor is washed out and germination is faster |
| Changing the water | Change the water once or twice during soaking | Throws away the substance that was washed out |
| Hot-water dip | 10-15 minutes in 50-55°C water | Kills pathogens on the seed surface |
| Coating with ash | After soaking, coat with dry wood ash | Disinfects and dries at once, and makes the seed easier to handle |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Soaked seed dies if it dries out, so after treatment sow it right away and keep the soil from drying. Even without a seed disinfectant, Swiss chard tends to have a high germination rate, so this is not a big problem.

### 6-3. Sowing and Thinning

| Item | Standard | Notes |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Sowing depth | 1-2.5cm | The seed ball is large, so bury it deeper than lettuce |
| Row spacing | 30-50cm | |
| Plant spacing (final) | 10-15cm | 20cm to grow large plants |
| Sowing method | Drill sowing or spot sowing | |
| Seeding rate | 2-3 seed balls per spot for spot sowing | One seed ball holds several seeds, so this is ample |
| Germination period | 7-14 days | Slower than lettuce |

| Thinning round | Timing | Spacing left | What is removed |
|:---|:---|:---:|:---|
| 1st | 2-3 true leaves | 5cm | Eaten as baby leaves |
| 2nd | 4-5 true leaves | 10-15cm | Eaten as namul (seasoned vegetable side dish) |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The trick is not to throw away what is thinned out. The young shoots from the first thinning can go straight into salads or geotjeori (fresh, lightly seasoned salad), and those from the second thinning are blanched and eaten as namul. The first thinning comes one month after sowing, so there is already something to eat well before the regular harvest at 40-60 days. If you sow at the start of a disaster, with nothing in the field, this difference in time matters.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;When thinning, it is better not to pull but to cut with scissors close to the ground. Pulling shakes even the roots of the plant to be left beside it. This is especially so because seedlings from a seed ball have intertwined roots.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;What final spacing to choose depends on what you want. Narrowing it to 10cm gives many small leaves; widening it to 20cm gives large leaves and thick leaf stalks. If you plan to keep picking and grow the plants for a long time, it is better to set the spacing wide, because each plant has to live long, and for that it needs room for its roots to spread.

## 7. Water Management

| Period | Water management | Reason |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Sowing to germination | Keep the soil from drying out | The seed ball must take up enough water to germinate |
| Seedling stage | When the topsoil dries | Guides the roots downward |
| Growth period | Thoroughly when the topsoil dries | Occasional soaking is better than frequent small amounts |
| Repeated harvest period | Water after picking leaves | Making new leaves takes water |
| Monsoon season | Maintain drainage ditches | Excess moisture causes root rot |
| Winter | Almost none | It does not grow, so the need is small |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Watering heavily but occasionally suits Swiss chard. If small amounts are given often, the water stays only in the topsoil and the roots crowd near the surface, and then the deep root that is Swiss chard's strength never forms. If enough is given at one time to soak in deeply and then an interval is left before the next, the roots follow the water downward. Plants grown that way withstand drought.

| Symptom | Cause | Response |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Wilts by day, recovers in the evening | Temporary shortage | Within the normal range |
| Does not recover even in the evening | Severe shortage | Water thoroughly at once |
| Leaves small and tough | Continuous water shortage | Increase watering and mulch |
| Lower leaves turn yellow and the base turns soft | Excess moisture | Open the drainage ditch and stop watering |
| Leaf stalks split | A lot of water suddenly after drying out | Even out the watering intervals |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Lengthwise splitting of leaf stalks appears when the water supply is uneven. If the soil stays dry for a long time and then a heavy rain comes, the cells inside the thick leaf stalk swell suddenly and push the outer layer until it bursts. It does not interfere with eating, but pathogens can enter through the split, so split leaf stalks are picked and eaten first. Mulching keeps moisture even and greatly reduces this problem.

| Water-saving method | Effect |
|:---|:---|
| Mulching with straw and dry grass | Greatly reduces evaporation. On salt-affected land, it also slows the rise of salt |
| Early-morning watering | Avoids midday evaporation, and the leaves dry out during the day |
| Deep tillage | Roots go deep and use water from below |
| Partial-shade placement | Less evaporation in summer and more tender leaves |
| Furrow irrigation | Does not wet the leaves, which reduces disease |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There is one more reason mulching is especially important for Swiss chard. On salt-affected land, thickly covering the soil with straw or dry grass reduces evaporation from the soil surface, and when evaporation is reduced, the rate at which salt rises slows. It is the cheapest means of easing salt damage and also a means of saving water.
