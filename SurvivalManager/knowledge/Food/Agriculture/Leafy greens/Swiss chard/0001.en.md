## Introduction

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Swiss chard (Beta vulgaris subsp. cicla) is a leafy vegetable of the goosefoot family (Chenopodiaceae), which current classification places in the amaranth family (Amaranthaceae). The first part of its scientific name is exactly the same as that of beetroot and sugar beet. The three are different subspecies of one species, and they cross with each other. What differs is the part that people have bred up. Beetroot and sugar beet were bred to swell the root, while Swiss chard was bred to grow leaves and leaf stalks. The thin, tough root of Swiss chard is not a degeneration; it is simply the result of never having been selected in that direction.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This origin explains one of Swiss chard's most important traits. The ancestor of the plants of the genus Beta is the wild beet that grew on the shores of the Mediterranean and the Atlantic coast. To survive where waves reach and the wind carries salt spray, a plant needs a mechanism for tolerating the sodium that enters its cells, and this group has one. It locks sodium away in the vacuole to protect the cytoplasm, and even uses the sodium to regulate osmotic pressure. That ability remains intact in Swiss chard today.

> **Swiss chard ranks among the most salt-tolerant of the leafy vegetables. On land that a storm surge has swept over, beside wells that seawater has seeped into, in fields where salt has built up, Swiss chard keeps growing while every other leafy vegetable dies. This is the first reason for putting this crop on this list.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In a disaster, salt damage is a more common condition than one might expect. When a storm surge comes in along a coastal area, salt is left in the farmland, and it only leaves after rain has washed it out over several years. Lettuce, napa cabbage and spinach cannot grow in that ground. When irrigation systems collapse and groundwater has to be pumped up and used, salts build up on the soil surface, and the same thing happens inland. Whether greens can be harvested from such a field or not decides whether nutrient deficiency sets in or not.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In the evaluation table, Swiss chard's Overall grade is D, with 39.4 points. Its Supplementary grade is SS with 64.4 points, higher than lettuce's 62.1 points. One criterion worth noting is Temperature range, rated SS. The growth range is 5-30°C, a span of 25°C, the widest of the leafy vegetables covered in this document. This contrasts with lettuce, confined to a narrow band of 15-22°C, and iceberg lettuce, whose band is narrower still. Swiss chard grows even in summer and withstands temperatures down to minus 5°C. That means there is almost no gap in the seasons.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Perenniality is rated A, which reflects the way it is harvested, by picking the outer leaves continuously, and this too is part of the crop's character. Once planted, it can be harvested from spring to autumn, and in mild regions through the winter into the following year. The basis for the Pest-control self-reliance grade carries the phrase "pest control almost unnecessary." Few assessments are worth more in a situation without pesticides. Seed longevity is S: 5 years sealed at room temperature, far longer than lettuce's 2 to 3 years.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There are three weaknesses. First, Calories per area is rated D. At 18kcal per 100g, it cannot serve as a source of calories.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Second, oxalic acid. Because it belongs to the same group as spinach, its oxalic acid content is high, so it is hard to eat in large amounts raw and needs a blanching step. This is why its Cooking & processing grade stays at A rather than lettuce's SS.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Third, seed saving. It is a biennial, so it must get through a winter before seed can be collected, and it is wind-pollinated, with pollen carried off on the wind, so it crosses with beetroot and sugar beet if they are growing nearby. The Seed saving grade of A reflects these circumstances.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In summary, Swiss chard is a quiet, sturdy crop. It has no showy criterion, but none that collapses either. It holds up against heat, cold, pests and diseases, and salt, and once planted it is harvested for a long time. And under the conditions in which every other leafy vegetable fails, that is, in salty ground, it is almost the only one left standing. Swiss chard's use is to take its place not as the star of the field but as the crop that is still standing at the end.
