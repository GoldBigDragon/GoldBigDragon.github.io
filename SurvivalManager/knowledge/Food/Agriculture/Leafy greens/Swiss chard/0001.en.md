## Introduction
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Swiss chard (Beta vulgaris subsp. cicla) is a leafy vegetable that belongs, under today's classification, to the amaranth family (the goosefoot family has been merged into it), and it is a different subspecies of the same species as beet and sugar beet. Beets and sugar beets diverged toward swelling the root, chard toward growing leaves and leaf stalks. Its ancestor is the wild beet of Mediterranean and Atlantic seashores, and thanks to that origin it is among the most salt-tolerant of leafy vegetables. Its overall grade is D (39.4 points, 118th of 183 valid species) and its supplementary-food grade is SS (64.4 points). The item to note is temperature range SS (growth 5-30℃, a width of 25℃): it grows in summer too and withstands -5℃, so there is almost no gap in the seasons. Perenniality A, from picking outer leaves continuously, an assessment that it needs almost no pest control, and a seed life of 5 years sealed at room temperature (S) are also strengths. Its weaknesses are calories per area D at 18 kcal per 100 g, high oxalic acid that makes eating it raw in quantity hard and requires blanching (cooking and processing A), and seed saving (A) complicated by being a biennial whose pollen is carried by wind and crosses with beet and sugar beet. It has no flashy item but no collapsing one either, and it is a crop standing to the last that almost alone remains on salty ground where all other leafy vegetables fail.

## 2. Plant Overview
| Item | Content |
|:---|:---|
| Classification | Biennial of the genus Beta in the amaranth family. It is the same species as beet, sugar beet, fodder beet, and wild beet, so they cross with each other, and spinach is in the same amaranth family. Other names: Swiss chard, geundaenamul, baekgeundae |
| Leaves, stem | Leaves are broad ovate and glossy with thick veins and a puckered surface. Leaf stalks are thick and juicy, in white, red, yellow, and pink. The stem is compressed to almost nothing and the leaves come out radially from the base |
| Roots, flowers, fruit | The taproot goes deep and, unlike beet, does not swell but stays thin. In the second year a flower stalk rises with small green flowers in spikes. The fruit is a seed ball, a cluster of several seeds |

| Stage | Period | Key points |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Germination | 7-14 days after sowing | Several sprouts come up together from one seed ball |
| Seedling stage | 2 weeks after germination | 2-3 true leaves. Time for first thinning |
| Vegetative growth | 3-7 weeks after sowing | Leaves grow quickly |
| Harvest, repeat harvest | From 40-60 days | Outer leaves 20-30 cm. Pick from the outside and new leaves rise from the center |
| Bolting | Following spring | The flower stalk rises after cold exposure |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Per 100 g: 18 kcal, water over 90%, protein 1.78 g (39.6% of calories), carbohydrate 3.5-4.0 g, dietary fiber 1.5-2.0 g, and sugars 0.00 g, so there is almost no sweetness. It is rich in vitamins A, C, E, and K, folate, calcium, iron, potassium, and magnesium, but absorption of calcium and iron is low because of oxalic acid, so counting chard as a calcium source is not accurate.

## 3. Growing Environment and Varieties
| Condition | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Temperature | Growth range 5-30℃ (a width of 25℃), optimum growth 15-18℃. Optimum germination 20-25℃ (below 10℃ and above 35℃ are the limits). It grows up to 30℃ though the leaves get tough and oxalic acid rises, and it keeps growing down to -5℃ (more tolerant if covered with straw) |
| Light | 6 hours or more a day. It tolerates partial shade (growth is slower but the leaves are soft), and summer shading delays toughening of the leaves |
| Water, soil | Total evapotranspiration about 500 mm. Roots are deep, so it is stronger than lettuce in drought. Not particular about soil; pH 6.0-6.8 (around 6.5 is best). Excess water causes root rot |

| Crop | Salt tolerance |
|:---|:---|
| Chard, beet, sugar beet | Very high. Among the top of leafy vegetables |
| Spinach | Medium. The same family but weaker than chard |
| Lettuce, Chinese cabbage, radish | Low. Cannot grow on salt-damaged land |
| Legumes | Very low. The first to die |

> **On land with salt from tsunami or flooding, or in fields where salts built up from groundwater irrigation, chard is almost the only leafy vegetable that remains. Whether it can supply vitamins and minerals on that land is the reason this crop exists.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Chard traps sodium in its vacuoles to protect the cytoplasm and even uses it for osmotic adjustment. But its salt tolerance is not unlimited.

| Variety | Characteristics |
|:---|:---|
| Green chard (white chard) | Leaf stalks white to light green. The most common, with stable yield and a chewy texture |
| Red chard, yellow chard | Leaf stalks red to pink (anthocyanin) and yellow to light green (beta-carotene). Soft leaves |
| Fordhook, Ruby | Fordhook is the representative white variety with large, thick leaves; Ruby is a representative red strain with good cold tolerance |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Color has little effect on difficulty or yield, so it is one of the few crops where variety choice does not decide success or failure. If yield comes first choose green chard and Fordhook; for overwintering choose Ruby; and to widen the range of nutrition, mix red and yellow chard.

## 4. Field Preparation and Sowing
| Item | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Preparing the soil | Dig 20-30 cm deep (the taproot goes deep), bed height 15-20 cm, width 90-120 cm, and secure a drainage ditch. Because it is harvested for a long time, put in plenty of organic matter |
| Fertilization (1,000㎡) | Nitrogen 8-10 kg and fully matured compost 1,000-1,500 kg. Apply the compost, all the phosphorus and potassium, and nitrogen as basal fertilizer 10 days before sowing, and generally skip top dressing. When new leaves come out smaller and paler, nutrients have run out, and that is when to give dilute fertilizer. Boron is an important micronutrient |
| Substitute resources | Wood ash (a handful per plant; too much causes boron deficiency), urine diluted 10-20 times (between plants; it burns if it touches the leaves), dried seaweed (supplies potassium and boron; chard tolerates it unrinsed), and fermented oil cake |

| Cropping type | Sowing | Harvest | Notes |
|:---|:---|:---|:---|
| Spring crop | Mid March-early May | From mid May onward | The most common. If sown very early, cold makes it bolt that year |
| Summer crop | June-July | Aug-Sep | Relatively strong in heat |
| Autumn crop | Aug-Sep | Oct-Nov | Soft leaves |
| Overwintering crop | Sep-Oct | Following spring | Central region and south |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The seed of chard is not a single seed but a seed ball, a cluster of 2-5 seeds. Several sprouts come up in one spot, so thinning is essential, and the husk has a germination inhibitor, so soaking in water improves germination.

> **Thinning is not optional for chard; it is essential. Three or four sprouts come from one seed ball, so if left alone, several plants in one spot grow pressing on each other. All of them end up small and weak, and none of them grows properly.**

| Sowing | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Seed treatment | Soak in water for 2-3 hours (overnight at most), changing the water once or twice. Soak in 50-55℃ water for 10-15 minutes to kill surface pathogens and coat with dry wood ash to make it easy to handle. Sow soaked seed right after treatment |
| Sowing | Depth 1-2.5 cm (deeper than lettuce because seed balls are large), row spacing 30-50 cm, 2-3 balls per spot if sowing in hills. Germination 7-14 days |
| Thinning | First to 5 cm at 2-3 true leaves (eat as baby leaves), second to 10-15 cm at 4-5 (eat as greens). Final 20 cm for larger plants. Do not pull; cut with scissors |

## 5. Water Management and Cultivation Management
| Period | Water management |
|:---|:---|
| Sowing to germination | Keep the soil from drying (the seed balls must take up plenty of water to germinate) |
| Seedling stage, growth stage | When the topsoil dries, give plenty (an occasional soaking is better than frequent small amounts, and it leads roots downward) |
| Repeat-harvest period | Water after picking leaves |
| Rainy season, winter | Put drainage ditches in order in the rainy season, and give almost none in winter |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Small, tough leaves mean persistent water shortage, and lower leaves turning yellow with a soft base mean excess water. Leaf stalks splitting lengthwise come from giving a lot of water suddenly after drying out. Straw and dry-grass mulch greatly reduces evaporation and also slows the rise of salt on salt-damaged land, and partial-shade placement reduces evaporation in summer and softens the leaves.

| Management | Method |
|:---|:---|
| Thinning, weeding | Thinning is essential. Weed only early on, because once leaves cover the ground weeds are suppressed. Hilling, pinching, and staking are generally unnecessary |
| Removing dry leaves | Remove yellowed, dried lower leaves from time to time |
| Bolting | If young seedlings go through cold below 10℃ for long, or meet late spring cold, the flower stalk rises that same year. Sow after frost has completely passed, or if sowing early, keep soil temperature up with straw or dry grass. A flower stalk that has risen cannot be reversed |
| Long-term use | Water after picking and give dilute fertilizer when new leaves get smaller. Always leave six or more center leaves. Give partial shade in summer and cover with straw in autumn to prepare for winter. In winter pick the existing leaves one at a time (almost no new leaves come out) |

## 6. Pests, Diseases, and Nutrient Problems
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Cabbage-family pests (cabbage white, diamondback moth, flea beetle, clubroot), the aster-family disease (sclerotinia rot), and nightshade-family pests and diseases do not come to chard; the aphids, mites, and leaf beetles of the amaranth family itself do.

| Pest or disease | Symptoms and conditions | Control without chemicals |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Cercospora leaf spot | Brown round spots form on leaves and turn gray in the center (the most common). Hot, humid | Removing diseased leaves, ventilation, rotation |
| Downy mildew, powdery mildew | White mold on the underside of leaves and yellowing on top / white powder on the leaf surface. Cool, humid / dry with a large day-night gap | Ventilation, morning watering / ventilation and sunlight, diluted milk |
| Root rot, damping-off | The base browns and wilts / young seedlings pinch at the base and fall over. Excess water, dense sowing | Raising beds, drainage, thinning, hot-water soaking |
| Yellows | Leaves yellow with prominent veins. Continuous cropping, a virus carried by aphids | Aphid control, rotation, removing diseased plants |
| Aphids, mites, leaf miners | Suck sap on the underside of leaves and carry viruses (aphids are the most common) / webbing / burrow inside leaves leaving white tracks | Wash off with a strong water jet, soapy water, protecting natural enemies / water spray, raising humidity / removing damaged leaves |
| Slugs, cutworms | Holes in leaves / cut young seedlings at the base | Catching at night, bands of ash or rice hulls / finding them in the soil at night and catching them |

> **Most diseases of chard only cost leaves and do not kill the plant. This is the basic reason chard is strong in chemical-free farming. Remove diseased leaves and new leaves rise, and that repetition is itself control.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Dust sifted wood ash on the leaves and field or use wood vinegar at 300-500 times dilution, and rest the field 2 years or more, counting spinach, beet, and sugar beet together. Raising beds to secure drainage is the basic remedy for root rot, diseased leaves are carried out of the field or burned, and picking diseased and damaged leaves first while harvesting is chard's own form of control.

| Symptom | Cause and remedy |
|:---|:---|
| Whole leaf yellow, small plant | Nitrogen deficiency. Matured compost, urine diluted 10-20 times |
| New leaves twisted and split, growing point blackened | Boron deficiency. Dried seaweed, well-matured compost, less lime |
| Small, thin leaves | Shortage of water and nutrients. Increase watering and fertilizing together |
| Overly soft leaves that attract aphids | Nitrogen excess. Stop the nitrogen supply |
| Leaf tips dry and growth stops | Salt excess. Give plenty of water to wash it down |

## 7. Intercropping and Crop Rotation
| Compatibility | Companions |
|:---|:---|
| Good | Aster-family crops such as lettuce and garland chrysanthemum, cabbage-family crops such as radish and Chinese cabbage (they share no pests or diseases), Welsh onion, spring onion, and garlic (the smell confuses pests), and tall crops such as corn (partial shade in summer) |
| Bad | Spinach, beet, sugar beet (the same family or species, so they share diseases), continuous chard cropping (pathogens build up) |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Going around in a 3-year cycle of chard and other amaranth-family crops → legumes (fix nitrogen) → cabbage-family crops or grasses (the amaranth-family pathogens starve), chard is started again with a lower density of pathogens. The criterion for continuous-cropping tolerance A is a 2-year rest, more lenient than the 2-3 years for lettuce and iceberg lettuce.

## 8. Harvest and Seed Saving
| Harvest | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Timing | First thinning baby leaves at 25-30 days (2-3 true leaves, salad or fresh-salted greens), second thinning at 35-40 days (4-5 true leaves, blanched for seasoned greens), first harvest at 40-60 days (outer leaf length 20-30 cm), and then repeated once or twice a week |
| Picking | From the outside, cut fully grown leaves with a knife or scissors, leaving 3-5 cm of leaf stalk where it meets the stem. Take only one third to one half or less of all leaves at a time, and always leave six or more center leaves. Water after picking |
| Yield | 1-1.5 kg per plant per month. May to October for a spring sowing, and until the following spring if it overwinters. 5-10 plants per adult |

> **Always leave six or more center leaves. The leaves you leave are the photosynthesis factory, and that factory makes the next leaves. If you get greedy and pick them all, that plant simply stops.**

| Seed saving | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Two barriers | A biennial that must be exposed to cold before the flower stalk rises, and wind-pollinated cross-pollination in which wind carries pollen far. It crosses completely with beet, sugar beet, fodder beet, other varieties of chard, and wild beet, and does not cross with spinach, goosefoot, or amaranth |
| Preventing crossing | Isolate 1,000 m or more from other Beta vulgaris flowering plants (hard, since neighbors' fields cannot be controlled). Staggered cultivation is the most realistic; in a small field cover the flower stalks with cloth bags before flowering |
| Procedure | In autumn choose plants with large, disease-free leaves and clear variety color. Overwinter (in the central-northern region overwintering in the open is difficult, so pull them up, store them, and replant in spring), bolting in March-April (stake), flowering in May-June, and in July-August when seed balls dry brown, cut the whole stem, dry in shade for 1-2 weeks, strip or rub out the seed, winnow, and seal |
| Yield, life | One plant gives hundreds to thousands of seed balls, with 2-5 seeds in each ball. Seed life is 5 years sealed at room temperature; check germination every year with 20 balls |

> **Saving chard seed is not an annual job but one done once every 4-5 years with proper isolation that year. Keep at least 5 plants for seed, preferably 10 or more. If seed of a cross-pollinating crop is taken from only one or two plants, inbreeding depression appears within a few generations.**

## 9. Storage
| Method | Procedure and period |
|:---|:---|
| After removing moisture, in a cool place | Do not wash; just shake off the soil. 3-5 days |
| Wrapped in paper or cloth and stood up | Wrap in newspaper or dry cloth and stand upright (if the cloth is too wet, it goes soft). 5-7 days |
| Stem in water | Stand the base of the leaf stalks in water (change the water daily). 5-7 days |
| Cellar, pit | Stand upright in a cool underground space. 1-2 weeks |
| Dried greens | Separate leaves and leaf stalks, blanch leaves in boiling water for 1-2 minutes and stalks for 3-5 minutes, rinse in cold water, and dry. Keep in a sealed container with charcoal or roasted rice. 6 months to over 1 year |
| Pickling the leaf stalks | Salt curing (the shape is kept), lactic fermentation at 2-3% salt (a good part of the vitamin C remains), pickling in soy sauce or soybean paste (several months) |

> **Made into dried greens, most of the vitamin C is lost, but vitamin A, vitamin K, minerals, and dietary fiber remain. Winter vitamin C comes not from dried leaves but from fresh leaves standing in the field and from fermented foods.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Chard storage practically comes down to dried greens alone, and thoroughly dried chard can be rubbed into powder and kept.

## 10. Cooking and Precautions
| Part | Properties | Use |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Baby leaves | Soft, low in oxalic acid | Raw, fresh-salted greens, salad |
| Mature leaves | Thick, high in oxalic acid | Blanched and tossed, soup, dried greens |
| Leaf stalks | Crisp and juicy | Stir-fry, pickles, salt curing |
| Thick leaf veins | Tough | Chopped fine into soup or porridge |

| Treatment | Oxalic acid reduction |
|:---|:---|
| Raw | Does not decrease (baby leaves contain little) |
| Blanched and the water discarded | Decreases a good deal, the most reliable. Vitamin C falls to half or less and some folate is lost too |
| Blanching water used in soup, stir-frying | Does not decrease (the oxalic acid is in the broth) |
| Steaming, lactic fermentation | Decreases slightly. Fermentation keeps a good amount of vitamin C |

> **The point is not blanching itself but discarding the blanching water. Oxalic acid is not destroyed; it moves into the water. If you use that water in soup, the oxalic acid comes in with it.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The oxalic acid in chard and spinach is hundreds of mg per 100 g, higher than kale (20-50 mg) or lettuce (tens of mg), and it binds calcium and blocks its absorption. Eating it with calcium-rich ingredients is also one response. Chard has long been used in soybean-paste soup, mixing in a little oil is nutritionally advantageous, and it has no sweetness and a faint aroma. Chard has no acute toxicity.

## 11. Annual Work Schedule
| Month | Work |
|:---|:---|
| Jan-Feb | Harvest winter leaves from overwintered plants and check seed. Turn the compost in February |
| Mar-Apr | Spring sowing from mid March (basal fertilizer, building beds), seed plants begin bolting. First thinning (harvesting baby leaves), weeding, and stakes for seed plants in April |
| May-June | Second thinning and first harvest, flowering and isolation management of seed plants in May. Full repeat harvest, optional summer sowing, and seed set of seed plants in June |
| July-Aug | Repeat harvest, drainage management in the rainy season, and seed harvest from seed plants in July. Repeat harvest, autumn sowing from late August, and scouting for Cercospora leaf spot in August |
| Sep-Oct | Thinning of the autumn sowing and continued harvest of spring plants in September. Peak harvest (the month with the best leaves) and making dried greens in October |
| Nov-Dec | Continued harvest, selecting seed plants, and winter preparation (hilling, straw covering) in November. Picking existing leaves one at a time and checking stored goods in December |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;A spring sowing is harvested from mid May to October, with leaves getting tough in summer, and a late-summer sowing (late Aug-early Sep) is harvested from mid October, staying soft to the end with low bolting risk. Sown twice, it is practically harvested all year.

> **The reason to plant chard is not any one flashy item. It does not collapse in heat, cold, pests and diseases, or salt; once planted it is harvested for a long time; its seed lasts 5 years; and you do not tire of eating it every day. This trait of never being the worst in any condition shines more the longer a disaster lasts.**
