## 8. Crop Management - Flower Stalks and Clump Renewal

### 8-1. Removing Flower Stalks

| Item | Details |
|---|---|
| Timing | Around August, when flower stalks begin to come up |
| Identification | A stalk thicker and firmer than the leaf, solid inside, standing straight |
| Method | Every 7-10 days, snap or cut off at the base whichever ones are visible |
| Reason | Nutrients concentrate into making flowers and seed, and the clump loses vigor |
| Exception | Set aside and leave alone any clump you intend to collect seed from |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;A plant flowering means it has moved into its reproductive stage. From that point, the nutrients stored in the roots and leaves drain toward the flowers and seed. From a person's standpoint, the resources that would have made edible leaves flow into seed instead, and if you let it flower and the seed ripen, nearly all of that year's remaining harvest disappears. Root vigor drops along with it, leaving an effect into the following spring.

> **Missing the flower stalks in August reduces both that autumn's harvest and the following spring's harvest together. Of all the timed tasks in garlic chive care, missing this one costs the most.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;However, do not throw away the flower stalks you cut. Garlic chive flower stalks, while still in bud before the flower opens, make a very good vegetable. They are crisp and a little sweet, used in stir-fries and jangajji (pickled vegetables in soy sauce or paste), and they keep better than the leaves. This removal work becomes, in effect, another harvest. Once the flowers open white, the stalk toughens and becomes hard to eat, so the trick is to harvest while the buds are still closed.

### 8-2. Clump Renewal

| Item | Details |
|---|---|
| Interval | Every 3-4 years |
| Timing | Early spring (March-April) or autumn (September) |
| Signal | Leaves thin out, the clump becomes crowded, and the center starts to go hollow |
| Method | Dig up the whole clump, divide into clusters of 3-5 shoots, and replant in a new spot |
| Location | Move to a different area if possible |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The evaluation that garlic chive lasts 5+ years in one spot and the care guideline to renew every 3-4 years may look contradictory, but they are the same story. Garlic chive does not die; it keeps living. But its vigor declines as time passes. The rhizome spreads sideways and the number of shoots increases, and as that keeps increasing within one mass, the shoots eventually compete with each other for space. As density rises, each individual shoot gets less nutrient and light, and the leaves thin out.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;On top of this, the rhizome rises a little higher each year. This happens because new rhizome grows layered on top of the old, and after a few years the root portion rises close to the surface and is left exposed to winter cold and summer drought. When the middle of the garlic chive bed hollows out and it starts to look like a donut, it is time to renew.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Renewal is not a loss but multiplication. Dig up one mass and divide it, and it becomes three or four masses, and the garlic chive bed grows by that much. Go through this process every 3 years, and the original one pyeong (about 3.3 m²) becomes several pyeong a few years later. It is a way to expand area without seed, a way to route directly around the weakness of seed longevity D.

| Round | Point in time | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Initial planting | Year 0 | One mass |
| 1st renewal | Year 3-4 | Three or four masses |
| 2nd renewal | Year 6-8 | 10+ masses |
| 3rd renewal | Year 9-12 | Enough that you start worrying where to put them all |

### 8-3. Weed Management

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This is the fate of a perennial crop. With no chance to till the field under, weeds pile up year after year. In particular, a weed that has worked its way into a garlic chive clump gets its roots so tangled that pulling it out brings the garlic chive with it.

| Method | Details |
|---|---|
| Before planting | Thoroughly pick out perennial weed roots. This is the only real fix |
| Furrow mulching | Cover the furrows with straw and dry grass to block germination |
| Right after cutting | With the clump cut low right after cutting, weeds are easy to see |
| Early spring | Remove weeds that have come up before garlic chive does |
| Winter | Clear them out together when removing the dried leaves |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The advice to target right after cutting is practical. Normally garlic chive's leaves grow thick and hide any weeds among them, but once the leaves are cut, the ground is laid bare and weeds jump right into view. Cut, pull weeds, apply fertilizer, wait a day, then water: that sequence becomes the basic workflow for a garlic chive bed.

### 8-4. Overwintering Care

| Timing | Task | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Late October | Finish the last cutting | Stopping 3-4 weeks before frost lets nutrients build up in the root |
| November | Apply mainly potassium fertilizer | Raises cold hardiness |
| Late November | Above-ground parts dry out | A natural process |
| December | Leave dried leaves in place or cover with straw | Prevents frost heave and drying out |
| Late February-March | Remove the cover | Soil temperature rises faster, bringing harvest earlier |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Stopping cutting in late October governs next year's crop. Garlic chive cut in late autumn draws on the nutrients stored in its root to put out new leaves, but frost falls and kills those leaves before they can even grow. The result is that stored nutrients are lost with nothing gained in return. Overwintering in that state makes the leaves that come up the following spring noticeably thinner.

## 9. Pests and Diseases - Prevention Without Chemicals

### 9-1. Major Diseases

| Disease | Symptoms | Conditions | Response without chemicals |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sclerotinia rot | Base softens; white mycelium and black granules (sclerotia) form | Excess moisture, low temperature, poor ventilation | Remove the diseased clump along with its soil. Sclerotia remain in the soil, so do not use that spot for several years |
| Leaf blight | Leaves brown and wither from the tip | Poor ventilation, excess nitrogen | Remove diseased leaves and widen the space between clumps |
| Rust | Orange, powdery spots on the leaves | Humid conditions in late summer | Remove diseased leaves and carry them out of the field |
| Downy mildew | Leaves yellow, with grayish mold on the underside | Cool and humid, spring and autumn | Ensure drainage and ventilation. The biggest constraint for the genus Allium |
| Gray mold | Gray mold grows at the base of the leaf | Excess moisture, wounded areas | Let it dry for a day after cutting, then water |
| Soft rot | Base softens and smells foul | Bacteria enter through a cutting wound | Avoid cutting on rainy days |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The evaluation table's pest-control self-reliance B is a value based on six empirical studies on organic onion in Germany, and in them downy mildew was identified as the biggest constraint for the genus Allium. The figure of 96% disease severity in the untreated control means that, practically speaking, almost everything gets infected without control. However, disease severity is not the same as yield loss. Even if the leaves are damaged, the root survives and comes back up the next year. This is the buffering capacity of a perennial crop.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The response those studies proposed was not a chemical either. It was simply to use resistant strains, widen the planting distance so air can move, and secure drainage. These three things can all be done without chemicals, and they apply directly to a field in a disaster.

### 9-2. Major Pests

| Pest | Damage | Timing | Response without chemicals |
|---|---|---|---|
| Root maggots | Larvae eat into the below-ground parts, and the clump wilts and dies | Spring and autumn, in excess moisture | Do not apply under-composted material. Fix the drainage |
| Leafminer fly | Tunnels inside the leaf, leaving white lines | Spring, autumn | Remove damaged leaves. A net cover is reliable |
| Aphids | Attach to leaves, suck sap, and bring on sooty mold | Spring, autumn | Rinse off with water or spray soapy water |
| Thrips | White spots appear on leaves, turning silvery | Hot, dry periods | Keep conditions from drying out. Blue sticky traps help |
| Leek moth | Larvae chew on the leaves | Summer | Pick off by hand. Damage to garlic chive is not severe |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Garlic chive's pest problems are generally mild. This is because the smell of its sulfur compounds acts as a repellent in its own right. The fact that the evaluation table's pest & disease pressure grade B is one step above kale's C also reflects this circumstance. In practical terms, only the first row is truly dangerous.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The reason root maggots are especially dangerous is that the damage progresses somewhere you cannot see. By the time the above-ground parts start to wilt, the below-ground parts have already been eaten through quite a bit. And this insect lays its eggs in under-composted organic matter. In a disaster, with no chemical fertilizer and a need to rely on compost, this becomes a real danger. Use compost only once it is fully composted, no longer generating heat, and smelling like soil, and place it a little away from the clump rather than right beside it.

### 9-3. Prevention Without Chemicals

| Method | How to make and use it | Target |
|---|---|---|
| Ensure drainage | Raise the bed and dig the drainage channel deep | Sclerotinia rot, root maggots, downy mildew |
| Ensure planting distance | Keep 15-20cm between clumps | Downy mildew, leaf blight, gray mold |
| Drying after cutting | Withhold water and fertilizer for a day after cutting | Soft rot, gray mold |
| Removing diseased leaves | Carry out of the field and burn or bury deep | Rust, downy mildew |
| Wood ash | Scatter around the base after cutting | Root maggots, base rot, slugs |
| Soapy water | Dissolve a few grams of soap in 1L of water and spray | Aphids, thrips |
| Wood vinegar | Dilute 300-500× and spray | For prevention |
| Bordeaux mixture | Made from copper sulfate and quicklime | Rust, downy mildew. Only when the materials are available |
| Insect netting | Cover in spring and autumn | Leafminer fly, adult root maggot flies |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The first four rows of this table are the core of what can be done without chemicals, and together they cover all the moisture-related diseases that make up most of garlic chive's disease problems. Even with no materials to make a treatment, these four are always possible. All you need are a spade and your hands.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The reason wood ash works against root maggots is the adult fly's habit of laying eggs on a moist soil surface. Scattering ash around the base makes the surface dry and alkaline, reducing egg-laying. Since it also disinfects the wound after cutting and supplies potassium at the same time, ash is the most useful resource in a garlic chive bed.

### 9-4. When Garlic Chive Is Itself the Pest Control

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In the evaluation table, non-food value D is filled by the single criterion of pest control. This means the sulfur compounds that Allium plants secrete suppress the growth of pathogens in the soil.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In practice, this effect is used in the field in two ways. One is intercropping: it has long been observed that planting garlic chive next to crops such as tomato, eggplant, and strawberry reduces the occurrence of wilt and blight. The other is a following-crop effect: a spot where garlic chive has been removed has had its roots secreting compounds there for several years, so soil-disease density is lower, and the crop planted there afterward benefits. Section 11 covers this arrangement.

## 10. Nutrient Deficiency and Excess

| Symptom | Cause | Action with natural materials |
|---|---|---|
| Whole leaf pale green, thin, and short | Nitrogen deficiency | Give urine diluted 8-10× in the furrow the day after cutting. Lay well-rotted compost between clumps |
| Leaves unusually large and soft, falling over easily | Nitrogen excess | Stop fertilizing. The plant is in a disease-weakened state |
| Older leaves scorch brown starting from the edge | Potassium deficiency | Scatter one or two handfuls of wood ash per 1m² |
| Growth slow overall, leaves dark green with a purplish tinge | Phosphorus deficiency | Apply bone meal and wood ash. The same symptom also appears in cold periods |
| New leaves twist and their tips dry out | Calcium deficiency | Apply burned and ground shell and eggshell |
| Tissue between veins yellows while the veins stay green | Magnesium deficiency | Wood ash contains magnesium. Use dolomitic lime if you have it |
| Leaves noticeably thinner in spring after overwintering | Excess autumn cutting or potassium deficiency | Do not cut after late October. Apply potassium in November |
| Leaves get thinner with each successive cutting | Accumulated nitrogen depletion | Build a cycle of top-dressing at every cutting |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The last two rows are items unique to garlic chive. In other crops, a deficiency appears and resolves within one year, but because garlic chive is a perennial, a shortfall in one year's care carries over into the next. The consequences of over-cutting in autumn show up the following spring, and nitrogen depletion accumulated over several years shows up by year 4 or 5. Because this is a crop with a long time axis, the gap between cause and symptom is also long.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;So nutrient management for garlic chive should not be a matter of watching for symptoms and then fixing them, but of setting up in advance a cycle that puts back as much as goes out. That cycle is the habit of adding something every time you cut, and even if diluted urine is the only thing you have to add, that is better than adding nothing.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;It is also worth noting that the phosphorus-deficiency symptom in the fourth row is easy to confuse with a cold-period symptom. When soil temperature is low in early spring, the same symptom appears because the roots cannot absorb phosphorus even if the soil has enough of it. Since it recovers on its own once the temperature rises, purplish leaves seen in March usually just need waiting out.

## 11. Intercropping and Crop Rotation

### 11-1. Compatibility

| Compatibility | Crop | Reason |
|:---:|---|---|
| Good | Tomato, eggplant, chili pepper | Garlic chive root exudates suppress wilt and blight |
| Good | Strawberry | Long observed to reduce root rot |
| Good | Carrot | Each repels the other's pests |
| Good | Lettuce, spinach | Uses the open space between garlic chive plants without competing |
| Bad | Legumes (soybean, pea, kidney bean) | Allium exudates suppress root-nodule bacteria |
| Bad | Other Allium crops (green onion, garlic, onion) | Pathogens and pests build up |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The fifth row is especially practical information. The sulfur compounds Allium plants give off do not just suppress pathogens; they also affect the root-nodule bacteria living on legume roots. A legume's ability to make its own nitrogen comes from these bacteria, so soybean planted next to garlic chive develops fewer nodules and fixes less nitrogen. In a situation with no chemical fertilizer, nitrogen fixation by legumes is a pillar holding up the fertility of the whole field, so avoiding this combination is not a matter of simple compatibility.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This may seem to contradict what Section 5 said earlier about growing soybean the year before planting garlic chive, but it is different. Placing them in sequence over time is fine; the problem is planting them side by side at the same time. Harvest the soybean, bury the residue, then plant garlic chive, and the garlic chive receives that nitrogen.

### 11-2. Crop Rotation

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Since the evaluation table's continuous-cropping tolerance is SS, rotation for garlic chive's own sake is not necessary. Keeping it in one spot for 5 years is normal. However, once those 5 years are over, and if disease has gotten in, you need to move it.

| Situation | Response |
|---|---|
| Normal renewal | Divide and replant every 3-4 years, moving to an adjacent area |
| A spot where sclerotinia rot occurred | Do not plant any Allium crop there for 5+ years |
| A spot where garlic chive has been removed | Soil-disease density there is lower, which benefits the next crop |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;One example rotation sequence is: grow garlic chive for 4 years, then plant a legume for one year to restore nitrogen, then plant a leafy or fruiting vegetable the year after that, and only then bring garlic chive back in. This way, the cycle for one area to return to garlic chive becomes 6-7 years, giving Allium soil disease plenty of time to subside.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;However, on a small field in an actual disaster situation, this kind of rotation is often difficult. In that case, choose to fix the garlic chive area in place for good instead, and keep drainage and ventilation strictly in order so that disease does not get in. Since continuous cropping itself does no harm to garlic chive, as long as disease stays away it is fine to use the same spot for ten years.

### 11-3. Practical Placement

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Because garlic chive is a perennial, it becomes a fixed area on the map of the field. Where you place this area governs several years, so here are the criteria laid out.

| Criterion | Details |
|---|---|
| Where water does not pool | The top-priority condition |
| A spot you pass often | Since this is a crop cut a little at a time, often, the closer the better |
| Outside the area tilled every year | This spot will not be tilled for 5 years |
| 6+ hours of sun a day | It does not need sun all day |
| The edge of a fruiting-vegetable bed | Doubles as an intercropping benefit |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The second row matters more than you might expect. Garlic chive is not a crop you harvest a lot of at once; it is one you bring in a handful at a time, often, for a bowl of soup or a jeon (Korean savory pancake). If it sits at the far end of the field, you have to walk there every time for that one handful, and you end up using it less. Placing it near the doorway or close to the kitchen greatly increases how much it actually gets used.
