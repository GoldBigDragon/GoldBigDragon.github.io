## Introduction
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Garlic chive (Allium tuberosum) is a perennial of the genus Allium in the family Amaryllidaceae, and the only one in the group with scallions, garlic, and onions that is used for its leaves rather than its roots. New leaves rise from the underground bulb and short rhizome even after the leaves are cut, and once established it returns every year, so it needs no seed, no sowing, and no seedling raising. In the evaluation, perenniality SS and continuous-cropping tolerance SS give it an overall grade of S (52.2 points, rank 10 of 183 valid species), and its supplementary food grade is SS (61.1 points). Cold tolerance SS means the roots survive below -20℃, and since one planting is used for 5 years, the annual digging energy is as low as 26 kcal per ㎡. Its weak point is, unexpectedly, micronutrients D (8 nutrients meet 10% of the daily requirement, against 18 for kale). The reason to plant garlic chive is not nutrient density but aroma and time. The aroma of its sulfur compounds builds flavor on a table short of salt and soy paste (taste D reflects dislike of the smell), it can be cut and eaten 5-7 times a year at 10-20 day intervals from April to October, and it is the first green thing in early spring. Storability E (cut leaves last 2-3 days at room temperature) and seed longevity D (seed does not last past a year at room temperature) are solved by dividing and carrying on living clumps.

## 2. Plant Overview
| Item | Details |
|:---|:---|
| Classification | Order Asparagales, family Amaryllidaceae, genus Allium. Native to northern China and Northeast Asia |
| Life form | A perennial with persistent roots (the aboveground part dies back in winter and the roots overwinter). Propagation is possible by seed and by dividing clumps, but in practice dividing clumps is the main method |
| Leaves, underground parts | Flat and linear, 20-40 cm long and 0.5-1 cm wide. New clumps arise from the short bulb and the short rhizome spreading sideways, and the fibrous roots are concentrated within 20 cm of depth |
| Flowers, seeds | Small white flowers in an umbrella shape in August-September. The flower stalk is thicker and firmer than the leaves and solid. Seeds are small, black, and angular |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Flat, solid leaves are the first criterion separating garlic chive from Welsh onion (round and hollow).

| Stage | Timing | Key point |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Germination and seedling, early growth | Sowing to 12 weeks | One thread-thin leaf comes up very slowly. Phosphorus, nitrogen |
| Clump expansion | 1 year after transplanting | New plants form sideways and become a mass. Nitrogen, potassium |
| Harvest period | April-October from year 2 | Leaves come up at 10-20 day intervals. Nitrogen, potassium |

> **The first year of garlic chive is not a year for harvesting but a year for making roots. If you get greedy and cut it many times in the first year, the clump does not settle and the next year's yield falls sharply.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Per 100 g: 22-46 kcal, 88-92 g of water, 1.2-3.0 g of protein (10.4% of calories), and about 2.5 g of dietary fiber, with plenty of potassium, calcium, iron, and phosphorus, and plenty of beta-carotene, vitamin C (more than half is lost by blanching), vitamin K, and folate. The source of the smell and antibacterial action is sulfur compounds (alliin family).

## 3. Growing Environment and Varieties
| Condition | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Temperature | Minimum for germination 10℃ (best around 20℃, 7-14 days), best for growth 18-20℃, possible 5-30℃ (a 25℃ range). Above 25℃ the leaves get tough, and growth stops at 5℃ or below. The aboveground part dies back around the first frost, but the roots survive even below -20℃ |
| Light | 6 hours or more a day (the practical minimum). Yield is about 86% under 40% shading. In partial shade the leaves are tender and pale and the aroma is weak |
| Water, soil | Total evapotranspiration about 500 mm (fairly tolerant of drought). Well-drained sandy loam to loam, pH 6.0-6.5 (6.3-6.5 is best), plenty of well-rotted compost. Bed height 15-20 cm or more (25 cm on poorly drained land) |

> **Garlic chive disappears in winter not by dying but by withdrawing. Even when the aboveground part dries and turns brown, if you plow that spot under, nothing comes up the next year.**

> **What ruins a garlic chive crop is almost always excess water, not drought. On a spot where water pools in the rainy season, even a clump raised for 5 years can disappear whole in a few weeks.**

| Strain | Characteristics |
|:---|:---|
| Broad-leaf, Taiwanese chive | Broad leaves, vigorous tillering, and disease resistance give high yield; shallow dormancy means it wakes early (first harvest in early spring) |
| Landrace, Mongolian, Manchurian | The landrace has a strong aroma, reliable overwintering, and good field adaptability. The Mongolian type has especially strong cold tolerance, and the Manchurian type has deep dormancy and long harvest intervals |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;More important than choosing a variety is getting living clumps, not seed. If you divide clumps from a neighbor or nearby, you can harvest from the first year a strain already adapted to the area; if you have only seed, give up the first year's harvest and make roots.

## 4. Field Preparation, Sowing, and Transplanting
| Item | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Preparing the soil | Dig 20-30 cm deep (deep at the start since the spot will be used for 5 years); bed height 15-20 cm (25 cm or more if drainage is poor), width 90-120 cm (two or three rows), and a drainage ditch is a must (excess water is the biggest cause of failure). Remove stones and perennial weed roots beforehand. Add lime 2-3 weeks before planting to set pH 6.0-6.5 |
| Fertilization (1,000㎡) | Nitrogen 20-25 kg, phosphorus 12-15 kg (all basal), potassium 15-20 kg, lime 100-150 kg. Basal fertilizer 1-2 weeks before planting: all the compost, all the phosphorus, and half of the nitrogen and potassium |
| Top dressing | After every cutting, mainly nitrogen and potassium (the key). Before overwintering in November, mainly potassium (raises cold tolerance); when it wakes in spring in March, mainly nitrogen |
| Substitute resources | Urine diluted 8-10 times (poured in the furrow right after cutting; it burns if it touches leaves; wait a day so the cut heals), well-rotted compost (half-rotted compost attracts root maggots), wood ash, a handful or two per ㎡ (supplies potassium and brings the soil closer to the near-neutral that garlic chive likes), oil cake, bone meal, and grass liquid fertilizer, and growing beans the year before and burying the residue (the spot will be used 5 years, so before planting is the only chance) |

| Propagation | Timing and method |
|:---|:---|
| Dividing clumps (the standard) | In March-April when new shoots come up, or in September, lift the root surroundings widely with a spade and divide into masses of 3-5 plants. Cut the aboveground part to only 10 cm, trim the ends of long roots, and plant 5-8 cm deep so the growing point is not buried, with 20 cm between rows and 15-20 cm between plants; water well. First harvest a month after establishment |
| Sowing | In spring (mid March-early April) or autumn (mid August-early September), sow 2-3 seeds per hole with 20 cm between rows (around 20℃, germination in 7-14 days). Thin to 5 cm at 2-3 true leaves and to 15-20 cm at 4-5 true leaves, and do not cut in the first year. Move seedlings before the rainy season (May to mid June) |
| Seed treatment | Soak in 50-55℃ water for 10-15 minutes and cool in cold water (removes surface pathogens), coat wet seed with sifted wood ash, or soak in room-temperature water overnight to advance germination by a few days |

> **Dividing clumps is the standard way to start garlic chive. Starting from seed takes a year to the first harvest; starting from clumps takes a month.**

## 5. Water Management and Cultivation Management
| Period | Water management |
|:---|:---|
| Right after planting, establishment (2-3 weeks) | Water thoroughly once to bring roots and soil into contact. Afterward, when the topsoil dries |
| Growth period | 1-2 times a week, deeply each time (shallow, frequent watering makes shallow roots) |
| Right after cutting | Once, a day later (after the cut has healed) |
| Rainy season, dormancy | None (in the rainy season, fixing the drainage ditch is the water management; wet soil that freezes damages the roots) |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Leaf tips drying and thinning mean water shortage; leaves suddenly turning yellow and collapsing mean root rot from excess water. Without plastic, mulch with straw, dry grass, fallen leaves, or rice hulls (in summer to suppress weeds, in winter for insulation).

| Management | Details |
|:---|:---|
| Removing flower stalks | Around August, when flower stalks thicker and firmer than the leaves and solid come up, snap or cut them at the base as you see them every 7-10 days (nutrients go to flowers and seed, and the clump's vigor falls. Keep seed plants aside; eat the buds of the snapped stalks rather than throwing them away). If missed, both the autumn and the next spring harvests fall |
| Renewing clumps | Every 3-4 years in early spring or September, when leaves thin, the clump gets crowded, and the center begins to empty, dig up the whole clump, divide it into 3-5 plants, and replant them, preferably in another area (renewal is multiplication, not loss: 3-4 masses after 3-4 years, 10 or more after 6-8 years) |
| Weeds | The only fundamental measure is to pick out perennial weed roots thoroughly before planting. Cover furrows with straw and dry grass, and pull weeds right after cutting or in early spring |
| Overwintering | Finish the last cutting in late October (stopping the cutting 3-4 weeks before frost lets nutrients build in the roots and decides next year's crop). Potassium fertilizer in November; in December leave the dry leaves or cover with straw; remove the cover in late February-March |

## 6. Pests, Diseases, and Nutrient Problems
| Pest or disease | Symptoms and conditions | Response without chemicals |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Sclerotinia rot | Base softens, white mycelium and black grains. Excess water, cold | Remove diseased plants together with the soil. Sclerotia remain in the soil, so do not use that spot for years |
| Downy mildew | Leaves turn yellow with gray mold underneath. Cold and damp (the biggest constraint in Allium) | Drainage and ventilation, 15-20 cm between plants |
| Leaf blight, rust, gray mold | Leaf tips dry / orange powdery spots / gray mold at the base | Remove diseased leaves out of the field, widen spacing, dry a day after cutting |
| Soft rot | Base softens and stinks (bacteria enter through cut wounds) | Avoid cutting on rainy days |
| Root maggots | Larvae eat the underground part and the plant wilts and dies (spring and autumn, excess water) | Do not add half-rotted compost and fix drainage. Scatter wood ash at the base |
| Leaf miners, aphids, thrips | Tunnels and white lines inside leaves / sucking sap and sooty mold / white spots | Remove damaged leaves and use a net / wash off with water or soapy water / keep from drying out, blue sticky traps |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Most diseases are linked to excess water, so the core of what can be done without chemicals is drainage (raise the bed and dig the ditch deep), 15-20 cm spacing, drying for a day after cutting, and removing diseased leaves. Beyond that, use soapy water, wood vinegar diluted 300-500 times (prevention), and an insect net (leaf miners and root maggot adults in spring and autumn).

| Symptom | Cause and action |
|:---|:---|
| All leaves pale green, thin, and short | Nitrogen shortage. Pour urine diluted 8-10 times in the furrow the day after cutting and lay well-rotted compost between plants |
| Leaves unusually large and tender and falling over easily | Excess nitrogen. Stop fertilizing (it has become weak to disease) |
| Edges of old leaves scorch brown | Potassium shortage. A handful or two of wood ash per ㎡ |
| Slow growth, leaves dark green and purplish | Phosphorus shortage (the same symptom appears in cold weather). Bone meal, wood ash |
| New leaves twisted and tips dry | Calcium shortage. Burnt and ground eggshells and shells |
| Leaves noticeably thin in spring, or thinner with each cutting | Too much autumn cutting, potassium shortage, accumulated nitrogen loss. Do not cut after late October, give potassium in November, and top dress after every cutting |

## 7. Intercropping and Crop Rotation
| Compatibility | Companions |
|:---|:---|
| Good | Tomato, eggplant, and pepper (garlic chive root exudates suppress wilt and blight), strawberry (less root rot), carrot (repel each other's pests), lettuce and spinach (use the empty space between chives and do not compete) |
| Poor | Legumes (soybean, pea, kidney bean: Allium exudates suppress nodule bacteria), other Allium (Welsh onion, garlic, onion: pathogens and pests accumulate) |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Garlic chive has continuous-cropping tolerance SS, so it needs no rotation. When renewing, move to the neighboring area, and do not plant Allium for 5 years or more where sclerotinia rot has occurred. A good spot has no pooling water, is on a path you pass often, lies outside the area tilled every year, and gets 6 hours or more of sun (the edge of a fruit-vegetable bed also gives an intercropping effect).

## 8. Harvest and Seed Saving
| Harvest | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Timing | A month after establishment if started from divided clumps, at a leaf length of 20-25 cm (if started from seed, the next spring; do not cut in the first year). After that, 5-7 times a year in the open at 10-20 day intervals (2-3 times in spring, 1-2 in summer, 2 in autumn). Stop in late October |
| Cutting height | Cut the first time leaving 3-4 cm above the ground, then raise the cut 1-1.5 cm above the previous cut each time, and start again at 3-4 cm the next spring |
| Method | Cut a clump neatly in one stroke with a sharp knife or scissors (a dull blade crushes the cut face and lets disease in). Do it in the morning after the dew has dried and avoid rainy days. Give no water or fertilizer for a day after cutting. Soil lodges at the base, so split and rinse |

> **Almost the only way to fail at harvesting garlic chive is to cut too close. If you cut to the ground without leaving 3-4 cm, the growing point is cut off and the next leaves do not come or come much later.**

| Seed saving | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Policy | Seed longevity is under a year, so stockpiling seed is not a workable strategy. The seed store of garlic chive is not a packet but the field, so plant living clumps in several places |
| Procedure | Set aside a few healthy plants in their third year or older, do not cut them that year, and leave the flower stalks (flowering August-September). When the pods dry brown and split and black seeds show (they fall by themselves, so do not miss the time), cut the whole stalk, hang it upside down, rub out the seeds, winnow off the chaff with a sieve or the wind, dry it in shade, and keep it in a sealed container with ash or charcoal in a cool, dark place |
| Longevity | Under a year sealed at room temperature (the limit is next spring), 1-2 years in a cool, dry place, and it falls quickly within months in a damp or hot place. The fat in the seed oxidizes easily |

## 9. Storage
| Method | Period | Conditions |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Leave in the field (storehouse) | All of April-October | Do not cut; cut only what is needed on the day |
| Wrapped in wet cloth in a cool place | 3-5 days | Lasts longer in a pit or ground cellar |
| Stood up with the base in water | 2-3 days | Laid down, the leaves bend and spoil quickly |
| Drying | 6 months to 1 year | Wash, drain completely, and cut to 3-5 cm (whole, the inside does not dry), then dry in a well-ventilated place in shade until it crumbles. In the sun, color and aroma are lost. Store in a sealed container with ash or charcoal |

| Pickling, fermenting | Method |
|:---|:---|
| Garlic chive kimchi | Salt until limp, mix with chili powder, and keep 1-2 months in a cool place |
| Salting, jjanji | Layer with salt at 5-10% of the weight for 3-6 months. Submerged in strong brine of 10% or more it keeps half a year or more (soak in water before eating to remove the salt) |
| Pickles | Soy-sauce pickles and soybean-paste pickles keep a few months. Flower-stalk pickles of buds picked in August and kept in brine or soy sauce also keep a few months |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The biggest loss in drying garlic chive is aroma.

## 10. Cooking and Precautions
| Method | Fuel | Tips |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Raw, quick salad | None | Dress it as it is after washing or serve with wraps; salt briefly and then dress. Aroma and vitamin C are intact |
| Blanched greens | Little | 20-30 seconds in boiling water (blanched long, it goes soft and loses aroma) |
| Soups, stews, pancakes | Moderate | Add just before turning off the heat (boiled long, the aroma disappears). For pancakes, mix into flour or grain flour batter and fry |
| Stir-fry, kimchi | Little, none | 1-2 minutes over high heat (beta-carotene and vitamin K are fat-soluble and need oil to be absorbed properly). Kimchi serves as storage and cooking at once |

> **There is one rule for cooking garlic chive: add it as late as possible and cook it as briefly as possible. Its aroma is weak to heat, and if boiled long, the reason you added it is gone.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;It is safe in ordinary amounts, but eating too much, or eating it raw on an empty stomach, can irritate the stomach lining with sulfur compounds and cause heartburn and diarrhea. It is rich in vitamin K, which affects blood clotting, and the whole genus Allium is toxic to dogs, cats, and some livestock, so keep pets from eating it.

| Item | Allium (garlic chive, ramps, wild chive) | Lily of the valley | Daffodil and spider lily types |
|:---|:---|:---|:---|
| Smell of a cut leaf | Always smells of onion or garlic | No smell | No smell or only a grassy smell |
| Leaves | Thin and soft, with several leaves overlapping at the base into a sheath. Parallel veins are not distinct | Thick, stiff, and glossy. Two leaves wrap each other as they come up | Thick and smooth. Flat leaves come up overlapping |
| When eaten | Edible | Vomiting, dizziness, abnormal heartbeat | Severe vomiting, abdominal pain, and diarrhea |

> **Cut one leaf, rub it, and smell it; if it does not smell of onion or garlic, it is not an Allium. This one check filters out almost all the poisonous plants that resemble Allium.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Garlic chive grown in a field has no such risk.

## 11. Annual Work Schedule
| Month | Work |
|:---|:---|
| Jan-Feb | Overwintering as roots. Check the cover and keep people from stepping on the spot |
| March | Remove the cover and tidy dry leaves. First fertilizer, mainly nitrogen. Best time for dividing clumps |
| Apr-May | First harvest, cutting leaving 3-4 cm above the ground (a day after cutting, fertilizer and water). Second and third harvests in May, raising the cut 1-1.5 cm each time. Top dressing at every cutting |
| June-July | Keep harvesting. Fix the drainage ditch before the rainy season. In the heat of July the leaves get tough, so cook them or turn them into kimchi |
| Aug-Sep | When flower stalks come up, remove them every 7-10 days and harvest and eat the buds (leave the seed plants). Resume the autumn harvest in September (the best quality) and divide clumps at the best time |
| Oct-Dec | Finish the last harvest in late October, and take seed once the flower stalks for seed dry. Overwintering fertilizer, mainly potassium, in November. In December leave the dry leaves as a cover or cover with straw, and live on stored kimchi |

> **Five things not to forget: get living clumps rather than seed, cut leaving 3-4 cm above the ground, remove the flower stalks in August, stop cutting in late October and give potassium in November, and choose a spot where water does not pool.**
