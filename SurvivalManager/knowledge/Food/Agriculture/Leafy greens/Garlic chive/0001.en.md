## Introduction

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Garlic chive (Allium tuberosum) is a perennial herb in the genus Allium, family Amaryllidaceae. It belongs to the same group as green onion, garlic, onion, and shallot, and among them is the only one grown for its leaves alone, never dug up for its root. Below ground it keeps a bulb and a short rhizome, so even after the leaves above are cut away, new leaves push up from beneath. Once established, it comes back every year without anyone tending it, and it is not unusual to find a garlic chive clump holding the same corner of a garden for more than ten years.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This trait decides garlic chive's place in disaster preparedness. Most vegetables start over from seed every year: if the seed runs out, the sowing window is missed, or germination fails, that year's crop is lost. Garlic chive breaks that chain. Because its root overwinters and the same clump comes up again the following year, it needs no seed, no sowing, and no seedling-raising. The evaluation table's SS for perenniality and SS for continuous-cropping tolerance point to this trait, and it is also the backbone behind the strong overall score: grade S, 52.2 points.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Cold hardiness SS locks that backbone in place. Even below -20°C, the roots underground survive and come straight back up the following spring. When the parts above ground wither in winter, that is not death but a retreat: the plant drops its leaves and pulls back into the root, then comes out again when spring arrives. A crop like this is rare in a situation with no heating and no facilities. Infrastructure needs A and tillage & digging energy A follow the same logic. Since one planting lasts 5 years, the labor of tilling the field is amortized across those 5 years, and the yearly digging energy falls to 26kcal per 1m², giving an energy return of 34.5×.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;But garlic chive's evaluation table has one unexpected weak point buried in it. Micronutrients score D. Only 8 nutrients clear 10% of the daily recommended intake. That number cuts against the common belief that garlic chive is a nutritional powerhouse, and this document does not hide the mismatch. Garlic chive is not a multivitamin. Where kale fills 18 nutrients, garlic chive fills 8. The reason to grow garlic chive lies elsewhere, not in nutrient density.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;That something else is aroma and time. The low D grade for taste comes from a finding that the smell and aroma are off-putting, but that judgment flips in a disaster. The sulfur compounds in garlic chive are one of the few ways to lift flavor at a table short on salt and soy sauce, and they mask the smell of aging or fishy ingredients. When the same porridge or the same boiled grain keeps going for months, what breaks a person is not nutrition but a palate worn thin. A handful of garlic chive pushes that point back. And on the time side, the key fact is that it can be cut and eaten again and again, 5 to 7 times a year, at intervals of ten to twenty days from April through October.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There are two more weak points. Storability is E: cut leaves turn soft and limp within two or three days at room temperature. And seed longevity is D: seed in the genus Allium does not last past 1 year at room temperature. Stockpiling seed to use years later is not a strategy that works for this crop. The answer to this weakness is clump division, and the fact that garlic chive was never really a seed-propagated crop to begin with, but a clump-propagated one, is itself the solution.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In short, garlic chive is a crop you can set aside two or three pyeong (about 6.6-9.9 m²) in one corner of the field for, and then all but forget about. Its calories and nutrient density are nothing special, but it is the first green thing to come up in early spring when nothing else has, keeps giving a handful every ten days all summer, does not freeze to death in winter, and keeps going without seed. When no one knows how many years a disaster will last, that persistence alone offsets a great deal.
