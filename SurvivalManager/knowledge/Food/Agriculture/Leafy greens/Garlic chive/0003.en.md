## 2. What Kind of Plant Is This?

### 2-1. Taxonomy and Origin

| Item | Details |
|---|---|
| Scientific name | Allium tuberosum |
| Taxonomy | Order Asparagales, family Amaryllidaceae, genus Allium |
| Other names | jeongguji, sol, jol, buchae, guchae (韭菜) |
| Origin | Northern China to Northeast Asia |
| Growth habit | Perennial with a persistent rootstock. The above-ground parts wither in winter while the roots overwinter |
| Propagation | Both seed and clump division are possible; in practice, clump division is the main method |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The genus Allium is a large group that includes green onion, garlic, onion, shallot, dallae (Korean wild chive), and sanmanul (mountain garlic). What unites this group is a distinctive sulfur-containing smell compound, which the plant originally evolved as a chemical weapon to defend itself from insects and fungi that feed on it. When the tissue is injured, enzymes activate and produce the smell compound, which then blocks microbial growth. This is why the smell surges up the moment you cut garlic chive.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This has two implications for cultivation. One is that garlic chive itself has relatively few pests and diseases. The other is that its exudates remain in the soil where it grew and suppress soil-borne disease in the next crop. The fact that the evaluation table's non-food value D is filled by the single criterion of pest control points to this second effect.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The fact that its native range is northern China and Northeast Asia directly explains garlic chive's cold hardiness. It is a plant built to survive in regions where the ground freezes deep in winter, and dropping its leaves entirely to retreat into the root was the answer to that. The evaluation table's figure of withstanding -20°C is not a special ability but simply the condition of its homeland.

### 2-2. Appearance

| Organ | Feature |
|---|---|
| Leaf | Flat and linear. 20-40cm long, 0.5-1cm wide. Flat in cross-section |
| Below ground | A short bulb and a short rhizome that spreads sideways. New shoots arise from here |
| Root | Fibrous roots, concentrated within 20cm depth |
| Flower | Small white flowers cluster in an umbrella shape. August to September |
| Flower stalk | Thicker and firmer than the leaf, and solid inside |
| Seed | Small, black, angular seeds |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;That the leaf is flat is the first thing that tells garlic chive apart from Welsh onion. Welsh onion's leaf is a hollow tube, while garlic chive's leaf is a flat, solid strip. Pressing it between your fingers tells you right away. Cut a garlic chive leaf and the cross-section is thin like a line; cut a Welsh onion leaf and you see a round hole.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The fact that the rhizome spreads sideways to produce new shoots decides almost everything about growing garlic chive. Plant one clump, and a new shoot appears beside it; that spreads sideways again, and after a few years it becomes one solid mass. This spread is what supplies material for clump division, but it is also, after a few years, the reason the clump gets too crowded and loses vigor. This is why the clump renewal covered later becomes necessary.

### 2-3. Telling Allium Relatives Apart

| Name | Scientific name | Leaf cross-section | Part eaten | Feature |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Garlic chive | Allium tuberosum | Flat, solid | Leaf, flower stalk | Perennial. Leaves are cut repeatedly |
| Welsh onion | Allium fistulosum | Round, hollow | Blanched base, leaf | Biennial. Hilling produces the white part |
| Shallot (jjokpa) | Allium × wakegi | Round, hollow | Leaf, bulb | Sets no seed; propagated only by bulb |
| Dallae | Allium monanthum | Thin, round | Leaf, bulb | Wild. Appears in early spring |
| Sanmanul | Allium microdictyon | Broad, flat | Leaf | Grows wild in the mountains. Leaves are about palm-sized |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This table matters because, in a disaster, you are likely to find Allium plants in the mountains and fields rather than in a garden. Every species in the genus Allium is edible, so finding any of them is never a loss. There is, however, one thing to watch for, covered in Section 14.

### 2-4. Growth Stages

| Stage | Timing | What happens | Mainly needed |
|---|---|---|---|
| Germination and seedling stage | 4 weeks from sowing | One thread-thin leaf appears. Very slow | Phosphorus, a little nitrogen |
| Early growth | 4 to 12 weeks | Leaf count increases and the rhizome becomes established | Nitrogen |
| Clump expansion | 1 year after transplanting | New shoots form sideways, becoming a clump | Nitrogen, potassium |
| Harvest stage | From year 2 | April to October, putting out leaves at intervals of ten to twenty days | Nitrogen, potassium |
| Flowering and fruiting stage | August to September | Flower stalks come up and seed sets. The clump loses vigor | Phosphorus, potassium |
| Overwintering stage | November to March of the following year | The above-ground parts wither and die while the roots overwinter | Potassium |

> **The first year of garlic chive is not a year for harvesting but a year for building roots. Cutting it greedily several times in the first year keeps the clump from establishing, and next year's yield drops sharply.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The first-year growth of garlic chive started from seed is frustratingly slow. Even after months, all you have is a few thread-thin leaves. This is because garlic chive grows by building its below-ground parts before its above-ground parts. Out of sight, the bulb and rhizome are growing, and only once those are fully formed do the leaves thicken. Real harvesting begins from the second year. This slowness is exactly what makes garlic chive a crop you need to plant well before a disaster, not right before one.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Starting from clump division skips this sequence. Because you are transplanting a rhizome and roots that are already formed, it puts out leaves right after establishing. This is also why the evaluation table's seed saving SS is based on clump division rather than seed collection.

| Calcium | High | |
| Iron | High | |
| Phosphorus | High | |
| Vitamin A | Abundant, in the form of beta-carotene | Absorption improves when eaten with oil |
| Vitamin C | Abundant | More than half is lost when blanched |
| Vitamin K | Abundant | Involved in blood clotting and bone health |
| Folate | Abundant | |

| Sulfur compounds (alliin family) | Present | Source of the smell and antimicrobial action |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The energy figure spans as wide a range as 22-46kcal because there are several species within the genus Allium and tissue firmness varies with the season. Tender garlic chive cut first in spring is high in moisture and low in energy, while thick, tough garlic chive grown in summer has more dry matter and is higher in energy. The 46kcal used in the evaluation is the higher-end value.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The sulfur compounds in the last row are what make garlic chive what it is. This compound forms through enzyme action as the tissue is cut, and it has antimicrobial and antioxidant effects. It is no accident that garlic chive has long been called "the liver's vegetable" and regarded as a warming food. It is heat-sensitive, though, and a large share breaks down if it is cooked too long. There is a reason the custom of adding garlic chive last and then turning off the heat became fixed practice.

## 3. Growing Conditions

### 3-1. Temperature

| Category | Temperature | Result |
|---|:---:|---|
| Minimum for germination | 10°C | Below this, it will not germinate |
| Optimal germination temperature | Around 20°C | Sprouts in 7-14 days. On the slow side |
| Optimal growing temperature | 18-20°C | Spring and autumn are when it grows best |
| Growable range | 5-30°C | Range 25°C. Basis for temperature range SS |
| Heat stress | 25°C or above | Leaves toughen and fiber increases |
| Growth stops from cold | 5°C or below | |
| Above-ground die-back | Around the first frost | The roots remain alive |
| Root overwintering limit | Survives even at -20°C or below | Basis for cold hardiness SS |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The fact that garlic chive grows best at 18-20°C sets the rhythm of harvest. That range falls in spring, April to June, and autumn, September to October, and in practice most harvests cluster in those two periods. In the height of summer it keeps growing, but the leaves toughen and the aroma turns harsh.

> **When garlic chive disappears in winter, it is not dying but retreating. Even after the above-ground parts dry out and turn brown, that spot must not be tilled under.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Not knowing this, people often till under the dried garlic chive spot in winter, only to find nothing comes up the next year. Marking the spot in the field, or leaving the dried leaves uncut and letting them lie as cover, keeps you from losing the spot. Leaving the dried leaves as cover also insulates the area around the roots, so it serves two purposes at once.

### 3-2. Light

| Condition | Relative yield | Interpretation |
|---|:---:|---|
| Full direct sun all day | 100% | This is the baseline |
| 6+ hours a day | Nearly 100% | The practical minimum line |
| 40% shade | About 86% | CI 61-120%. Basis for sunlight B |
| Partial shade | Lower yield, leaves become tender | Aroma weakens and color fades |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Sunlight B means garlic chive is relatively tolerant of shade. Even with 40% of light blocked, yield stays at around 86%. That contrasts with purslane, which is cut in half to 45% under the same condition. This difference gives practical room in placement: garlic chive can go in a spot that gets no sun all day, such as along the base of a wall or on the north side of a tall crop.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;However, garlic chive grown in shade has tender leaves, a pale color, and a weak aroma. That is an advantage from the standpoint of getting something tender, but it is also a loss when you consider that the reason to grow garlic chive is its aroma. In fact, there is a method called blanched garlic chive (yeonbaek-buchu), which deliberately blocks light to grow it pale yellow; this reduces its aroma and pungency, making it milder.

### 3-3. Water and Soil

| Item | Standard | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Soil texture | Well-drained sandy loam to loam | Not very particular about soil type |
| Soil pH | pH 6.0-6.5 | 6.3-6.5 is best |
| Organic matter | Plenty of well-rotted compost | A heavy-feeding crop |
| Drainage | Very important | One of the crops most vulnerable to waterlogging |
| Irrigation | Total ET about 500mm | Relatively tolerant of drought |
| Bed height | 15-20cm or more | 25cm on poorly drained soil |

> **What ruins garlic chive growing is almost always excess moisture, not drought. In a spot where water pools during the monsoon season (jangma, late June to July in South Korea), even a clump grown for 5 years can vanish entirely within a few weeks.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Garlic chive's roots are fibrous roots concentrated within 20cm of depth, and when this shallow root layer is submerged, oxygen is cut off. On top of that, a circumstance specific to the genus Allium compounds the problem. Below-ground parts weakened by submersion become targets for sclerotinia rot and root rot, and because these are fungi that persist in the soil for many years, once they take hold, the whole area becomes unusable for a long time. Since this is a crop used in the same spot for 5 years, a disease getting in means a 5-year loss.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;So the first thing to check when choosing a spot for garlic chive is whether water pools there after rain. It is right to give garlic chive the highest, best-draining part of the field, and that decision governs the next 5 years. Building the bed up high and digging the drainage channel deep only has to be done once, so it is better not to skimp on that spot.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;On acidity, it prefers pH 6.0-6.5, in other words close to neutral. Fields in South Korea get a lot of rain and so tend naturally toward acidity, so if you have lime, it is best to work it in before planting to correct this. If you have no lime, burned and ground shell and eggshell, or wood ash, can substitute. Wood ash also supplies potassium, so it suits garlic chive especially well.
