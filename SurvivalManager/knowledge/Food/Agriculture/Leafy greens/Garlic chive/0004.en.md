## 4. Varieties

### 4-1. Characteristics by Strain

| Strain | Leaf | Cold hardiness | Yield | Disaster suitability |
|---|---|:---:|:---:|---|
| Broad-leaf strain (wide-leaf garlic chive) | Broad and thick | Average | High | Yields a lot at each cutting and is easy to trim |
| Landrace (narrow-leaf strain, Joseon garlic chive) | Narrow, with a strong aroma | Strong | Medium | Best adapted to open-field conditions, with reliable overwintering |

| Variety | Feature | Yield | Suitable environment |
|---|---|:---:|---|
| Taiwan garlic chive | Tillers vigorously and resists disease well | High | Both open field and under cover |
| Mongolian garlic chive | Especially strong cold hardiness | Medium | Cold regions |
| Landrace (Joseon garlic chive) | Strong aroma, reliable overwintering | Medium | Across South Korea |
| Manchurian garlic chive | Deep dormancy gives long harvest intervals | Medium | Open field |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Garlic chive's varietal distinctions are not as sharp as those of other vegetables. That is because, for a long time, it was not a crop whose seed was bought and sold but one whose clumps were obtained from a neighbor and planted. A strain that has circulated within one village for decades often carries that region's name, and that strain has already been filtered to fit that region's climate.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;It is worth pinning down the concept of dormancy here. In winter, garlic chive drops its above-ground parts and leaves only the root, and how deeply it sleeps at that point varies by strain. The Manchurian garlic chive strain, with deep dormancy, wakes late in spring and regrows slowly even after waking. In exchange, it is safer against winter cold. The Taiwan garlic chive strain, with shallow dormancy, comes up early in spring and regrows quickly, but it can wake half-heartedly during a mild winter and then take damage when the cold returns.

### 4-2. Selection Criteria by Situation

| Situation | Choice | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| A neighbor or a nearby spot has garlic chive | Get clumps divided from it | It is a strain already adapted to that area, and you can harvest from the first year |
| Want to increase yield | Taiwan garlic chive, broad-leaf strain | Tillers vigorously with broad leaves |
| Central South Korea and further north, cold regions | Mongolian garlic chive, landrace | Deep dormancy and reliable overwintering |
| Aroma and flavor matter most | Landrace | High sulfur-compound content gives a strong aroma |
| Want the earliest harvest in spring | Taiwan garlic chive | Shallow dormancy means it wakes early |
| Only seed is available | Any strain | Give up harvesting in the first year and build roots instead |

> **Far more important than choosing a variety of garlic chive is securing living clumps rather than seed. Because seed longevity is under 1 year, stockpiled seed is likely already dead by the time a disaster strikes.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;What this means is clear. Garlic chive is not a crop you secure after a disaster begins, but one you plant beforehand. Set aside even a single pyeong (about 3.3 m²), keep a clump alive there, and that clump multiplies year after year until, a few years on, there is enough to divide and replant. One plot of living garlic chive in the field is more certain than ten packets of seed.

## 5. Field Preparation and Fertilizer

### 5-1. Preparing the Field

| Item | Standard | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Tillage depth | 20-30cm | Since the spot will be used for 5 years, till deep the first time |
| Bed height | 15-20cm | 25cm or more on poorly drained soil |
| Bed width | 90-120cm | Based on planting two or three rows |
| Drainage channel | Must be secured | Waterlogging is the single biggest cause of failure |
| Acidity correction | Target pH 6.0-6.5 | Work it in 2-3 weeks before planting |
| Stones and roots | Pick them out beforehand | This spot will not be tilled again for 5 years |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;What sets garlic chive's field preparation apart from other crops is that this work is done only once every 5 years. The evaluation table's tillage & digging energy A comes from that fact. But if you do that one time carelessly, you suffer for 5 years. Preparing a field for a perennial crop is not building one season's crop but laying the foundation for several years, so it pays off in the end not to skimp on the labor.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;It is especially important to pick out the roots of perennial weeds. If a weed that spreads by root, such as horsetail or bindweed, gets into a garlic chive bed, it tangles among the garlic chive clumps with no way left to pull it out. Since garlic chive's field is not tilled for 5 years, there is no chance to bury and kill weeds in the soil, which is exactly why getting it clean at the start is especially valuable.

### 5-2. Fertilizer Amounts

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Garlic chive is a heavy-feeding crop. Its growing season is long and its leaves are cut five or six times a year, so a lot of nutrients leave the field. The evaluation table's fertilizer grade B comes from the figure that N, P, and K together need about 24g per 1m².

| Nutrient | Per 1,000m² | Per 1m² | Note |
|---|:---:|:---:|---|
| Nitrogen (N) | 20-25kg | 20-25g | Split between base fertilizer and top-dressing |
| Phosphorus (P) | 12-15kg | 12-15g | All as base fertilizer |
| Potassium (K) | 15-20kg | 15-20g | Split between base fertilizer and top-dressing |
| Compost | 1,500-2,000kg | 1.5-2kg | Well-rotted compost |
| Lime | 100-150kg | 100-150g | For correcting acidic soil |

| Category | Timing | Details |
|---|---|---|
| Base fertilizer | 1-2 weeks before planting | All the compost, all the phosphorus, half the nitrogen and potassium |
| Top-dressing | Every time it is cut | Mainly nitrogen and potassium |
| Before overwintering | November | Mainly potassium |
| When it wakes in spring | March | Mainly nitrogen |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Adding top-dressing every time you cut is the core of garlic chive fertilization. Cutting the leaves is an act of removing nitrogen from the field, and this drain builds up with every round. If you keep cutting without replenishing, the leaves from the third or fourth cutting onward come out thin and pale. This is the phenomenon behind the evaluation table's yield retention without synthetic inputs figure of C, 40.8%.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There is also a reason for applying mainly potassium in November. Potassium raises osmotic pressure inside the cell, lowering the freezing point and thickening the cell wall. It is the nutrient a plant needs to prepare for winter, the way a person prepares for winter with gimjang (making kimchi for the winter), and without enough of it, cold hardiness actually drops. The ability to withstand -20°C is innate, but for that ability to be fully expressed, potassium has to be there in autumn.

### 5-3. Disaster-Substitute Fertilizers

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;When chemical fertilizer runs out, the first thing to show it in garlic chive is the thickness of the leaves. They get thinner, shorter, and paler. Since this is a crop harvested from the same spot for 5 years, if you do not build a feedback cycle of returning nutrients, it gets worse year after year.

| Substitute resource | Nutrients supplied | How to use | Caution |
|---|---|---|---|
| Human urine | Nitrogen (mainly), potassium, phosphorus | Dilute 8-10× with water and pour into the furrow right after cutting | Burns if it touches the leaves. Wait a day for the cut wound to heal |
| Well-rotted compost | Nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, organic matter | Plenty as base fertilizer; as top-dressing, lay it between clumps | Under-composted material attracts root maggots |
| Wood ash | Potassium, calcium, phosphorus | One or two handfuls per 1m² after cutting | Alkaline. Helps bring the soil closer to the neutral pH garlic chive likes |
| Sesame/perilla oil cake | Nitrogen, phosphorus | Scatter between clumps after cutting, or use a fermented liquid | Scattering it raw damages roots with fermentation heat |
| Bone meal | Phosphorus, calcium | Burn and grind, then mix into the base fertilizer | Effects show slowly |
| Liquid plant fertilizer | Nitrogen, potassium | Soak cut weeds in water, ferment 2-3 weeks, then dilute 5× | Has a strong smell |
| Shell and eggshell | Calcium | Burn and grind, use for pH correction | A substitute for lime |
| Legume as the preceding crop | Nitrogen | Grow soybean the year before planting garlic chive and bury the residue | Since the spot will be used for 5 years, this is the only chance, before planting |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Pay close attention to the last row. Once garlic chive is planted, that spot will not be tilled again for 5 years, so turning in green manure or using a preceding crop has only one chance: before planting. So if you have decided on the garlic chive spot, the sensible order is to grow soybean or pea there the year before, leave nitrogen behind via their root nodules, bury the residue, and then plant garlic chive. Nitrogen stored this way is released little by little over the whole 5 years.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;It is also worth noting that wood ash suits garlic chive especially well. Garlic chive likes pH 6.0-6.5, and field soil in South Korea is generally more acidic than that. Because wood ash is alkaline, it raises the pH while also supplying potassium. Scattering ash over the cut area right after cutting even has a disinfecting effect on the wound. However, adding a lot at once blocks the uptake of boron and iron, so keep to one or two handfuls per 1m², no more than three or four times a year.

## 6. Planting - Seeds and Clump Division

### 6-1. Timing

| Propagation method | Planting time | First harvest | Note |
|---|---|---|---|
| Seed sowing (spring) | Mid-March to early April | The following spring | The first year is for building roots |
| Seed sowing (autumn) | Mid-August to early September | The following spring | Overwinters while still young |
| Transplanting seedlings | May to mid-June | That autumn or the following spring | Finish before the monsoon season (jangma, late June to July in South Korea) |
| Clump division | March to June, or September | One month after establishing | The fastest and most reliable method |

> **Clump division is the standard way to start garlic chive. Starting from seed takes 1 year to first harvest; starting from a clump takes only a month.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This difference is decisive in a disaster. If garlic chive exists somewhere nearby, digging it up and dividing it to plant is roughly ten times faster than sowing seed. Garlic chive is an extremely widespread crop, and the evaluation table's prevalence in South Korea SS reflects that fact. It is common to find garlic chive clumps surviving in an abandoned garden plot, the yard of an empty house, a rice-field embankment, or the edge of a field. In winter the above-ground parts wither and it is not visible, but it reveals itself once spring comes.

### 6-2. Clump Division

| Item | Standard |
|---|---|
| When to dig | March-April when new shoots come up, or September |
| How to dig | Use a spade to cut a wide circle around the roots and lift the whole clump out |
| Division unit | One cluster of 3-5 shoots |
| Leaf trimming | Cut, leaving only about 10cm of the above-ground part |
| Root trimming | Trim back the tips of any overly long roots a little |
| Planting depth | 5-8cm. Keep the growing point from being buried in soil |
| Row spacing | 20cm |
| Clump spacing | 15-20cm |
| After planting | Firm the soil down and water thoroughly |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There is a reason for cutting off the above-ground part before planting. Transplanting cuts the roots and greatly reduces their ability to draw up water, and if the leaves are left as they are in that state, more moisture leaves through the leaves than comes in, and the clump dries out. Reducing the leaves restores that balance. It is the same principle as pruning branches when transplanting a tree.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Keeping the growing point from being buried also matters. Garlic chive's growing point sits above the bulb, at the spot where the leaves begin to emerge. If this part is buried deep in soil, the new leaves either have to fight their way up through the soil or rot outright. The standard is to bury the roots while keeping the growing point level with the soil surface.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The reason to plant in clusters of 3-5 shoots is that garlic chive is a plant that grows in clumps. Planted one shoot at a time, it takes a long time to establish; with several shoots together, they support each other and build strength quickly. This cluster grows and, a few years later, becomes material for dividing again.

### 6-3. Sowing Seed

| Item | Standard |
|---|---|
| Sowing depth | 0.5-1cm |
| Days to germinate | 7-14 days at 20°C |
| Germination temperature | Around 20°C. Will not germinate at 10°C or below |
| Row spacing | 20cm |
| Per hole | 2-3 seeds |
| First thinning | At 2-3 true leaves, to 5cm spacing |
| Second thinning | At 4-5 true leaves, final spacing 15-20cm |
| First-year care | Grow without cutting |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Garlic chive seed germinates slowly and unevenly. It is common for nothing to have come up two weeks after sowing, leading people to assume it failed and sow again, when in most cases it is simply still on its way up. Keep the topsoil from drying out and wait up to 3 weeks.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Remember too that seed freshness is decisive. As seed longevity D indicates, garlic chive seed does not last 1 year at room temperature. Seed from last year already has a much lower germination rate, and seed 2 years old barely comes up at all. If you have to start from seed, use seed obtained or received that same year.

### 6-4. Seed Treatment

| Method | Procedure | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Hot-water soak | Soak in 50-55°C water for 10-15 minutes, then cool in cold water | Kills surface pathogens |
| Ash coating | Coat wet seed with sieved wood ash | Also disinfects the surface |
| Water soak | Overnight in room-temperature water | Speeds germination by a few days |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;For the hot-water soak, temperature is everything. Above 60°C the seed dies, and below 45°C it is not disinfected. Without a thermometer, mixing boiling water and cold water roughly half and half gives you something around 50°C: hot but bearable when you dip your hand in. Still, treating by feel risks losing all your seed, so it is safer to treat only part of what you have and plant it in batches.

## 7. Water Management

### 7-1. Water by Growth Stage

| Timing | Management | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Right after planting | Once, thoroughly | Brings roots and soil into close contact |
| Establishment period (2-3 weeks) | When the surface soil dries | The period when roots become established |
| Growing period | 1-2 times a week, deeply each time | Shallow, frequent watering makes roots shallow |
| Right after cutting | Once, a day later | Water after the cut has healed |
| Monsoon season | None | Maintaining drainage channels is the water management for this period |
| Overwintering period | None | Wet soil that freezes damages the roots |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There is a reason not to water right after cutting. The cut spot on a leaf is an open wound, and fungi enter along with water through that wound. This is why sclerotinia rot and soft rot occur often right after cutting. It is safer to wait about a day for the wound to dry and heal before watering or fertilizing. Avoiding cutting on a rainy day is for the same reason.

### 7-2. Symptoms of Too Little and Too Much Water

| Symptom | Cause | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| Leaf tips dry out and thin | Water shortage | It recovers, but that leaf does not come back |
| Leaves short and tough | Sustained water shortage | Fiber increases and texture worsens |
| Leaves suddenly yellow and collapse | Waterlogging, root rot | The below-ground parts are already damaged |
| White mold and black granules at the base | Sclerotinia rot | Sclerotia remain in the soil for many years |
| Leaf base softens with a smell | Soft rot | Fungi entered through a cutting wound |
| Small white maggots near the roots | Root maggots | Caused by under-composted material and excess moisture |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The fact that the bottom four rows of this table are all tied to excess moisture tells you something about the nature of water management for garlic chive. If water is short, the leaves just get smaller; if water is excessive, the whole clump can vanish. The scale of the damage is asymmetric. That is why it is always safer to err on the dry side with garlic chive, and this is where it differs from other leafy vegetables.

### 7-3. Mulching

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Without plastic film, mulching materials are straw, dry grass, fallen leaves, and rice husk. For garlic chive, mulching plays a different role in summer than in winter.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Summer mulching is mainly for suppressing weeds. Because garlic chive's field is not tilled for 5 years, weeds keep accumulating, and weeds growing among garlic chive clumps get their roots tangled together, making them hard to pull. Covering the furrows between clumps with straw greatly reduces this problem. However, covering the tops of the garlic chive clumps themselves worsens ventilation and invites disease, so mulch should be laid only in the furrows.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Winter mulching is for insulation. Once the above-ground parts dry out in November, either leave the dried leaves uncut and in place, or cover the area with 5-10cm of straw and fallen leaves. Since this crop withstands -20 degrees, it will not die, but repeated freezing and thawing lifts the soil and can push roots up toward the surface, causing frost-heave damage. Covering it prevents this. If you remove the cover before new shoots come up in spring, the soil temperature rises faster and harvest comes a few days earlier.
