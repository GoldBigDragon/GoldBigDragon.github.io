## 1. Disaster-Preparedness Evaluation

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Garlic chive's overall grade is S, with a score of 52.2 points. Broken down by scenario, its short-term ability to get through the early stage of a disaster is S (52.4), its long-term ability to be self-sufficient over several years is S (51.9), and its supplementary value as a side-dish resource beyond a staple food is SS (61.1). Few crops stand with short-term and long-term scores this close together. It means the crop's performance stays even from start to finish, and this is the number that best sums up garlic chive's character.

### 1-3. What It Means That Short-Term and Long-Term Match

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The most striking thing in this table is how close the two numbers, short-term 52.4 and long-term 51.9, sit to each other. For most crops, the two pull far apart. A crop like potato, which delivers calories quickly early on but falls off later, scores high short-term; a crop like a fruit tree, which takes several years to establish, scores high long-term. Garlic chive leans toward neither.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The reason lies in how garlic chive produces. Speed to harvest is A: 90 days in the first year from sowing is fast enough to be useful for an early response, and planting by clump division is even faster. Alongside that, perenniality SS carries straight through as persistence lasting 5+ years from a single planting. It is not a crop that spends its strength early and then fades, nor one that only picks up steam late. From the first year to the fifth, it puts out leaves at about the same pace.

> **When you cannot predict how long a disaster will last, the most valuable crop is not the one that is best at any single point, but the one that is never bad at any point. Garlic chive holds that place.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Added to this is yield stability A, with year-to-year variation of 6.3%. That means the harvest does not swing wildly from year to year. Set against purslane's 24.8%, the difference is stark. There is a wide gap between a crop you can use to plan next year's food around this year's harvest and one you cannot, and garlic chive is on the side you can plan around.

### 1-4. How to Read the Strengths

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The criteria marked SS are seven: perenniality, cooking & processing, temperature range, cold hardiness, continuous-cropping tolerance, prevalence in South Korea, and seed saving. A went to speed to harvest, infrastructure needs, tillage & digging energy, and yield stability.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Perenniality SS and continuous-cropping tolerance SS are essentially the same story told from two sides. For most crops, planting the same thing in the same spot year after year invites disease and drains the soil, and should be avoided. Garlic chive is the opposite. Keeping it in one spot for 5+ years is the normal way to grow it, and moving it often actually costs time in re-establishment. This changes how you plan the field itself. The garlic chive spot drops out of the crop-rotation plan and becomes a fixed plot.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Cold hardiness SS is one of the standout grades in this table. The roots underground survive even below -20°C. That is a full grade above kale, which holds up to -10°C, and it means the plant can overwinter outdoors anywhere in South Korea. Thanks to this trait, there is no need to worry about sourcing seedlings again each winter. And garlic chive is the first green thing to come up in a field where nothing else has appeared yet in spring. In late March to early April in South Korea, when winter-stored food is running out and spring vegetables cannot yet be planted, it is often the only fresh vegetable the field can provide.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;It is also worth noting how tillage & digging energy A comes about. Deep tillage of the full surface to 12cm is done once, but that labor is amortized over 5 years, so the digging energy that comes due each year falls to 26kcal per 1m². The result is an energy return of 34.5×: the calculation is that 34 times the effort put into turning the soil comes back as food, a large gap compared with purslane's 3.7× and kale's 12.9×. When you are farming with a single spade, this multiple is, in effect, your body's energy budget.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;It also matters that the basis for seed saving SS is not seed collection but clump division. This pairs with seed longevity D, which comes up later. Garlic chive seed does not keep long, but garlic chive clumps multiply the more you divide them. It is a structure where the field itself becomes the seedling store, instead of depending on a seed store, and in a world where seed supply chains have broken down, this structure carries enormous value.

### 1-5. How to Read the Weaknesses

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The criterion marked E is storability alone; D went to micronutrients, non-food value, taste, and seed longevity; and C went to yield retention without synthetic inputs.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Micronutrients D is the criterion most worth paying attention to. Garlic chive is widely believed to be a healthy vegetable, but by micronutrient density per calorie it ranks in the lower-middle tier among leafy vegetables. Eight nutrients clear 10% of the daily recommended intake: vitamin A, vitamin C, thiamine, riboflavin, calcium, iron, phosphorus, and potassium make up that list. Set against kale's 18, that is less than half. What this fact means is clear: garlic chive alone cannot prevent winter vitamin deficiency. It needs to be planted alongside a high-density leafy vegetable such as kale or leaf mustard, and if you had to pick only one on nutritional grounds, it would not be garlic chive.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Storability E is a problem for the whole Allium genus, not just garlic chive. On top of a base period grade of E, the measured FAO median storage loss for the same genus, 6.50%, is high enough to knock it down one more grade. Cut garlic chive turns soft within two days at room temperature and its characteristic smell grows stronger. The tissue is tender and moisture-rich, and as its sulfur compounds break down they hasten their own spoilage. However, this weakness is largely neutralized when paired with the perenniality discussed earlier. Garlic chive standing in the field does not spoil. If you cut only as much as you need on the day you need it, the problem of storage never even arises.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Seed longevity D is a weakness of a different character. Allium seed rarely lasts past 1 year at room temperature. That is the exact opposite of purslane seed, which can live for decades in the soil. The strategy of storing seed in a jar for a few years and using it later does not work for garlic chive. So putting garlic chive on a disaster-preparedness list means securing living clumps, not stockpiling seed. This distinction matters enormously in practice. Whether or not you have garlic chive clumps in the field right now becomes a condition you cannot undo once a disaster hits.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Yield retention without synthetic inputs C, 40.8%, means that cutting off chemical fertilizer drops yield to 40%. Garlic chive is a heavy feeder, and because cutting the leaves removes nitrogen from the field each time, the leaves get thinner with each round if nothing is added back. The very advantage of a crop planted once and harvested for 5 years also means the drain on soil fertility continues for those same 5 years. Section 5 covers this cycle.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Taste D is a criterion open to interpretation. The finding is that its smell and aroma are off-putting, and it is true that quite a few people dislike the smell of garlic chive's characteristic sulfur compounds. But this grade is set based on ordinary, everyday preference. At a table where seasoning, oil, and meat have disappeared, a strong aroma becomes an asset instead. Section 16 returns to this reversal.
