## 12. Harvest - Cutting Height Is Everything

### 12-1. Judging Timing

| Category | Standard |
|---|---|
| First harvest (started from seed) | The spring after planting. Do not cut in the first year |
| First harvest (clump division) | One month after establishing, at leaf length 20-25cm |
| Subsequent harvest interval | 10-20 days. 25-30 days by the evaluation table's standard |
| Times per year | 5-7 in the open field |
| Seasonal distribution | 2-3 times in spring (April-June), 1-2 times in summer (July-August), 2 times in autumn (September-October) |
| Harvest stop | Late October. 3-4 weeks before frost |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Leaf length of 20-25cm is the practical standard. Left longer, the leaf tips dry out and fiber increases, making it tough; cut earlier, the clump gets no chance to rest and loses vigor. In summer, growth is fast enough to reach this length in ten days; in spring and autumn, it takes fifteen to twenty days.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Summer harvest is allotted fewer times than spring or autumn not because growth is poor, but because of quality. Above 25°C, garlic chive grows fast, but its fiber thickens and its aroma turns harsh. Garlic chive cut at this time is tough and pungent, hard to eat raw. It is better to cook summer garlic chive or turn it into kimchi and jangajji.

### 12-2. Cutting Height

| Round | Where to cut |
|---|---|
| First harvest | Cut leaving 3-4cm above ground level |
| From the second on | 1-1.5cm above the previous cut |
| After that | Raise the cut by 1-1.5cm each time |
| The following spring | Start over, back down to 3-4cm above ground |

> **Almost the only way to get garlic chive harvesting wrong is to cut too close. Cutting all the way to the ground without leaving 3-4cm slices off the growing point, so the next leaf either fails to come or comes much later.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Garlic chive's growing point sits at the base, close to the ground. New leaves form at this point and are pushed upward. So cutting anywhere above it lets the next leaf keep coming, but if you cut through the growing point itself, that clump loses the place where it makes new leaves. The figure of 3-4cm is the safety margin left above the growing point.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This is also why the cutting height is raised with each successive round. A cut spot leaves behind the severed leaf sheath, which hardens, and cutting through that hardened part again roughens the cut surface and makes it easier for disease to get in. And, as seen earlier, the rhizome rises a little higher each year, so the growing point rises with it. Raising the cut by 1-1.5cm each time is a way of following that upward movement.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Keep raising it through the year like this, and by autumn the cutting height has risen quite a bit. Since the above-ground parts dry out in winter and new leaves come up again from the base the following year, spring starts back at the original height. This one-year cycle is the rhythm of garlic chive harvest.

### 12-3. Harvest Tips

| Item | Details |
|---|---|
| Tool | A sharp knife or scissors. A dull blade crushes the cut surface |
| Time of day | Morning, once the dew has dried. Avoid rainy days |
| Cutting direction | Cut one clump evenly in a single stroke |
| After cutting | Withhold water and fertilizer for a day |
| Washing | Soil collects near the base, so split and rinse it |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The advice to use a sharp tool is a matter of hygiene. Cutting with a dull knife tears and crushes the surface, leaving a wide wound, and soft-rot bacteria and gray mold enter through that wound. Most cases of the base going soft within days of cutting start here. Sharpening your tool does more good than spraying anything.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Avoiding rainy days and early mornings with dew still on the leaves is for the same reason. A wound made while wet does not dry well and gives fungi a good place to settle in.

## 13. Seed Saving - A Crop Whose Seed Doesn't Last a Year

### 13-1. Clump Division Over Seed Collection

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The evaluation table gives seed saving SS and seed longevity D. Placing these two grades side by side sums up garlic chive's propagation strategy exactly: propagation is very easy, and storing seed is almost impossible.

| Method | Time required | Storage feasibility | Practicality |
|---|---|---|---|
| Clump division | Harvest one month later | Stored as a living clump | The most reliable |
| Seed collected, sown the following year | Harvest 1 year later | Under 1 year | Must be used within that year |
| Seed collected, stored several years | Almost no germination | Not feasible | Does not work as a strategy |

> **Garlic chive's seed bank is not a packet but the field. For this crop, stockpiling means dividing living clumps and planting them in several spots, not collecting seed.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;That does not mean seed collection is pointless, though. Seed is small and easy to carry over distance, useful when you have to cross a distance too far to carry a dug-up clump. And if you only ever repeat clump division, every clump ends up genetically identical to one individual, so when a new disease arrives, all of them can collapse the same way. Continuing through seed mixes things a little each generation, reducing that risk.

### 13-2. How to Collect Seed

| Step | Details |
|---|---|
| Selecting seed clumps | Set aside a few healthy clumps that are at least 3 years old |
| Care | Do not cut those clumps that year, and leave the flower stalks alone |
| Flowering | August to September, white flowers bloom in an umbrella shape |
| Judging ripeness | When the pods where the flowers were dry brown, split open, and black seed shows |
| Harvesting | Cut the whole flower stalk and hang it upside down over paper or cloth |
| Separating | Rub the dried stalk by hand to shake the seed out |
| Cleaning | Sieve or winnow in the wind to blow away the chaff |
| Drying | Dry completely in the shade |
| Storage | Put in an airtight container with ash or charcoal and keep it in a cool, dark place |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The reason to set aside and manage clumps for seed collection separately is nutrients. Making flowers and seed takes a considerable amount of nutrients, so a clump used for seed collection loses vigor noticeably the following year. Try to get both a harvest and seed from the same clump, and both suffer. It is cleanest to keep three or four clumps at one end of the field dedicated to seed collection and never cut them at all.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Once the pods dry, they split open on their own and drop the seed, so if you miss the timing, it spills onto the ground. It is safer to cut the whole flower stalk just before it fully dries and hang it upside down; laying cloth underneath catches the seed as it falls while drying.

### 13-3. Storage and Longevity

| Condition | Germination retained |
|---|---|
| Sealed at room temperature | Under 1 year. The following spring is the limit |
| Cool, dry place | 1-2 years |
| Humid or hot place | Drops sharply within a few months |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Allium seed is known to be unusually short-lived because the fat it contains oxidizes easily. Over time, this component changes and the embryo dies. Low temperature and low humidity slow oxidation, so keep it as cool and dry as possible, and put ash or charcoal in the airtight container with it to trap moisture.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There is one practical conclusion. Use garlic chive seed the same autumn you collect it, or the following spring. Do not put garlic chive on the list of seed you set aside for years down the road; instead, substitute keeping garlic chive clumps alive in the field.

## 14. Toxicity and Compounds of Concern

### 14-1. The Safety of Garlic Chive Itself

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Garlic chive is a safe food at ordinary intake levels. It can be eaten raw or blanched. Still, two things are worth knowing.

| Item | Details |
|---|---|
| Overconsumption | Sulfur compounds can irritate the stomach lining, causing heartburn and diarrhea |
| Raw on an empty stomach | Eating a lot of raw garlic chive on an empty stomach irritates more |
| Vitamin K | High content, involved in blood clotting |
| Dogs and cats | The whole genus Allium is toxic to some pets and livestock |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The last row is a practical caution in a disaster. Allium plants, including garlic chive, green onion, garlic, and onion, destroy red blood cells in dogs and cats, causing anemia. A compound that causes people no problem at all is poison to these animals. In a situation where feeding leftovers to livestock or dogs becomes common, food containing garlic chive needs to be left out of that. Cattle and horses can also develop the same kind of anemia if they eat large amounts.

### 14-2. Look-Alike Poisonous Plants - Telling Them Apart by Smell

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;When looking for Allium plants in the mountains and fields rather than in a garden, it can be dangerous to pick by leaf appearance alone. Poisonous plants that resemble garlic chive, sanmanul (mountain garlic), and dallae (Korean wild chive) genuinely exist, and poisoning incidents are reported every year.

| Category | Allium (garlic chive, sanmanul, dallae) | Lily of the valley | Narcissus and Lycoris species |
|---|---|---|---|
| Smell of a cut leaf | Always smells of green onion or garlic | No smell | No smell, or just a grassy smell |
| Leaf base | Several leaves overlap to form a sheath | Two leaves wrap around each other as they emerge | Flat leaves emerge overlapping |
| Leaf texture | Thin and soft | Thick, stiff, and glossy | Thick and smooth |
| Veins | Parallel veins are not distinct | Parallel veins are distinct | Parallel veins are distinct |
| Below ground | Bulb or rhizome | A thin rhizome | A round bulb (onion-shaped) |
| If eaten | Edible | Vomiting, dizziness, abnormal heartbeat | Severe vomiting, abdominal pain, diarrhea |

> **Cut one leaf, rub it, and smell it: if it does not smell of green onion or garlic, it is not in the genus Allium. This one check filters out almost every poisonous look-alike of Allium.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Lily of the valley calls for particular caution. The leaves that come up in early spring look very similar to sanmanul and grow in the same mountain habitats, and accidents from mixing them up while foraging greens have happened repeatedly. The compounds in lily of the valley act on the heart and disrupt its rhythm, so it is dangerous even in a small amount. Narcissus and Lycoris species have bulbs that look like onion or garlic, leading to accidents when people dig them up by mistake.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The reason telling them apart by smell is reliable is that the very compound that makes a plant Allium is that smell compound itself. Rub or cut the leaf, and the tissue breaks, enzymes activate, and the smell appears immediately. This reaction has no exceptions. However, if your nose is blocked or you cannot smell, this test is not possible, and in that case it is safer not to forage wild plants at all.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Garlic chive grown in a garden carries none of this risk, because you know exactly where you planted it. However, if narcissus or lily of the valley has been planted at the edge of the garlic chive bed, the two can get mixed up in spring, so keeping such plants away from the garlic chive area is a simple precaution.

## 15. Storage Without Refrigeration

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Storability E, 2-3 days at room temperature, is a definite weakness of garlic chive. However, this weakness is largely offset when paired with perenniality SS. That is because, for the seven months from April through October, the field itself acts as storage.

### 15-1. The Field as Storage

| Method | Duration | Condition |
|---|---|---|
| Left uncut in the field | All through April to October | Cut only as much as needed, only on the day it is needed |
| Dug up whole and buried in soil | A few weeks | Temporarily replanted in soil in a cool spot |
| Wrapped in a damp cloth in a cool spot | 3-5 days | Lasts longer with a root-cellar pit or earth cellar |
| Stood upright with the base in water | 2-3 days | Laid flat, the leaves curl and spoil faster |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There is a small trick in the fourth row. Laid flat, garlic chive's leaves try to curl back upward, spending energy on that effort, and the tissue breaks down faster. Stood upright, this reaction does not happen, buying an extra day or two. The method is to stand the bases upright in a jar or container and pour a little water in the bottom.

### 15-2. Drying

| Step | Details |
|---|---|
| Preparation | Wash off the soil and drain completely |
| Cutting | Cut into 3-5cm lengths. Dried whole, the inside will not dry |
| Laying out | A shaded, well-ventilated spot. Drying in the sun drives off color and aroma |
| Judging done | Until it crumbles when rubbed between your fingers |
| Storage | Put in an airtight container along with ash or charcoal |
| Duration | Half a year to a year if well dried and sealed |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The biggest loss in drying garlic chive is aroma. The sulfur compounds that are the source of the smell are volatile, so a large share of them escapes during drying. It is more accurate to think of dried garlic chive as a different ingredient from fresh, one that settles into a role of adding color and fiber to broth. Drying slowly in the shade reduces the loss a little, and sealing it immediately once fully dry protects what aroma remains.

### 15-3. Pickling and Fermentation

| Method | Procedure | Duration |
|---|---|---|
| Garlic chive kimchi | Salt it to wilt it, then toss with chili powder | 1-2 months in a cool place |
| Salt-curing | Layer with 5-10% of its weight in salt | 3-6 months |
| Jjanji (a heavily salted pickle) | Submerge in brine of 10% or stronger | 6 months or more. Soak in water to remove excess salt before eating |
| Soy-sauce jangajji | Submerge in soy sauce | A few months |
| Doenjang-baji (packed in doenjang) | Bury in doenjang (Korean soybean paste) | A few months |
| Flower-stalk jangajji | Submerge flower-stalk buds picked in August in brine or soy sauce | A few months |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There is a reason garlic chive suits pickling especially well. Because its sulfur compounds themselves have an antimicrobial effect, it does not spoil even with less salt than other vegetables need. In a disaster where salt is scarce, this difference is not small. Garlic chive kimchi holds up on half the salt used for napa cabbage kimchi.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The flower-stalk jangajji in the last row is especially practical. Since removing flower stalks in August is a task that must be done every year, a fairly large quantity of flower stalks comes out of it all at once. Their tissue is firmer than the leaf's, so pickling keeps a crisp texture for a long time, and it means material for a winter side dish arises on its own.

## 16. Cooking and Processing

| Method | Fuel | Procedure | Note |
|---|:---:|---|---|
| Eaten raw | 0 | Wash and toss as is, or serve alongside a wrap | Aroma and vitamin C stay fully intact |
| Geotjeori (a quick, unfermented salad) | 0 | Salt briefly, then toss | Makes one side dish with no fuel |
| Blanched namul (seasoned vegetable side dish) | Little | 20-30 seconds in boiling water | Blanching too long makes it mushy and drives off the aroma |
| Soups and stews | Medium | Add just before turning off the heat | Long boiling drives away the aroma |
| Jeon (Korean savory pancake) | Medium | Mix into a wheat or grain-flour batter and fry | A small amount makes a whole meal |
| Stir-frying | Little | 1-2 minutes over high heat | Improves beta-carotene absorption |
| Kimchi | 0 | See Section 15 | Serves as both storage and cooking |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Cooking & processing being SS in the evaluation table means it can be washed and eaten right away, which is valuable when fuel is scarce. Geotjeori alone gives you a side dish with no fire at all, and this becomes the method used most often in actual practice.

> **There is one principle for cooking garlic chive: add it as late as possible and cook it as briefly as possible. Its aroma is fragile against heat, and long boiling erases the whole reason for adding it.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The taste grade D seen earlier, based on a finding that the smell and aroma are off-putting, needs to be read in reverse in a disaster. Ordinarily, a strong aroma splits people into those who like it and those who do not, but at a table where seasoning, oil, and meat have disappeared, that aroma becomes almost the only means left for building flavor. Season with salt and add aroma with garlic chive, and a bland grain dish becomes something worth eating.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;On top of this, there is an antimicrobial effect. Without refrigeration, cooked food starts to spoil within a few hours. Food containing garlic chive spoils a little more slowly. This is because its sulfur compounds suppress bacterial growth, and this difference carries real weight when leftover summer food has to be kept until the next meal. Traditionally, putting plenty of garlic chive and garlic into summer food was not just about flavor.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Beta-carotene and vitamin K are fat-soluble and need oil to be properly absorbed. Stir-frying with a spoonful of oil, or frying it into jeon, increases the nutrition you get from the same amount of garlic chive. Conversely, vitamin C dissolves in water and is fragile against heat, so it makes sense to split your garlic chive between a raw portion and a cooked one.

## 17. Annual Work Calendar

| Month | Task |
|---|---|
| January-February | Overwintering as roots. Check the cover and avoid stepping on the spot |
| March | Remove the cover and clear away dried leaves. Apply the first fertilizer, mainly nitrogen. The right time for clump division |
| April | First harvest. Cut leaving 3-4cm above ground. Wait a day after cutting, then fertilizer and water |
| May | 2nd and 3rd harvest. Raise the cut by 1-1.5cm each time. Top-dressing at every cutting |
| June | Harvest continues. Maintain the drainage channel before the monsoon season (jangma, late June to July in South Korea). Clump division still possible |
| July | Hot period. Leaves toughen, so cook them or turn them into kimchi |
| August | Flower stalks come up. Remove them every 7-10 days and harvest the buds to eat. Leave seed-collection clumps alone |
| September | Autumn harvest resumes. The best-quality period. The right time for clump division |
| October | Finish the last harvest in late October. Collect seed once the seed-collection flower stalks have dried |
| November | Apply overwintering fertilizer, mainly potassium. Above-ground parts dry out |
| December | Leave dried leaves as cover or cover with straw. Rely on stored kimchi and jangajji |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The two rows in this table most easily missed are August and October. Putting off flower-stalk removal in August makes both that autumn and the following spring worse, and continuing to cut past late October keeps nutrients from building up in the root, which shows the following spring. Both are tasks where no harm is visible right away, with the result only showing up months later, which is exactly why someone handling a perennial crop for the first time skips them most often.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The clump division in the March row is also worth watching closely. It is the only real way to expand a garlic chive bed, and if you do not skip this task once every 3 years, the garlic chive area grows larger year after year. For a crop whose seed does not last a year, this shovel work in March and September is, in effect, seed storage.
