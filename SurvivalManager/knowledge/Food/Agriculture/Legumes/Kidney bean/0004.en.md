## 5. Field Preparation and Fertilizer - Nitrogen Balance and Fertilization

### 5-4. The Trap in Nitrogen Fixation

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The saying that planting beans improves the soil has hardened into farming common sense. It is half right and half wrong. Without understanding this point precisely, a field planted with legumes for several years running can end up growing more depleted instead. This problem is more serious for the kidney bean than for other legumes, because its nitrogen-fixation efficiency is on the lower side among legumes.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;When kidney beans grow well, 1,000m² yields 200kg of dry seed. Kidney bean seed is 21% protein, and dividing protein by 6.25 gives the nitrogen amount. 21 ÷ 6.25 is 3.36, meaning about 3.4% of the seed's weight is pure nitrogen. The nitrogen contained in 200kg is about 6.7kg, or 67kg converted to a hectare basis.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Meanwhile, the nitrogen the kidney bean's root nodules fix, under good conditions, is 40-70kg per hectare. That is clearly less than soybean's 100-150kg or fava bean's 150-200kg. Setting what leaves the field beside what comes in looks like this.

| Item | Nitrogen per hectare | Direction |
|---|---|---|
| Root-nodule fixation (good conditions) | 40-70kg | In |
| Root-nodule fixation (poor conditions) | 15-30kg | In |
| Carried off in the seed | 60-75kg | Out |
| Nitrogen remaining in the stems and leaves | 20-30kg | Stays if returned to the field; leaves if burned or carried off |
| Nitrogen remaining in the roots and nodules | 8-15kg | Usually stays in the field |

> **For the kidney bean, the nitrogen carried off in the seed is about equal to, or even more than, the nitrogen the root nodules fix. If the stems and leaves are not returned to the field, a kidney bean field will almost certainly end up with a negative nitrogen balance.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This point matters especially for the kidney bean. Soybean or fava bean have some margin even if their stems and leaves are carried off, but the kidney bean has no such margin. If the vines are collected and burned, or fed to livestock and carried away, that field grows more depleted every year. If you expect the soil to improve while growing kidney beans, returning the stems and leaves is not an option but a minimum condition.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This is also why the Three Sisters planting has an advantage here. A kidney bean grown alongside maize hands over some of its nitrogen directly to the maize through its roots, and the residues of all three crops stay in the same field. When maize stalks, squash vines, and kidney bean vines decompose together in one place, the soil is noticeably softer the following year.

| Principle | Details |
|---|---|
| Stems and leaves must go back to the field | Return all the vines, leaves, and pod husks left over after threshing to the field |
| Do not burn them | Burning releases the nitrogen as gas, leaving only potassium and phosphorus behind in the ash |
| Do not dig up the roots | The nitrogen in the roots and nodules is the share that is easiest of all to leave behind |
| Cut into 20-30cm lengths and spread as cover | Serves as both mulch and fertilizer |
| Sort out only the diseased residue | Burn anthracnose-affected residue and return the rest |
| If fed to livestock, return the manure | If the vines were fed to livestock, be sure to put that animal's manure back on the same field |

### 5-5. Field Preparation

| Item | Method | Under disaster conditions |
|---|---|---|
| Tilling | Till to a depth of 20-30cm and level evenly | Turn with a spade or dig up with a hoe |
| Ridge | Height 15-20cm or more | The key to preparing for the monsoon season (jangma, late June to July in South Korea) |
| Drainage ditch | Depth 30cm or more | Check that water drains all the way through |
| Acidity correction | pH 6.0-7.0 | Do this 3-4 weeks before sowing |
| Organic matter | Well-rotted compost 1,500-2,000kg/1,000m² | Gains both water retention and drainage at once |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Working in plenty of compost matters especially for growing kidney beans. The kidney bean is weak against both waterlogging and drought, and soil rich in organic matter eases both at once: it holds water to delay drought, and its crumbly structure lets water drain quickly. At 1,500-2,000kg, well-rotted compost is on the high side among legumes, and the point of it is not to supply nitrogen but to build the soil's structure.

### 5-6. Fertilizer Rates

| Fertilizer | Per 1,000m² | Per m² | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Nitrogen | 4-6kg | 4-6g | Too much makes only the leaves lush and reduces pods |
| Phosphorus | 7-10kg | 7-10g | All applied as base fertilizer. Important for roots and flowering |
| Potassium | 6-8kg | 6-8g | All applied as base fertilizer |
| Compost | 1,500-2,000kg | 1.5-2kg | Use fully rotted compost |
| Lime | Only for acidic soil | - | 3-4 weeks before sowing |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;At 4-6kg, nitrogen is more than soybean's 3kg but far less than maize's 20kg. This is because the kidney bean's nitrogen-fixation efficiency is on the low side among legumes, so it draws nearly half from the soil. Even so, there is a clear ceiling. Giving more than this only makes the vines and leaves lush while reducing pods, and the vines, grown heavy, fall over, blocking airflow and letting anthracnose spread.

> **Sparing nitrogen with kidney beans is not thrift; it is technique. Give too much and the yield is bound to fall.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The usual practice for top-dressing is to put half the nitrogen in the base fertilizer and add the rest, watching growth, from flowering through pod filling. In a disaster, an alternative is to dilute urine tenfold in water and pour it onto the soil between plants, keeping it off the leaves. If the leaves are already lush and dark green, skip it.

### 5-7. Disaster-Substitute Fertilizers

| Substitute resource | Nutrients supplied | How to use | Caution |
|---|---|---|---|
| Well-rotted compost | Comprehensive, organic matter | Apply 1.5-2kg per m² as base fertilizer | The single most important resource for kidney beans |
| Wood ash | Potassium, phosphorus, calcium | Scatter 100-200g per m² and mix in | Alkaline; too much raises pH above 7 |
| Diluted urine | Nitrogen, potassium | Dilute tenfold in water and pour onto the soil between plants | Keep off the leaves |
| Bone meal | Phosphorus, calcium | Roast, grind, and mix into compost | Effect appears slowly |
| Fish bones and guts | Phosphorus, nitrogen | Bury and let them break down before use | If buried as is, animals will dig them up |
| Shells and eggshells | Calcium | Roast, grind, and scatter | A substitute for lime. Reacts slowly |
| Leaf mold | Comprehensive, organic matter | Mix into the soil | Greatly increases water retention |
| Rice hulls, rice straw | Potassium, silica | Spread as mulch | Lowers soil temperature and reduces flower drop |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Boron is also worth remembering. The kidney bean needs boron, and a shortage causes flowers to drop and pods to fail to set. Without chemical borax, the most realistic alternative is compost made from a mix of vegetable scraps, fruit peels, and fallen leaves together. Compost made from a single material has a skewed micronutrient profile.

## 6. Sowing

### 6-1. Sowing Time

| Cropping type | Region | Sowing time | Harvest time |
|---|---|---|---|
| Spring open-field (pole type) | All of South Korea | Early to late April | July-September |
| Spring open-field (bush type) | All of South Korea | Mid-April to late May | Late June-August |
| Early-maturing culture | Southern South Korea | Early to mid-March | Early to mid-June |
| High-elevation culture | Mountain areas | May-July | August-October |
| Latest sowing limit | All of South Korea | Late June-early July | After this, it will not ripen before frost |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The rule is to plant only after the last frost is completely over. Kidney beans die outright from frost and will not even germinate below 10 degrees. Plant too early and the seed rots in the soil. Plant too late, on the other hand, and the flowering period runs into a heat wave, or the ripening stage gets caught in the monsoon.

> **Within the proper window, sow two or three times at two-week intervals, and the flowering period spreads out so that a single heat wave or drought cannot take everything at once. The labor is spread out too.**

### 6-2. Seed Preparation

| Item | Method | Under disaster conditions |
|---|---|---|
| Sorting | Discard seed with spots, discoloration, wrinkles, or insect holes | Anthracnose is seed-borne. Sorting is half the control effort |
| Sizing | Choose plump, heavy seeds | Float in water and skim off any that float |
| Hot-water treatment | Soak in 50-55 degree water for 10-15 minutes | Kills seed-borne pathogens |
| Ash treatment | Coat with fine wood ash | Aids disinfection and drying, and helps repel birds and soil insects |
| Root-nodule inoculation | Coat with soil from a field that grew kidney beans | See Section 5-3. Not done together with disinfection |
| Pre-soaking | Only within 12 hours, the day before sowing | Soaking too long splits the seed coat and it rots in the soil |
| Germination test | Wrap 20 seeds in a wet cloth, leave for a few days, and count | Always check old seed first |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Pre-soaking calls for caution. Kidney bean seed absorbs water quickly, and past 12 hours the seed coat splits and the inside turns soft. Planted in that state, it rots before it can germinate. If the soil is sufficiently moist, it is safer to sow without soaking at all; if the soil is dry, soak for only about six or seven hours, dry off the surface water, and sow.

### 6-3. Sowing Method

| Item | Standard | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Sowing depth | 3-5cm | Two to three times the seed size. Too shallow and birds dig it up |
| Seeds per hole | 2-3 | Allows for losses to birds and soil insects |
| Bush-type spacing (early) | 40cm × 15cm | |
| Bush-type spacing (late) | 50cm × 15cm | |
| Pole-type spacing | 75cm × 30-40cm | Plant wide apart to ensure airflow |
| Thinning | Leave 1-2 plants at the 3-4 true leaf stage | |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The biggest threat right after sowing is birds and soil insects. Because the kidney bean's cotyledons come up above the ground and are large and tender, pigeons, magpies, and pheasants love to peck them off. A plant that loses its cotyledons usually dies. Cover the sown row thinly with twigs or straw, or drape netting or an old window screen over it until germination, and crisscross shiny string above it. Coating the seed with fine wood ash also reduces the approach of soil insects.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;For pole types, set up the support beforehand, before sowing or at the one- or two-true-leaf stage. Driving in a stake after the vine has already grown cuts the roots and damages the vine. When planting together with maize, sow the maize first, let it grow to knee height, and only then plant the kidney bean at its base. Sow both at the same time, and the kidney bean grows first and winds around and topples the still-young maize.

## 7. Water Management

| Timing | Water management | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Sowing to germination | Keep the soil from drying out completely | The seed must absorb water to germinate |
| Early growth to before flowering | Keep somewhat on the dry side | Overwatering causes leggy growth and damages the roots |
| Flowering to pod filling | Water thoroughly every 10-15 days | Directly determines pod swelling |
| Hot spells above 30 degrees | Run water along the furrows to lower soil temperature | Reduces flower drop and sterility |
| The monsoon | Dig drainage ditches | Otherwise the lower leaves turn yellow and drop |
| Ripening stage | Stop irrigation | Overwatering causes shattering and quality loss |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The kidney bean is weak against both waterlogging and drought, so the margin for water management is narrow. Because the roots spread shallowly, the plant wilts as soon as the topsoil dries, and the roots rot if water stands. Rather than giving a little water often, it is better to water thoroughly all at once every 10 to 15 days, enough to wet the soil to a depth of 20cm, since this encourages deeper roots.

| Symptom | Cause | Action |
|---|---|---|
| Leaves droop at midday and recover by evening | Temporary moisture shortage. Within the normal range | Leave as is |
| Leaves are still drooping in the morning | The roots cannot draw up water | Water thoroughly |
| Flowers drop in large numbers | High heat above 30 degrees, or drought | Run water along the furrows to lower soil temperature and mulch |
| Pods have set but the seeds are small | Water shortage during pod filling | Irrigation at this stage directly governs yield |
| Lower leaves turn yellow and drop | Waterlogging during the monsoon | Dig drainage ditches and mound up the soil |
| The base of the stem turns brown and softens | Root rot from overwatering | Pull out the plant if severe. It spreads to the whole field if left unchecked |
| The whole leaf is pale yellow but not wilting | Nitrogen shortage because the nodules are not working | Dig up the roots and check the nodules |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Running water along the furrows during hot spells is especially effective for kidney beans. When days above 30 degrees continue, flowers drop, and this is often because the soil temperature near the roots rises and the roots stop functioning properly, more than because of the air temperature itself. Letting water flow along the furrows in the early morning or at dusk drops the soil temperature by a few degrees and noticeably reduces flower drop. Combined with mulching, the effect lasts longer.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Mulching is also the cheapest way to save water. Even without plastic sheeting, covering the ground with 5 to 10cm of straw, rice hulls, fallen leaves, or cut weeds cuts surface evaporation by more than half and eases swings in soil temperature. During the monsoon, though, it is better to pull some of it back to ensure airflow, which helps prevent disease.
