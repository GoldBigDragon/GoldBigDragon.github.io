## 3. Growing Conditions

### 3-1. Temperature and Light

| Condition | Limit | Optimum | Outside this range |
|---|---|---|---|
| Germination temperature | 10°C or above | 26-30°C | Below 10°C the seed does not germinate and rots |
| Growing temperature | 10-30°C | 15-21°C | Growth stops at 5°C or below |
| Fruit-set temperature | 18-28°C | 20-25°C | A narrow range spanning just 10°C |
| High heat while flowering | Several days at 30°C or above | - | Flowers and young pods drop |
| Cold hardiness | None | - | Killed by frost. Plant only after the last frost is over |
| Sunlight | 6 hours a day or more | 8 hours a day or more | Yield drops by half at 40% shade |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The hardest call in growing kidney beans is where to place the flowering period. Above 30°C the flowers drop, and if pods get wet during the monsoon season (jangma, late June to July in South Korea), they rot. South Korean summers bring both at once. Sow in April and flowering falls in early June, ahead of the monsoon; sow in late May and flowering runs into the heat wave of late July. Spring sowing is generally favorable, and summer sowing is stable only at high elevation.

> **Staggering the sowing date is especially worthwhile for kidney beans. Sow two or three times at two-week intervals, and the flowering period spreads out so that a single heat wave or monsoon spell cannot take everything at once.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;A practical response during hot spells above 30°C is to run water along the furrows to lower the soil temperature. When soil temperature drops, the roots can draw up water and flower drop decreases. Covering the ground with mulch produces the same effect.

### 3-2. Soil and Water

| Condition | Limit | Optimum | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Soil texture | Sandy soil to loam | Water-retentive, well-drained sandy loam to loam | Waterlogged ground is worst of all |
| pH | 5.5-7.5 | 6.0-7.0 | Root nodules decrease in acidic soil |
| Effective soil depth | 30cm or more | 50cm or more | Roots spread relatively shallowly |
| Season evapotranspiration | - | About 400mm | Middling among legumes |
| Flooding | Roots are damaged if submerged for a day or more | - | Sensitive to waterlogging |
| Ridge height | 15-20cm or more | 20cm | Key to monsoon preparedness |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The kidney bean is vulnerable to both waterlogging and drought. Because the roots spread shallowly, the plant wilts as soon as the topsoil dries out, and conversely the roots rot if water stands. This is why soil that is both water-retentive and well-drained, that is, a sandy loam rich in organic matter, is ideal. Working in plenty of compost to build the soil's structure is a more fundamental measure than irrigation or drainage.

## 4. Varieties

### 4-1. Classification by Growth Habit

| Type | Height | Support | Growing period | Characteristics |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Bush type (dwarf) | 40-60cm | Not needed | 50-80 days | Harvested quickly with less labor. Harvest period is short |
| Pole type (climbing) | 2-3m | Required | 100-130 days | Harvest period is long and yield per area is high |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In a disaster, this choice comes down to materials and labor. The pole type yields far more from the same area but needs supports over 2m tall. Without bamboo or long poles, go with the bush type. But the picture changes if maize is planted alongside: the maize stalk itself becomes the support, so the pole type can be grown without any material investment.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Ideally, secure both at once. The bush type is harvested first, from late June to July, filling the summer food gap, while the pole type continues over a long stretch from July to September. Spreading out the harvest this way means a single weather disaster cannot take everything.

### 4-2. Classification by Use and Color

| Type | Characteristics | Suitable use |
|---|---|---|
| White kidney bean | Large, mealy seeds. Good storability | Long-term storage, boiling |
| Black kidney bean | Firm, high in anthocyanins | Storage, mixing into rice |
| Mottled kidney bean | Often early-maturing, with tender pods | Quick harvest, green kidney beans |
| Tiger kidney bean | Pole type, high-yielding | Securing bulk quantities |
| Snap type (pod stays tender, not fibrous) | Pods do not become tough | Eating pod and all |

> **Even in snap-type varieties eaten pod and all, lectin increases once the seeds inside the pod begin to swell. Cooking young pods is safe, but pods with fully formed seeds must always be boiled thoroughly.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;From a disaster-preparedness standpoint, the first criterion for choosing a variety is whether it can be seed-saved. An F1 hybrid scatters its traits if you sow it the following year. Most cultivated kidney bean varieties are fixed lines and self-pollinating, so seed received can be sown as it is, but if the seed packet says hybrid, do not expect a repeat the next year. Local landraces yield less but are already adapted to the local climate and diseases, and their seed can be carried forward.

### 4-3. Selection Criteria by Situation

| Situation | Priority | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| No stakes available | Bush type | Stands without support |
| Maize is being planted | Pole type | The maize stalk becomes the support |
| Food is needed quickly | Early bush type | Harvested in 60-80 days |
| Storage food is needed | White or black kidney bean | Good storability |
| Fuel is scarce | Small-seeded lines | Cook faster than large-seeded ones |
| The field is small | Pole type | Grows upward, giving a higher yield per area |
| The monsoon arrives early | Early bush type | Brings ripening forward to avoid it |

## 5. Field Preparation and Fertilizer - Root-Nodule Bacteria

### 5-1. How Nitrogen Fixation Happens

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;78% of the air is nitrogen. Even though a limitless amount of nitrogen floats above every head, plants cannot use it. Nitrogen in the air is a molecule in which two nitrogen atoms are locked together by a triple bond, and breaking that bond takes an enormous amount of energy. The industrial process that breaks this bond to make ammonia is the Haber-Bosch process, and the world's nitrogen fertilizer comes from it. It requires high temperature, high pressure, a catalyst, and vast amounts of fossil fuel.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Root-nodule bacteria accomplish this at ordinary temperature and pressure. Using an enzyme called nitrogenase, they break the triple bond and turn nitrogen into ammonia. There is a price, though. The reaction needs a great deal of energy, and the bacteria cannot photosynthesize on their own. So they live inside the roots of legumes, receiving sugar the plant made by photosynthesis, and in exchange hand over the nitrogen they fixed. It is a trading relationship.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The bacteria that partner with the kidney bean belong to the Rhizobium group. Their lineage differs from soybean's Bradyrhizobium or the bacteria of pea, so coating kidney bean seed with soil from a field that grew soybean or pea has little effect. Soil from a field that has grown kidney beans is the most reliable.

| Step | What happens |
|---|---|
| 1. Signal exchange | The kidney bean's roots secrete flavonoids, and bacteria in the soil respond to this signal by releasing Nod factors |
| 2. Infection | Root hairs curl inward to enclose the bacteria, and the bacteria travel into the root along an infection thread |
| 3. Nodule formation | Cells inside the root divide, the nodule swells, and the bacteria settle inside it |
| 4. Nitrogen fixation | Nitrogenase acts inside the nodule, turning atmospheric nitrogen into ammonia |
| 5. Distribution | Ammonia is converted into amino acids and transported to the stems, leaves, and pods |
| 6. Senescence | On entering the ripening stage, the nodule loses function and returns to the soil along with the root |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Nitrogenase is extremely sensitive to oxygen and loses function the instant oxygen reaches it. This is why the inside of the root nodule is packed with a red pigment protein called leghemoglobin, which captures oxygen and keeps the oxygen concentration around the enzyme extremely low. It works on the same principle as why human blood is red, and the red or pink color you see when you cut a nodule open is this pigment. This fact matters enormously in practice, because that red color is visible proof that the nodule is fixing nitrogen right now.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The kidney bean is not among the more efficient nitrogen fixers within the legume family. It makes only about half of the nitrogen it needs on its own and draws the rest from the soil. This is why its nitrogen fertilizer rate is set a little higher than for other legumes. This is only relative, though; next to maize it is still only about 25%.

### 5-2. How to Check Whether Nodules Have Formed

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Whether or not you inoculated, you must check whether root nodules actually formed. Without checking, you cannot know the cause of a nitrogen shortage, and you will repeat the same mistake the following year.

| Step | Details |
|---|---|
| 1 | Around the start of flowering, about 5-7 weeks after sowing, is the best time |
| 2 | Choose two or three plants that look healthy |
| 3 | Do not pull it out; use a spade to lift a wide clump of soil around the plant |
| 4 | Put it in a bucket of water and gently wash the soil off |
| 5 | Count the nodules on the taproot and the thick lateral roots |
| 6 | Split a few nodules open with a fingernail and look at the color inside |

| Observation | Meaning | Action |
|---|---|---|
| 15 or more nodules per plant | Inoculation succeeded | Leave as is |
| The cut inside is red or pink | It is fixing nitrogen | A good state |
| The cut inside is white or pale green | Nodules formed but are not fixing | Check acidity, waterlogging, and phosphorus deficiency |
| The cut inside is brown or black | Already senesced or dead | Normal at ripening, a problem at flowering |
| 5 nodules or fewer | Inoculation failed | If the leaves are yellow, supplement nitrogen |
| Many tiny nodules only at the tips of the lateral roots | Infected by native local bacteria but at low efficiency | Change the inoculum source next year |

> **This one habit — digging up the root, splitting a nodule open, and checking for red — is the surest signal there is of whether a legume crop will succeed, in a world without nitrogen fertilizer.**

### 5-3. Where to Find an Inoculum Source

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Commercial inoculants are products that carry live bacteria in peat or clay. They cannot be obtained in a disaster. But because the kidney bean is grown all across South Korea, soil from a field that grew kidney beans is relatively easy to find.

| Method | Procedure | Effect | Caution |
|---|---|---|---|
| Coating with soil from a kidney bean field | Scoop soil from the top 5-15cm of a field that grew kidney beans last year and formed nodules well. Lightly wet the seed with water, coat it with this soil, and sow right away | The most reliable, and free | Soil from a diseased field also carries anthracnose fungus along with it |
| Using the nodules themselves | Collect nodules from dug-up roots, crush them, mix into water, and dip the seed in that water before draining | Bacterial density is highest | Must be used the same day the nodules are dug up |
| Scattering soil over the field | Scatter the fetched soil along the row to be sown and till it in together | The bacteria spread through the soil | About one spadeful per 10m² is the standard |
| Leaving root residue | At harvest, cut only the stems and leave the roots in the field | The bacteria remain in the soil until the following year | The least labor-intensive way to maintain them |
| Soil from a neighboring field | Get a spadeful from a neighboring field where kidney beans were planted | Easy to find since it is grown throughout South Korea | Check that the field is free of disease |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There is something you must always observe when inoculating. Root-nodule bacteria are very sensitive to direct sunlight, drying, and heat. A few minutes of ultraviolet exposure kills a large share of them, and drying out kills them even faster. Do the work of coating the seed with soil in the shade, and sow the coated seed right away rather than laying it out in the sun. It is best to sow within 30 minutes, and finish within a few hours at the latest. One more thing: seed disinfection and inoculation cannot be done together. If you disinfected with a hot-water treatment, let the seed cool completely before inoculating.

| Conditions that cause inoculation failure | Reason |
|---|---|
| Soil pH 5.5 or below | The bacteria cannot survive |
| A field given plenty of nitrogen fertilizer | The plant feels no need for the trade and reduces nodule formation on its own |
| A waterlogged field | They are aerobic bacteria and die without oxygen |
| A field short on phosphorus | Nitrogen fixation needs a lot of energy, and phosphorus is used in that metabolism |
| Deficiency of molybdenum, iron, or cobalt | These are components of nitrogenase |
| Drying inoculated seed in the sun | The bacteria die from ultraviolet light and drying |
| Using soil from a soybean or pea field | The bacterial lineage differs, so infection efficiency is lower |
| Cold temperatures after sowing | Below 10°C, infection itself does not occur |
