## Introduction
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Kidney bean (Phaseolus vulgaris) is an annual legume of Central and South America, derived from wild beans of Mexico and the Andes, and together with maize and squash it formed the three pillars of Native American agriculture. Early upright types give dry beans in 70-80 days from sowing and green beans in 60 days. Seed can be had anywhere in the country, and being self-pollinating the pure line holds. Its overall grade is C (40.7 points, 107th of 183 valid species), with short term D (39.1), long term C (42.3), and supplementary food SS (61.4 points). Calories per area are D, at 696 kcal per ㎡, only 0.27 of the self-sufficiency line, and cold tolerance D and cooking and processing D add to it, so its place is as a side dish rather than rice, and as a companion in the maize field. The thing to read before anything else is section 10.

> **Just four or five raw or undercooked kidney beans bring severe vomiting and diarrhea within hours. More dangerous still, warming them at a low temperature of about 80℃ can raise the toxicity to as much as five times that of raw beans. Only beans soaked at least five hours, with the soaking water discarded, and boiled hard at 100℃ for at least 10 minutes are safe.**

> **The place of kidney bean is not in sole cropping but in combination. Climbing up a maize stalk as its stake, it gives more protein from one patch of field with no stake materials and no extra area.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Domestic cultivation spread S is the most valuable in practice: once you have seed you can carry it on by saving your own, and dry beans keep 3 years at room temperature. Protein density S and micronutrients S are the basis of the supplementary grade SS. Cooking and processing D is, for kidney bean, a safety matter rather than a nuisance. Cold tolerance D and temperature range C narrow the growing period, so plant after the last frost, and continuous-cropping tolerance B is a 3-year rest group. ## 2. Plant Overview
| Item | Content |
|:---|:---|
| Classification | Genus Phaseolus of the legume family, annual. Native to Central and South America from Mexico to the Andes, and self-pollinating |
| Leaves, stem | Trifoliate leaves, three leaflets to a set, heart-shaped. Climbing types twine up to 2-3 m and upright types stop at 40-60 cm |
| Roots, flowers | A straight taproot with fine roots spreading relatively shallowly and nodules. Flowers are butterfly-shaped, white, light pink, or purple |
| Pods, seeds | Cylindrical or flat, 10-20 cm long, with 4-10 seeds. The seed is kidney-shaped and large, in white, black, speckled, or red |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The cotyledons come above ground carrying the initial nutrients, so if birds pick them off the plant usually dies, which makes the ten days right after germination the most dangerous. Per 100 g of dried seed: 348 kcal, protein 21.01 g (24.1% of calories), fat 0.5-1.5 g, dietary fiber 24.4 g, sugars 3.32 g, vitamin B1 0.48 mg, and rich in calcium, iron, and magnesium, and black kidney beans contain anthocyanins. The protein is rich in lysine and slightly short in methionine, so eaten with maize, which is the opposite, each fills the other's lack. The seed is large and kidney-shaped, so heat takes long to reach the center, and the state of soft outside and hard inside is the most dangerous.

## 3. Growing Environment and Varieties
| Condition | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Temperature | Germination 10℃ or above (optimum 26-30℃). Growth 10-30℃ (optimum 15-21℃), and it stops at 5℃ or below. Seed set 18-28℃, a range of only 10℃. If 30℃ or above lasts a few days at flowering, flowers and young pods drop. It has no cold tolerance and frost kills it |
| Water, soil | Moisture-retentive, well-drained sandy loam to loam, pH 5.5-7.5 (optimum 6.0-7.0), effective soil depth 30 cm or more (optimum 50 cm). Seasonal evapotranspiration about 400 mm. The roots spread shallowly so it is weak to both waterlogging and drought, and the roots are damaged if submerged for a day or more |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;If sown in April flowering falls in early June, before the rainy season, and if sown in late May it meets the heat of late July, so spring sowing is favorable and summer sowing is stable only in the highlands.

| Type | Height, support | Characteristics |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Upright | 40-60 cm, not needed | Growth 50-80 days. Harvested quickly and little work, but a short harvest period |
| Climbing | 2-3 m, absolutely needed | Growth 100-130 days. Long harvest period and high yield per area |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Without stake materials go with upright types, and if planting with maize go with climbing types; upright types are harvested first in late June-July and climbing types over a long July-September, so securing both means one disaster does not take everything. Even the snap-bean types eaten pod and all gain lectin once the seeds swell, so cook mature pods thoroughly. F1 hybrids scatter in traits the next year, so choose fixed varieties or local landraces.

## 4. Field Preparation and Sowing
| Item | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Making the field | Plow 20-30 cm deep, beds 15-20 cm or higher, drainage ditches 30 cm or deeper. Fully matured compost 1,500-2,000 kg per 1,000㎡ (organic matter for soil structure) |
| Fertilization (1,000㎡) | Nitrogen 4-6 kg (more makes only leaves lush and reduces pods), phosphorus 7-10 kg, potassium 6-8 kg (all as basal fertilizer), lime on acidic soil (3-4 weeks before sowing). Substitute resources are wood ash (100-200 g per ㎡), urine diluted 10 times, and leaf mold |
| Nodule inoculation | Coat seed lightly moistened with water with topsoil 5-15 cm deep from a field, yours or a neighbor's, that grew kidney bean, and plant at once. Soil from soybean or pea fields is less effective because the bacterial strain differs, infection does not occur below 10℃, and soil from a diseased field also carries anthracnose |

> **In kidney bean, the nitrogen leaving with the seed is about equal to, or even more than, the nitrogen the nodules fix. If the stalks and leaves are not returned to the field, a kidney bean field almost surely ends with a negative nitrogen balance.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Fixation per hectare is 40-70 kg under good conditions (15-30 kg if poor), while 60-75 kg leaves with the seed and 20-30 kg remains in the stalks and leaves, so return all the vines, leaves, and pod husks to the field, and if you fed them to livestock put the manure in that field. Around flowering (5-7 weeks after sowing), lift with a spade with the soil, wash, and split the nodules; with 15 or more per plant and red inside, inoculation went well.

| Sowing | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Timing | After the last frost has completely ended. Climbing types early-late April, upright types mid April-late May, early cultivation in the south early-mid March, highlands May-July. The limit is late June-early July (after that it does not ripen before frost). Plant in two or three batches 2 weeks apart within the proper window so flowering is spread and a heat wave or the rainy season cannot take everything at once |
| Seed preparation | Discard seeds with spots, discoloration, wrinkles, or insect holes (anthracnose is seed-borne, so selection is half of control). Coat with fine wood ash to keep off birds and soil pests. Soak for no more than 12 hours (beyond that the seed coat bursts and rots, so do not soak if the soil is moist) |
| Method | Depth 3-5 cm, 2-3 seeds per hill. Spacing is 40 cm × 15 cm for upright early types and 50 cm × 15 cm for late types, 75 cm × 30-40 cm for climbing types. Thin at 3-4 true leaves to 1-2 plants. For climbing types set the stakes first, before sowing or at one or two true leaves |

## 5. Water Management and Cultivation Management
| Period | Water management |
|:---|:---|
| Sowing to germination | Keep the soil from drying out completely |
| Early growth to before flowering | Keep somewhat dry (too wet makes it leggy and damages roots) |
| Flowering to pod filling | Water enough every 10-15 days to wet the soil 20 cm deep at once (tied directly to pod swelling) |
| Heat above 30℃ | Run water along the furrows in early morning or at dusk to lower soil temperature (flower drop and sterility fall, and mulching makes it last) |
| Rainy season | Dig drainage ditches (lower leaves yellowing and dropping is waterlogging) |

| Management | Method |
|:---|:---|
| Stakes | The best thing for vines to twine around is about 2 cm across, and they cannot twine around thick posts. A-frame stakes (3-4 long poles tied at the top), net training (the highest yield), line training (string tied in several tiers between posts), and maize stalks (no materials needed) |
| Pinching out the tips | The 1st pinch is the growing point at the stem tip at 3-5 nodes of true leaves, and the 2nd is when new branches reach 3-5 nodes, increasing branches and flowers. Do not do it once flowers start to open, or on poor plants with stunted growth. Climbing types only once, when they reach the top of the stake |
| Thinning, weeding | Thin at 1-2 true leaves only where crowded, and at 3-4 true leaves leave 1-2 per hill. Weed 2-3 weeks after sowing and before flowering by laying the hoe flat and scraping only the top 2 cm (the roots are shallow and break if the hoe goes deep) |

## 6. Pests, Diseases, and Nutrient Problems
| Pest or disease | Symptoms and conditions | Control without chemicals |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Anthracnose | Dark red cankers on leaf veins, stems, and pods, with sunken spots on pods. Hot and humid, and seed-borne | Seed selection and hot-water disinfection are the core. Rotation of 3-4 years, airflow, burning diseased residue |
| Sclerotinia, root rot | Stems turn soft with white cottony mycelium and black bodies (cool and humid) / base and roots turn brown and soft (excess moisture, continuous cropping) | Dig out diseased plants with the soil and burn them, and avoid dense planting / drainage, raising the beds, rotation |
| Virus diseases | Leaves mottled and crinkled. Spread by aphids | No cure. Pull and burn, and reduce aphids |
| Aphids, stink bugs | Suck sap and carry viruses / pierce pods and make them empty | Spray soapy water (20 g of unscented soap per 1 L of water; spraying at midday heat scorches leaves) on the underside of leaves and protect ladybugs / catch by hand early in the morning while dew is on |
| Root-knot nematodes, bean weevil | Root galls and stunted growth / lays eggs on pods in the field and eats the seed in storage | Rotation of 3-4 years, marigold intercropping / storage management is key (see section 9) |
| Birds, soil pests | Pick off cotyledons right after germination / eat planted seed and young stems | Cover with straw or netting until germination / coat seed with wood ash and dig around cut plants to catch the pests |

> **Anthracnose passes to the next year with the seed. Seed taken from a plant with sunken dark red spots on the pods is itself next year's source of disease.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Bordeaux mixture is made by dissolving 1 kg of copper sulfate and 1 kg of quicklime separately in 100 L of water and mixing, used at once, and it is for preventing anthracnose and bacterial diseases, not for curing them. Brushing wet leaves spreads anthracnose across the whole field, so do not enter the field after rain.

| Symptom | Cause and remedy |
|:---|:---|
| Whole plant pale yellow from the lower leaves and small | Nitrogen deficiency (poor nodules are common). First dig up the roots and check the nodules, and if there are none, give a little urine diluted 10 times |
| Flowers open but pods do not set | Boron deficiency, or heat above 30℃. Compost of mixed materials, and run water along furrows to lower soil temperature |

## 7. Intercropping and Crop Rotation
| Compatibility | Companions |
|:---|:---|
| Good | Maize and squash (Three Sisters planting), lettuce and spinach (cover the ground and keep moisture), potato (disease does not overlap) |
| Fair | Cabbage and Chinese cabbage group (use the nitrogen left after kidney bean) |
| Poor | Continuous cropping with other legumes (anthracnose and nematodes accumulate) |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Three Sisters planting sows maize first at 30 cm spacing, and when it reaches knee height plants two or three climbing kidney bean seeds about 10 cm from the foot of each plant (sown together, the bean twines around young maize and pulls it down), with squash scattered in the open places between the maize rows. The maize becomes the stake, the bean shares nitrogen, and the squash leaves cover the ground and keep weeds down. Kidney bean yield halves under 40% shade, so plant maize widely and run the rows north-south.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The rest period is 3-4 years (a 3-year rest group by the Rural Development Administration), and anthracnose and root-knot nematodes accumulate. An example rotation is year 1 kidney bean (with maize and squash) → year 2 leafy vegetables or the onion group → year 3 root vegetables or grain → kidney bean again.

## 8. Harvest and Seed Saving
| Harvest | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Timing | Snap types pod and all at 45-55 days, green beans at about 60 days (when pods are plump and the seeds large but still green), and dry seed at 70-80 days for early upright and 100-130 days for late climbing types, when 70-80% of pods have dried yellow and the seeds are hard. Climbing types ripen from the bottom, so pick in turn; upright types are cut all at once with a sickle |
| Drying | Dry in the pod in the sun for 4-5 days, and after threshing always dry another 5-7 days spread thin in ventilated shade (seeds in direct sun fade or turn red and the seed coat cracks, letting insects in). Moisture 12-13% |
| Threshing, winnowing | Stamping on a sack (seeds do not fly), flail with little force and many strokes (hard blows break seeds), shelling by hand (for seed). Finally spread on a white cloth in a bright place and sort by hand, discarding seeds with the dark red spots of anthracnose |

> **If rain is forecast, do not wait for full ripeness and harvest. Dry pods that get wet and dry again repeatedly burst and drop the seed on the ground, and wet pods grow mold.**

| Seed saving | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Selection, harvest | Mark in advance, from the inner part of the field, plants with no anthracnose symptoms, many pods, and early ripening. Keep other varieties several meters away, and harvest after the pods have dried completely brown |
| Sorting, storage | Discard spots, broken seeds, wrinkles, and discoloration, keeping only large, glossy seeds, dry 5-7 days in shade to moisture of 12-13%. Germination is 65-80% after 2 years and 45-65% after 3, so every 3-4 years obtain seed from elsewhere and mix some in |

## 9. Storage
| Step | Method |
|:---|:---|
| Bean weevil control | Eggs already come in stuck on pods in the field, and a generation takes less than a month, so in half a year a sack becomes a hollow shell with holes. Complete sealing matters most, with wood ash (2-5% of the volume), diatomaceous earth (1-3 g per 1 kg), and oil coating (2-5 mL of cooking oil per 1 kg) as aids. Heat treatment spreads the beans thin in the sun for half a day to reach 60℃ and kill eggs and larvae, but discoloration must be accepted and it is not for seed |
| Containers, checking | Glass jars last 3 years or more but do not block light, so keep them in the dark. Put an ear to the container: rustling means insects, a musty smell is mold, and small round holes on the surface mean the weevils have passed a generation. Check every two months, and if there is damage take everything out, dry it in shade, skim off empty seeds floating in water, mix in ash, and seal again |

## 10. Cooking and Precautions
| Poisoning | Content |
|:---|:---|
| Cause | Phytohaemagglutinin (Phaseolus lectin). Red types are especially high and white types about a third of that |
| Toxic dose | 4-5 raw or undercooked beans |
| Symptoms | 1-3 hours after eating, severe nausea, violent vomiting, abdominal pain, then diarrhea. Usually recovery comes within hours to a day, but dehydration is the danger, and children, the elderly, and the frail are at the greatest risk |

| Treatment | Lectin activity | Safety |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Raw | Baseline | Dangerous |
| Long heating at around 80℃ | Up to 5 times baseline | More dangerous than raw |
| 100℃ boiling water for 10 minutes or more | Nearly 0 | Safe |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Heat of about 80℃ only helps the lectin come out and does not destroy it, so more absorbable active lectin results, and only at 100℃ does the structure collapse. That is why the methods that save fuel well for soybean and lentil, retained-heat cooking in a heat-retaining box started directly, banked embers in a hearth, overnight heating over a brazier, soaking in lukewarm broth, and mixing ground dry-bean powder into water to drink, are forbidden for kidney bean.

| Step | Method |
|:---|:---|
| 1. Soaking | At least 5 hours, 8 hours or more recommended, in room-temperature water (change the water in summer). Unsoaked beans cook only on the outside and stay raw inside, which is the most dangerous, and fuel use more than doubles |
| 2. Discard water, rinse | Always discard the soaking water and rinse two or three times in fresh water |
| 3. Boiling | Pour in fresh water and boil hard, keeping 100℃ for at least 10 minutes and 30 minutes to be safe (the water must keep surging in large bubbles; quietly steaming is not 100℃) |
| 4. Check, discard water | Press with a finger to see it mashes completely (a hard core left inside means undercooked), and discard the cooking water too |

| Method | Safe condition |
|:---|:---|
| Pressure cooking | 15-20 minutes under pressure for soaked beans. Safest and uses the least fuel |
| Retained-heat cooking after boiling | Always boil first at 100℃ for 10-15 minutes, then put in a heat-retaining box |
| Green beans | Young ones are low in lectin and need only a few minutes in boiling water |
| Forbidden | Retained-heat cooking started directly, roasting, and eating sprouts raw (sprouts keep lectin) |
| Boil and dry | Boil thoroughly, then dry hard in the sun until a bean snaps with a crack when broken and it keeps for months; afterwards only a short soak and reheating is needed, removing the burden of reaching 100℃ every time. Salt-pickled boiled beans, boiled, mashed and dried beans, blanched and dried green beans, and bean paste work on the same principle |
| In case of poisoning | Stop at once and discard everything cooked. Have them sip a little at a time of boiled, cooled water with a very little salt and sugar, and keep them lying at rest. Watch children, the elderly, and the frail especially, as they dehydrate fast |
| Others | Trypsin inhibitors need 30 minutes or more at 100℃ after soaking, oligosaccharides are removed by discarding the soaking and boiling water, and saponins by skimming off the foam. Moldy beans are not made safe by washing or cooking, so discard them |

## 11. Annual Work Schedule
| Month | Work |
|:---|:---|
| Jan-Feb | Check storage, seed germination test, and repair stake materials. In February, field planning, turning the compost, and sorting out seeds with anthracnose spots |
| Mar-Apr | In March, liming, plowing, beds and drainage ditches, and early sowing in the south. In April, sow climbing types, set stakes (before sowing), and keep off birds |
| May-June | In May, sow upright types (2 weeks apart), thin, weed, and the 1st pinching. In June, flowering, the 2nd pinching and then stop, checking nodules, and starting the green-bean harvest |
| July-Aug | In July, pod filling, furrow irrigation in heat, and harvesting dry seed of early varieties. In August, harvesting climbing types in turn, hand-picking stink bugs, and watching for anthracnose |
| Sep-Oct | In September, finish harvest, dry in the pod in the sun, and thresh. In October, shade drying, winnowing, storage, and returning stalks and leaves |
| Nov-Dec | In November, boil and dry a pot of beans while firewood is available, and check storage. In December, check storage and plan next year |

> **Kidney bean is a crop that, if soaked 5 hours or more, with the water discarded, boiled at 100℃ for 10 minutes or more, never kept warm at a low temperature for long, cleared of anthracnose seed, and with its stalks and leaves returned to the field, gives protein when planted together with maize.**
