## 8. Crop Management - Staking and Pinching

### 8-1. Staking

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;For pole-type kidney beans, staking is not optional. If a vine that grows up to 2-3m cannot find anything to climb, the vines tangle together and lie flat on the ground, and from that point the leaves overlap and lose light, airflow is blocked and anthracnose spreads, and the pods touch the soil and rot.

| Method | Materials | Characteristics |
|---|---|---|
| A-frame stakes | 3-4 long poles | Tie the tops together to stand them as a triangle. Sturdy against wind |
| Netting trellis | Posts and netting | Gives the highest yield. Best option if netting is available |
| String trellis | Posts and string | Tie string between posts in several tiers going up |
| Maize stalk | Maize | Requires no materials at all. Three Sisters planting |
| Twig staking | Small branches | Suitable only for shorter pole varieties |

> **The best thing for a vine to wind around is about 2cm in diameter. It cannot wind around a thick post. Drive in only thick stakes, and the vine will find nothing to grip and end up lying flat on the ground.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Timing matters for installation. Set up the support before sowing or at the one- or two-true-leaf stage. Drive in the stake after the vine has already grown, and the shallowly spread roots are cut and the vine breaks. Set it up ahead of time, and the vine finds its way up on its own.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The Three Sisters planting is a way to get a support without any materials. Plant maize first, let it grow to knee height, plant the kidney bean at its base, and plant squash in the open space between the two crops. The maize becomes the support, the kidney bean shares nitrogen, and the squash leaves cover the ground, blocking weeds and holding in moisture. Remember, though, that the kidney bean is a crop whose yield drops by half at 40% shade. Plant the maize too densely, and the kidney bean gets buried in shade. Space the maize generously and run the rows north-south so that shade does not linger long on one side.

### 8-2. Pinching

| Round | Timing | Method | Effect |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1st | 3-5 true-leaf nodes | Pinch off the growing point at the tip of the stem | Side branches increase |
| 2nd | New branches reach 3-5 nodes | Pinch off the growing point again | Flowers and pods increase |
| Stop | Once flowering begins | Do not pinch | Pinching after flowering reduces yield |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Pinching is especially effective for bush-type kidney beans. The energy that was growing upward turns sideways, side branches increase, and the number of nodes that will carry flowers grows. For pole types, since the goal is upward growth, pinch only once, when the vine reaches the top of its support.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;It is not beneficial in every situation, though. Pinch a kidney bean that is already growing poorly in a poor field, and it has no strength left to recover, so the yield drops even further. Walk the field and pinch where the leaf color is dark and the internodes are open; leave alone where the leaf color is pale and growth has stalled. And do not pinch after the flowers have opened, because after flowering the growing point is no longer a place that extends height but a spot where new pods will set.

### 8-3. Thinning and Weeding

| Task | Timing | Method |
|---|---|---|
| 1st thinning | 1-2 true leaves | Clear only spots that are excessively crowded |
| 2nd thinning | 3-4 true leaves | Leave 1-2 plants per hole |
| 1st weeding | 2-3 weeks after sowing | Scrape shallowly. The roots are shallow and get cut if you dig deep |
| 2nd weeding | Before flowering | Do not enter the field after this |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The kidney bean's roots spread shallowly. Push a hoe in deep and the fine roots are cut, actually worsening growth. Lay the hoe flat and scrape only the top 2cm of soil, and pull weeds by hand near the plants themselves. Hilling is generally unnecessary, but if the base of the plant wobbles, mound a little soil around it.

## 9. Pests and Diseases

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The kidney bean's pest & disease pressure is C. Its estimated yield loss without pest control is 48.9%, more than double that of soybean. Anthracnose in particular is the kidney bean's signature disease, seed-borne and recurring year after year.

### 9-1. Major Diseases

| Disease | Symptoms | Conditions for outbreak | Response without chemicals |
|---|---|---|---|
| Anthracnose | Dark reddish cankers form on leaf veins, stems, and pods. Sunken spots on the pods are characteristic | Hot, humid conditions. Seed-borne | Seed sorting and hot-water treatment are key. 3-4 year rotation, ensuring airflow, burning diseased residue |
| Downy mildew | Yellow spots on the upper leaf surface, grayish-white mold on the underside | High humidity | Adequate spacing, drainage, Bordeaux mixture |
| Sclerotinia stem rot (white mold) | The stem softens, with cottony white mycelium and black sclerotia forming | Cool, humid conditions | Dig up diseased plants with their soil and burn them. Avoid dense planting |
| Purple leaf spot | Purple spots form on the leaves and pods | High humidity | Ensure airflow, rotate crops |
| Viral disease | Leaves develop a mottled mosaic pattern and curl | Spread by aphids | Cannot be cured. Pull up and burn affected plants, and reduce aphids |
| Root rot | The base of the stem and the roots turn brown and soften | Overwatering, continuous cropping | Drainage, raising ridges, rotation |

> **Anthracnose carries over to the next year by riding along with the seed. Seed taken from a plant whose pods show sunken, dark reddish spots is itself next year's source of infection. Culling it at the seed-saving stage is the cheapest control there is.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The response to anthracnose follows a sequence. First, seed sorting: pick out and discard spotted seed one by one, and apply hot-water treatment. Second, rotation: because the pathogen survives in residue in the soil for several years, leave the field free of the crop for 3-4 years. Third, airflow: keep proper spacing and set up supports so the leaves dry quickly. Fourth, do not enter the field after rain; if a person brushes against wet leaves, the spores spread across the whole field. Fifth, do not return diseased residue to the field; burn it instead.

### 9-2. Major Pests

| Pest | Damage | Control without chemicals |
|---|---|---|
| Aphids | Suck sap and spread viral disease | Spray soapy water (20g of unscented soap per 1L of water) on the underside of the leaves. Protect ladybugs |
| Stink bugs | Pierce the pods and suck sap, producing empty seeds | Catch them by hand early in the morning while dew is still on the plants and drop them into a bucket of water |
| Mites | Suck sap from the underside of leaves, leaving yellow mottling | They increase in dry conditions. Spray leaves hard with water |
| Root-knot nematodes | Cause gall-like swellings on the roots and stunt growth | 3-4 year rotation, intercropping with marigold |
| Bean weevil | Lays eggs on pods in the field and eats into the seed during storage | Storage management is key. See Section 15 |
| Birds (pigeons, magpies, pheasants) | Peck off the cotyledons right after germination | Cover with straw or netting until germination |
| Soil insects (grubs, cutworms) | Eat the sown seed and young stems | Coat the seed with wood ash, and in the morning dig around cut-off plants to catch them |

### 9-3. Materials for Control Without Chemicals

| Material | How to make | Target | Caution |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bordeaux mixture | Dissolve 1kg of copper sulfate and 1kg of quicklime separately in 100L of water, then mix | Prevents anthracnose and bacterial disease | Requires having copper on hand. Use immediately after mixing. It prevents, it does not cure |
| Soapy water | 20g unscented soap per 1L of water | Aphids, mites | Spraying at midday in hot weather scorches the leaves |
| Dusting with dry ash | Dust fine ash on the leaves on a dewy morning | Repels leaf-eating insects and fungi | Also supplies potassium |
| Wood ash water | Soak 1 doe of ash in 10L of water overnight and use the water on top | Suppresses fungi | Alkaline, so use it diluted |
| Wood vinegar | Dilute the liquid produced when making charcoal 300-500 times | Repellent, promotes growth | The concentrate is strongly acidic, so always dilute it |
| Hot pepper or garlic water | Grind, steep in water, strain, and use | Repellent | Effect does not last long, so spray often |
| Diluted milk | Dilute milk 5-10 times in water | Powdery mildew, mites | Leftovers spoil and smell |

### 9-4. Cultural Control

| Method | Principle |
|---|---|
| Seed sorting | Anthracnose is seed-borne. Culling spotted and discolored seed alone greatly reduces outbreaks |
| Hot-water treatment | 50-55°C for 10-15 minutes kills pathogens on the seed surface |
| 3-4 year rotation | Waits for the anthracnose fungus and nematodes to decline in the soil |
| Staking and spacing | With airflow, leaves dry quickly and fungi cannot take hold |
| Drainage | A good many kidney bean diseases are related to overwatering |
| Staggered sowing | Sowing in batches two weeks apart spreads out the risk |
| No entering the field after rain | If a person brushes against wet leaves, anthracnose spreads across the whole field |
| Residue management | Always burn diseased residue. Return only healthy residue to the field |

## 10. Nutrient Deficiency and Excess

| Symptom | Cause | Natural-material remedy |
|---|---|---|
| Pale yellow overall starting from the lower leaves, plants are small | Nitrogen deficiency. Poor nodulation is a common cause | First dig up the roots and check the nodules. If there are none, give a small amount of urine diluted tenfold |
| Leaf edges dry up brown | Potassium deficiency | Scatter wood ash between plants and lightly work it in |
| Growth is slow and leaves take on a purplish tint | Phosphorus deficiency | Bone meal, roasted ground fish bone, wood ash |
| Flowers open but pods fail to set | Boron deficiency, or heat above 30 degrees | Compost made from a mix of materials. Run water along the furrows to lower soil temperature |
| Yellowing between the leaf veins | Magnesium or iron deficiency | Stop using ash. Increase organic matter with leaf mold and well-rotted compost |
| New leaves are small and malformed, and the growing point dies | Calcium or boron deficiency | Roasted ground eggshell, ground shells |
| Only the leaves grow lush and dark green, with few pods | Nitrogen excess | Stop the nitrogen supply immediately. Induce side branches with pinching |
| Lower leaves turn yellow and drop | Waterlogging during the monsoon season (jangma, late June to July in South Korea) | Dig drainage ditches. Not a fertilizer problem |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Yellowing of the leaves in kidney beans has several possible causes, so it needs to be distinguished carefully. If the lower leaves gradually turn yellow and the plants are small, it is nitrogen deficiency. If the lower leaves turn yellow all at once and drop after the monsoon, it is waterlogging. If the veins stay green while only the tissue between them turns yellow, it is magnesium or iron deficiency. Because the remedies differ, look closely at the shape of the symptom before rushing to apply fertilizer.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Micronutrient deficiencies are hard to address one by one. Without chemical fertilizer, there is effectively no way to obtain molybdenum or cobalt. Instead, compost rotted down from a mix of vegetable scraps, fruit peels, eggshells, fish bones, fallen leaves, weeds, and ash together contains micronutrients across the board. The principle of putting everything into the compost heap is, in practice, the real strategy for managing micronutrients.

## 11. Intercropping and Crop Rotation

### 11-1. Compatibility

| Compatibility | Crop | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Good | Maize, squash | Three Sisters planting. They exchange support, nitrogen, and ground cover |
| Good | Marigold | Root exudates suppress nematodes |
| Good | Lettuce, spinach | Covers the ground to hold in moisture. Little competition for nutrients |
| Good | Potato | Their diseases do not overlap |
| Neutral | Cabbage, napa cabbage group | Planted after kidney beans, they use the leftover nitrogen |
| Caution | Tomato, chili pepper, eggplant | Can share pests and diseases |
| Bad | Garlic, onion group | Their exudates are reported to suppress root-nodule bacteria |
| Bad | Continuous cropping with other legumes | Anthracnose and nematodes accumulate |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Here are the specifics of the Three Sisters planting. Plant maize first at 30cm intervals, and once it reaches knee height, sow two or three seeds of pole-type kidney bean about 10cm from the base of each maize plant. Plant squash sparsely in the wide open space between the maize rows. The kidney bean vine climbs the maize, and the squash leaves cover the ground. Maize demands a lot of nitrogen, and the kidney bean supplies part of it, while the squash's broad leaves suppress weeds and block moisture evaporation, and its thorny stems discourage animals from approaching.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;One thing to watch in this arrangement is light. The kidney bean's yield drops by half at 40% shade. Plant the maize densely, and the kidney bean gets buried in shade, growing long vines without setting pods. Set the maize spacing wider than usual, and run the rows north-south so that shade does not linger long on one side.

### 11-2. Crop Rotation

| Item | Standard |
|---|---|
| Break period | 3-4 years. RDA 3-year break group |
| Ideal cycle | 4 years |
| Continuous-cropping injury | Anthracnose and root-knot nematodes accumulate |

| 4-Year Rotation Example |
|---|
| Year 1: Kidney bean (with maize and squash) |
| Year 2: Leafy vegetables or the onion/scallion group |
| Year 3: Root vegetables or cereals |
| Year 4: Potato or sweet potato |
| Back to kidney bean |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Here is a compromise for when the field is too small to allow room for rotation. The simplest approach is to divide the field into four plots and move the kidney bean planting one plot over each year. If the field is small and cannot be divided into plots, at least shift the position of the row where kidney beans are planted every year. Simply planting potato this year where kidney beans grew last year, and kidney beans this year where potato grew last year, considerably slows the buildup of anthracnose and nematodes.

## 12. Harvest and Threshing

### 12-1. Judging Harvest Time

| Use | Harvest time (after sowing) | Criteria |
|---|:---:|---|
| Whole pod (snap type) | 45-55 days | When the pod is still tender and the seeds inside are small |
| Green kidney bean | About 60 days | When the pod is plump and the seeds have swollen but are still green |
| Dry seed (early bush type) | 70-80 days | When the pod dries yellow and the seed hardens |
| Dry seed (late pole type) | 100-130 days | Ripens progressively from the bottom up |
| Harvest decision | When 70-80% of the pods have dried and turned brown | |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Pole-type kidney beans ripen from the bottom upward. By the time the lower pods have fully dried, flowers may still remain higher up. Pick from the bottom up in turn, and the plant keeps producing new flowers, lengthening the harvest period; leave it alone, and it stops flowering. Bush types ripen relatively evenly, so cutting and harvesting all at once causes little loss.

> **If rain is forecast, harvest without waiting for full ripeness. A dry pod that gets rained on and dries out again and again eventually splits and drops its seeds to the ground, and a wet pod grows mold.**

### 12-2. Harvesting and Drying

| Step | Details |
|---|---|
| 1 | Do this on a clear, dry day, after the dew has dried |
| 2 | Pick the lower pods first, or for the bush type cut the base with a sickle |
| 3 | Leave the roots in the field |
| 4 | Dry the whole pods in the sun for 4-5 days |
| 5 | After threshing, dry for another 5-7 days in the shade |

> **Drying threshed kidney beans in direct sunlight turns them reddish and degrades their quality. Dry the whole pods in the sun, but once the seeds are out, always dry them in the shade.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This two-stage drying is especially important for kidney beans. The pod is thick and firm and needs sun to open up well, but the seed, with its thin and vividly colored skin, fades or turns reddish and its coat cracks when exposed to strong sun. A cracked seed coat becomes the first entry point for insects during storage. The rule is to spread the seed thinly in a ventilated shade and dry it slowly.

### 12-3. Threshing - Methods Without Tools

| Method | Tool | Procedure | Efficiency |
|---|---|---|---|
| Treading in a sack | A thick burlap sack or bag | Put the dry pods in the sack, tie the opening, and tread on it with your feet | Most convenient for small amounts. Seeds do not scatter |
| Flail | A tool with a swinging rod attached to a long handle | Spread a mat, lay the pods out thinly, and strike down on them from above | Suitable for a medium scale. Hitting too hard cracks the seeds |
| Beating with a stick | A wooden stick | Put in a sack or spread on a mat and beat | Used in place of a flail when none is available |
| Shelling by hand | None | Twist the pod open | Fast, since the seeds are large. Suitable for seed stock |
| Striking against a board | A board or barrel | Hold a bundle and strike it against the inside wall of the barrel | The seeds collect inside the barrel, so losses are low |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Kidney bean seeds are large and crack easily if beaten hard. A cracked seed is the first place insects get into during storage, and it cannot be used as seed stock either. Putting the pods in a sack and treading on them causes the least loss, and when using a flail, it is better to strike lightly many times rather than hard. For the portion meant for seed, hand-shelling is the sure method.

### 12-4. Cleaning - Winnowing by Wind

| Method | Principle | Procedure |
|---|---|---|
| Winnowing in the wind | Seeds are heavy, husks and debris are light | On a windy day, hold a winnowing basket or wide vessel high and pour out the contents slowly |
| Winnowing basket | Shake up and down while tossing forward | The heavy seeds stay at the back while the light material is pushed forward |
| Fanning | One person pours while another fans a breeze | Used on windless days |
| Sieving | Sorts by hole size | Filters out cracked seeds and debris |
| Floating in water | Empty and insect-eaten seeds are light | Pour into a bucket of water and skim off what floats. Dry again the same day |
| Hand sorting | Done by eye | The seeds are large, making anthracnose spots easy to spot |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Always include a final hand-sorting step for kidney beans. Because the seeds are large, the dark reddish spots of anthracnose-infected seed are easy to see. This one round of visual checking greatly reduces disease in next year's field. Spread the seed on white cloth in a bright spot, and the spots stand out clearly.
