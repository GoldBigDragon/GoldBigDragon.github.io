## 13. Seed Saving

| Item | Method |
|---|---|
| Selecting seed-saving plants | Mark plants in the field in advance that show no anthracnose symptoms, carry many pods, and mature early |
| Isolation | Keep a few meters away from other varieties, and choose seed-saving plants from inside the field |
| Harvest timing | Harvest after the pods have dried completely brown |
| Shelling | Shell by hand so the seeds don't crack |
| Sorting | Discard all spotted, cracked, wrinkled, or discolored seeds. Keep only plump, glossy seeds |
| Drying | 5-7 days in the shade. Moisture 12-13% |
| Storage | Keep in an airtight container in a cool, dark place. Use a container separate from food stock |
| Storage life | 3-4 years airtight at room temperature |
| Renewal | Every 3-4 years, obtain seed from elsewhere and mix in some of it |

> **The most important task in saving kidney bean seed is picking out anthracnose-infected seed. A single seed with a dark reddish-brown spot can spread the disease through an entire field the following year. This task needs nothing but bright light, your eyes, and time, and it is the strongest control measure available where no chemicals are on hand.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Kidney beans are self-pollinating, so pure lines are maintained well, though not as perfectly as with peas. Cross-pollination by bees occurs occasionally, so if you grow several varieties, plant them a few meters apart and choose seed-saving plants from inside the field. Plants at the field's edge are the most likely to receive pollen from another variety.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The habit of marking seed-saving plants in the field beforehand makes a big difference to the outcome. If you select only by looking at the seed after harvest, you end up selecting only for size and shape. Selecting in the field lets you also select for traits that actually matter, such as anthracnose resistance, pod count, heat tolerance, and lodging resistance. A plant that alone stays clean in a year when disease is spreading is especially valuable seed.

| Seed storage duration and germination rate |
|---|
| Year of harvest: 90% or higher |
| After 1 year: 80-90% |
| After 2 years: 65-80% |
| After 3 years: 45-65% |
| After 4 years: drops sharply |

## 14. Toxicity - Phaseolus Lectin Poisoning

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This section is the most important in this document. Kidney beans are a safe and highly nutritious food when properly cooked, but raw or undercooked beans can actually make a person sick. And the scale and nature of that risk differ from other beans.

### 14-1. What Is Phytohaemagglutinin

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Seeds of the genus Phaseolus contain a lectin called phytohaemagglutinin. Lectins are proteins that bind to sugar chains, a chemical defense the plant makes to keep its seeds from being eaten. When this substance enters the human gut, it binds to the sugar chains on the surface of intestinal lining cells, interfering with absorption and damaging the cells. The "haemagglutinin" in the name means it clumps red blood cells together, and this clumping is used as the unit for measuring the substance's toxicity.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The problem is the amount. Soybeans, peas, and lentils also contain lectins, but Phaseolus beans are at a different level. The lectin activity of red kidney beans reaches dozens of times that of other beans. That's why undercooked soybeans end up causing no more than stomach discomfort, while just four or five undercooked kidney beans can trigger an emergency.

| Item | Details |
|---|---|
| Causative substance | Phytohaemagglutinin (Phaseolus lectin) |
| Poisoning dose | 4-5 raw or undercooked beans |
| Symptom onset | 1-3 hours after eating |
| Main symptoms | Severe nausea, violent vomiting, abdominal pain, followed by diarrhea |
| Recovery | Usually recovers within hours to a day, but dehydration is dangerous |
| Highest-risk group | Children, the elderly, and people already weakened. They tolerate dehydration poorly |
| High-content strains | Red kidney bean strains are especially high. White strains are at about one-third the level |

> **Kidney bean poisoning is neither rare nor mild. Just four or five raw beans - an amount easily eaten by mistake - can bring on violent vomiting and diarrhea within hours. Where medical care is out of reach, dehydration alone is a real danger.**

### 14-2. Why a Low Temperature Is More Dangerous

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Here is the most counterintuitive and most important fact in this section. Warming kidney beans to an in-between temperature actually makes them more toxic than eating them raw.

| Treatment | Lectin activity | Safety |
|---|---|---|
| Raw | Baseline | Dangerous |
| Long heating around 80°C | Up to 5x baseline | More dangerous than raw |
| 100°C boiling water for 10+ minutes | Nearly 0 | Safe |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Here is the explanation for why this happens. Inside a dry bean, the lectin is clumped together with other proteins or trapped inside cell walls, so it can't fully release in the digestive tract. Heat around 80°C loosens the cell wall and protein structure enough to let the lectin come out, but it isn't strong enough to destroy the lectin itself. The result is more active lectin in a form that is easy to absorb. Only at 100°C does the lectin protein's structure itself break down, so its activity disappears.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This is exactly why it is especially dangerous in a disaster situation. When fuel is scarce, people naturally turn to a low flame and long simmering. Residual-heat cooking in an insulated box, leaving a pot on a stove's dying embers, and warming it overnight on a brazier all fall into this category. These methods are excellent fuel-saving techniques for soybeans or lentils, but for kidney beans they become exactly the most dangerous way to cook.

> **Simmering on a low flame to save fuel is forbidden for kidney beans. Even if you use residual-heat cooking, you must first boil them at a full, rolling boil for 10 minutes or more before putting them into the insulated box.**

### 14-3. Procedure for Eating Safely

| Step | Details | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Soaking | Soak in room-temperature water for at least 5 hours, ideally 8 hours or more | Part of the lectin dissolves into the water, and a path opens for heat to reach the interior |
| 2. Discarding the soak water | Always discard it. Do not use it in cooking | This discards the lectin and oligosaccharides that leached into it |
| 3. Rinsing | Rinse two or so times in fresh water | Washes off what remains on the surface |
| 4. Boiling on high heat | Add fresh water and bring to a full boil. It must reach 100°C | This is where the lectin is destroyed |
| 5. Holding for 10+ minutes | At least 10 minutes after boiling starts, 30 minutes to be safe | 10 minutes is the minimum, and larger beans take longer |
| 6. Checking doneness | Press with a finger to see if it mashes completely | A hard core remaining inside means it's undercooked |
| 7. Discarding the water | It is safer to discard the cooking water too | This removes the remaining lectin and oligosaccharides along with it |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Let's underline once more why soaking is necessary. An unsoaked dry kidney bean has a hard exterior that keeps heat from reaching the interior. The result is a bean cooked on the outside but undercooked inside - and that is precisely the most dangerous state. On top of that, skipping the soak more than doubles the fuel needed. Soaking benefits both safety and fuel use. In summer, the water can spoil, so change it every three or four hours, or soak in the evening and drain in the morning.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;If a pressure cooker is available, it is the safest option and uses the least fuel. Pressure cooking reaches a temperature above 100°C, so 15 to 20 minutes is enough. But assuming no pressure cooker is available, the basic approach is a large pot with plenty of water, covered, boiled on high heat. Check whether the water is actually boiling by sound and by steam. A surface with only calm wisps of steam is not yet at 100°C. The water must keep surging up in large bubbles.

### 14-4. When Poisoning Occurs

| Action | Details |
|---|---|
| Stop immediately | Stop eating any more of the beans and discard everything that was cooked |
| Fluid replacement | Replace the water and salts lost through vomiting and diarrhea. Dissolve a very small amount of salt and sugar in boiled, cooled water and have the person drink it in small, frequent sips |
| Rest | Lay the person down and keep them calm. They usually recover within hours to a day |
| Watch closely | Children, the elderly, and the weakened dehydrate quickly, so watch them especially closely |
| Identifying the cause | Check which pot and which batch of beans it was, so the same mistake isn't repeated |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The symptoms can be mistaken for bloating from dietary fiber. The way to tell them apart is intensity and timing of onset. Bloating from fiber is just a gurgling, gassy feeling that comes on gradually over half a day. Lectin poisoning starts with sudden nausea and violent vomiting within one to three hours of eating. The characteristic order is vomiting first, followed by diarrhea.

### 14-5. Other Antinutrients

| Compound | What it does | How to remove it |
|---|---|---|
| Trypsin inhibitors | Block the action of protein-digesting enzymes | Soaking, then 30+ minutes at 100°C |
| Phytic acid | Binds iron, zinc, and calcium, blocking their absorption | Soaking, sprouting, or fermenting reduces it by up to 60-80% |
| Oligosaccharides | Undigestible by humans, so gut bacteria ferment them | Discard the soaking and cooking water |
| Saponins | Produce foam | Skim off the foam while boiling |
| Mold toxins | Produced by mold during storage | Beans with mold are not made safe by washing or cooking. Discard them |

## 15. Storage Without Refrigeration

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The storability grade of kidney beans is SS. The dry seed holds up for 3 years at room temperature, and with fat content around only 1%, rancidity is barely an issue. But if conditions go wrong, weevils can turn the entire stored batch into empty shells.

> **Beans are lost not to rot but to insects eating them. The art of storage is, in essence, the art of keeping insects out.**

### 15-1. Drying Comes First

| Stage | Method | Check |
|---|---|---|
| First drying | Pods in the sun, whole, for 4-5 days | The pods dry out completely |
| Threshing | Do this after they are dry | If not dry enough, the pods won't split open |
| Second drying | 5-7 days in the shade, spread thin for airflow | Direct sunlight causes discoloration and seed-coat cracking |
| Target moisture | 12-13% | When bitten, teeth don't sink in - it just snaps |
| Salt test | Put dry salt in a jar with the beans and shake | If the salt sticks to the jar wall, it's still too damp |
| Cooling | Cool completely before packing away | Sealing while still warm causes condensation |

### 15-2. Controlling Bean Weevils in Storage

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The bean weevil group is the greatest threat to stored legumes. The frightening part is that it already begins in the field. Adult weevils lay eggs on pods still hanging on the plant, and those eggs hatch inside storage and bore into the beans. The eggs are already riding along when the beans come in, and since one generation takes less than a month, a single sack can be reduced to riddled shells within half a year.

| Method | Principle | Application | Effect |
|---|---|---|---|
| Complete sealing | Cuts off oxygen to suffocate them | Fill a glass jar or metal container completely and seal it | Most reliable. Kills even eggs and larvae within 3-4 weeks |
| Mixing in wood ash | Fine particles scratch the waxy exoskeleton, causing dehydration | Mix in 2-5% of the beans' volume | Rinse off with water before eating |
| Diatomaceous earth | Silica shells damage the exoskeleton | 1-3g per 1kg of beans | More effective than ash. Works only when dry |
| Oil coating | An oil film blocks eggs from attaching and hatching | Rub 2-5mL of cooking oil into 1kg of beans | Do not use on seed stock |
| Cold treatment | Eggs and larvae die below freezing | Set outside for a few days in the depth of winter, then bring back in | Watch for condensation when bringing it back inside |
| Heat treatment | Eggs and larvae die at 60°C | Spread thin in the sun for half a day | You must accept some discoloration. Do not use on seed stock |
| Dried chili, Sichuan pepper, mugwort | Essential-oil compounds suppress egg-laying | Layer them in | A supplementary measure |
| Charcoal, roasted salt | Absorb moisture | Wrap in cloth and add | Also helps prevent mold |
| Burying in ash | Combines sealing with physical control | Layer ash and beans in a crock | Widely used traditionally |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The most important thing is complete sealing. Insects also need to breathe. Inside a sealed container, as the beans and insects use up oxygen, oxygen drops and carbon dioxide rises, killing the insects. The beans, being dormant, tolerate this condition. The more full the container and the less empty space, the faster the effect. Packing tightly into several small containers is better than one large one, which also has the advantage that if one container goes bad, you don't lose everything.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Oil coating and mixing in wood ash are methods long used on small farms in India and Africa. Rub 2-3ml of cooking oil into 1kg of beans so a thin film forms on every seed, or mix in sieved fine ash at 2-5% of the volume. Insects cannot develop resistance to this, and it is harmless to people. Since kidney beans are soaked in water for five hours or more before cooking anyway, the ash simply washes off in that process.

| Container | Sealing | Duration | Notes |
|---|:---:|---|---|
| Glass jar | Very good | 3+ years | Doesn't block light, so keep it in a dark place |
| Metal container | Very good | 3+ years | Completely blocks rats and insects |
| Earthenware crock | Good | 2-3 years | Temperature changes slowly, and rats can't gnaw through |
| Thick plastic | Fair | 1-2 years | Rats can gnaw through |
| Underground storage pit | Good | 2-3 years | If water seeps in, the whole stock is lost |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The most reliable senses for inspection are hearing and smell. If you put your ear to the container and hear a rustling sound, insects are active; a musty smell means mold has begun. Small round holes on the surface mean the weevils have already gone through a generation. Even at this point, you can save what remains by taking everything out, drying it in the shade, floating it in water to skim off the hollow seeds, mixing in ash, and resealing it. Checking once every two months is the standard.

## 16. Cooking and Processing

### 16-1. Saving Fuel Without Sacrificing Safety

| Method | Fuel | Safety condition |
|---|:---:|---|
| Pressure cooking | Least | Pressure-cook soaked beans for 15-20 minutes. The safest and most economical |
| Boil then residual-heat cooking | Little | Always boil at 100°C for 10-15 minutes first, then place in the insulated box |
| Residual-heat cooking with no prior boil | - | Never do this. Toxicity actually rises |
| Roasting | Little | Roasting dry beans cannot reliably guarantee safety. Not recommended |
| Blanching young green kidney beans | Very little | Young beans have little lectin, so a few minutes in boiling water is enough |
| Sprouting | None | Kidney bean sprouts still contain lectin and are not eaten raw. Always cook them |

> **For kidney beans, the order of fuel-saving is this: first soak thoroughly to shorten the cooking time, then always boil at 100°C for at least 10 minutes, and only after that finish with residual heat. Reversing this order breaks down safety.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Remember that soaking is the single biggest way to save fuel. Kidney beans soaked for eight hours cook in less than half the time of unsoaked ones. It costs water and time, but not fuel. And that same soaking also raises safety. In a disaster situation, the single habit of soaking tomorrow's beans every evening takes care of fuel and safety together.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Here, separately, are methods useful for other beans that must not be applied to kidney beans. First, putting soaked beans straight into the insulated box. Second, leaving a pot on a stove's dying embers or on a brazier overnight. Third, leaving beans soaking in lukewarm broth for a long time. Fourth, grinding dry beans into flour and mixing it into water to eat. All of these are ways of cooking that never reach 100°C, so all are forbidden for kidney beans.

### 16-2. Processing for Storage

| Processing | Procedure summary | Storage | Value |
|---|---|---|---|
| Boiling and drying | Boil thoroughly, then dry completely in the sun | Months | Boiling once shortens all later cooking |
| Salt-cured boiled beans | Boil, then layer with salt in a crock | Months | Soak in water to remove the salt before eating |
| Mashing and drying boiled beans | Boil, mash, spread thin, and dry | Months | Mixing with water turns it straight into porridge |
| Blanching and drying young green kidney beans | Blanch, drain, and dry | Months | Add to soups and porridge in winter |
| Kidney bean paste | Boil, mash, and strain out the skins | Days; months if dried | Easy to digest, good for children and the elderly |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Boiling and drying is especially valuable processing for kidney beans. It handles the long heating needed to eliminate the lectin all at once, and afterward all that's needed is a brief soak and reheat. It's a structure that front-loads fuel use and saves fuel afterward, and above all it removes the burden of having to reach 100°C every single time you cook. If you boil and dry a full pot in autumn when firewood is plentiful, you can eat safely all winter long.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Boiled and dried kidney beans have no moisture left, so they also resist insects and mold well. However, if they aren't dried completely, mold appears right away, so dry them until they snap with a crisp sound when broken. Kept sealed, they last for months.

### 17-1. Five Things Not to Forget

| Item | Details |
|---|---|
| Cooking safety | Poisoning comes from just 4-5 raw beans. Soak for 5+ hours, discard the soaking water, then boil at 100°C for 10+ minutes |
| No low temperatures | Long heating at 80°C can raise toxicity up to five times that of raw beans. Always boil first before any residual-heat cooking |
| Seed selection | Anthracnose spreads through seed. Pick out and discard seeds with dark reddish-brown spots |
| Staking | The vines wrap around supports about 2cm in diameter. Interplanting with maize needs no extra staking material |
| Vines and leaves | For kidney beans, the nitrogen leaving in the seed is roughly on par with the amount fixed. Failing to return the vines to the field depletes the soil |
