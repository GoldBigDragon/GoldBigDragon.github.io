## 1. Disaster-Preparedness Evaluation

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The chickpea's overall grade is C, 41.2 points. Broken down by scenario, its short-term ability to get through the early stage of a disaster is D (39.7), its long-term self-reliance ability across multiple years is C (42.8), and its value as a supplementary food beyond the staple is SS (62.2). The fact that only the supplementary grade stands out so high sums up the chickpea's character. It is not a crop that fills the stomach, but one that changes the quality of the table.

### 1-3. How to Read the Strengths

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The criteria graded SS are three: fertilizer demand, infrastructure needs, and storability. Those graded S are protein density, micronutrients, water use, taste, and seed saving.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Water use grade S is the chickpea's defining trait. Total evapotranspiration of 250mm is among the lowest of all legumes, and only lentil, mung bean, and cowpea receive the same grade. This ability comes not from physiological thrift but from structure. The chickpea's taproot goes straight down to 1m or deeper, drawing on deep soil moisture even after the surface has completely dried out. Its leaves are also small and thick, so it loses little water to transpiration. Where droughts are becoming more frequent, and where carrying water by hand costs human labor, this difference becomes, directly, a difference in labor.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Storability's SS is also a value worth noting. In the FAO Food Loss Index, the median loss rate at the storage stage is a very low 0.49%, and dried seed holds up for 3 years at room temperature. The seed coat is thick and hard, so insects have trouble boring in, and fat content is a moderate 6% or so, so rancidity does not set in quickly either. This means that once harvested, it can be kept and used for a long time.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Micronutrients' S is the basis for the SS supplementary-food grade. Eating 557g, the amount equal to 2,100kcal a day, covers 10% of the recommended intake for as many as 16 nutrients. Iron in particular, at 4.6-8.3mg per 100g, and calcium at 127-184mg, are both high for a plant food. When meat and dairy disappear, anemia and declining bone density are threats that creep in quietly over months, and the chickpea addresses both at once.

> **The chickpea is not a crop that fills the stomach, but one that delivers protein and iron even from dry land. Harvesting something in the very spot where other beans fail in a year without rain — that is this crop's place.**

### 1-4. How to Read the Weaknesses

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The criteria graded D are six: calories per area, perenniality, sunlight response, cooking & processing, tillage energy, and non-food value. C went to pest & disease pressure and prevalence in South Korea.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Prevalence in South Korea at C is the most realistic barrier. Commercial cultivation of the chickpea is almost nonexistent in South Korea; at most it is planted experimentally in one or two provinces. Once distribution networks break down, obtaining seed should be considered impossible. To include this crop in a plan, there is no choice but to secure seed now, and once secured, you must be prepared to spend the first two or three years on seed saving rather than harvest for eating. Put the other way around, if you have seed in hand, being self-pollinating means the line stays pure and, kept at room temperature, it lasts 3 years, so you can carry it forward on your own.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Calories per area at D is also a heavy weight. It comes to 679kcal per 1m², only 0.26× the self-sufficiency line. The yield itself is set low, at 180kg per 1,000m², compared with soybean's 250kg or fava bean's 300kg. This is due to the structural limit of each pod holding only one or two seeds. Building a diet on chickpea alone is impossible; grain or tuber crops (tubers/storage roots) should carry the calorie load, with chickpea layered on top to add protein and micronutrients.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Cooking & processing at D is a cost paid every day. Dried chickpeas must be soaked for 8 hours and boiled for over an hour. The seed coat is thick and the tissue is firm, so it takes time for heat to reach the interior. Where fuel is scarce, this burden is not small, and Section 16 deals separately with ways to reduce it. Peeling and splitting the seed in half cuts cooking time to less than half — this is the form known in India as chana dal.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Continuous-cropping tolerance at B and pest & disease pressure at C should be read together. The chickpea's most fearsome diseases are Fusarium wilt (Fusarium oxysporum f. sp. ciceris) and Ascochyta blight (Ascochyta rabiei), both of which persist in the soil and on seed for many years. Ascochyta blight in particular can bring down an entire field within a few days in a wet year. Rotation of 3 years or more and thorough seed selection are the only countermeasures.

## 2. What Kind of Plant Is This?

### 2-1. Classification and Origin

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The chickpea is an annual herb in the legume family (Fabaceae), genus Cicer. The chickpea is the only cultivated crop in this genus, so it sits a little apart, phylogenetically, from other legumes. This fact matters in practice. The root-nodule bacteria that pair with the chickpea are a chickpea-only strain called Mesorhizobium ciceri, and they do not work with the strains that live symbiotically with soybean, pea, or kidney bean. Using soil from a field that grew a different bean as an inoculant has no effect.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Its center of origin is the region stretching from southeastern Turkey to Syria. Its wild ancestor, Cicer reticulatum, still grows there today. As a plant adapted to a Mediterranean climate with wet winters and bone-dry summers, the chickpea's physiology developed toward conserving water and withstanding dry land. Rooting deep, keeping its leaves small, and covering the leaf surface densely with fine hairs and glands are all results of this.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The chickpea self-pollinates. Its flowers are small and the petals enclose the reproductive parts, so outside pollen has trouble getting in. Even when several lines are planted side by side, their traits do not mix, and seed saved and replanted grows into the same thing as its parent. This is where the seed-saving grade of S comes from. The harder a crop is to obtain seed for, the more this trait matters, because once you obtain it a single time, you can carry it forward on your own.

### 2-2. Appearance

| Organ | Characteristics |
|---|---|
| Leaves | Pinnately compound. Several pairs of small leaflets, with toothed edges. Densely covered in fine hairs and glands |
| Stem | Upright, branching heavily from the base. Height 20-100cm, usually around 40cm |
| Roots | A straight taproot goes down to 1m or deeper. The deepest of any legume |
| Flowers | Butterfly-shaped, white, pale pink, or purple. Small, blooming singly in the leaf axils |
| Pods | Short and plump, covered in fine hairs. Hold only 1-2 seeds |
| Seeds | Wrinkled surface, with a pointed beak on one side. The resemblance to a small bird's head is how the crop got its name |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The chickpea's most distinctive structure is the dense covering of glands on its leaves and pods. These glands secrete organic acids such as malic acid and oxalic acid, making the plant's surface acidic. Walk into a chickpea field in the morning and touch the leaves, and your fingertips turn sticky and sour. Brush dew-covered leaves and you actually collect droplets that taste sour. In India and the Middle East, people have even collected this dew to use in place of vinegar.

> **The acidic secretions of chickpea leaves are a natural defense that fends off pests. This is why the crop suffers less damage from leaf-chewing insects than other beans do, and it is also why handling it bare-handed for long periods leaves the skin stinging.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In practice, this acidic secretion means two things. One is that the chickpea fends off a good share of its own leaf pests without any pesticide. The other is that it is better to wear gloves during harvest and other handling. People with sensitive skin will find it reddens and stings after long contact. Right after rain, or on a morning when the dew has not yet dried, the secretion dissolves into the water and becomes even more irritating.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Having only one or two seeds per pod is a structural limit on yield. It is clearly fewer than soybean's two or three, fava bean's two or three, or pea's four to ten. Instead, the chickpea compensates in part by setting many pods and branching heavily, so that each plant carries a large number of pods.

### 2-3. Growth Stages

| Stage | Timing | Key Physiology | Key Nutrients |
|---|---|---|---|
| Germination & seedling stage | Up to 4 weeks after sowing | Germination takes 10-14 days. Roots go down deep first | Phosphorus, a small amount of nitrogen |
| Vegetative stage | Weeks 4-8 | Stems stand upright and branch heavily | Nitrogen supplied by root nodules |
| Flowering stage | Weeks 8-12 | Flowers open in order starting from the lower nodes | Phosphorus, boron |
| Pod-filling stage | Weeks 10-14 | Pods plump up and seeds thicken | Potassium, phosphorus |
| Ripening & maturity stage | Weeks 12-17 | Leaves turn yellow and pods dry to brown | Stop irrigation |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;It helps to know in advance that germination is slow. After sowing, the chickpea takes ten to fifteen days to send up a shoot. That is considerably slower than soybean's three to five days or mung bean's two or three days. If the soil dries out during this period, germination is delayed further, while standing water simply rots the seed. Take care not to assume failure and resow just because you see nothing ten days after sowing.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;It also matters that during this slow germination period, the roots go down deep first. The chickpea's strategy is to secure a water supply before building up its above-ground parts. So even though early growth looks slow, the roots have already spread considerably, and this carries forward into later drought tolerance. Watering frequently in the early stage interferes with this strategy and, if anything, keeps the roots shallow.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There is also something worth noting about nitrogen in the early stage. The root nodules do not start working right after sowing. It takes 3 to 4 weeks for the seed to germinate, the roots to extend, the bacteria to infect them, and the resulting nodules to start handing over nitrogen. In the meantime, the young chickpea plant lives on nutrients stored in the seed and nitrogen already in the soil. This is why it makes sense to put a small amount of nitrogen into the base fertilizer — but it must stay small. If the soil has plenty of nitrogen, the plant no longer feels the need to trade with the bacteria, and it scales back nodule formation on its own.

### 2-4. Nutrition per 100g

| Nutrient | Amount | Notes |

| Carbohydrates | About 60g | Mostly starch |

| Sugars | 2.55g | On the less-sweet side |
| Calcium | 127-184mg | High for a plant food |

| Phosphorus | 286-528mg | Involved in energy metabolism |
| Folate | Abundant | Involved in preventing anemia and in fetal development |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The chickpea's 377kcal is on the high side among legumes. It comes after soybean's 401 and peanut's 520, and is higher than pea's 354, fava bean's 341, or mung bean's 352. This is because its fat content, at around 6%, is roughly three times that of pea or fava bean. It is worth keeping straight that its D grade for calories per area is not because its calories per 100g are low, but because its yield itself is small.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The amino acid profile is also worth a look. Chickpea protein is rich in lysine and a little short on methionine. Grains are exactly the opposite. Eating it together with wheat or rice fills in each other's gaps and noticeably raises protein utilization. The Middle Eastern habit of pairing wheat bread with chickpea preparations, and the Indian habit of serving chana or dal over rice, both rest on this same principle.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;It is worth noting that calcium and iron are both high at once. However, phytic acid interferes with the absorption of both, so treatments that reduce phytic acid — soaking, sprouting, and fermentation — are nutritionally meaningful too. The chickpea in particular is an easy bean to sprout, so there is a lot of room to put sprouting to use.
