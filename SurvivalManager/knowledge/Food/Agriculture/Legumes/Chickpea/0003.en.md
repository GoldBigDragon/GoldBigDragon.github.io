## 3. Growing Conditions

### 3-1. Temperature and Light

| Condition | Limit | Optimum | Outside This Range |
|---|---|---|---|
| Germination temperature | 10°C or above | 20-25°C | Germination slows further at low temperatures. It already takes 10-14 days |
| Growth temperature | 12-35°C | 21-26°C | Nighttime temperature of 15°C or above is needed |
| Fruiting temperature | 15-28°C | 18-26°C | Range of 13°C, wide among legumes |
| Low temperature at flowering | Night 10°C or below | - | Pollen fails to form properly, so pods do not set |
| High temperature at flowering | 35°C or above | - | Flowers drop. However, heat tolerance is better than in most legumes |
| Cold hardiness | Tolerates light frost | - | Survives a brief, few-degree frost below freezing |
| Sunlight | 6 hours a day or more | 8 hours a day or more | Yield drops by half at 40% shade |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;A surprisingly tricky factor for the chickpea is nighttime temperature during flowering. If it drops below 10°C at night, pollen fails to form properly, and even flowers that open do not set pods. In South Korea, sowing in late March puts flowering in June and avoids this problem, but sowing too early can catch flowering in the cool nights of May. Sowing too late, on the other hand, runs into the monsoon and Ascochyta blight breaks out. This is why the sowing window is so narrow.

> **The most dangerous thing in growing chickpeas is not drought but the monsoon. In a year with too little water, yield simply falls, but if rain drags on while the pods are ripening, Ascochyta blight can wipe out the entire field.**

### 3-2. Soil and Water

| Condition | Limit | Optimum | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Soil texture | Sandy soil to loam | Well-drained sandy loam to loam | Absolutely avoid clay soil and land where water pools |
| pH | 5.5-8.6 | 6.0-8.0 | The widest tolerance range among legumes |
| Effective soil depth | 60cm or more | 1m or more | The taproot goes down to 1m |
| Season evapotranspiration | - | About 250mm | Among the lowest of all legumes |
| Flooding | Roots are damaged after just one day underwater | - | The most vulnerable of all legumes to waterlogging |
| Salt tolerance | Low | - | Not suited to land with salinity |
| Groundwater table | 80cm or lower | 1m or lower | Because roots run deep, a high water table damages them immediately |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;A pH tolerance range of 5.5 to 8.6 is the widest among legumes. This means it holds up fairly well in both acidic and alkaline soil, which is a real advantage where lime is hard to obtain. Below 5.5, though, nodule formation worsens — not because the plant itself cannot tolerate it, but because the bacteria cannot survive there.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Vulnerability to waterlogging is the condition that most needs emphasis for the chickpea. It is as weak in wet ground as it is strong in dry ground. Because the taproot runs deep, on land with a high water table the root tip stays constantly submerged, and root rot follows immediately. Raising beds 20cm or higher and digging deep drainage channels are a precondition for growing chickpeas. Avoid former paddy fields converted to dry fields, valley bottoms, and low-lying fields where water collects.

## 4. Varieties

### 4-1. Two Types

| Type | Seed | Characteristics | Disaster Suitability |
|---|---|---|---|
| Desi type | Small and angular, brown or black. Thick seed coat | Excellent cold hardiness and drought adaptation. Short growing period, resilient on poor soil | High |
| Kabuli type | Large and round, cream or white. Thin seed coat | High yield under good conditions. Long growing period, demanding | Medium |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Over 80% of the world's chickpeas are the Desi type, and the large, white chickpea commonly seen in stores is the Kabuli type. From a disaster-preparedness standpoint, Desi is clearly the better choice. Its thick seed coat resists rotting in the soil, its roots go in deeper, its shorter growing period lets it finish before the monsoon, and its yield drops less on poor, dry land. A thick seed coat also means it stands up better to insects during storage.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The Desi type's drawback is that its small seeds and thick skin take more work to cook. This is why, in India, Desi chickpeas are peeled and split in half and used as chana dal. Prepared this way, cooking time actually ends up shorter than for Kabuli.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;South Korea has practically no registered chickpea varieties, and in RDA adaptability trials, the yield of introduced landrace lines varied wildly, from 9 to 217kg per 1,000m². That is a difference of more than twentyfold depending on the line and the year, and which line you happen to get largely decides success or failure. If you have secured seed, it is reasonable to use the first two or three years planting several lines side by side on a small plot to sort out which one suits your field.

### 4-2. Selection Criteria by Situation

| Situation | Priority | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| The field is poor and dry | Desi type | Yield drops the least under these conditions |
| The monsoon comes early | Early-maturing Desi lines | Must finish before the rain arrives |
| Seed is scarce | Desi type | Smaller seeds let the same weight cover two to three times the area |
| Cooking fuel is scarce | Process into peeled, split form | Cooking time is cut in half regardless of the type |
| Seed obtained for the first time | Split among several lines | Adaptability varies enormously; a selection process is needed |

## 5. Field Preparation and Fertilizer - Root-Nodule Bacteria

### 5-1. How Nitrogen Fixation Happens

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;78% of the air is nitrogen. Even though effectively limitless nitrogen floats over everyone's head, plants cannot use it. Nitrogen in the air is a molecule with two nitrogen atoms locked together by a triple bond, and breaking that bond takes an enormous amount of energy. Breaking this bond industrially to make ammonia is the Haber-Bosch process, and the world's nitrogen fertilizer comes from it. It requires high temperature, high pressure, a catalyst, and huge amounts of fossil fuel.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Root-nodule bacteria (rhizobia) do this same job at room temperature and normal pressure. Using an enzyme called nitrogenase, they break the triple bond and turn nitrogen into ammonia. But there is a price. This reaction needs a great deal of energy, and the bacteria cannot photosynthesize on their own. So they live inside the roots of legume plants, receive the sugar the plant makes through photosynthesis, and hand over the nitrogen they fix in return. It is a trade relationship.

> **The bacterium that pairs with the chickpea is a dedicated strain called Mesorhizobium ciceri. Soil from a field that grew soybean, pea, or kidney bean cannot serve as an inoculant for chickpeas. Inoculating this crop is trickier than for any other legume.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This is the single biggest practical difficulty in growing chickpeas. Soil from a field that has never grown chickpeas has almost none of this bacterium, and most fields in South Korea fall into that category. Planting without inoculation produces almost no nodules; unable to make its own nitrogen, the plant's leaves turn yellow and yield falls sharply. This is thought to be one reason yields varied so wildly in South Korea's adaptability trials.

| Stage | What Happens |
|---|---|
| 1. Signal exchange | The chickpea's roots release flavonoids, and bacteria in the soil respond to that signal by releasing Nod factors |
| 2. Infection | Root hairs curl inward, wrapping around the bacteria, which then travel into the root along an infection thread |
| 3. Nodule formation | Cells inside the root divide, the nodule swells, and the bacteria settle inside it |
| 4. Nitrogen fixation | Inside the nodule, nitrogenase gets to work, turning atmospheric nitrogen into ammonia |
| 5. Distribution | Ammonia is converted into amino acids and transported to the stems, leaves, and pods |
| 6. Senescence | As the plant enters the ripening stage, the nodule loses function and returns to the soil along with the root |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Nitrogenase is extremely sensitive to oxygen and loses its function the instant oxygen reaches it. So the inside of the nodule is packed with a red pigment protein called leghemoglobin, which binds oxygen and keeps the oxygen concentration around the enzyme extremely low. It works on the same principle as why human blood is red, and the red or pink color you see when you cut a nodule open is this same pigment. This fact matters enormously in practice, because that red color is visible proof that the nodule is fixing nitrogen right now.

### 5-2. How to Check Whether Nodules Have Formed

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Whether or not you inoculated, you must check whether nodules actually formed. For the chickpea, this check matters more than for other legumes, because so many fields lack the native bacterium and the chance of failure is high.

| Step | Details |
|---|---|
| 1 | The best time is around when flowering begins, roughly 8 weeks after sowing |
| 2 | Choose two or three plants that look healthy |
| 3 | Do not pull it up; use a spade to dig widely around the plant and lift it out with the soil intact. Dig deep, since the taproot runs deep |
| 4 | Put it in a bucket of water and gently rinse the soil off |
| 5 | Count the nodules on the taproot and the thicker lateral roots |
| 6 | Split a few nodules open with a fingernail and check the color inside |

| Observation | Meaning | Action |
|---|---|---|
| 15 or more nodules per plant, especially clustered near the top of the taproot | Inoculation succeeded | Leave as is |
| The cut interior is red or pink | It is fixing nitrogen | Good condition |
| The cut interior is white or pale green | Nodules formed but are not fixing nitrogen | Check soil acidity, waterlogging, and phosphorus or sulfur deficiency |
| The cut interior is brown or black | Already senescent or dead | Normal at the ripening stage; a problem at the flowering stage |
| Almost no nodules | A field without the native bacterium. The most common case | Supplement nitrogen this year, and next year use this field's soil as an inoculant |

> **This one habit — digging up roots, splitting the nodules, and checking for red — is the single most reliable sign of whether a bean crop will succeed or fail in a world without nitrogen fertilizer.**

### 5-3. Where to Find an Inoculant

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Commercial inoculants are products that sell live bacteria carried in peat or clay. They will not be available in a disaster situation. Because the chickpea requires a dedicated strain, securing an inoculant is trickier than for other legumes, but it is not impossible.

| Method | Procedure | Effect | Caution |
|---|---|---|---|
| Coating seed with soil from a chickpea field | Scoop soil from 5-15cm below the surface of a field that grew chickpeas last year and formed good nodules. Lightly moisten the seed with water, coat it in this soil, and sow it right away | The most reliable method | Requires a field where chickpeas have been grown before |
| Using the nodules themselves | Collect nodules from dug-up roots, crush them, dissolve them in water, then dip the seed in that water and take it back out | Gives the highest bacterial density | Must be used the same day the nodules are dug up |
| No inoculation in year one | In the first year, plant without inoculation out of necessity and supplement with a small amount of nitrogen | Yield is low, but the bacterium is left behind in that field | Starting the following year, that field's soil becomes an inoculant |
| Setting up a dedicated inoculant plot | Plant chickpeas intensively in a small plot to build up the bacterium | Becomes the inoculant for a wider field the following year | A sensible strategy when seed is scarce |
| Scattering soil over the field | Scatter the collected soil along the sowing rows and till it in together | Spreads the bacterium through the soil | A rule of thumb is about one shovelful per 10m² |
| Leaving root residue | At harvest, cut only the stems and leave the roots in the field | The bacterium remains in the soil until the next year | The least labor-intensive way to maintain it |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Setting up a dedicated inoculant plot is the recommended strategy. If you have just secured seed for the first time, plant it densely in a small plot in the first year to let the bacterium settle into the soil, then use that soil as an inoculant the following year. Since the chickpea is a crop whose first two or three years must go toward seed saving anyway, this effectively builds up an inoculant source over that same period. A field where the bacterium has once become established keeps its inoculating power for several years.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There is something you must observe when inoculating. Root-nodule bacteria are very sensitive to direct sunlight, dryness, and heat. A few minutes of UV exposure kills a large share of them, and drying out kills them even faster. Do the work of coating seed with soil in the shade, and sow the coated seed right away rather than spreading it out in the sun. Traditionally, a sticky liquid such as molasses, sugar water, or rice-rinse water was brushed thinly onto the seed so the soil would stick well. One more thing: seed disinfection and inoculation cannot be done together. If you disinfected with a hot-water treatment, let it cool completely before inoculating.

| Conditions That Cause Inoculation to Fail | Reason |
|---|---|
| Using soil from a field that grew a different bean | The bacterial species differs and will not infect chickpea |
| Soil pH 5.5 or below | The bacteria cannot survive |
| A field given plenty of nitrogen fertilizer | The plant feels no need to trade and scales back nodule formation on its own |
| A field where water pools | They are aerobic bacteria and die without oxygen |
| A field deficient in phosphorus | Nitrogen fixation needs a lot of energy, and phosphorus is used in that metabolism |
| A field deficient in sulfur | In the chickpea, sulfur deficiency shows up directly as poor nodule formation |
| Molybdenum, iron, or cobalt deficiency | These are components of nitrogenase |
| Drying inoculated seed in the sun | UV and dryness kill the bacteria |
