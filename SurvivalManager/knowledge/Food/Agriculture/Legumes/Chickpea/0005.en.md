## 8. Crop Management

### 8-1. Weed Management

| Timing | Task | Key Point |
|---|---|---|
| 2-3 weeks after emergence | First weeding | Pull weeds while they are small. Scrape shallowly |
| Around 45 days after emergence | Second weeding | Combine with hilling |
| Before flowering | Finish all weeding | Do not enter the field afterward |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The chickpea is especially weak against weed competition. Germination takes ten to fifteen days and early growth is slow, so weeds get established first in the meantime. On top of that, its height of around 40cm is small, so if weeds cover it, it loses out on light. It is no exaggeration to say that weeding in the first 6 weeks determines the chickpea's yield.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There is something to be careful about with weeding, though. The chickpea's fine lateral roots spread widely near the surface, so scraping deep cuts them. Hold the hoe flat and scrape only the top 2cm of soil to lift weeds out, and pull weeds near the plant itself by hand. Also, because of the acidic secretions on the leaves, long bare-handed contact leaves the skin stinging, so it is better to wear gloves.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The rule is not to enter the field after flowering begins. When people walk through, flowers drop, and walking through in the morning while dew has not yet dried carries Ascochyta blight spores on clothing and shoes and spreads them across the whole field. Entering right after rain in particular is the surest way to spread the blight.

### 8-2. Hilling and Lodging Management

| Task | Timing | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Hilling | Around 45 days after sowing, together with the second weeding | Covers the base with soil for support, and the deepened furrow drains better |
| String support | When lodging is a concern | Drive stakes around the field and run a string at knee height |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;At a height of 20-100cm, usually around 40cm, the chickpea does not need staking. Its stems are upright and branch heavily, so the plants lean on and support each other. This is a clear advantage the chickpea has over pea. It can be grown without bamboo stakes or string. However, in a year with excess fertilizer causing overly lush growth, or heavy wind and rain, it can lodge, and hilling plus a string around the field are enough to deal with it.

## 9. Pests and Diseases

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The chickpea's pest and disease pressure grade is C. Thanks to the acidic secretions on its leaves, damage from leaf-chewing insects is lower than for other legumes, but two fungal diseases decide this crop's fate.

### 9-1. Major Diseases

| Disease | Symptoms | Conditions for Outbreak | Response Without Chemicals |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ascochyta blight | Concentric brown spots form on leaves, stems, and pods, and the stem snaps at that point | During cool, prolonged rain. Spreads through seed and plant residue | Seed sorting and hot-water treatment, rotation of 3 years or more, ensuring airflow, burning diseased residue. Harvest early if it spreads |
| Fusarium wilt | Starting at flowering, lower leaves wilt first and the whole plant dries up and dies. Splitting the stem shows brown tissue inside | Soil-borne. Survives in the soil for many years | Rotation of 3 years or more is the only countermeasure. Select resistant lines |
| Root rot | The base of the stem and the roots turn brown and soft | Excess moisture | Drainage, raising the beds |
| Powdery mildew | White powder covers the leaf surface | Dry conditions with large day-night temperature swings | Ensure airflow; milk diluted 5-10× |
| Sclerotinia rot | The stem turns soft and grows white, cottony mycelium along with black kernels | Cool, damp conditions | Dig up diseased plants along with the soil and burn them |

> **Ascochyta blight and Fusarium wilt — these two decide success or failure in growing chickpeas. Ascochyta blight arrives through seed and residue and explodes with rain, while Fusarium wilt lingers in the soil and waits for years. Without chemicals, the response comes down to just three things: seed sorting, long rotation, and airflow.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;What makes Ascochyta blight so frightening is its speed. If rain continues for a few days, small spots on the leaves spread to the stems within days, the stems snap, and the whole plant collapses. In a bad year, you lose the entire field. Here is the order of response. First, since it starts with the seed, thoroughly sort out spotted seed and treat it with hot water. Second, plant sparsely so leaves dry quickly. Third, do not enter the field after rain. Fourth, do not return diseased residue to the field — burn it instead. Fifth, if it has already started to spread, harvest without waiting for full ripening.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Fusarium wilt behaves differently. It survives for years in the soil as dormant spores and wakes up once chickpeas are planted again. In a field that has once become contaminated, plants wilting and dying at every flowering season repeats itself. There is no chemical for it and no way to wash it out. Rotation of 3 years or more, ideally 4 to 5 years, is the only countermeasure, and a second countermeasure is to select and carry forward the individuals that survive to set seed in that field. Because resistance is heritable, repeating this over several years builds up a line that can hold out in that field.

### 9-2. Major Pests

| Pest | Damage | Control Without Chemicals |
|---|---|---|
| Pod borers (cotton bollworm and related moths) | Larvae bore into pods and eat the seed. The worst pest | Hand-pick eggs and larvae. Plant a trap crop around the field. Avoid the outbreak period with early sowing |
| Cutworms | Cut young stems off at the base at night | Dig the soil around a cut plant in the morning and you will find the larva. Catch and destroy it |
| Root-knot nematodes | Cause knot-like swellings on the roots and stunt growth | Rotation, intercropping with marigold |
| Aphids | Suck sap and transmit viruses | Soapy water (5g soap per 1L water). Damage is relatively light thanks to the leaves' acidic secretions |
| Birds | Eat the seed right after sowing and the seeds again at ripening | Cover with straw or netting until germination |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Pod borers are the chickpea's worst pest. The leaves' acidic secretions fend off leaf-chewing insects, but they do not stop larvae that bore into the pods. A pod can look fine from the outside, but open it up and the seed is missing or a larva is inside. There are three ways to deal with it without chemicals. First, from around when pods start setting, walk the field early each morning and hand-pick egg clusters and young larvae from the undersides of leaves. Second, plant a crop the moths prefer even more around the field to draw them in, then concentrate on catching them there. Third, use early sowing to put the pod stage ahead of the moths' peak emergence period.

### 9-3. Chemical-Free Control Materials

| Material | How to Make | Target | Caution |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bordeaux mixture | Dissolve 400g copper sulfate and 400g quicklime separately in 100L of water, then mix | Ascochyta blight, fungal diseases | Requires access to copper. Use immediately after mixing |
| Soapy water | 5g unscented soap per 1L water | Aphids, mites | Spraying in the hot midday sun scorches leaves |
| Diluted milk | Dilute milk 5-10× with water | Powdery mildew | Spray in the morning on a clear day |
| Dusting with dry ash | Dust fine ash on leaves in the morning while dew is present | Suppresses fungus, deters leaf-eating insects | Washes off in rain |
| Wood ash water | Soak 1 doe of ash in 10L of water overnight and use the water on top | Suppresses fungus | Alkaline, so use it diluted |
| Wood vinegar | Dilute the liquid produced when making charcoal 300-500× | Repellent, growth promotion | The concentrate is strongly acidic, so dilution is essential |
| Hot pepper and garlic water | Grind, steep in water, strain, and use | Repels moths | Effect does not last long, so spray often |

### 9-4. Cultural Control

| Method | Principle |
|---|---|
| Seed sorting | Ascochyta blight spreads through seed. Sorting out spots and discoloration alone greatly reduces outbreaks |
| Hot-water treatment | 50-55°C for 10-15 minutes kills pathogens on the seed surface |
| Rotation of 3 years or more | Waits for wilt spores in the soil to decline |
| Sparse planting | With airflow, leaves dry quickly and Ascochyta blight cannot take hold |
| Drainage | A large share of chickpea diseases are linked to excess moisture |
| Early sowing | Puts ripening ahead of the monsoon and avoids the pod borer's outbreak period |
| No field entry after rain | If people brush against wet leaves, Ascochyta blight spreads across the whole field |
| Residue handling | Always burn diseased residue. Return only healthy residue to the field |
| Saving seed from survivors | In a field affected by wilt, select and carry forward the plants that survive to the end |

## 10. Nutrient Deficiency and Excess

| Symptom | Cause | Natural-Material Response |
|---|---|---|
| The whole plant is pale yellow and small | Nitrogen deficiency. In the chickpea, failed inoculation is the most common cause | First dig up a root and check for nodules. If there are none, give a very small amount of urine diluted 10× this year, and take care of inoculation next year |
| Almost no root nodules form | Absence of the dedicated bacterium, or sulfur/molybdenum deficiency | Securing an inoculant comes first. Supplement sulfur with livestock manure compost and Brassica residues |
| Leaf edges dry brown | Potassium deficiency | Scatter wood ash between plants and lightly work it in |
| Growth is slow and leaves turn purplish | Phosphorus deficiency. The chickpea has a high phosphorus demand | Bone meal, powder from baked fish bones |
| Flowers open but pods do not set | Boron deficiency, or nighttime temperature of 10°C or below during flowering | Compost mixed from several materials. If low temperature is the cause, adjust next year's sowing date |
| Yellowing between leaf veins | Iron or manganese deficiency. Common in soil that has drifted alkaline | Stop using ash. Increase organic matter with leaf mold and well-rotted compost |
| Lush stems and leaves but few pods | Nitrogen excess | Stop nitrogen supply immediately |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;When you see nitrogen-deficiency symptoms in a chickpea, the first thing to do is not to apply fertilizer, but to dig up a root and look. Fields in South Korea have almost no chickpea-specific bacterium, so it is common for fields planted without inoculation to form no nodules at all. In that case, giving nitrogen improves that year's yield a little, but it does not fix the root problem, and it can even further reduce the function of the few nodules that do exist. The right order is to get by on a minimal amount this year and fix the root problem with inoculation the following year.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Sulfur deficiency is something that needs separate attention specifically for the chickpea. It is easy to confuse with nitrogen deficiency since the symptoms look similar, but nitrogen deficiency yellows the lower leaves first, while sulfur deficiency pales the newer, upper leaves first. When sulfur is short, nodules may form but fail to function properly. Compost made with livestock manure, well-broken-down fish guts, and compost containing garlic, onion, and napa cabbage residue are practical sources.

## 11. Intercropping and Crop Rotation

### 11-1. Companion Compatibility

| Compatibility | Crop | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Good | Wheat, barley | Different root depths draw water and nutrients from different layers |
| Good | Maize, sorghum | Uses the nitrogen the chickpea leaves behind afterward |
| Good | Marigold | Root exudates suppress nematodes |
| Good | Mustard, rapeseed | Residue leaves sulfur compounds in the soil that reduce pathogens |
| Neutral | Leafy vegetables | They grow together early on, but shade appears once the chickpea gets bigger |
| Bad | Other legumes | Pathogens and nematodes build up |
| Bad | Crops that need frequent watering | The chickpea is the most vulnerable of all to excess moisture |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;A factor that needs special consideration in placing the chickpea is water. Plant it on the same bed as, or in a furrow next to, a crop that is watered often, and that water reaches the chickpea's roots. Because the chickpea is the most vulnerable of all legumes to excess moisture, it should be placed where the field drains best and irrigation water does not reach. On a sloped field, the upper part is where the chickpea belongs.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Using Brassica crops such as mustard and rapeseed as a preceding crop suits the chickpea especially well. As their residue decomposes in the soil, it releases sulfur compounds, which both lower the density of the wilt pathogen and supply the sulfur the chickpea needs. One arrangement handles two problems at once.

### 11-2. Crop Rotation

| Item | Standard |
|---|---|
| Break period | 3 years or more. Wilt disease persists in the soil |
| Ideal cycle | 4-5 years |
| Continuous-cropping damage | Wilt disease and root-knot nematodes build up |

| Example 4-Year Rotation |
|---|
| Year 1: Chickpea |
| Year 2: Grain (wheat, barley, maize) |
| Year 3: Mustard or rapeseed, followed by leafy vegetables |
| Year 4: Tuber crops (tubers/storage roots) or root vegetables |
| Back to chickpea |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;One more constraint applies here that is unique to the chickpea: the need to maintain an inoculant source. Go 3 years or more without planting chickpeas, and the density of the dedicated bacterium drops right along with it. While rotation reduces pathogens, it reduces the beneficial bacterium too. The solution is to set aside a small, dedicated inoculant plot. In a small section of the field, keep replanting a small amount of chickpea every year to maintain the bacterium, and use that soil as the inoculant for the main field every year. Fusarium wilt will build up in that plot, but since the goal there is not to obtain seed but to maintain the bacterium, that is a cost you can live with.

## 12. Harvest and Threshing

### 12-1. Judging Harvest Time

| Criterion | Details |
|---|---|
| Leaves | Leaves have yellowed and mostly fallen |
| Pods | 80% or more of them have turned brown |
| Sound | Shake the plant, and you can hear the seeds rattling inside the pods |
| Seed | When bitten, your teeth do not sink in — it cracks instead |
| Moisture | 18% at harvest, 14% or lower for storage |
| Timing | 100-120 days after sowing |

> **If rain is in the forecast, harvest without waiting for full ripeness. Rain during the chickpea's ripening stage brings not only shattering but Ascochyta blight as well. A slightly underripe bean can still be dried and used, but a diseased field yields nothing at all.**

### 12-2. Harvest Technique

| Step | Details |
|---|---|
| 1 | Do it on a clear, dry day, after 10am once the dew has dried |
| 2 | Wear gloves. The acidic secretions on leaves and pods irritate the skin |
| 3 | Cut the stems close to the ground. Leave the roots in the field |
| 4 | Bundle into sheaves and dry in the sun for 4-6 days, or hang under the eaves |
| 5 | Thresh once the pods are bone-dry and rattle when shaken |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Gloves are not a mere formality in chickpea harvest. The dense glands on the leaves and pods release malic acid and oxalic acid, making the plant surface acidic, so working bare-handed for half a day leaves your fingertips stinging and cracked. The secretions are especially irritating when dew is still on the plants or right after rain, since they dissolve into the water. Even just wrapping your hands in cloth makes a big difference.

### 12-3. Threshing - Methods Without Tools

| Method | Tool | Procedure | Efficiency |
|---|---|---|---|
| Treading in a sack | A thick burlap sack or bag | Put the dried stems in the sack, tie the opening, and tread on it with your feet | Most convenient for small amounts. Seeds do not scatter |
| Flail | A tool with a beating stick hung from a long handle | Spread a mat, lay the stems out thin, and strike down from above | Suited to medium quantities. Striking too hard cracks the seeds |
| Beating with a stick | A wooden stick | Put in a sack or spread on a mat and beat | A substitute when no flail is available |
| Rubbing with feet | None | Spread on a mat and grind with a shod foot in a twisting motion | Suited to small amounts |
| Striking against a board | A board or a bin | Hold a bundle of stems and strike it against the inside wall of the bin | Seeds collect inside the bin, so loss is low |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Something to be especially careful about in threshing chickpeas is that the seeds split easily. Even though the seed coat is thick, the seed itself is structured to split cleanly into two halves, so beating it hard produces a lot of split seed. That is not a problem for eating, but it cannot be used as seed, and insects get into it first during storage. Treading in a sack causes the least loss, and when using a flail, it is better to strike lightly and repeatedly rather than hard.

### 12-4. Cleaning - Sorting by Wind

| Method | Principle | Procedure |
|---|---|---|
| Winnowing | Seeds are heavy, husks and debris are light | On a windy day, hold a winnowing basket or wide bowl of it up high and pour it out slowly |
| Basket tossing | Shake up and down while flicking forward | Heavy seeds stay at the back while light material is pushed forward |
| Fanning | One person pours while another fans a breeze | Used on a windless day |
| Sieving | Separates by hole size | Especially useful for filtering out split half-seeds |
| Floating in water | Empty husks and insect-damaged seeds are light | Pour into a tub of water and skim off what floats. Dry whatever you fish out again the same day |
| Rolling down a slanted board | Round seeds roll well while debris catches | Set a board at an angle and roll the seeds down it |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There are three tricks to winnowing. Choose early morning or dusk when the wind blows steadily, pour in a thin stream rather than all at once, and repeat it two or three times. For the chickpea, it is worth adding one more step of sieving. Filtering out the split half-seeds from threshing with a fine sieve and setting them aside lets you eat those first, since they are unfit for storage, and keep only whole seeds for storage.
