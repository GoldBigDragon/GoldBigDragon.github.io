## 5. Field Preparation and Fertilizer - Nitrogen Balance and Fertilizer Application

### 5-4. The Trap in Nitrogen Fixation

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The idea that planting beans improves the soil has hardened into farming common sense. It is half right and half wrong. Without understanding this point precisely, a field that has grown legumes for several years running can end up more depleted, not less.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Let's work through the numbers. When the chickpea grows well, it yields 180kg of dried seed per 1,000m². Chickpea seed is 17.8% protein, and dividing protein by 6.25 gives the nitrogen content. 17.8 ÷ 6.25 is 2.85, meaning about 2.9% of seed weight is pure nitrogen. The nitrogen contained in 180kg is about 5.1kg, or 51kg converted to a hectare basis.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Meanwhile, the nitrogen chickpea root nodules fix, when inoculation goes well, is 60-100kg per hectare. If inoculation fails, it drops below 10kg. Laying what leaves against what comes in looks like this.

| Item | Nitrogen per Hectare | Direction |
|---|---|---|
| Nodule fixation (inoculation successful) | 60-100kg | In |
| Nodule fixation (inoculation failed) | 5-15kg | In |
| Removed in seed | 45-60kg | Out |
| Nitrogen remaining in stems and leaves | 20-35kg | Stays if returned to the field; leaves if burned or hauled away |
| Nitrogen remaining in roots and nodules | 10-15kg | Usually stays in the field |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Two things stand out in this table. First, when inoculation succeeds, nitrogen still remains even after the seed is hauled away, because the chickpea seed's protein share is much lower than soybean's, so the amount removed is small to begin with. Second, when inoculation fails, this relationship reverses completely. If fixation is 10kg but 50kg leaves in the seed, the field clearly comes out at a loss. The importance of inoculation for the chickpea is not only a matter of yield, but also a matter of the field's long-term sustainability.

> **Legumes enrich the soil not because they fix nitrogen, but because part of that fixed nitrogen stays in the field, held in the stems and leaves. Haul those stems and leaves away, and the bean becomes a crop that depletes the land instead.**

| Principle | Details |
|---|---|
| Stems and leaves must go back to the field | Return all the stems, leaves, and pod husks left after threshing to the field |
| Do not burn them | Burning releases the nitrogen as gas, leaving only potassium and phosphorus behind in the ash |
| Do not dig up the roots | They run deep and are hard to pull anyway. Cutting only the stems and leaving the roots is correct |
| Cut into 20-30cm pieces and use as cover | Serves as both mulch and fertilizer |
| Do not skip inoculation | A failed inoculation means both lost yield and nitrogen depletion |
| If fed to livestock, return the manure | If you feed the stems and leaves to livestock, be sure to put that animal's manure back on this same field |

### 5-5. Field Preparation

| Item | Method | Under Disaster Conditions |
|---|---|---|
| Tillage | Till to a depth of 20-30cm and level evenly | Since roots go in deep, deeper tillage is better |
| Raised beds | Height 20cm or more | The key to preventing waterlogging. A precondition for the chickpea |
| Drainage channels | Depth 30-50cm | Dig deep with the monsoon in mind |
| pH correction | pH 6.0-8.0 | Not urgent, given the wide tolerance range |
| Organic matter | Well-rotted compost 1,000-1,500kg/1,000m² | Apply as base fertilizer |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The chickpea's pH tolerance range of 5.5-8.6 is wide, so liming has a lower priority than for other legumes. As long as the field is not severely acidic, it grows fine without lime. Below 5.5, though, root-nodule bacteria cannot survive, so it is better to raise the pH at least a little with powder made from baked, ground shells or eggshells.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Instead, effort should go into drainage. Till deep to open a path for water to drain downward, raise the beds, and dig deep drainage channels. The surest approach is to choose a spot where water does not collect in the first place, and on a sloped field, the upper part is where the chickpea belongs.

### 5-6. Fertilizer Rates

| Fertilizer | Per 1,000m² | Per m² | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Nitrogen | 0-2kg | 0-2g | Almost unnecessary. Too much suppresses nitrogen fixation |
| Phosphorus | 20-40kg | 20-40g | Apply the full amount as base fertilizer. Essential for root development and flowering |
| Potassium | 3-5kg | 3-5g | Apply the full amount as base fertilizer |
| Sulfur | 10-20kg | 10-20g | A shortage worsens nodule formation |
| Compost | 1,000-1,500kg | 1-1.5kg | Use fully rotted compost |

> **What is unusual about fertilizing chickpeas is sulfur. A sulfur shortage keeps root nodules from forming properly, and that in turn undermines nitrogen fixation itself. It is an element rarely mentioned for other legumes.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Sulfur is needed because both the nitrogenase enzyme and leghemoglobin use sulfur-containing amino acids. There are a few ways to supply sulfur without chemical fertilizer. Compost made with livestock manure contains sulfur, and adding residues from Brassica crops to compost brings in sulfur compounds. A practical method is to separately collect things like garlic and onion skins, the outer leaves of napa cabbage and radish, and leaf-mustard stems, and mix them into compost. These should not be put directly onto the chickpea field, though — they need to be broken down into compost first.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Phosphorus at 20 to 40kg is a fairly large amount. This is because both the chickpea's deep root development and the energy metabolism of nitrogen fixation demand phosphorus. Without chemical fertilizer, bone meal and fish bones are practically the only way to obtain it.

### 5-7. Emergency Substitute Fertilizers

| Substitute Resource | Nutrients Supplied | How to Use | Caution |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bone meal | Phosphorus, calcium | Bake, grind, and mix into compost | Key to meeting the chickpea's high phosphorus demand |
| Fish bones and guts | Phosphorus, nitrogen, sulfur | Bury and let break down before use | Supplies phosphorus and sulfur together |
| Wood ash | Potassium, phosphorus, calcium | Spread 100-200g per m² and mix in | Alkaline, but the chickpea tolerates pH up to 8.6 |
| Well-rotted compost | All-around | Apply 1-1.5kg per m² as base fertilizer | Under-rotted compost burns roots and spreads disease |
| Compost with Brassica residues | Sulfur | Mix garlic and onion skins and napa cabbage outer leaves into compost | Must be fully broken down before applying |
| Livestock manure compost | All-around, sulfur | Fully rot it, then use as base fertilizer | Valuable as a sulfur source |
| Diluted urine | Nitrogen, potassium, sulfur | Dilute 10× with water and pour on the soil between plants | Only a very small amount for chickpeas — it suppresses nitrogen fixation |
| Shells and eggshells | Calcium | Bake, grind, and spread | Only for strongly acidic soil |
| Leaf mold | All-around, organic matter | Mix into the soil | Improves water retention |

## 6. Sowing

### 6-1. Sowing Time

| Cropping Pattern | Sowing Time | Harvest Time | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Spring cultivation (South Korea) | Late March-mid April | Mid July-August | After frost has completely ended |
| Fall sowing | Better not attempted | - | Cannot survive South Korea's winter cold |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The chickpea's sowing window is narrow. Sow too early, and flowering catches the cool nights of May, so pollen fails to form; sow too late, and ripening catches the monsoon, and Ascochyta blight brings the crop down. Keeping to this window of late March to mid-April is the single most important decision.

> **Sowing time governs the chickpea's yield. Miss the window from late March to mid-April, and that year's ceiling is already set.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In the region of origin, sowing in fall and overwintering is the common practice, but in South Korea, winter cold exceeds the chickpea's cold-hardiness limit and the crop fails. Tolerating a light frost and surviving winter are two different things. The chickpea's cold-hardiness grade of B means it can get through a late spring frost, not that it can overwinter.

### 6-2. Seed Preparation

| Item | Method | Under Disaster Conditions |
|---|---|---|
| Sorting | Discard seed with spots, discoloration, wrinkling, or insect holes | Ascochyta blight spreads through seed. Sorting is half the battle in control |
| Sizing | Choose the plumpest, heaviest seeds | Float them in water and skim off any that float |
| Hot-water treatment | Soak in 50-55°C water for 10-15 minutes | Kills seed-borne pathogens |
| Ash treatment | Coat with fine wood ash | Aids disinfection and drying, and helps repel birds and insects |
| Nodule inoculation | Coat with soil from a field that grew chickpeas | See Section 5-3. Not done together with disinfection |
| Soaking | Better not to do it | Soaking softens the seed coat, making it prone to rotting in the soil |
| Germination test | Wrap 20 seeds in a damp cloth, leave for 2 weeks, and count how many sprout | Germination is slow, so wait the full 2 weeks |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Seed sorting matters especially for the chickpea. Ascochyta blight clings to the seed surface and the inside of the seed coat and carries over to the next year. Diseased seed shows brown or black blotches on the surface, or is shriveled. A single seed like this can spread the disease through an entire field the following year. Without pesticides, seed sorting is the strongest tool for control, and all it takes is good light, your eyes, and time. Combining it with hot-water treatment can kill off a large share of the surface pathogens as well.

### 6-3. Sowing Method

| Item | Standard | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Sowing depth | 3-5cm | Too deep slows germination further |
| Seeds per hole | 1-2 | An insurance margin, since germination is slow |
| Row spacing | 25-60cm | Space generously, since it branches heavily |
| Plant spacing | 20-25cm | |
| Seeding rate (Desi, small-seeded) | 2.5-4kg/1,000m² | Small seeds mean less is needed |
| Seeding rate (Kabuli, large-seeded) | 8-12kg/1,000m² | Large seeds mean more is needed |
| Germination period | 10-14 days | Patience is needed |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Planting density is directly tied to airflow. Ascochyta blight, the chickpea's worst disease, worsens the longer leaves stay wet, so planting too close together sharply raises the risk. Even with plenty of seed, it is better not to plant densely. Spacing rows generously to let air move through helps, and so does aligning the rows with the field's long side facing the prevailing wind direction.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;It bears repeating that nothing comes up for ten to fifteen days right after sowing. If the soil hardens into a crust during this period, the young shoot cannot push through it. If the soil has crusted after rain, gently rake the surface along the row to break it up. Conversely, watering too often and keeping the soil constantly wet will rot the seed.

## 7. Water Management

| Stage | Water Management | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Sowing to germination | Keep the soil from drying out completely | Germination takes 10-14 days |
| Early growth | Do not water | Dry conditions push the roots down to 1m or deeper |
| Flowering to pod-filling | Irrigate if drought is severe | Water shortage during this period cuts yield the most |
| Ripening stage | Stop irrigation | Excess moisture causes shattering and quality loss |
| Monsoon season | Clear the drainage channels | The most dangerous period for the chickpea |

> **Water management for the chickpea is not the art of giving water but the art of holding back. Water often in the early stage, and the roots stay shallow — and shallow roots cannot survive the drought that comes later.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This is the point that sets the chickpea fundamentally apart from other crops. It evolved to send roots deep in dry land. If the surface stays constantly wet early on, the plant feels no reason to go deep and spreads its roots shallowly instead. Then, when summer drought arrives, those shallow roots cannot find water and the plant collapses. Letting the plant tolerate early dryness is, in the end, the way to beat drought later.

| Symptom | Cause | Action |
|---|---|---|
| Leaves fold at midday and open again in the evening | A normal water-conserving response in the chickpea | Leave as is |
| Leaves are still folded in the morning | Severe dryness | Irrigate |
| Leaves yellow and dry from the bottom up | Severe drought or poor nodulation | Dig up a root, check the nodules, then decide |
| Flowers drop in large numbers | Dryness during flowering, or nighttime temperature of 10°C or below | Irrigate. If low temperature is the cause, adjust next year's sowing date |
| Lower leaves yellow and the base of the stem turns brown and soft | Root rot from excess moisture | Clear the drainage channels. Pull out the plant if severe |
| Brown spots appear on leaves and the stem snaps | Ascochyta blight. Appears after a stretch of rain | Pull up and burn diseased plants. If it spreads, consider an early harvest |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Chickpea leaves folding at midday is not disease but normal behavior. When the small leaflets fold upward and face each other, the area exposed to sunlight shrinks and transpiration is held down. They open again by evening. This movement is one reason the chickpea can finish a growing season on just 250mm of water, and if the leaves are still folded in the morning, that is when water is genuinely short.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Mulching needs to be used carefully with chickpeas. It does reduce surface evaporation and save water, but a thick layer that keeps the soil constantly damp increases Ascochyta blight and root rot. Keep any mulch thin, and once the monsoon starts, it is better to pull some of it back to allow airflow.
