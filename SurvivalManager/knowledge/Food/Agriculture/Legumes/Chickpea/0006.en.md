## 13. Seed Saving

| Item | Method |
|---|---|
| Selecting plants for seed | Mark plants in the field in advance that show no disease symptoms, carry many pods, and — in a field affected by wilt — survived to the end |
| Harvest time | Harvest after the pods have turned fully brown, a bit later than for eating |
| Separation | Thresh separately. Shelling by hand keeps the seed from splitting |
| Sorting | Discard all spotted, split, wrinkled, or discolored seed. Keep only plump, whole seed |
| Drying | Moisture 12-13%. Dry slowly in the shade |
| Storage | Keep in a sealed container in a cool, dark place, separate from the container used for eating |
| Storage life | 3 years sealed at room temperature |
| Renewal | If possible, mix in some seed from elsewhere every 3-4 years |

> **The chickpea is a crop for which seed is hard to obtain again in South Korea. Seed saving is not a choice but a precondition for continuing to grow this crop. Secure seed first, even if it means eating less.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The most important trait in seed saving is resistance to Fusarium wilt. In a field where the disease is active, if every surrounding plant wilts and dies but one survives on its own and sets seed, that is the most valuable seed there is. Because resistance is heritable, selecting and carrying forward such individuals builds, within a few years, a line that holds out in that field. The fact that yields varied more than twentyfold between lines in South Korea's adaptability trials means, looked at the other way, that there is just as much room for selection.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The next trait is tolerance to Ascochyta blight and an early-ripening habit. A plant that finishes ripening before the monsoon avoids the disease. The habit of tying a string on plants whose pods turn brown ahead of the others and harvesting them separately makes a big difference after a few years.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Because the chickpea is a self-pollinating crop, there is no need to worry about isolation distance. Traits do not mix even when several lines are planted side by side. This is an advantageous condition for testing and selecting among several lines at once. Just be sure to harvest and store each line separately so the seed does not get mixed, and label each container with which field and which year it came from.

| Seed Storage Time and Germination Rate |
|---|
| Harvest year: 85% or higher |
| After 1 year: 75-85% |
| After 2 years: 60-75% |
| After 3 years: 40-60% |
| After 4 years: Drops sharply |

## 14. Toxicity and Antinutrient Compounds

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The chickpea has a relatively low toxicity risk among legumes. It has neither the strong lectins that can cause poisoning from just a few raw kidney beans, nor a compound like fava bean's that causes acute hemolysis in certain individuals. Still, dried chickpeas, like other beans, contain antinutrient compounds, and eating them raw is indigestible.

| Compound | What It Does | Symptoms | How to Remove It |
|---|---|---|---|
| Trypsin inhibitors | Block the action of protein-digesting enzymes | Abdominal pain, diarrhea, reduced protein utilization | Soak 8 hours, then boil for 1 hour or more |
| Lectins | Bind to the intestinal lining and interfere with absorption | Digestive discomfort | Destroyed by heat. Much weaker than in kidney beans |
| Phytic acid | Binds iron, zinc, and calcium and blocks their absorption | Anemia, zinc deficiency with long-term intake | Reduced by up to 60-80% through soaking, sprouting, and fermentation |
| Oligosaccharides (raffinose, stachyose) | Indigestible to humans, so gut bacteria ferment them | Severe bloating and gas | Always discard the soaking water afterward |
| Saponins | Produce foam | Harmless in small amounts | Skim off the foam while boiling |
| Organic acids in leaves and pods | Malic and oxalic acid | Skin irritation. Eating a lot can irritate the stomach | Do not eat the leaves. Wear gloves while working |
| Mold toxins | Produced by mold during storage | Dangerous | Moldy seed does not become safe by washing or cooking. Discard it |

> **What actually harms people with chickpeas is not the bean itself, but beans that grew moldy from poor storage, and beans eaten undercooked. If you smell mustiness or see a bluish powder on the surface, do not hesitate — throw it out.**

| Treatment | Time/Temperature | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Soaking | 8 hours or more in room-temperature water. In summer, 6 hours with a water change | The thick seed coat needs a thorough soak |
| Discarding the soaking water | Always | Discards the oligosaccharides and antinutrients that leached into it |
| Boiling | 1 to 1.5 hours in boiling water | A relatively long cooking time among legumes |
| Peeling and splitting | Soak, peel off the skin, and split in half | Cuts cooking time to 20-30 minutes |
| Baking soda instead of salt | A very small amount in the boiling water | Softens the skin faster and saves fuel. If unavailable, the clear water from steeped wood ash can substitute |
| Checking doneness | Presses easily flat between your fingers | If a hard core remains, it is undercooked |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The chickpea's oligosaccharide content is on the high side even among legumes, so eating a large amount suddenly causes severe bloating and gas. Extra care is needed for people who have gone hungry for a long time, or for children. Start with a small amount and increase it over several days, and always discard the soaking water. Discarding the boiling water too and reboiling in fresh water reduces it further, but nutrients leach out along with it, so judge according to your circumstances.

## 15. Storage Without Refrigeration

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The chickpea's storability grade is SS. In the FAO Food Loss Index, the median loss rate at the storage stage is a very low 0.49%, and dried seed holds up for 3 years at room temperature, thanks to a seed coat thick and hard enough that insects have trouble boring in. But if conditions go wrong, weevils still get in.

### 15-1. Drying Comes First

| Stage | Method | Check |
|---|---|---|
| Preliminary drying | Dry whole plants, stems and all, in the sun for 4-6 days, or hang under the eaves | The pods become bone-dry |
| Post-threshing drying | Spread thin on a mat in the sun for 2-3 days | Turn often for even drying |
| Target moisture | 14% or below for eating, 12% for seed | When bitten, teeth do not sink in — it cracks instead |
| Salt test | Put dry salt in a jar with the beans and shake | If salt sticks to the jar walls, it is still damp |
| Cooling | Bring in from the sun and let cool completely before sealing | Sealing while warm causes condensation |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;If drying is insufficient, every measure that follows falls apart. Mold grows above 15% moisture, and weevil breeding speeds up above 13%. On the other hand, seed that gets too dry splits. Since the chickpea is naturally structured to split easily into two halves, spreading it out too long on a very sunny day increases the number of seeds that split on their own. It is better to avoid the strong afternoon sun, spreading it out in the morning and bringing it in by afternoon.

### 15-2. Controlling Bean Weevils During Storage

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Bean weevils are the greatest threat to stored legumes. The frightening part is that it already starts in the field. Adult weevils lay eggs on pods still on the plant, and those eggs hatch inside storage and bore into the seed. The eggs are already along for the ride when the seed comes in, and since one generation takes less than a month, in half a year a whole sack can end up filled with holed-out husks. The chickpea's thick seed coat gives it more resistance than other beans, but it is not immune.

| Method | Principle | Application | Effect |
|---|---|---|---|
| Full sealing | Cuts off oxygen to suffocate them | Fill a glass jar or metal tin completely and seal it | The most reliable method. In 3-4 weeks, even eggs and larvae die |
| Mixing in wood ash | Fine particles scratch the waxy exoskeleton and dehydrate the insects | Mix in 2-5% of the bean volume | Rinse with water before eating |
| Diatomaceous earth | Silica shells damage the exoskeleton | 1-3g per 1kg of beans | More effective than ash. Only works when dry |
| Oil coating | An oil film prevents eggs from sticking and hatching | Add 2-5mL cooking oil per 1kg of beans and rub in | Highly effective for legumes. Do not use on seed stock |
| Cold treatment | Eggs and larvae die below freezing | Leave outside for a few days in the dead of winter, then bring back in | Watch for condensation when bringing it back in |
| Heat treatment | Eggs and larvae die at 60 degrees | Spread thin in the sun for half a day | Do not use on seed stock |
| Dried chili, sansho pepper, mugwort | Essential-oil compounds suppress egg-laying | Layer them in | A supplementary measure |
| Charcoal or roasted salt | Absorbs moisture | Wrap in cloth and place inside | Also helps prevent mold |
| Burying in ash | Combines sealing and physical control | Layer ash and beans in a jar | Traditionally widely used |
| Eating split seed first | Insects get into split seed first | Sieve it out, store separately, and use it first | Especially useful for the chickpea |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The single most important thing is complete sealing. Insects breathe too. Inside a sealed container, as the beans and insects use up oxygen, oxygen falls and carbon dioxide rises, killing the insects. The beans, being dormant, tolerate this condition. The fuller the container and the less empty space, the faster it works. Packing several small containers tightly is better than one large one, and this also has the advantage that if one container spoils, you do not lose everything.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Oil coating is a method long used by smallholder farms in India and Africa. Add 2-3ml cooking oil per 1kg of beans and rub by hand so a thin film forms over every seed. The oil film makes it hard for adult weevils to attach eggs, and it blocks the breathing pores of eggs already attached, preventing them from hatching. Use it only on the portion you will eat within half a year, not on seed stock. Sieve wood ash to select only the fine part and mix in 2-5% of the bean volume. Insects cannot build resistance to it, and it is harmless to people.

### 15-3. Storage Containers and Locations

| Container | Sealing | Duration | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|:---:|---|---|---|
| Glass jar | Very good | 3 years or more | You can check condition visually | Small capacity and does not block light |
| Metal tin | Very good | 3 years or more | Completely blocks rodents and insects | Condensation spots spoil |
| Earthenware jar | Good | 2-3 years | Slow temperature change; rodents cannot gnaw through | Heavy and breakable |
| Heavy-duty plastic | Fair | 1-2 years | Light and inexpensive | Rodents can gnaw through |
| Underground storage pit | Good | 2-3 years | Steady, low temperature | Losing it all if water seeps in |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;For checking, the most reliable senses are hearing and smell. If you put your ear to the container and hear a rustling sound, insects are active; a musty smell means mold has started. Small round holes on the surface mean weevils have already gone through a generation. Even then, you can save what remains by taking everything out, drying it in the sun, floating it in water to skim off empty seeds, mixing in ash or oil, and sealing it again. Checking once every two months is the standard.

## 16. Cooking and Processing

### 16-1. Peeled and Split Form - Cutting Fuel in Half

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The most valuable technique in cooking chickpeas is peeling off the skin and splitting the seed in half ahead of time. This is the form known in India as chana dal, and it cuts cooking time from an hour down to 20 to 30 minutes. Where fuel is scarce, this difference adds up every single day.

| Step | Details |
|---|---|
| 1 | Briefly dip dried chickpeas in water to lightly dampen the surface |
| 2 | Leave in the shade for half a day so only the skin absorbs moisture |
| 3 | Put in a mortar and pound lightly — just enough to remove the skin, not to crush the seed |
| 4 | Winnow with a basket or fan to blow away the loosened skins |
| 5 | Pound the remaining seed lightly again and it splits into two halves |
| 6 | Dry completely and store sealed |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Prepared this way, it cooks down soft like porridge in 20 to 30 minutes with no soaking needed. However, peeled beans lose storability. With the seed coat, their protective barrier, gone, insects get in easily and oxidation speeds up too. So the rule is not to process everything at once, but only as much as you will eat within a month or two. Do not throw away the peeled skins — collect them for livestock feed or compost.

### 16-2. Fuel-Saving Cooking

| Method | Fuel | Procedure |
|---|:---:|---|
| Split chickpea porridge | Very little | Cooks soft in 20-30 minutes with no soaking |
| Pressure cooking | The least | Pressure-cook soaked beans for 15-20 minutes |
| Residual-heat cooking | Very little | Boil for 20 minutes, turn off the heat, wrap in a straw- or husk-filled box or blankets, and leave for 3-4 hours |
| Roasted chickpeas | Little | Roast soaked-and-dried beans without oil. Can be eaten as is |
| Chickpea flour | Almost none | Roast, grind, and store; mix with water into a dough or a drink |
| Sprouting | None | Once sprouted, it can be eaten without any heat at all |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Chickpea flour is especially valuable in disaster conditions. Roasted and ground flour becomes bread when kneaded with water, rolled thin, and baked; becomes porridge when mixed with water and boiled; and becomes a coating for frying when vegetables are dipped in it. India's besan and the Middle East's falafel batter are both this same flour. Where wheat flour is unavailable, mixing chickpea flour into grain flour completes the amino acid profile and also helps the dough's texture hold together. However, because it is 6% fat, the flour develops a rancid smell within a few months, so make only as much as you will use.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Residual-heat cooking is also highly effective for chickpeas. Boil for just 20 minutes, put it in an insulated box, and the internal temperature stays high for over three hours while cooking continues. A wooden box filled with straw or husks, or thick blankets wrapped entirely around the pot, serves as that insulated box. For a crop like the chickpea with a long cooking time, this method saves a substantial amount of fuel.

### 16-3. Sprouting and Fermentation

| Method | Procedure | Value |
|---|---|---|
| Sprouting | Soak 8 hours, drain, cover with a damp cloth for 2-3 days. Harvest once the sprout is about 1cm | New vitamin C forms, phytic acid decreases, and digestion becomes easier |
| Blanched namul | Lightly blanch the sprouted seed and season it | A way to get greens when winter vegetables are unavailable |
| Fermented batter | Grind and leave overnight to ferment sourly | It rises, softens, and becomes easier to digest |
| Chickpea doenjang | Boil, mash, ferment, then soak in brine | An alternative when soybean is scarce |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The chickpea is an easy bean to sprout. Soak for 8 hours, drain, cover with a damp cloth, and leave for two or three days, and a sprout about 1cm long appears. In this process, vitamin C forms that was not present in the dried bean, phytic acid and trypsin inhibitors decrease, and the oligosaccharides break down so there is less gas. When there are no fresh vegetables in winter, this sprouting becomes a cheap way to prevent vitamin C deficiency. Even sprouted chickpeas, though, should not be eaten raw — blanch lightly or roast them first, because of the inhibitors that remain.

## 17. Annual Work Calendar

| Month | Task | Key Point |
|---|---|---|
| January | Storage check, seed germination test, sorting seed by line | Since germination is slow, wait 2 weeks for the test too |
| February | Field planning, turning compost, planning to secure inoculant soil | Assign the best-draining spot to the chickpea |
| March | Tilling, making raised beds, digging drainage channels, sowing in late month | Drainage channels are half of growing chickpeas |
| April | Finishing sowing (by mid-month), keeping birds off | Nothing comes up for ten to fifteen days |
| May | First weeding, thinning | Early weed control determines yield. Do not water |
| June | Second weeding, hilling, flowering, nodule check | Dig up a root, split a nodule, and check for red |
| July | Pod filling, catching pod borers, watching for Ascochyta blight | Stay out of the field during prolonged rain |
| August | Harvest, hang-drying, threshing | Do not wait for full ripeness if rain is forecast |
| September | Cleaning, drying, storage, returning stems and leaves to the field, sorting saved seed | Burn only diseased residue; cover the field with the rest |
| October | Storage check, storing inoculant soil | Scoop out and set aside soil where chickpea roots were |
| November | Processing (peeling and splitting), making flour | Process only as much as you will eat within a month or two |
| December | Storage check, growing sprouts, planning for next year | Get winter vitamin C from sprouting |

### 17-1. Five Things Not to Forget

| Item | Details |
|---|---|
| Securing seed | A crop hard to obtain again in South Korea. Seed saving is a precondition for growing it |
| Inoculation | Requires a dedicated bacterium. Soil from a field that grew other beans is useless. Set aside a dedicated inoculant plot |
| Drainage | The most vulnerable of all legumes to excess moisture. Do not plant in a field where water pools |
| Do not water in the early stage | Dry conditions push the roots down to 1m. Those roots are what beat the drought later |
| Rotation | 3 years or more. Wilt disease lingers in the soil, waiting |
