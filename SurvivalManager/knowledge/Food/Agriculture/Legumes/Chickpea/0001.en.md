## Introduction

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The chickpea (Cicer arietinum) comes from the dry lands of southwestern Asia. Its ancestor is the wild chickpea of southeastern Turkey, and together with wheat, barley, and lentils it was one of the founder crops of Neolithic agriculture. Today it holds a place close to a staple food in India, Pakistan, Turkey, and Ethiopia, and it is the world's second most widely grown legume crop. Hummus, falafel, and Indian chana and dal are all made from this bean.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The chickpea's clearest strength is its ability to withstand dry land. Its water-use grade is S: total evapotranspiration of 250mm is enough to finish a full growing season. That is less than half of soybean's 450 to 700mm or pea's 400mm. This is because its roots go straight down to 1m or deeper, drawing on water in the deep soil. Even in a year without rain, in a field where other beans wither and die, the chickpea still produces something. This ability shines once irrigation systems have broken down.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;However, the chickpea's overall grade is C, 41.2 points. Its short-term score is D (39.7), and its long-term score is C (42.8). It sits on the low side among legumes, for two reasons. First, its calories per area is D. At 180kg per 1,000m², that is 679kcal per 1m², only 0.26× the self-sufficiency line. That is a little over half of soybean's level. Second, and more importantly, its prevalence in South Korea is C. It is grown only experimentally, in one or two provinces, and seed is practically impossible to obtain elsewhere.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This second problem should not be waved away. When distribution breaks down, seed you do not already have is as good as nonexistent. To fit the chickpea into a disaster-preparedness plan, you need to secure seed now and build up your stock over several years through seed saving. Fortunately, because it is self-pollinating, the line stays pure, and sealed at room temperature the seed keeps for 3 years. Once you have secured seed, this is a crop you can carry forward on your own. If you have not secured seed, it is more realistic to build your plan around soybean, adzuki bean, or cowpea instead.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There is also a misunderstanding common to legume crops as a whole: the idea that planting beans by itself improves the soil. That is only half true. The chickpea, too, draws nitrogen from the air through symbiosis with root-nodule bacteria (rhizobia), but once the seed is harvested and carried off the field, more nitrogen can leave the field than was fixed. Whether a chickpea field builds up the soil or depletes it depends on how the stems and leaves are handled afterward. This document works through that fork in the road with numbers in Section 5.
