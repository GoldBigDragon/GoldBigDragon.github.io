## Introduction
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Chickpea (Cicer arietinum) is an annual legume of the dry lands of southwest Asia, descended from the wild chickpea of southeastern Turkey, and the second most widely grown legume in the world. Its clearest strength is tolerance of dry ground: water consumption is S, and total evapotranspiration of 250 mm completes a season (soybean 450-700 mm, pea 400 mm). The root goes straight down more than 1 m and draws on water deep in the soil. Its overall grade is C (41.2 points, 92nd of 183 valid species), with short term D (39.7), long term C (42.8), and supplementary food SS (62.2 points). Calories per area are D, at 180 kg per 1,000㎡ and 679 kcal per ㎡, only 0.26 of the self-sufficiency line, and domestic cultivation spread is C, so it is grown only on a trial basis in one or two provinces and seed is practically hard to get. When distribution stops, seed you do not hold might as well not exist, so secure seed now and multiply it by saving your own, and if you cannot secure it, planning around soybean, adzuki bean, or cowpea is more realistic. It is self-pollinating so the pure line holds, and it keeps 3 years sealed at room temperature, so once you have seed you can carry it on yourself.

> **Chickpea is not a crop that fills the stomach but one that yields protein and iron even on dry ground. Harvesting something in the year without rain, on the spot where other beans fail, is this crop's place.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Micronutrients S is the basis of the supplementary grade SS, and storability SS means it keeps long sealed at room temperature. Cooking and processing D is a cost paid every day because of the hard seed coat, and continuous-cropping tolerance B and pest and disease pressure C should be read together, keeping a rotation of 3 years or more. The grade for each item is in the Crop Evaluation Results document.

## 2. Plant Overview
| Item | Content |
|:---|:---|
| Classification | Annual of the genus Cicer in the legume family. Native from southeastern Turkey to Syria, and self-pollinating |
| Leaves, stem | Leaves are pinnately compound, thick with fine hairs and glands. The stem stands upright and branches heavily from the base, 20-100 cm tall (usually around 40 cm), so no stakes are needed |
| Roots, flowers | A straight taproot goes down more than 1 m, the deepest among legumes. Flowers are small white, pale pink, or purple butterfly-shaped blooms, one in each leaf axil |
| Pods, seeds | Short, plump, and fine-haired, with only 1-2 seeds, a structural limit on yield. The seed is wrinkled with a pointed beak on one side, resembling a bird's head |

| Stage | Period | Key points |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Germination, seedling stage | 4 weeks after sowing | Germination takes 10-14 days, and meanwhile the root goes deep first |
| Vegetative growth | Weeks 4-8 | The stem stands and branches heavily |
| Flowering, pod filling | Weeks 8-14 | Flowers open in turn from the lower nodes and pods plump |
| Ripening, maturity | Weeks 12-17 | Leaves turn yellow and pods dry to brown |

> **The acidic exudate on chickpea leaves is a natural defense that repels pests. It is why this crop suffers less than other beans from insects chewing the leaves, and also why handling it bare-handed for long stings the skin.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Per 100 g of dried seed: 377 kcal, protein 17.78 g (18.9% of calories), fat about 6 g, carbohydrate about 60 g, dietary fiber 10-26 g, sugars 2.55 g, calcium 127-184 mg, iron 4.6-8.3 mg, and phosphorus 286-528 mg, and it is rich in folate. High in both calcium and iron, it is an important source when meat is absent, but its many oligosaccharides cause belly gas if a lot is eaten suddenly.

## 3. Growing Environment and Varieties
| Condition | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Temperature | Germination 10℃ or above (optimum 20-25℃). Growth 12-35℃ (optimum 21-26℃, nights 15℃ or above), seed set 15-28℃. If nights fall to 10℃ or below at flowering, pollen does not form properly and pods do not set, and above 35℃ flowers drop. It tolerates light frost |
| Light | 6 hours or more a day (8 hours or more is optimum); under 40% shading yield halves |
| Water, soil | Well-drained sandy loam or loam (never clay or ground where water stands), pH 5.5-8.6 (the widest among legumes), effective soil depth 60 cm or more. Seasonal evapotranspiration about 250 mm. Roots are damaged by even one day under water, the weakest of the legumes against waterlogging, and salt tolerance is also low. Water table 1 m or below |

> **The most frightening thing in chickpea growing is not drought but the rainy season. In a dry year the yield falls, but if rain continues long as the pods ripen, the whole field is lost to Ascochyta blight.**

| Type | Characteristics |
|:---|:---|
| Desi type | Small, angular brown or black seeds with a thick seed coat; excellent cold tolerance and drought adaptation, a short growing period, and strong on poor ground. High disaster suitability. Over 80% of the world's chickpeas |
| Kabuli type | Large, round cream or white seeds with a thin seed coat; high yield in good conditions but a long growing period and demanding. The big white chickpea seen in shops |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There are practically no registered varieties in Korea and the yields of introduced lines vary wildly from 9 to 217 kg per 1,000㎡, so when you first get seed, plant several lines separately and select. Choose the Desi type where the field is poor and dry or seed is short (the seeds are small, so the same weight is planted two or three times wider), and an early Desi line if the rains come early.

## 4. Field Preparation and Sowing
| Item | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Making the field | Dig 20-30 cm deep, with beds 20 cm or higher (a precondition for preventing waterlogging) and drainage ditches 30-50 cm deep. Fully matured compost 1,000-1,500 kg per 1,000㎡ |
| Fertilization (1,000㎡) | Nitrogen 0-2 kg (more suppresses nitrogen fixation), phosphorus 20-40 kg, potassium 3-5 kg, sulfur 10-20 kg (all as basal fertilizer). If sulfur is short, nodules do not form properly and nitrogen fixation collapses |
| Substitute resources | Bone meal (phosphorus, calcium), fish bone and offal (phosphorus, nitrogen, sulfur), wood ash (100-200 g per ㎡), livestock manure compost and compost of garlic and onion skins and cabbage outer leaves (sulfur), urine diluted 10 times (very little) |
| Nodule inoculation | It needs its own bacterium (Mesorhizobium ciceri), so soil from fields that grew soybean, pea, or kidney bean is useless. Coat seed lightly moistened with water with topsoil 5-15 cm deep from a field that has grown chickpea and plant at once. In the first year plant without inoculation and supplement a little nitrogen, and plant a small plot densely to multiply the bacteria as inoculant for the next year |

> **The bacterium that pairs with chickpea is its own specific one. Soil from fields that grew soybean, pea, or kidney bean cannot be an inoculant for chickpea. Inoculating this crop is more demanding than for any other legume.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Around flowering (8 weeks after sowing) lift deeply with a spade, wash, and split the nodules. With 15 or more per plant and red inside, inoculation worked; if white or pale green, check acidity, waterlogging, and phosphorus and sulfur deficiency; and if there are almost none, it is a field without native bacteria. Standing water, excess nitrogen fertilizer, and too little phosphorus and sulfur also make inoculation fail. Fixation is 60-100 kg per hectare (5-15 kg if inoculation fails) while 45-60 kg leaves with the seed, so unless the stalks and leaves are cut into 20-30 cm lengths and returned to the field, not burned, and the roots left undug, the crop plunders the soil.

| Sowing | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Timing | Late March-mid April, after frost has completely ended (harvest mid July-August). Autumn sowing does not survive the winter cold |
| Seed preparation | Discard seeds with spots, discoloration, wrinkles, or insect holes (Ascochyta blight is seed-borne). Skim off floating beans, disinfect in 50-55℃ water for 10-15 minutes, and coat with fine wood ash. Do not soak (the seed coat softens and rots). Wait 2 weeks for a germination test |
| Method | Depth 3-5 cm, 1-2 seeds per hill, row spacing 25-60 cm, plant spacing 20-25 cm. Seeding rate per 1,000㎡ is 2.5-4 kg for small Desi seed and 8-12 kg for large Kabuli seed. For ten days to two weeks after sowing nothing comes up, and to keep off birds cover with straw or netting until germination |

## 5. Water Management and Cultivation Management
| Period | Water management |
|:---|:---|
| Sowing to germination | Keep the soil from drying out completely |
| Early growth | No watering (dry conditions push the root down beyond 1 m) |
| Flowering to pod filling | Irrigate if drought is severe (water shortage here cuts yield the most) |
| Ripening, rainy season | Stop irrigation (if too wet, shattering and lower quality). Dig drainage ditches in the rainy season |

> **Water management for chickpea is not the art of giving but the art of holding back. If you water often early on, the roots turn shallow, and shallow roots cannot survive a later drought.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Leaves folding at midday and opening in the evening is a normal water-saving response. If they are still folded in the morning it is severe dryness, and if lower leaves are yellow and the base is soft and brown it is root rot from excess water, so dig drainage ditches.

| Management | Method |
|:---|:---|
| Weeding | Scrape shallowly 2-3 weeks after emergence for the 1st, around day 45 for the 2nd (together with hilling), and finish all weeding before flowering. It is especially weak against weed competition, and after flowering do not enter the field |
| Hilling, line support | Around day 45 after sowing, cover the base with soil to build support and deepen the furrows so water drains well. If lodging is a risk, drive stakes around the field and run a line at knee height |

## 6. Pests, Diseases, and Nutrient Problems
| Pest or disease | Symptoms and conditions | Control without chemicals |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Ascochyta blight | Brown spots in concentric rings on leaves, stems, and pods, and the stem snaps. Rain that continues and cool weather; spread by seed and residue | Seed selection and hot-water disinfection, rotation of 3 years or more, airflow, burn diseased residue. If it spreads, harvest early |
| Fusarium wilt | At flowering, lower leaves wilt and the whole plant dries and dies, with a brown stem interior. Survives in soil for years | A rotation of 3 years or more is the only measure. Save seed from survivors |
| Root rot, sclerotinia, powdery mildew | Base and roots turn brown and soft (excess water) / white cottony mycelium and black bodies / white powder on leaves | Drainage and raised beds / dig out diseased plants with the soil and burn them / airflow, milk diluted 5-10 times |
| Pod borers | Larvae bore into pods and eat the seeds, the worst pest | Catch eggs and larvae by hand, plant a trap crop around the field, and avoid the pest season with early sowing |
| Cutworms, root-knot nematodes, aphids | Cut young stems at the base at night / gall-like deformities on roots / suck sap and carry viruses | Dig up the soil around cut plants to catch larvae / rotation, marigold intercropping / soapy water (5 g of soap per 1 L of water) |

> **Ascochyta blight and Fusarium wilt decide the success of chickpea growing. Ascochyta blight comes through seed and residue and explodes with the rain, while Fusarium wilt stays in the soil and waits for years. Without chemicals the response is only these three: seed selection, a long rotation, and airflow.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Bordeaux mixture is made by dissolving 400 g of copper sulfate and 400 g of quicklime separately in 100 L of water, mixing, and using it at once. Cultural controls are wide spacing, drainage, and staying out of the field after rain (brushing wet leaves spreads Ascochyta blight across the whole field).

| Symptom | Cause and remedy |
|:---|:---|
| Whole leaf pale yellow and plant small | Nitrogen deficiency (failed inoculation is the most common cause). First dig up roots and check the nodules, and this season give urine diluted 10 times in very small amounts |
| Almost no nodules form | Absence of its specific bacterium, or sulfur or molybdenum deficiency. Securing inoculant comes first, and supplement sulfur with livestock manure compost and brassica residue |
| Slow growth and purplish leaves | Phosphorus deficiency. Bone meal, roasted fish bone |
| Flowers open but pods do not set | Boron deficiency, or nights of 10℃ or below at flowering. Adjust the sowing time next year |
| Only stems and leaves lush and few pods | Nitrogen excess. Stop nitrogen at once |

## 7. Intercropping and Crop Rotation
| Compatibility | Companions |
|:---|:---|
| Good | Wheat, barley (different rooting depths use water and nutrients from different layers), maize, sorghum (use the nitrogen chickpea leaves behind), mustard, rapeseed (the sulfur compounds in their residue reduce pathogens) |
| Poor | Other legumes (pathogens and nematodes accumulate), crops that are heavily watered (chickpea is weakest against excess water) |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The rest period is 3 years or more and the ideal cycle 4-5 years, as Fusarium wilt and root-knot nematodes accumulate. An example rotation is chickpea → wheat, barley, maize → leafy vegetable after mustard or rapeseed → potato or root vegetable → chickpea. Putting brassicas such as mustard and rapeseed before it suits chickpea especially well.

## 8. Harvest and Seed Saving
| Harvest | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Timing | 100-120 days after sowing, when leaves have turned yellow and mostly fallen, 80% or more of pods are brown, and a bitten bean splits crisply. Moisture 18% at harvest and 14% or below for storage |
| Method | On a clear, dry day after the dew has dried, after 10 a.m., wear gloves because the acidic exudate on leaves and pods irritates the skin, and cut close to the ground, leaving the roots. Tie into bundles and dry in the sun for 4-6 days or hang under eaves |
| Threshing, winnowing | Stamp on dry stalks in a sack or flail them (hitting hard splits the seeds), or strike against the inner wall of a tub. On a windy day blow off the husks by tossing in a winnowing basket, sift out split half-seeds, skim off floating ones in water, and dry them again that same day |

> **If rain is forecast, do not wait for full ripeness; harvest. Rain at chickpea's ripening brings Ascochyta blight along with shattering. Slightly under-ripe beans can be dried and used, but from a diseased field you harvest nothing.**

| Seed saving | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Selection, harvest | Mark in advance plants with no disease symptoms, many pods, early ripening, and those that survived to the end in a field with Fusarium wilt. Thresh separately, and shelling by hand keeps the seeds from splitting |
| Sorting, drying | Discard spots, split seeds, wrinkles, and discoloration, leaving only large, intact seeds. Moisture 12-13%, dried slowly in shade |
| Storage | Keep in a sealed container separate from food, in a cool, dark place. 3 years sealed at room temperature, and germination is 85% or more in the harvest year, 75-85% after 1 year, and 60-75% after 2. If you can, mix in some seed from elsewhere every 3-4 years |

> **Chickpea is a crop whose seed is hard to obtain again in Korea. Seed saving is not an option but a precondition for growing this crop on. Secure seed first even if it cuts what you eat.**

## 9. Storage
| Step | Method |
|:---|:---|
| Drying | 4-6 days in the sun on the stalk, then 2-3 days spread thin after threshing, to moisture of 14% or below for food and 12% for seed (a bite does not dent it). Shake the beans in a dry bottle with dry salt; if the salt sticks to the wall, dry more, and cool completely before packing |
| Bean weevil control | Full sealing matters most. Use cold treatment, putting it outside for a few days in midwinter (watch for condensation when bringing it back in), and wood ash, diatomaceous earth, and oil coating. Split seeds are entered by insects first, so sift them out and eat them first |
| Containers, place | Clay jars and underground storage pits last 2-3 years (a pit loses everything if water seeps in). Beans with mold are not made safe by washing or cooking, so discard them, and check by sound and smell |

## 10. Cooking and Precautions
| Component | What it does | How to remove |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Trypsin inhibitors, lectins | Block protein digestion and cause abdominal pain and diarrhea. Chickpea lectin is far weaker than kidney bean's | Soak 8 hours, then heat in boiling water for 1 hour or more |
| Oligosaccharides, saponin | Fermentation in the gut causes heavy belly gas / foam | Always discard the soaking water / skim off the foam while boiling |
| Organic acids of leaves and pods, mold toxins | Malic and oxalic acid irritate the skin and stomach / toxins made by mold during storage | Do not eat the leaves and wear gloves at work / discard anything moldy |

> **What actually harms people with chickpea is not the bean itself but beans stored wrongly until moldy and beans eaten undercooked. If it smells musty or a bluish powder shows on the surface, discard it without regret.**

| Treatment | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Basic | Soak in room-temperature water for 8 hours or more (6 hours in summer, changing the water) and discard the soaking water, then boil in boiling water for 1 to 1.5 hours. A very little baking soda in the cooking water, or the clear water of steeped wood ash, softens the skin quickly and saves fuel. It is done when it mashes easily under a finger |
| Hulled and split | Moisten briefly, leave half a day in shade, pound lightly in a mortar to loosen the husks, blow them off in a winnowing basket, pound again to split in two, dry completely, and store sealed. It softens like porridge in 20-30 minutes without soaking, halving the fuel |
| Saving fuel | Retained-heat cooking is boiling 20 minutes, then 3-4 hours in a straw or rice-hull box or under bedding. Soaked and dried beans roasted without oil can be eaten as they are, and roasted and ground flour is mixed with water or stirred into drinks |
| Sprouting, fermenting | Soak 8 hours, drain, cover with a wet cloth for 2-3 days, and harvest when sprouts reach about 1 cm; vitamin C forms and phytic acid falls (a quick blanching and seasoning also works). Ground and left overnight it ferments sour, rises, and is easier to digest, and chickpea doenjang, boiled, mashed, and fermented, is an alternative when soybeans are short |

## 11. Annual Work Schedule
| Month | Work |
|:---|:---|
| Jan-Feb | Check storage, test seed germination (wait 2 weeks), and sort seed by line. In February, plan the field, turn the compost, and plan to secure inoculant soil |
| Mar-Apr | In March, plow, make beds, dig drainage ditches, and sow in late month. Finish sowing by mid April and guard against birds |
| May-June | In May, 1st weeding and thinning (no watering). In June, 2nd weeding, hilling, flowering, and nodule check |
| July-Aug | In July, pod filling, hand-picking pod borers, and watching for Ascochyta blight (do not enter the field if rain continues). In August, harvest, hang to dry, and thresh |
| Sep-Oct | In September, winnow, dry, store, return stalks and leaves, and sort seed. In October, check storage and set aside the soil where chickpea roots grew as inoculant |
| Nov-Dec | In November, hull and split and make flour (only as much as will be eaten in a month or two). In December, check storage, grow sprouted vegetables, and plan next year |

> **Chickpea is a crop that yields protein and iron even on dry ground, if you secure seed, inoculate with its own bacterium, plant in a field where water does not stand, give no water early on, and rotate for 3 years or more.**
