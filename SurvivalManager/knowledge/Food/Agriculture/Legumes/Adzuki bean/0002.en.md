&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The adzuki bean's overall grade is C, a score of 40.6. The gap between overall and supplementary is as much as 21 points. This large gap sums up the adzuki bean's character: a crop that cannot serve as a staple but excels as a side dish.

### 1-3. How to Read the Strengths

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Four criteria carry an SS: protein density, fertilizer demand, infrastructure needs, and storability. S goes to micronutrients, water use, taste, prevalence, and seed saving. This lineup is almost the same as soybean's, but with one decisive difference: the adzuki bean's calories per area is D. So even with the same combination of strengths, its total score is 9 points lower than soybean's.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Water use A is a real strength. Total evapotranspiration of about 400mm is clearly less than soybean's 450 to 700mm, and among legumes it is lower than only mung bean and cowpea. Where the well has stopped and water must be hauled in, this difference is not small. There is a point easy to misread, though: using little water and tolerating standing water are two different things. The adzuki bean uses little water while being the least able to tolerate waterlogging of any crop. It withstands drought but dies in the monsoon season (jangma, late June to July in South Korea).

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Speed to harvest B also has value. It can be harvested in 100 to 120 days, so it is sown in mid-to-late June and harvested in October. This schedule lets adzuki beans follow garlic, onion, barley, or potatoes harvested in June. That means two harvests from the same field in one year, which has the effect of doubling the usable area where fields are small — something soybean or seoritae (black soybean with green cotyledons) cannot do.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Storability SS, seed longevity A, and seed saving S together form a closed loop. Dried adzuki beans keep for 3 years at room temperature. Part of that stock can be used as next year's seed as is, and because the plant self-pollinates, its traits do not drift. One thing not to miss, though, is the parenthetical note next to the storability grade: it holds only once the weevil is kept out.

> **The adzuki bean's storability grade of SS is conditional. If the weevil is not kept out, the grade is not SS but E. This is why Section 15 covers this problem at greater length than any other.**

### 1-4. How to Read the Weaknesses

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;E is given to a single criterion, cold hardiness. D goes to calories per area, perenniality, sunlight response, cooking & processing, tillage energy, and non-food value. C goes to temperature range and pest & disease pressure, and B to speed to harvest, pest-control self-reliance, and continuous-cropping tolerance.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Calories per area D is the biggest constraint. At 1m², it is 660kcal, 0.25× the self-sufficiency line. For one adult to fill 2,100kcal a day on adzuki beans alone would take 636g of dried beans, which is not realistically possible. With more than 15g of dietary fiber per 100g, eating that much would overwhelm the digestive system. Structurally, the adzuki bean cannot be a staple crop. A realistic field-allocation figure is within one-tenth of the total area.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Tillage & digging energy D is another face of the same problem. The energy return is only 4.0×, meaning the calories that come back are small relative to the human labor spent tilling the field and building the raised bed. On top of that, because of its vulnerability to waterlogging, adzuki beans need higher raised beds, so tillage labor runs higher than for other legumes. Where there is no power source besides human muscle, this criterion really hurts.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Continuous-cropping tolerance B looks fine on the surface, but the practical burden is large. A 3-year break group means that once adzuki beans have been grown in a field, that field cannot grow adzuki beans again for 3 years. Only if the field can be split into four sections can adzuki beans be harvested every year. Planting them again right away lets leaf-drop disease and viruses build up in the soil, visibly collapsing yield, and there is no cure for these diseases. Compared with soybean, which needs only a 1-year break, the adzuki bean demands far more land.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The combination of pest & disease pressure C with pest-control self-reliance B is also worth reading closely. A yield loss without pest control of 48.9% means that without any control, half the crop is lost, and a large share of that half disappears not in the field but in the storehouse. That is exactly why fumigation before storage appears in the basis for pest-control self-reliance. The adzuki bean can succeed in the field and still fail in storage.

## 2. What Kind of Plant Is This?

### 2-1. Taxonomy and Origin

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The adzuki bean is an annual herb of the legume family (Fabaceae), genus Vigna. It shares its genus with mung bean and cowpea, but not with soybean, and this has a practical meaning. The adzuki bean's pests, diseases, and root-nodule bacteria (rhizobia) are closer in lineage to mung bean and cowpea than to soybean. Soil used as an inoculant also works better if it comes from a field that grew mung bean or cowpea rather than from a soybean field.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Its center of origin is East Asia, the region stretching from north-central China to the Korean Peninsula. Its wild ancestor, saepat (Vigna angularis var. nipponensis, the wild adzuki bean), still grows today in the fields and field banks of South Korea. It is adapted to a climate with rain concentrated in summer and frost in winter, so it grows in South Korea's soil without special protection. Still, where wild saepat grows reveals the adzuki bean's true nature: not inside a waterlogged paddy bank, but on well-drained field banks, slopes, and gravelly ground.

### 2-2. Appearance

| Organ | Characteristics |
|---|---|
| Leaf | Trifoliate, with three leaflets to a set, close to heart-shaped |
| Stem | Upright, 30-50cm tall. Lower than soybean, and branching. Vining varieties exceed 1m |
| Root | A straight taproot with fine lateral roots. Nodules form on the lateral roots |
| Flower | Yellow. Butterfly-shaped, self-pollinating |
| Pod | Slender and cylindrical. Turns brown when ripe. Each pod holds 6-10 seeds |
| Seed | Red is the standard color; black, white, and mottled forms also exist. The hilum shows as a white line |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The adzuki bean's yellow flower is an easy way to tell it apart from soybean: soybean flowers are white or pale purple, while the adzuki bean's are a vivid yellow. Its flowers also do not open all at once. As the stem grows, flowers keep opening from the bottom upward, so while the lower pods are ripening, the top of the plant is still flowering. This trait makes it hard to judge harvest timing, which is why Section 12 sets out criteria for that judgment.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Its low height of 30cm to 50cm also matters. It is much shorter than soybean, and two consequences follow. First, there is little risk of lodging, so no stakes are needed. Second, the pods hang close to the ground, so they easily touch the soil or pick up moisture. This is why hilling (earthing up) to raise the bed and mulching to cover the soil pay off particularly well for adzuki beans.

### 2-3. Growth Stages

| Stage | Timing | Key Physiology | Key Nutrients |
|---|---|---|---|
| Germination & seedling stage | 3 weeks from sowing | Germination and early growth begin; root nodules start forming | Phosphorus, a small amount of nitrogen |
| Vegetative stage | 3-6 weeks | Stems and leaves increase; root nodules become active | Nitrogen supplied by the nodules |
| Flowering stage | 6-8 weeks | Yellow flowers open in sequence from bottom to top | Phosphorus, boron |
| Pod-filling stage | 8-10 weeks | Pods and seeds enlarge | Potassium, phosphorus |
| Ripening & harvest stage | 10-14 weeks | Lower pods turn brown and ripen first | Stop irrigation |

> **The seedling stage is the most vulnerable to waterlogging. If the monsoon (jangma) sets in right after sowing, the young plants' roots are damaged and they simply die. In years when sowing in mid-to-late June coincides with the monsoon (jangma), bed height decides whether the plants survive.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;What is distinctive about the adzuki bean's growth stages is how long flowering continues. Soybean finishes flowering in about 2 weeks, but the adzuki bean keeps flowering over 3-4 weeks. Looked at positively, this means a single spell of high heat or drought will not wipe out the whole crop. Looked at negatively, it means ripening is spread out and cannot all be harvested at once. The standard practice of harvesting all at once when 70-80% of the pods have ripened is a compromise for this problem.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Germination temperature is also worth noting. The adzuki bean's optimal germination temperature is 32-34°C, distinctly higher than other legumes. Its lower limit is 6-10°C, and germination and early growth are normal only above 15°C. This is why adzuki beans are sown in June: sowing in April or May leaves the soil too cold, germination is delayed, and the seed rots in the meantime. Sowing late is not laziness — it is what the plant's physiology requires.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Vitamin B1 deserves separate emphasis. 0.66mg is more than half of an adult's daily recommended intake, and about ten times the level in white rice. Vitamin B1 is essential to converting carbohydrates into energy, and a shortage brings on beriberi: the legs go numb and swell, strength drains away, and in severe cases heart function declines. It is a disease that actually appears on diets centered on milled white rice with little else to go with it.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In a disaster situation, this combination matters. Filling calories with rice or potatoes tends to leave vitamin B1 short, and a handful of adzuki beans fills that gap. Patbap (rice cooked with adzuki beans) is not really a matter of taste but a way of solving a nutritional problem, by the same logic as the custom of mixing beans into sorghum.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;It is also worth noting that fat is very low, under 1%, next to nothing compared with soybean's 16% or peanut's near 50%. This is part of why its calorie count is low, but it also favors storage: low fat means little rancidity, so dried adzuki beans develop no odor even after several years. Even ground into flour, it keeps far longer than soybean flour.
