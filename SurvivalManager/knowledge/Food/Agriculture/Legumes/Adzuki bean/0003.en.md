## 3. Growing Conditions

### 3-1. Temperature and Light

| Condition | Limit | Optimum | Outside This Range |
|---|---|---|---|
| Germination temperature | Min 6-10°C, max 40-44°C | 32-34°C | Below 15°C, germination slows and seeds rot |
| Soil temperature at sowing | 15°C or above | 20°C or above | Sowing into cold soil ruins early growth |
| Growing temperature | 15-35°C | 20-24°C | Especially vulnerable to low temperature |
| Fruiting temperature | 18-32°C | 20-30°C | A narrow 10°C range |
| Frost | Killed below 0°C | - | No cold hardiness |
| Sunlight | 6+ hours a day | 8+ hours a day | Yield is cut in half at 40% shade |
| Day length | Short-day plant | Flowers as days grow shorter | Sown late, it flowers while still small |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;An optimal germination temperature of 32°C to 34°C is the highest of any legume in this crop table, and that single fact explains why the adzuki bean is sown late. Field soil temperature in South Korea does not reach 30°C until after mid-June, which is why the right time to sow adzuki beans falls in mid-to-late June. That timing comes right after garlic, onion, and barley are harvested, so it naturally fits as a follow-on crop. It is no accident that the adzuki bean has long held the place of a second crop in South Korea's cropping system.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Its vulnerability to low temperature cuts both ways. In spring, sowing must wait until the soil warms; in autumn, ripening must finish before frost. Fortunately, its short growing period of 100 to 120 days leaves a comfortable window. Sown in mid-June and harvested in early-to-mid October, it finishes before frost arrives. Yield drops noticeably once sowing slips into July, so early July is taken as the cutoff for central and northern South Korea.

### 3-2. Soil and Water - Waterlogging Is Everything

| Condition | Limit | Optimum | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Soil texture | Cannot be grown on poorly drained land | Well-drained sandy loam to loam | Drainage comes before every other condition |
| pH | 5.5-7.0 | 6.0-6.5 | Root nodules decrease in acidic soil |
| Bed height | 20cm or more | 25-30cm | Growing on flat ridges cuts yield by 34% |
| Drainage channel | Required | 30cm deep or more | The drainage channel comes before the raised bed |
| Season evapotranspiration | - | About 400mm | Low among legumes |
| Flooding | Hard to recover once submerged a day or more | - | The most vulnerable of any legume |
| Water table | 60cm or deeper | 80cm or deeper | The most important condition when planting on paddy land |

> **Adzuki beans planted on flat ridges yield 34% less than those on a raised bed. In growing adzuki beans, bed height is a condition that comes before fertilizer.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;It helps to understand why the adzuki bean is so vulnerable to waterlogging. Two reasons overlap. First, root-nodule bacteria (rhizobia) are aerobic and need oxygen. When soil is submerged, oxygen disappears and the nodules lose function before the roots themselves do. This is true of all legumes, but because the adzuki bean's roots are shallow, they are more directly exposed to the moisture state of the topsoil. Second, its roots have almost no structure adapted to low oxygen. Unlike rice, it has no aerenchyma to carry air down through the stem to the roots, and unlike soybean, it cannot send roots deep enough to reach a layer that stays above the water.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This is why preparing an adzuki bean field begins with drainage. The order matters too. First, look at the field's water flow and identify the low spots and places where water collects; do not plant adzuki beans there. Next, cut drainage channels: run furrows around and through the field so water drains toward the low side. After that, build the raised bed — 20cm or higher, preferably 25 to 30cm, rounded on top so water does not pool. Fertilizer goes in last.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There are also situations where adzuki beans must go into paddy land. When fields are scarce, a paddy gets converted to dry-field use, and adzuki beans are about the worst-suited crop for that setting. If they must be planted there anyway, choose a flood-tolerant variety, dig drainage channels 50 to 80cm deep, raise the bed to 30cm or more, and time sowing to avoid the monsoon season (jangma, late June to July in South Korea). Even then the risk stays high, so on paddy land it is more sensible to plant cowpea than adzuki beans.

## 4. Varieties

### 4-1. Classification by Growth Habit

| Type | Characteristics | Suitability for Disaster Conditions |
|---|---|---|
| Upright type (common adzuki bean) | 30-50cm tall; needs no stakes and resists lodging | Easy to manage; the default choice |
| Vining type (vining adzuki bean) | Vines exceed 1m and need stakes. Higher yielding | When staking material is available and labor is not scarce |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Under disaster conditions, the upright type is the default. The vining type yields more but needs stakes put up and labor spent training the vines. If bamboo or branches are available and labor is not tight, the vining type's higher yield is appealing; otherwise the upright type is the safer choice. There is one exception, though: if the field is extremely small, using the vining type to make use of vertical space works out better in yield per area. Leaving cornstalks standing to serve as stakes is one way to do this.

### 4-2. Major Varieties

| Variety | Type | Characteristics | Suited To |
|---|---|---|---|
| 'Jungwonpat' | Upright | Resists lodging. Easy to manage | Default choice. Across South Korea |
| 'Chungjupat' | Upright | Adapted to central South Korea | Central South Korea |
| 'Chilbopat' | Vining | High yielding. Needs stakes | When yield matters most |

| 'Arari', 'Hongda', 'Geomguseul', 'Yeondduchae' | Upright | Strongly flood-tolerant; adapted to paddy cultivation | Poorly drained fields, converted paddy land |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;It is worth remembering that separate flood-tolerant varieties exist. 'Arari', 'Hongda', 'Geomguseul', and 'Yeondduchae' were bred with paddy cultivation in mind, so they tolerate waterlogged conditions relatively well. The word "relatively" matters: these varieties still die if submerged. They simply hold out a little longer than others, and their root development is less suppressed in wet soil. If a field drains poorly, it is worth seeking out one of these lines.

## 5. Field Preparation and Fertilizer - Rhizobia and Nitrogen Balance

### 5-1. How Nitrogen Fixation Happens

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;78% of the air is nitrogen, yet plants cannot use it. Atmospheric nitrogen is a molecule (N2) with two atoms locked together by a triple bond, and breaking that bond takes enormous energy. The industrial process that breaks this bond to make ammonia is the Haber-Bosch process, which uses high temperature, high pressure, a catalyst, and huge amounts of fossil fuel.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Root-nodule bacteria (rhizobia) do this same job at ordinary temperature and pressure. An enzyme called nitrogenase breaks the triple bond and turns nitrogen into ammonia. This reaction still needs a great deal of energy, though, and the bacteria cannot photosynthesize. So they live inside the roots of legume plants, take the sugar the plant makes, and hand over the nitrogen they fix in return. It is a trade.

| Stage | What Happens |
|---|---|
| 1. Signal exchange | The adzuki bean's roots release flavonoids, and bacteria in the soil respond by releasing Nod factors |
| 2. Infection | Root hairs curl to enclose the bacteria and draw them into the root |
| 3. Nodule formation | Root cells divide, the nodule swells, and the bacteria settle inside |
| 4. Nitrogen fixation | Inside the nodule, nitrogenase converts atmospheric nitrogen into ammonia |
| 5. Distribution | Ammonia is converted into amino acids and moved to the stems, leaves, and pods |
| 6. Senescence | During ripening the nodules lose function and return to the soil along with the roots |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Nitrogenase is extremely sensitive to oxygen and loses function the moment oxygen touches it. To manage this, the inside of the nodule is filled with a red pigment protein called leghemoglobin, which holds oxygen in check. It works on the same principle that makes human blood red, and that pigment is what makes the inside of a nodule look red or pink when it is split open. This fact matters a great deal in practice: red color is visible proof that a nodule is fixing nitrogen right now.

### 5-2. How to Check Whether Nodules Have Formed

| Step | Details |
|---|---|
| 1 | The best time is around when flowering begins, 5-7 weeks after sowing |
| 2 | Choose two or three plants that look healthy |
| 3 | Do not pull them up — dig a wide area around them with a spade and lift them out with the soil intact. Pulling leaves the nodules on the fine roots behind in the soil |
| 4 | Put them in a bucket of water and gently wash the soil off |
| 5 | Count the nodules on the taproot and the thicker lateral roots |
| 6 | Split a few nodules open with a fingernail and check the color inside |

| Observation | Meaning | Action |
|---|---|---|
| 15 or more nodules per plant | Inoculation succeeded | Leave as is |
| Red or pink inside when split | Fixing nitrogen | A good sign |
| White or pale green inside when split | Nodule formed but not fixing | Check acidity, waterlogging, and phosphorus deficiency |
| Brown or black inside when split | Senescent or dead | Normal during ripening; a problem during flowering |
| 5 or fewer nodules | Inoculation failed | If leaves are yellow, add a small amount of nitrogen |

> **A single habit — digging up roots, splitting a nodule, and checking for red — is the surest signal of whether an adzuki bean crop will succeed in a world without nitrogen fertilizer.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There is a specific reason checking root nodules is especially useful for adzuki beans. Yellowing leaves in an adzuki bean field have three possible causes: nitrogen deficiency, root damage from waterlogging, and leaf-drop disease. The fix for each is completely different — fertilizer for nitrogen deficiency, drainage for waterlogging, and removing plants plus rotation for leaf-drop disease. Digging up the roots tells these three apart: no nodules means nitrogen deficiency; nodules present but roots rotted brown means waterlogging; discoloration at the base of the stem with intact roots means disease.

### 5-3. Where to Get an Inoculant

| Method | Procedure | Caution |
|---|---|---|
| Coat seed with soil from a field that grew adzuki bean, mung bean, or cowpea | Scoop topsoil from 5-15cm deep in a field that grew the same genus (Vigna) last year and had good nodulation. Coat lightly moistened seed evenly with it and sow right away | Work in the shade and sow within 30 minutes. Drying in the sun kills the bacteria |
| Scatter soil along the sowing row | Scatter the soil along the row to be sown and till it in together. One shovelful per 10m² | Uses more soil, but spreads the bacteria through the ground |
| Crush nodules for use | Gather nodules from dug-up roots, crush them, mix into water, and soak the seed in that water before draining | Must be used the same day |
| Leave the roots in place | Leave the roots in the field instead of digging them up at harvest | The lowest-effort way to maintain it |
| Wild saepat roots | Collect nodules from saepat roots on field banks | Being a wild form of the same species, the bacteria match well |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;For an adzuki bean inoculant, soil from a field that grew adzuki bean, mung bean, or cowpea works better than soil from a soybean field. Root-nodule bacteria are host-specific, and which plants a given strain infects varies. Bradyrhizobium japonicum mainly associates with soybean, while a different Bradyrhizobium strain mainly associates with the genus Vigna. Soybean-field soil is not entirely useless, but infection rate and fixation efficiency are lower. If soil from a field that grew a Vigna crop is available, use that first.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Wild saepat is an especially valuable inoculant. It is a vining legume common on field banks and in open fields, and a wild form of the same species as the adzuki bean, so its roots carry a strain of bacteria that suits the adzuki bean exactly. If you find saepat in summer, dig up a little root to check for nodules, and remember the spot — it becomes a source of inoculant the following year.

| Condition That Causes Inoculation to Fail | Reason |
|---|---|
| Soil pH 5.5 or below | The bacteria cannot survive |
| Field given plenty of nitrogen fertilizer | The plant feels no need for the trade and forms fewer nodules |
| Field where water collects | Being aerobic, the bacteria die without oxygen. The most common cause of failure with adzuki beans |
| Field low in phosphorus | Phosphorus is used in the energy metabolism of nitrogen fixation |
| Inoculated seed dried in the sun | UV light and drying kill the bacteria |
| Inoculating right after a fungicide or hot-water treatment | Kills the bacteria. Let the seed cool completely first |

### 5-4. The Trap in Nitrogen Fixation

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The saying that planting legumes improves the soil is true, but only with conditions attached. Let's check it with numbers. Adzuki bean seed is 20.63% protein, and dividing protein by 6.25 gives nitrogen content: about 3.3% of seed weight is pure nitrogen. Harvesting 200kg from 1,000m² means about 6.6kg of nitrogen is in that harvest, or 66kg converted to a hectare.

| Item | Nitrogen per Hectare | Direction |
|---|---|---|
| Nodule fixation (good conditions) | 60-100kg | In |
| Nodule fixation (poor conditions) | 20-40kg | In |
| Removed in the harvested seed | 60-70kg | Out |
| Nitrogen remaining in stems and leaves | 20-30kg | Stays if returned to the field; leaves if burned or carried away |
| Nitrogen remaining in roots and nodules | 8-15kg | Usually stays in the field |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The adzuki bean's seed has a lower nitrogen concentration than soybean's, so it removes less. Soybean seed is about 6% nitrogen by weight, versus 3.3% for adzuki bean. So under good conditions, there is more room for the balance to stay positive than with soybean. But under poor conditions — that is, if waterlogging or acidity keeps the nodules from working properly — fixation falls to only 20 to 40kg, which is less than the 60 to 70kg removed in the seed, and the balance turns negative. And because the adzuki bean is so vulnerable to waterlogging, the odds of poor conditions are higher for it than for other legumes.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;For reference, maize seed is about 1.4% nitrogen by weight. Adzuki bean seed at 3.3% is 2.4 times that, and soybean seed at 6% is 4.5 times that. Growing a legume for its seed is, in the same motion, pulling nitrogen out of the field.

> **A legume improves the soil not because it fixes nitrogen, but because part of the nitrogen it fixes stays in the field, held in its stems and leaves. Carry those stems and leaves away, and the adzuki bean becomes a crop that strips the soil instead.**

| Principle | Details |
|---|---|
| Stems and leaves always go back to the field | Return all the stalks, leaves, and pod husks. Cut into 20-30cm pieces and spread as cover |
| Do not burn them | Burning releases nitrogen as gas and leaves only potassium and phosphorus behind |
| Do not dig up the roots | Cut only the stem and leave the roots in place |
| Diseased residue is an exception | Burn plants infected with leaf-drop disease or viruses, or let them rot for a long time outside the field |
| If burned, return the ash | Nitrogen is lost, but potassium and phosphorus are recovered |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;For adzuki beans, this principle runs into a conflict. Continuous-cropping damage is severe, and leaf-drop disease and viruses build up in the soil. Keeping nitrogen means returning the stems and leaves, but returning diseased ones leaves the pathogens behind too. Here is a rule of thumb: if leaves show only a few spots, cutting and spreading them as cover is fine. Plants with the leaf-drop symptom of lower leaves browning and dropping in quantity, or showing mosaic symptoms, should be gathered separately and burned. In a year when disease has wrecked the whole field, it is better to carry all the stems and leaves off the field, and make up the nitrogen lost that way through some other means the following year.

### 5-5. Fertilizer Rates and Disaster Substitutes

| Fertilizer | Per 1,000m² | Per m² | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Nitrogen | 2-4kg | 2-4g | Minimal amount only, for early use before the nodules start working |
| Phosphorus | 4-6kg | 4-6g | All as base fertilizer. More than for soybean |
| Potassium | 4-6kg | 4-6g | All as base fertilizer. Involved in seed filling and stem strength |
| Compost | 1,000kg | 1kg | Fully rotted |
| Lime | 150-200kg (acidic soil) | 150-200g | 3-4 weeks before sowing |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;What sets adzuki bean fertilization apart from soybean's is putting in more phosphorus and potassium: 4 to 6kg for adzuki beans versus 3.4kg each for soybean. There are two reasons. Phosphorus is used in the energy metabolism of nitrogen fixation, and because the adzuki bean's nodules are smaller and fewer than soybean's, phosphorus needs to be generous to raise their efficiency. Potassium is involved in stem strength and seed filling, and because the adzuki bean's flowering runs long, its ripening period is long too, keeping potassium demand going the whole time.

> **Giving more nitrogen only makes the leaves lush while fewer pods set. Worse, an overgrown plant shades its own lower pods, and they fail to ripen.**

| Substitute Resource | Nutrients Supplied | How to Use | Caution |
|---|---|---|---|
| Wood ash | Potassium, phosphorus, calcium | Spread 100-200g per m² and mix in | Too much pushes pH above 7 and blocks iron and zinc uptake |
| Well-rotted compost | All-around, organic matter | 1kg per m² | Under-rotted compost burns roots and carries disease |
| Diluted urine | Nitrogen, potassium | Dilute 10× with water and pour onto the soil between plants | Small amounts only. Keep off the leaves |
| Bone meal, fish bones | Phosphorus, calcium | Roast, grind, and mix into compost or apply directly | Especially good for adzuki beans |
| Shellfish shells, eggshells | Calcium | Roast, grind, and spread | Used in place of lime |
| Leaf mold | All-around, organic matter | Mix into the soil | Improves both drainage and moisture retention |
| Rice hulls, rice straw | Potassium, silica | Spread over the bed as mulch | Because adzuki bean pods hang close to the ground, mulching is especially useful |
| Coarse sand, gravel | Drainage | Mix into clay soil | Soil improvement as a countermeasure against waterlogging |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The last item is worth paying close attention to. For adzuki beans, improving drainage does more than any fertilizer. In a field with heavy clay where water collects, mixing in plenty of coarse sand, rice hulls, well-rotted sawdust, and leaf mold to change the soil's structure raises yield more than any amount of fertilizer. This cannot be done in one go, so add a little each year and change the soil over several years. This kind of improvement is an investment that carries over to every crop grown in that field, not just adzuki beans.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In principle, top-dressing is not needed. If growth is clearly poor, add a small amount at flowering or ten days after flowering. Standard practice uses a foliar spray of urea at 0.1 to 0.5% concentration; under disaster conditions, dilute urine to at least 20 times with water and spray it on the leaves, or pour a 10× dilution onto the soil between plants. Always spray leaves after the sun has gone down — spraying at midday scorches them.
