## 6. Sowing

### 6-1. Sowing Time

| Region | Optimal Sowing Time | Sowing Deadline | Harvest Time |
|---|---|---|---|
| Central and northern South Korea | Mid-to-late June | Early July | Early-to-mid October |
| Southern South Korea | Mid-June to early July | Mid-July | Mid-to-late October |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The reason the adzuki bean's sowing time falls in mid-to-late June is its high optimal germination temperature of 32-34°C. That timing comes right after garlic, onion, barley, and potatoes are harvested, so it naturally fits as a follow-on crop. Where fields are small, this trait is a real advantage: it allows two harvests from the same field in one year.

> **The later adzuki beans are sown, the more sharply yield drops. Once sowing slips into July, the flowering period shortens, fewer pods set, and flowers that open late do not ripen before frost.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There is a set response for when sowing is delayed: increase the sowing rate. At the right time, 3-4kg is sown per 1,000m²; when delayed, this rises to 5-7kg. Because a late-sown plant grows smaller, raising plant numbers is how yield per area is kept up, and plant spacing is also narrowed. This correction has its limits, though — past mid-July there is little to harvest unless it is the early-maturing 'Yepat'.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Sowing time also has to be weighed against the monsoon season (jangma, late June to July in South Korea). Mid-to-late June is when South Korea's monsoon begins, and the adzuki bean's seedling stage is its most vulnerable to waterlogging. If the sowing date can be adjusted based on the monsoon forecast, it is better to sow right after one round of monsoon rain has passed, so the young plants face less of it. If the date cannot be adjusted, prepare by raising the bed as high as possible.

### 6-2. Seed Preparation

| Item | Method | Under Disaster Conditions |
|---|---|---|
| Sorting | Discard seeds with spots, discoloration, wrinkling, or insect holes | The adzuki bean weevil's holes are small, round, and look like white specks on the surface |
| Floating in water | Soak in water and skim off anything that floats | Weevil-eaten seeds are hollow inside and float. Drain and dry them right away |
| Hot-water soak | Soak 5 minutes in 65°C water to kill seed-borne disease | Use at least ten times the seed's volume in water. Going over time damages germination ability |
| Ash treatment | Coat with fine wood ash | Helps disinfect and dry the seed, and repels birds and insects |
| Nodule inoculation | Coat with soil from a field that grew adzuki bean, mung bean, or cowpea | Do this only after the hot-water treatment and complete cooling |
| Germination test | Wrap 20 seeds in a wet cloth and keep in a warm place for a few days | Since adzuki beans need warmth to sprout, run the test in a warm spot too |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The adzuki bean's hot-water soak differs from other legumes'. Soybean uses 50-55°C for 10-15 minutes, while adzuki bean uses around 65°C for 5 minutes — higher temperature, shorter time. Keeping this difference matters: 10 minutes in 65°C water badly damages germination, while 5 minutes at 50°C has almost no disinfecting effect. Without a thermometer, mixing boiling water and room-temperature water at about a 2-to-1 ratio gives roughly 65°C, hot enough that it is hard to keep a hand in. Count 5 minutes, then cool immediately in cold water.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;What must be checked during sorting is the adzuki bean weevil's hole. If a round hole under 1mm across shows on the seed coat, or the surface has something that looks like a white speck, that seed already holds an egg or a larva. Sown, it will not germinate; left in storage, it becomes the starting point for the next generation. Floating seed in water makes this sorting much easier, since a hollowed-out seed floats.

### 6-3. Sowing Method

| Item | Standard | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Sowing depth | 3-5cm | Too shallow and it dries out or birds eat it. Too deep and emergence suffers |
| Seeds per hole | 2-3 | Thin later to leave 2 plants |
| Sowing rate (on time) | 3-4kg/1,000m² | |
| Sowing rate (delayed) | 5-7kg/1,000m² | The later the sowing, the denser it goes |
| Single-row planting | Row spacing 60-70cm, plant spacing 10-15cm | Suited to the upright type |
| Double-row planting | Two rows 40cm apart on a 120cm bed, plant spacing 10-15cm | Higher area efficiency |
| Bed height | 20cm or more, preferably 25-30cm | The key to preventing waterlogging |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The effect of mulching is especially large for adzuki beans. In standard trials, plots without plastic film yielded 24% less than mulched plots. The same principle works without plastic film: covering the bed 5cm to 10cm deep with straw, rice hulls, fallen leaves, or cut weeds brings three effects together. Weeds are suppressed, surface evaporation drops, and rain-splashed soil is kept off the lower pods. Since no herbicide is registered for adzuki beans and weeding must be done by hand, mulching directly cuts the labor needed.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There is one caution with organic mulch, though. A thick layer right after sowing can keep young sprouts from coming up, and soil under damp mulch may not dry out, making waterlogging worse. For adzuki beans it is safer to apply mulch after germination is finished, two or three true leaves have appeared, and the first hilling is done.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The threat right after sowing is birds. Pigeons, magpies, and pheasants peck the cotyledons off freshly sprouted adzuki beans, and a plant that loses its cotyledons usually dies. Lay a thin cover of branches or straw over the sowing row, cover it with netting or an old insect screen until germination, or crisscross shiny string over it.

## 7. Water Management - Waterlogging Is the Number One Enemy

| Stage | Water Management | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Sowing to germination | Keep the soil from drying out completely | Germination needs moisture |
| Seedling stage | Manage on the dry side | The stage most vulnerable to waterlogging |
| Monsoon season | The job is drainage, not irrigation | Clear drainage channels and remove standing water |
| Flowering to pod-filling | If drought hits, irrigate generously | Dryness at this stage reduces pod count |
| Ripening stage | Stop irrigation | Excess moisture keeps pods from drying and lets mold grow on the seed |

> **For adzuki beans, "water management" usually means removing water, not adding it. Raising the bed and clearing furrows comes before irrigation.**

| Symptom | Cause | Action |
|---|---|---|
| Leaves droop at midday, recover by evening | Temporary lack of moisture. Normal | Leave as is |
| Leaves still drooping in the morning, soil dry | Drought | Irrigate |
| Leaves still drooping in the morning, soil wet | Roots damaged by excess moisture | Clear the furrows immediately to drain water. Do not add more water |
| Lower leaves yellow, roots rotted brown | Waterlogging | Drainage and hilling. Hard to recover if severe |
| Whole leaf pale yellow but not wilting | Nitrogen deficiency from poor nodulation | Dig up roots and split a nodule to check |
| Brown blotches spread on lower leaves and they drop | Leaf-drop disease or brown leaf spot | Disease. Improve airflow and remove diseased leaves |
| Flowers dropping, few pods | Drought or high heat during flowering | Irrigate and mulch to lower soil temperature |
| Many pods but small seeds | Water shortage or potassium deficiency during pod-filling | Irrigate; wood ash |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The surest way to tell drought from excess moisture apart is to dig into the soil. When leaves are drooping, dig about 10cm deep beside the plant and squeeze the soil in your hand. If it crumbles apart, it is dry; if it clumps and water seeps out, it is too wet. Because the response to these two situations is the exact opposite, watering without checking first is risky. More than half of drooping-leaf cases in adzuki bean fields come from excess moisture, and adding more water to those simply kills the plant.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There are three actual tasks during the monsoon season. First, check that water standing in the furrows drains off the field, and clear any blockage. Second, if water pools anywhere on top of the bed, add soil so it flows away. Third, do not go into the field right after rain stops: walking on wet soil compacts it and worsens drainage, and brushing against wet leaves spreads disease. Go in only after the rain has stopped and the soil has dried.

## 8. Crop Management

### 8-1. Hilling - Three Rounds

| Round | Timing | Where to Hill |
|---|---|---|
| 1st | 2-3 true leaves | Mound soil up to the cotyledon node |
| 2nd | 4-5 true leaves | Mound up to the unifoliate-leaf node |
| 3rd | 6-7 true leaves | Mound up to the first trifoliate-leaf node. Finish before flowering |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Hilling matters more for adzuki beans than for other legumes. Four things happen at once, and two of them target the adzuki bean's weaknesses directly. First, the bed gets higher and drainage improves — since waterlogging is the adzuki bean's enemy, hilling matters especially here. Second, young weeds get buried in soil, which is real value too, since there is no herbicide available for adzuki beans. Third, new roots grow from the buried stem, increasing the absorbing area. Fourth, the buried base reduces lodging.

> **Hilling must always finish before flowering. Going into the field to turn soil after flowers have opened knocks flowers off and severs fine roots, reducing yield.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;If there is not enough labor to do all three rounds, there is a priority order: the 3rd round matters most, followed by the 1st. The 3rd round raises the bed to its highest, guarding against monsoon-season waterlogging and lodging. The 1st round helps young plants get established and buries the weeds present at that stage. Skipping the 2nd round causes little loss.

### 8-2. Weed Management

| Timing | Method | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| 2-3 weeks after sowing | 1st weeding. Scrape shallowly | Combine with the 1st hilling |
| 4-5 weeks after sowing | 2nd weeding | Combine with the 2nd hilling |
| Before flowering | Finish all weeding | Do not go into the field after this |
| Right after the 3rd hilling | Cover with mulch | Almost no labor is needed after this |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The fact that no herbicide is registered for adzuki beans is a nuisance in conventional farming, but under disaster conditions it turns into useful information: it means this crop has already been weeded by hand, and that the growing method does not change once chemicals are gone. In return, the labor cost is firmly fixed. Weeding in the first month to month and a half is what makes the yield, and once the leaves cover the ground afterward, weeds do not increase much.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Combining mulching and weeding in this order cuts labor the most: do the 1st and 2nd weeding together with hilling, and after the 3rd hilling cover the bed with straw, rice hulls, or cut weeds. Weeding the field clean once before covering it means almost no new weeds come up underneath. Since there is no more need to enter the field after flowering, the worry about knocking off flowers disappears too.

### 8-3. Lodging and Stakes

| Type | Management |
|---|---|
| Upright type | No stakes needed. Hilling alone is enough |
| Vining type | Set up bamboo, branches, or cornstalks and train the vines onto them |
| Overgrown from excess nitrogen | Reduce fertilizer the following year. For the current year, run string around the plants for support |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Stakes for the vining type are not hard to put together under disaster conditions. Leaving cornstalks standing uncut after harvest turns that spot into next year's stakes. Common methods include tying three poles together into a tripod, or setting up two rows of poles connected by crossbars into a net-like frame. Adzuki bean vines are not particularly heavy, though, so a sturdy structure is not necessary — supports at knee to waist height are enough to keep the pods off the ground and prevent rot.

## 9. Pests and Diseases

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The adzuki bean's pest & disease pressure is C. Estimated yield loss without pest control is 48.9%, meaning nearly half the crop is lost without any control. What is distinctive about the adzuki bean is that this loss splits between the field and the storehouse: stink bugs and leaf-drop disease take their share in the field, and the adzuki bean weevil takes its share in storage.

### 9-1. Major Diseases

| Disease | Symptoms | Conditions | Response Without Chemicals |
|---|---|---|---|
| Viral disease (mosaic virus) | Leaves mottle in a mosaic pattern and growth stops | Spread by aphids | Cannot be cured. Pull and burn as soon as found, and reduce aphids |
| Leaf-drop disease | Lower leaves brown first and drop in large numbers | Fungal. Builds up in the soil of continuously cropped fields | A 3-year or longer rotation is the only real defense. Burn diseased residue |
| Brown leaf spot | Brown blotches appear on lower leaves | High humidity in August-September | Ensure airflow, Bordeaux mixture, remove diseased leaves |
| Brown spot disease | Brown spots appear on the underside of leaves | High humidity | Bordeaux mixture 10 days before flowering and during flowering |
| Powdery mildew | White powder covers the leaf surface | Dry conditions with poor airflow | Milk diluted 5-10×, ensure airflow |
| Anthracnose | Dark brown lesions form and leaves drop | High humidity | Seed sorting, rotation, Bordeaux mixture |
| Root rot | Roots rot brown and lower leaves yellow | Excess moisture, continuous cropping | Drainage, raising the bed, rotation |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Leaf-drop disease deserves separate emphasis. It is the main reason continuous-cropping tolerance stays at B, and it is the signature problem that shows up when adzuki beans are planted in the same field again and again. The fungus stays in the soil and builds up year after year; as lower leaves brown and drop, photosynthetic area shrinks and the seed fails to fill. Even with chemicals, full control is difficult, and without chemicals, rotation is the only tool. The rule of a 3-year break comes directly from this disease.

### 9-2. Major Pests

| Pest | Timing | Damage | Control Without Chemicals |
|---|---|---|---|
| Stink bugs | Increase from August | Pierce pods and suck sap, leaving empty husks | Hand-catch in early morning, trap crops, insect screens |
| Adzuki bean weevil | Lays eggs in the field, hatches in storage | Hollows out the seed from inside | Covered in detail in Section 15 |
| Cutworms | Early stage | Cut young stems at the base | Dig through soil at night to catch larvae. Sprinkle ash around the base of plants |
| Bean pod borer moth | Summer | Chews on leaves and pods | Hand-pick. Remove egg masses along with the leaf |
| Aphids | July-August | Suck sap and spread viral disease | Spray soapy water (5g soap per 1L water) on leaf undersides |
| Birds | Right after sowing | Peck off cotyledons | Cover with straw or netting until germination |
| Water deer, roe deer | Summer | Eat leaves and stems | Put up fencing or netting |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Cutworms cause heavy early damage to adzuki beans. The larvae hide in the soil by day and come out at night to cut young stems off at the base, so a plant that looked fine the day before is simply lying flat when you check the field in the morning. There are two responses. Dig 2-3cm into the soil around a fallen plant with your fingers, and you will find a plump larva curled up — pick it out. And ringing the base of each plant with fine wood ash or rice hulls makes the larvae reluctant to cross.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Stink bugs increase from August, overlapping with the pod-filling stage. The control method is the same as for soybean. In the cool early morning their movement is sluggish, so search under the pods, shake them by hand, and catch them in a bucket of water. Planting sesame, foxtail millet, or sorghum around the field edge to lure them in and catching them there in concentration also works. Because the adzuki bean self-pollinates, covering it with floating row cover or insect screen during flowering does not hurt fruit set, and on a small area this is the surest method.

### 9-3. Control Materials Without Chemicals and Cultural Control

| Material | How to Make | Target |
|---|---|---|
| Bordeaux mixture | Dissolve 400g copper sulfate and 400g quicklime separately in 100L water and combine | Fungal and bacterial diseases |
| Soapy water | 5g unscented soap per 1L water | Aphids, mites |
| Diluted milk | Dilute milk 5-10× with water | Powdery mildew |
| Wood ash water | Soak 1 doe of ash in 10L water overnight and use the water on top | Fungal suppression |
| Dusting with dry ash | Dust leaves and plant bases on a dewy morning | Leaf-eating insects, cutworms |
| Wood vinegar | Dilute 300-500× | Repellent |
| Hot pepper or garlic water | Grind, steep in water, strain, and use | Repellent |

| Cultural Control | Principle |
|---|---|
| 3-year or longer rotation | There is no defense against leaf-drop disease and viruses other than rotation |
| Raising the bed | More than half of adzuki bean diseases are related to excess moisture |
| Seed sorting | Anthracnose and the adzuki bean weevil travel with the seed |
| Keeping planting distance | With airflow, leaves dry quickly and fungus cannot take hold |
| Mulching | Keeps splashed soil from carrying pathogens onto lower leaves |
| No entering the field after rain | Brushing against wet leaves spreads pathogens |
| Burning diseased residue | Do not leave leaves infected with leaf-drop disease in the field |
| Clearing field banks | Leguminous weeds serve as an intermediate host for stink bugs and viruses |

> **Without chemicals, controlling pests and diseases in adzuki beans comes down to two things: draining water and rotating fields. These two decide most of what happens with adzuki bean disease.**
