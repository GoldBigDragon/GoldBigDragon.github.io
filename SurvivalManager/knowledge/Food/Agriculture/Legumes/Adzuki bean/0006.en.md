## 14. Toxicity and Antinutrients

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Adzuki beans are safe to eat once thoroughly cooked. They do not carry defensive compounds as strong as those in soybeans or kidney beans, so the risk is relatively low, but eating them raw or undercooked causes digestive trouble, because they contain the antinutrients common to legumes.

### 14-1. What the Antinutrients Are

| Compound | What it does | Symptoms | How to remove it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Trypsin inhibitor | Blocks the action of trypsin, a protein-digesting enzyme | Stomach pain, diarrhea, reduced protein utilization | Soak 6 hours, then heat at 100°C for 30-60 minutes |
| Lectin | Binds to the intestinal lining and interferes with absorption | Stomach pain, vomiting, diarrhea | Soak, then heat thoroughly in vigorously boiling water |
| Saponin | Foams and irritates the stomach and intestines | Upset stomach and diarrhea | Discard the first boiling water and skim off the foam |
| Phytic acid | Binds iron, zinc, and calcium and blocks their absorption | Anemia and zinc deficiency with long-term intake | Soaking, sprouting, and fermentation reduce it by 60-80% |
| Tannin | Gives an astringent taste and hinders iron absorption | Astringent taste | Soaking and discarding the first water |
| Oligosaccharides | Not digestible by humans, so gut bacteria ferment them | Bloating and gas | Discard the soaking water and the first boiling water |

> **Adzuki beans are especially high in saponin. The foam that rises while boiling is saponin, and discarding this first water is the first step of cooking adzuki beans. Skip it, and they turn astringent and cause stomach pain.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The saponin in adzuki beans has two faces. Eaten in excess, it irritates the stomach and intestines and causes diarrhea and stomach pain, but a moderate amount acts as an antioxidant and has a diuretic effect. The old saying that adzuki bean porridge eaten on the winter solstice reduces the body's swelling reflects this diuretic effect, learned through experience. Discarding the first water removes the risk of overconsumption while keeping the benefit of the saponin that remains.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The danger from lectin varies greatly by bean type. The lectin in adzuki beans is far less toxic than the lectin in kidney beans (Phaseolus). Eating just four or five raw kidney beans can bring on severe vomiting and diarrhea within hours, and food poisoning from undercooked kidney beans has been reported many times. Kidney beans in particular have a range in which prolonged heating at low temperature actually raises lectin activity, which makes cooking that leaves them in lukewarm water for a long time the most dangerous approach. Adzuki beans are not that extreme, but the same principle applies. Enough time in vigorously boiling water is the only safe method, and it also holds true here that undercooked beans can be more dangerous than raw ones.

### 14-2. Standards for Soaking and Heating

| Treatment | Time / temperature | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Soaking | 6 hours in room-temperature water | Lets heat penetrate to the core later. Some saponin and oligosaccharides leach into the water |
| Discarding the soaking water | Mandatory | Contains dissolved saponin, oligosaccharides, and tannin |
| Discarding the first boiling water | Boil 5-10 minutes, discard the water, then boil again in fresh water | The key step in cooking adzuki beans. The astringent taste and foam disappear |
| Main boiling | 40-60 minutes in vigorously boiling water | Trypsin inhibitor and lectin are inactivated |
| Pressure cooking | 120°C for 10-15 minutes | Uses the least fuel |
| Residual-heat cooking | Boil 15 minutes, then 2 hours in an insulated box | Cuts fuel use by more than half |
| Checking doneness | Crushes easily when pressed with a finger | A hard core remaining means it is undercooked |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Adzuki beans need shorter soaking and shorter heating than soybeans. Soybeans need 8 hours of soaking and more than an hour of heating, while adzuki beans need only 6 hours and 40 to 60 minutes, because their seed coat is thin and they carry almost no fat, so heat penetrates quickly. Where fuel is scarce, this is a real advantage.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;It is important not to skip the step of discarding the first boiling water. Soaking alone does not remove enough saponin. Boiling for 5 to 10 minutes brings up billowing foam, which is the saponin; discard that water and boil again in fresh water. Adzuki beans that skip this process taste astringent and leave the stomach uncomfortable afterward. It can feel wasteful when water is scarce, but the discarded water is not wasted if it is poured on the field or used for washing dishes.

### 14-3. Sprouting and Fermentation

| Method | Compounds reduced | Degree |
|---|---|---|
| Sprouting | Phytic acid, trypsin inhibitor, oligosaccharides | Phytic acid down 50-70%, inhibitor down 30-40% |
| Fermentation | Phytic acid, inhibitors, oligosaccharides | Mostly broken down, and absorption improves |
| Soaking and discarding the first water | Saponin, tannin, oligosaccharides | A substantial portion is removed |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Adzuki beans can also be sprouted, and vitamin C forms once they sprout, a useful trait when there are no fresh vegetables in winter. However, adzuki bean sprouts have not become part of South Korea's food culture, and their taste and texture fall short of mung bean sprouts or soybean sprouts. If the goal is a namul (seasoned vegetable side dish), mung beans or soybeans work better for sprouting, and sprouting adzuki beans is mainly used as a treatment to reduce antinutrients and ease digestion. Wrapping soaked adzuki beans in a damp cloth for about a day, until the sprouts just begin to show, then boiling them, makes them easier to digest and improves nutrient absorption compared to plain boiling.

## 15. Storage Without Refrigeration - Adzuki Bean Weevil

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Storability for adzuki beans is SS, but a condition applies: it holds only when weevils are kept out. With fat below 1%, they hardly go rancid, and dried adzuki beans show no odor even after three years. Mold is barely an issue either, as long as they are dried properly. There is really only one cause of failure in storing adzuki beans: the adzuki bean weevil.

> **Adzuki beans are not lost to rot; they are lost to being eaten by insects. The art of storing them is, in effect, the art of keeping out the adzuki bean weevil.**

### 15-1. Drying Comes First

| Stage | Method | Check |
|---|---|---|
| Preliminary drying | Spread whole plants on a straw mat for 4-6 days | Until the pods pop open when twisted |
| Drying after threshing | Spread thinly on a straw mat in the sun for 2-3 days | Turn often for even drying |
| Target moisture | 12% or below | Bites cleanly with a crack instead of denting under the teeth |
| Salt test | Shake adzuki beans together with roasted salt in a dry jar | If salt sticks to the jar wall, dry them further |
| Cooling | Cool completely before storing | Sealing them while still warm causes condensation |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The 12% target moisture for adzuki beans is a little lower than the 12-13% for soybeans. The reason is the weevil. Once moisture passes 13%, the adzuki bean weevil breeds noticeably faster. Dropping below 12% slows breeding, and below 10% it nearly stops. But drying too far, below 9%, cracks and splits the seed coat, and lowers germination if the beans are meant for seed. 11-12% is the balance point.

### 15-2. The Adzuki Bean Weevil's Life Cycle and How to Block It

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;To understand the adzuki bean weevil (Callosobruchus chinensis), you first need to know that it starts in the field. Adults lay eggs on pods still on the plant. The eggs are smaller than a millet grain and stick tightly to the seed coat, so they are hard to spot. Once the beans are harvested and brought into storage, those eggs hatch, and the larvae bore through the seed coat into the seed and grow by eating it from the inside. The beans look fine outside while hollowing out inside. Once fully grown, the larvae bore a round hole to emerge, mate, and lay eggs again. A single generation takes 25-30 days, so numbers grow exponentially.

| Point in time | What happens | Response |
|---|---|---|
| In the field | Adults lay eggs on the pods | Insect netting after flowering; do not delay harvest |
| Right after harvest | Eggs come into storage still stuck to the seed coat | Float in water and skim off eaten beans |
| Early storage | Eggs hatch and larvae bore through the seed coat | Full sealing, cold or heat treatment |
| One month into storage | Larvae grow inside the seed | Not visible from outside |
| Two months into storage | Adults bore out through a hole | First becomes visible at this stage |
| Six months into storage | The whole sack fills with shells and powder | Cannot be undone |

| Method | Principle | Application | Effect |
|---|---|---|---|
| Full sealing | Cuts off oxygen and suffocates them | Fill a glass jar or metal container completely and seal it | Most reliable. Kills even eggs and larvae in 3-4 weeks |
| Mixing in wood ash | Fine particles scratch the waxy exoskeleton and dehydrate the insect | Mix in 2-5% of the adzuki bean volume | Rinse off with water before eating. Can also be used for seed stock |
| Diatomaceous earth | The silica shells damage the exoskeleton | 1-3g per kg of adzuki beans | More effective than ash. Works only when dry |
| Oil coating | An oil film keeps eggs from sticking and hatching | Mix 2-5mL of cooking oil per kg of adzuki beans | Highly effective. Not used for seed stock |
| Cold treatment | Eggs and larvae die below freezing | Leave outside for a few days in midwinter, then bring in | Since harvest is in October, winter arrives soon after |
| Heat treatment | Eggs and larvae die at 60°C | Spread thinly in the sun for half a day | Not used for seed stock |
| Cold storage at 12°C or below | Breeding effectively stops | Cellar, north-facing room, underground storage pit | The ideal condition for storing adzuki beans |
| Dried chili peppers, Sichuan pepper, mugwort, garlic | Essential-oil compounds suppress egg-laying | Layer them in | A supplementary measure |
| Burying in ash | Combines sealing with physical control | Layer ash and adzuki beans in a jar | Widely used traditionally |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Full sealing matters most. Insects breathe too. Inside a sealed container, the adzuki beans and insects use up the oxygen, oxygen falls, carbon dioxide rises, and the insects die. The beans, being dormant, tolerate this condition. The fuller the container and the less empty space left, the faster this works, and it is better to pack several small containers tightly than one large one. If one container spoils, you do not lose everything.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Adzuki beans have a circumstance in their favor for cold treatment. Since they are harvested in October, midwinter arrives soon after drying and sorting are done. Leaving a sealed container outside for a few days to expose it to below-freezing temperatures kills the eggs and larvae. When bringing it back in, do not open the container right away; let it sit indoors for half a day to reach room temperature first. Opening a cold container immediately causes condensation inside, which seeds mold. This cold treatment largely replaces the fumigation used in conventional practice.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Use only the fine part of wood ash. Sift it to remove coarse charcoal pieces, then mix the fine part in at 2-5% of the adzuki bean volume so it coats every bean thinly. The red beans turn a dusty gray, but rinsing three or four times in water when you cook them brings back the original color. Insects cannot build resistance to it, it is harmless to people, and it can be used even on beans kept for seed — advantages that make it better than oil coating.

### 15-3. Storage Containers and Locations

| Container | Sealing | Duration | Notes |
|---|:---:|---|---|
| Glass jar | Very good | 3+ years | Lets you check condition by eye. Does not block light |
| Metal container | Very good | 3+ years | Fully blocks rats and insects. Watch for condensation |
| Earthenware jar | Good | 2-3 years | Slow temperature change; rats cannot get through |
| Thick plastic | Fair | 1 year | Rats and sometimes weevils can get through |
| Underground storage pit | Good | 2-3 years | Keeps temperature below 12°C. Losing everything if water seeps in |

| Storage condition | Target |
|---|---|
| Temperature | 12°C or below is ideal. Lower is better |
| Humidity | Air humidity 65% or below |
| Light | Fully blocked |
| Location | A cellar or north-facing room, on a stand off the ground |
| Inspection | Once a month. More often than for soybeans |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Adzuki beans need more frequent inspection than other legumes. Since one generation of the weevil takes just over a month, checking only once every two months can mean a generation has already passed. Once a month, shake the container, listen with your ear against it, and look at the surface for round holes. Finding even one hole means the infestation has already spread considerably, so take everything out, float it in water to skim off hollowed beans, dry it in the sun, mix in ash or oil, and reseal it.

## 16. Cooking and Processing

### 16-1. Cooking Adzuki Beans

| Dish | Procedure | Keeping | Value |
|---|---|---|---|
| Adzuki bean rice | Boil soaked beans, discard the first water, then cook with rice | Made fresh each time | Supplies the vitamin B1 and lysine rice lacks |
| Adzuki bean porridge | Boil beans until soft, mash them, and simmer with grain | A few days | Uses a lot of fuel but is easy to digest and calorie-dense |
| Adzuki bean topping | Use boiled beans, mashed or whole, on tteok (rice cake) | A few days | Eaten with tteok, it combines grain and legume |
| Sweet adzuki bean paste | Strain boiled beans, remove the skins, and reduce | A few days somewhere cool, months if dried | Keeps longer with a sweetener |
| Adzuki bean water/tea | Boil roasted beans in water | Made fresh each time | When you must boil water to drink anyway, this adds flavor and minerals |
| Dried adzuki bean powder | Boil, dry, and grind | 2-3 months sealed | Having no fat, it keeps well even as a powder |
| Fermented adzuki beans | Leave boiled beans somewhere warm for two or three days | A few days | Antinutrients decrease and digestion becomes easier |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The principle to remember when cooking adzuki beans is boiling in two stages. Boil the soaked beans for 5-10 minutes and discard the first water, then add fresh water and boil again for 40-60 minutes. Saponin, tannin, and oligosaccharides leach out into that first water, so this step removes the astringent taste and leaves the stomach settled. Failures with adzuki bean porridge or adzuki bean rice usually come from skipping this step.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;That adzuki bean powder keeps well is valuable under disaster conditions. Soybean powder is 16% fat and develops a rancid smell within months, but adzuki beans are below 1% fat, so even ground into powder they keep for a long time. Boiled, dried, and ground, the powder can be mixed with water into a porridge or stirred into tteok. It is a good form of nutrition relative to weight for situations where you need to move.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The sweetness in adzuki bean dishes does not come from the beans themselves. Sugar content is only 0.43g, next to nothing. All the sweetness in adzuki bean porridge and sweet adzuki bean paste comes from sugar or honey. Once sweeteners are gone, adzuki beans are eaten without sweetness, and in that state they taste nutty and plain but not sweet. Knowing this in advance keeps it from being a disappointment. If sweetness is needed, use sweet sorghum syrup, jocheong (malt-fermented Korean grain syrup), or a reduction of sweet potato or sugar beet.

### 16-2. Fuel-Saving Cooking

| Method | Fuel | Procedure |
|---|:---:|---|
| Pressure cooking | Least | Pressure-cook soaked beans for 10-15 minutes |
| Residual-heat cooking | Very little | After discarding the first water, boil 15 minutes, turn off the heat, wrap in a straw or rice-hull box or a blanket, and leave for 2 hours |
| A big pot all at once | Little | Boil several days' worth at once and eat it over time |
| Adzuki bean tea | Little | Boil roasted beans in water for a few minutes |
| Dried adzuki bean powder | Almost none | Boil, dry, and grind ahead, then mix with water to eat |
| Fermentation | Paid up front | Boil once and ferment, and later cooking uses less fuel |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There is a caution with residual-heat cooking. The temperature inside an insulated box stays around 80°C, which may not be enough to fully destroy lectin and trypsin inhibitor. So after discarding the first water, keep the initial boil in the main cooking stage at 15 minutes or more, and when you take the beans out, check that they crush easily with a finger. If a hard core remains, boil again.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Boiling several days' worth at once is especially efficient for adzuki beans. Once boiled, they keep for a few days, and longer still in winter. Filling the pot and boiling a full batch each time you light the fire, then portioning it out, saves a great deal of fuel. In summer, though, boiled adzuki beans spoil within two days, so boil only as much as will be eaten that same day.

## 17. Annual Work Calendar

| Month | Task | Key point |
|---|---|---|
| January | Check stored beans, test seed germination | Leave sealed containers outside for a few days to double as cold treatment |
| February | Plan the field, turn the compost | Check for a plot where a 3-year rotation break can be kept |
| March | Apply lime, finish the compost | Can be worked in earlier, not only 3-4 weeks before sowing |
| April | Manage the preceding crop, improve drainage | Mix sand, rice hulls, and leaf mold into clay soil |
| May | Prepare to harvest the preceding crop, secure inoculant soil | Set aside soil from a field that grew adzuki bean, mung bean, or cowpea |
| June | Harvest the preceding crop, cut drainage channels, raise beds, sow (mid to late month) | Raise beds to 25-30cm. This is the whole answer to waterlogging damage |
| July | First and second hilling, weeding, drainage during the monsoon (jangma, late June to July in South Korea) | Do not enter the field right after rain |
| August | Third hilling, mulching, flowering, check root nodules, watch for stink bugs | Finish hilling before flowering |
| September | Pod filling, watering, catching and killing stink bugs, mark plants for seed | Tie string on plants that ripen evenly |
| October | Harvest, dry, thresh, sort, return stalks and leaves to the soil | Harvest all at once when 70-80% of pods have ripened |
| November | Finish drying, sort by floating in water, treat against insects, store | Remove hollowed beans and seal |
| December | Cold treatment, check storage, winter-solstice adzuki bean porridge | Use midwinter cold to control the adzuki bean weevil |

### 17-1. Five Things Not to Forget

| Item | Details |
|---|---|
| Raised beds | Adzuki beans are the legume most vulnerable to waterlogging. Growing on flat rows cuts yield by 34% |
| 3-year rotation break | Leaf-drop disease and viruses cannot be cured with chemicals; only time off the field solves it |
| Harvest timing | Harvest all at once when 70-80% of pods have ripened. Waiting too long causes shattering and a 30% loss |
| Adzuki bean weevil | The SS storability rating holds only when weevils are kept out. Sealing and cold are the whole answer |
| Discarding the first water | Boil 5-10 minutes, discard the first water, and boil again in fresh water. This removes the saponin |
