## Introduction

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The adzuki bean (Vigna angularis) came from East Asia. It has been grown for thousands of years in China, the Korean Peninsula, and Japan, and its place in Korean food has more to do with meaning than with calories. Patjuk (red bean porridge) on the winter solstice, siru-tteok (steamed rice cake) in October, the red bean coating at feasts and ancestral rites. Behind this lies the idea that the color red wards off misfortune, but the real reason was probably a bit more practical. Adzuki beans were a side dish that added protein when grain ran short, and a stockpiled food that was easy to keep.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Under disaster conditions, the adzuki bean's standing is ambiguous. Its overall grade is C, a score of 40.6, placing it in the lower-middle of this crop table. Its short-term capability is D, and its long-term capability is also C. The reason is clear. Calories per area are D: 660kcal at 1m², only 25% of the self-sufficiency line — lower even than soybean. Calories are low, yield is small, labor demand is high, and continuous-cropping damage is severe. Where fields are small and people are scarce, assigning a large area to adzuki beans is not a rational choice.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;However, its supplementary-food grade is SS, a score of 61.8. As a side-dish resource, it is at the very top. Protein density is 25% of calories, dietary fiber exceeds 15g per 100g — more than three times that of brown rice — and vitamin B1 is 0.66mg, among the highest of any grain or legume. This last item matters more than it seems. On a diet centered on white rice, a shortage of vitamin B1 brings on beriberi, a disease that numbs the legs, drains strength, and weakens the heart, and one that actually was common in East Asia in the early 20th century. The old custom of mixing adzuki beans into rice kept that disease at bay.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;If the adzuki bean's strength had to be summed up in one line, it is that it makes a side dish with little water in a short time. Total evapotranspiration is about 400mm, low among legumes, and it takes only 100 to 120 days from sowing to harvest, which suits it well as a follow-on crop after potatoes or garlic. Dried adzuki beans keep for 3 years at room temperature as long as insects are kept out. Because it self-pollinates, seed can be saved and carried forward year after year, and seed can be obtained anywhere across South Korea.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;It has three clear weaknesses. First, it is extremely vulnerable to waterlogging. It cannot grow at all in poorly drained soil, and if planted on flat ridges instead of raised beds, yield drops by 34%. It is hardly an overstatement to call it the legume least tolerant of water of them all.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Second, continuous-cropping damage is severe. The field must rest for 3 years; if adzuki beans are planted again right away, leaf-drop disease and viruses build up in the soil and yield collapses.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Third, damage from the adzuki bean weevil during storage is significant. The SS storability grade holds only once the weevil has been kept out; if it is not, the whole stock can be lost within half a year. This document places particular weight on how it handles these three weaknesses.
