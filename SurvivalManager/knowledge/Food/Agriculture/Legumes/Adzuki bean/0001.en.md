## Introduction
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Adzuki bean (Vigna angularis) is an annual legume native to East Asia. Its overall grade is C (40.6 points, 110th of 183 valid species), with short term D (38.9), long term C (42.3), and supplementary food SS (61.8 points); it cannot be a staple but is an excellent side-dish crop. Calories per area are D, at 660 kcal per ㎡, only 25% of the self-sufficiency line, so it is not suited to a large acreage. In return, protein density is 25% of calories, dietary fiber exceeds 15 g per 100 g, three times brown rice, and 0.66 mg of vitamin B1 guards against beriberi in a diet centered on white rice. It uses little water, with total evapotranspiration of about 400 mm, is harvested in 100-120 days so it can follow garlic or potatoes, and dried adzuki keeps 3 years at room temperature if insects are kept out. It has three weaknesses: it is extremely weak against waterlogging, with flat rows yielding 34% less than raised beds; continuous-cropping injury requires a 3-year rest; and during storage the adzuki bean weevil can ruin everything within half a year.

## 2. Plant Overview
| Item | Content |
|:---|:---|
| Classification | Annual of the genus Vigna in the legume family. Being in the same genus as mung bean and cowpea, its pests, diseases, and nodule bacteria are closer to theirs than to soybean's. Native from north-central China to the Korean Peninsula, and its wild ancestor, wild adzuki, grows on well-drained field banks |
| Leaves, stem | Leaves are trifoliate and close to heart-shaped. The stem stands upright at 30-50 cm and needs no stakes; climbing types exceed 1 m. The pods hang close to the ground and easily pick up moisture |
| Roots, flowers | A straight taproot with spreading fine roots, on which the nodules form. Flowers are yellow and butterfly-shaped and self-pollinating (soybean flowers are white or pale purple, which tells them apart) |
| Pods, seeds | Slender cylindrical pods with 6-10 seeds each. Red is the basic seed color, but black, white, and mottled adzuki also exist |

| Stage | Period | Key points |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Germination, seedling stage | 3 weeks after sowing | Germination, early growth, and the start of nodule formation |
| Flowering | Weeks 6-8 | Yellow flowers open in turn from the bottom up over 3-4 weeks |
| Pod filling | Weeks 8-10 | Pods and seeds enlarge |
| Maturity, harvest | Weeks 10-14 | Pods ripen to brown starting from the bottom |

> **The seedling stage is the most vulnerable to waterlogging. If the rainy season overlaps right after sowing, young adzuki roots are damaged and the plants simply die. In years when sowing in mid-to-late June coincides with the rains, bed height decides survival.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Per 100 g of dried adzuki: 330-356 kcal, protein 20.63 g (25.0% of calories), carbohydrate about 60 g, fat 0.5-1 g, dietary fiber 14.9-16.9 g, vitamin B1 0.66 mg (ten times white rice, over half the adult daily requirement), and sugars 0.43 g. Anthocyanin is in the seed coat and saponin throughout. With fat under 1% it does not go rancid and keeps well even as flour, and its sweetness comes not from the bean but from sugar or honey.

## 3. Growing Environment and Varieties
| Condition | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Temperature | Optimum germination 32-34℃ (the highest among legumes); below 15℃ germination is slow and seed rots, so soil temperature at sowing should be 15℃ or above (20℃ or above is better). Growth 15-35℃ (optimum 20-24℃), seed set 18-32℃, killed by frost |
| Light | 6 hours or more a day (8 hours or more is optimum); under 40% shading yield halves. Being a short-day plant, late sowing makes it flower at a small size |
| Water, soil | Well-drained sandy loam or loam, pH 5.5-7.0 (optimum 6.0-6.5; in acid soil nodules decrease). Seasonal evapotranspiration about 400 mm. Recovery is difficult after more than a day under water |
| Beds, drainage ditches | Bed height 20 cm or more (25-30 cm optimum), drainage ditch depth 30 cm or more. Flat-row cultivation yields 34% less, so beds come before fertilizer |

| Type | Characteristics |
|:---|:---|
| Upright type (common adzuki) | 30-50 cm tall, needs no stakes, resists lodging; the basic choice. Jungwon (nationwide), Chungju (central region) |
| Climbing type (vine adzuki) | Vines exceed 1 m and need stakes; high yield. Chilbo. When stake material and labor are plentiful |
| Early type, wet-tolerant | Ye is early with a short growing period, for when sowing slips into July. Arari, Hongda, Geomguseul, and Yeondoochae are strongly wet-tolerant, for poorly drained fields or converted paddies |

## 4. Field Preparation and Sowing
| Item | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Drainage | Build beds and drainage ditches first, and improve drainage in clay fields by mixing in sand, rice hulls, and leaf mold |
| Fertilization (1,000㎡) | Nitrogen 2-4 kg (a minimum for the early period before the nodules work), phosphorus 4-6 kg (more than for soybean), potassium 4-6 kg (all as basal fertilizer), fully rotted compost 1,000 kg, and in acid soil lime 150-200 kg (3-4 weeks before sowing) |
| Substitute resources | Wood ash (100-200 g per ㎡; too much raises pH), matured compost (1 kg per ㎡), urine diluted 10 times (a little), bone meal and roasted fish bone (phosphorus), shell and eggshell (in place of lime) |
| Nodule inoculation | Coat seed lightly moistened with water with topsoil 5-15 cm deep from a field that grew adzuki, mung bean, or cowpea, and sow within 30 minutes in shade (drying in sun kills the bacteria). Nodules from the roots of wild adzuki also suit it. It fails with pH 5.5 or below, excess nitrogen fertilizer, standing water, or too little phosphorus |

| Check | Method |
|:---|:---|
| Nodule check | Around flowering (5-7 weeks after sowing), lift two or three healthy plants with soil using a spade, wash them, and split the nodules. With 15 or more nodules per plant and red or pink inside, it is fixing nitrogen. If white or pale green, check acidity, waterlogging, and phosphorus deficiency; with 5 or fewer, inoculation failed, and if leaves are yellow give a little nitrogen |

> **Legumes improve the soil not because they fix nitrogen, but because they leave part of the fixed nitrogen in the stems and leaves in the field. If those stems and leaves are carried away, adzuki becomes a crop that plunders the soil.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;So return the stalks, leaves, and pod husks to the field cut into 20-30 cm lengths, do not burn them (the nitrogen escapes), and do not dig up the roots. Only diseased residue is burned or rotted outside the field.

| Sowing | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Timing | Central-north: mid to late June (limit early July); south: mid June to early July (limit mid July). Sowing right after a rain spell has passed means young plants take less rain. The later it is, the fewer the pods, and late flowers cannot ripen before frost |
| Seed preparation | Discard seeds with spots, discoloration, wrinkles, or insect holes (weevil holes are small and round and look like white dots), and skim off beans that float in water. Soaking in 65℃ water for 5 minutes reduces seed-borne disease, but exceeding the time damages germination; inoculate only after it has cooled completely |
| Method | Depth 3-5 cm, 2-3 seeds per hill (thin later, leaving 2 plants). Single-row planting has row spacing 60-70 cm and plant spacing 10-15 cm; double-row planting is two rows 40 cm apart on a 120 cm bed. Seeding rate is 3-4 kg per 1,000㎡, raised to 5-7 kg when late, planted more densely |
| Birds | Birds that peck cotyledons right after sowing kill the plants, so cover with straw, netting, or old insect screen until germination |

## 5. Water Management and Cultivation Management
| Period | Water management |
|:---|:---|
| Seedling stage | Relatively dry (the most vulnerable to waterlogging) |
| Rainy season | Drainage, not irrigation. Dig ditches and drain standing water |
| Flowering to pod filling | Irrigate plentifully if drought comes |
| Maturity | Stop irrigating (if too wet, pods do not dry and mold forms) |

> **In adzuki cultivation, water management usually means not giving water but removing it. Raising the beds and digging furrows come before irrigation.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;If leaves still droop in the morning and the soil is wet, the roots are damaged by excess water, so dig furrows at once and drain it, and never add more water.

| Management | Method |
|:---|:---|
| Hilling | Three times. Pile soil up to the cotyledon node at 2-3 true leaves, to the unifoliate node at 4-5, and to the first trifoliate node at 6-7, finishing before flowering. Entering the field after flowering and turning soil makes flowers drop and cuts fine roots, lowering yield |
| Weeding | Scrape shallowly 2-3 weeks after sowing (together with the 1st hilling) and 4-5 weeks (together with the 2nd), and finish all weeding before flowering. No herbicide is registered for adzuki |
| Mulching | Right after the 3rd hilling, cover 5-10 cm thick with straw, rice hulls, fallen leaves, or cut grass (without it yield falls 24%, and covering right after sowing keeps sprouts from rising). It blocks weeds, evaporation, and soil splash together |

## 6. Pests, Diseases, and Nutrient Problems
| Pest or disease | Symptoms and conditions | Control without chemicals |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Virus disease | Leaves mottled in a mosaic and growth stops. Spread by aphids | Cannot be treated. Pull and burn on discovery and reduce aphids |
| Leaf-fall disease | Browning from the lower leaves and heavy leaf drop. Fungus accumulates in soil of fields cropped continuously | Rotation of 3 years or more is the only measure. Burn diseased residue |
| Brown spot, anthracnose, powdery mildew | Brown blotches or dark brown lesions on leaves (humid August-September) / white powder (dry, poor airflow) | Secure airflow, remove diseased leaves, Bordeaux mixture, seed selection / milk diluted 5-10 times |
| Root rot | Roots turn brown and lower leaves yellow (excess water, continuous cropping) | Drainage, raising beds, rotation |
| Stink bugs | From August, pierce pods and make empty pods | Hand-pick in early morning, trap crop around the field, insect netting at flowering (no harm to seed set since it self-pollinates) |
| Cutworms, aphids | Cut young stems at the base / suck sap and carry viruses | Dig up the soil around fallen plants to catch larvae and ring the base with ash / soapy water (5 g of soap per 1 L of water) |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Bordeaux mixture is made by dissolving 400 g of copper sulfate and 400 g of quicklime separately in 100 L of water and mixing. Cultural controls are raising beds (over half of adzuki's diseases are tied to excess water), plant spacing, mulching, staying out of the field after rain, burning diseased residue, and tidying field banks (legume weeds are intermediate hosts for stink bugs and viruses).

> **Without chemicals, the heart of adzuki control comes down to two things: draining the water and rotating the field. These two decide most adzuki disease.**

| Symptom | Cause and remedy |
|:---|:---|
| Whole leaf pale yellow and plant small | Nitrogen deficiency (poor nodules are the most common cause). Dig up roots and check the nodules; this season give urine diluted 10 times in small amounts |
| Edges brown, dry, and curled | Potassium deficiency. Scatter wood ash between plants |
| Slow growth and purplish leaves | Phosphorus deficiency. Bone meal, roasted fish bone, wood ash |
| Only leaves lush and dark green with few pods | Nitrogen excess. Stop nitrogen |

## 7. Intercropping and Crop Rotation
| Compatibility | Companions |
|:---|:---|
| Good | Maize, sorghum, foxtail millet (intercropped, they share the nitrogen adzuki fixed), marigold (suppresses nematodes). Garlic, onion, barley, and potato are preceding crops, with adzuki planted where they were harvested in June |
| Poor | Tomato, pepper, eggplant (shared pests and diseases), legumes such as soybean, mung bean, cowpea, and peanut (the same pathogens and nematodes accumulate), and adzuki after adzuki (leaf-fall disease and viruses) |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The rest period is 3 years or more (ideal cycle 4 years), because leaf-fall disease, viruses, root rot, and nematodes build up in the soil. Planting soybean or mung bean after adzuki also counts as continuous cropping, so other legumes are included in the rest period. An example 4-year rotation is garlic or onion then adzuki → maize, sorghum, foxtail millet → Chinese cabbage, cabbage, radish → potato or sweet potato → adzuki.

> **Adzuki's continuous-cropping injury comes from leaf-fall disease fungus and viruses built up in the soil. It cannot be cured with chemicals; only time with the field empty solves it. To harvest adzuki every year, you need four plots of field.**

## 8. Harvest and Seed Saving
| Harvest | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Timing | Harvest all at once when 70-80% of pods have ripened brown (50-55 days after flowering; central-north early to mid October, south mid to late October). A bean bitten splits crisply, the seed coat is deep red, and lower leaves yellow and fall |
| Method | On a clear, dry day after the dew has dried, after 10 a.m., cut the stalks at the base with a sickle (leave the roots). Move onto a mat or cloth (dragging bursts pods) and dry for 4-6 days, and thresh when pods snap open on twisting |
| Threshing | Half fill a sack with dry stalks and stamp on it, or flail it; striking against the inner wall of a tub gathers the beans in the tub. For a small amount of seed, shell by hand |
| Winnowing | On a windy day pour from a winnowing basket or bowl to blow off chaff. Skim off floating empty beans and weevil-eaten beans in water (dry the rescued beans again the same day) |

> **When adzuki's ripe pods dry they burst on their own. This is called shattering, and harvesting late can lose up to 30%. When the lower pods begin to snap open on a hand twist, do not wait any longer.**

| Seed saving | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Selection, harvest | Mark in advance plants with no disease symptoms, many pods, and even ripening. Harvest a little later than for food, after pods are completely brown, and shell by hand |
| Sorting, drying | Discard beans with pale or mottled seed coats, wrinkles, or weevil holes. Moisture 12% or below |
| Storage | Keep in a sealed container in a cool, dark place below 12 degrees. Germination is 90% or more in the harvest year, 85-90% after 1 year, 70-85% after 2, 50-70% after 3, and drops sharply from year 4. Repeated self-saved seed accumulates seed-borne disease |

## 9. Storage
| Step | Method |
|:---|:---|
| Drying | 4-6 days on the stalk, then spread thin and sun-dry for 2-3 days after threshing to moisture of 12% or below (a bite does not dent it and it splits crisply). Shake the beans in a dry bottle with roasted salt; if the salt sticks to the wall, dry more; cool before packing |
| Full sealing | Fill a glass jar or metal can completely and seal, and the oxygen is cut off, killing even eggs and larvae in 3-4 weeks. Pack several small containers full rather than one big one so that one spoiling does not lose everything |
| Wood ash, diatomaceous earth, oil | Mix fine wood ash at 2-5% of the bean volume (usable for seed; rinse before eating). Diatomaceous earth 1-3 g per 1 kg of beans. Rubbing in 2-5 mL of cooking oil is very effective but not used for seed |
| Cold, heat | Put a sealed container outside for a few days in midwinter, then without opening it bring it indoors for half a day to match the temperature before opening (to prevent condensation). Storage below 12 degrees practically stops reproduction |

> **Adzuki is lost not by rotting but by being eaten by insects. The art of storage is the art of keeping out the adzuki bean weevil.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The adzuki bean weevil lays eggs on pods in the field, and the eggs come into storage still stuck to the seed coat; for the first month it cannot be seen from outside, in the second month adults emerge, and by 6 months the whole sack is filled with husks and powder. Containers last 3 years or more for glass jars and metal cans, 2-3 years for clay jars and underground storage pits (a pit loses everything if water seeps in), and 1 year for thick plastic. Keep it below 12 degrees, at air humidity of 65% or less, shielded from light and on a stand, and check once a month.

## 10. Cooking and Precautions
| Component | What it does | How to remove |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Trypsin inhibitors, lectins | Block protein digestion and cause abdominal pain, vomiting, and diarrhea. Adzuki lectin is weaker than kidney bean's | Soak 6 hours, then heat thoroughly in vigorously boiling water |
| Saponin | Foams and irritates the stomach, causing discomfort and diarrhea. A moderate amount is antioxidant and diuretic | Discard the first cooking water and skim off the foam |
| Phytic acid, tannin, oligosaccharides | Hinder iron, zinc, and calcium absorption / astringency / gas | Soaking, sprouting, and fermenting reduce phytic acid; discard the soaking water and the first cooking water |

> **Adzuki is especially rich in saponin. The foam that comes up when boiling is saponin, and discarding this first water is the first step of cooking adzuki. If not discarded, it is astringent and gives stomachaches.**

| Treatment | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Order | Soak in room-temperature water for 6 hours and discard the soaking water. Boil 5-10 minutes, discard the first water, and cook in new vigorously boiling water for 40-60 minutes. It is done when it mashes easily under a finger |
| Saving fuel | Pressure cooking is 10-15 minutes at 120℃. Retained-heat cooking is boiling for 15 minutes, then 2 hours in an insulated box (the box stays around 80 degrees, so secure at least 15 minutes of first boiling and reboil if a hard core remains). Cook several days' portions at once |
| Sprouting, fermenting | Sprouting cuts phytic acid 50-70% and inhibitors 30-40% and produces vitamin C. Fermenting cooked adzuki for two or three days makes it easier to digest |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Adzuki rice supplies the vitamin B1 and lysine that rice lacks. Dried adzuki flour, made by cooking, drying, and grinding, has no fat and keeps 2-3 months sealed. When sweetness is needed, use sorghum syrup or malt syrup together.

## 11. Annual Work Schedule
| Month | Work |
|:---|:---|
| Jan-Feb | Check stored adzuki, test seed germination, cold treatment. In February, plan the field, turn the compost, and confirm the plots that can keep the 3-year rest |
| Mar-Apr | In March, spread lime and finish the compost. In April, manage the preceding crop and improve drainage by mixing sand, rice hulls, and leaf mold into clay fields |
| May-June | In May, prepare to harvest the preceding crop and secure inoculant soil. In June, harvest the preceding crop, dig drainage ditches, raise beds to 25-30 cm, and sow in mid to late June |
| July-Aug | In July, 1st and 2nd hilling, weeding, and rainy-season drainage (stay out of the field right after rain). In August, finish the 3rd hilling before flowering, then mulch, check nodules, and watch for stink bugs |
| Sep-Oct | In September, irrigate, hand-pick stink bugs, and mark seed plants. In October, harvest all at once when 70-80% of pods have ripened, then dry, thresh, winnow, and return the stalks and leaves |
| Nov-Dec | In November, finish drying, sort by floating in water, treat against insects, and store. In December, cold treatment, check storage, and midwinter adzuki porridge |

> **Adzuki cannot be a staple, but once waterlogging, continuous cropping, and weevils are overcome, it is a side-dish crop that with little water and a short season supplies the vitamin B1 that grains lack.**
