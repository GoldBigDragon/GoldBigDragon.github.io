## 10. Nutrient Deficiency and Excess

| Symptom | Cause | Natural-Material Response |
|---|---|---|
| Whole leaf pale yellow, plant small | Nitrogen deficiency. Poor nodulation is the most common cause in adzuki beans | First dig up roots and split a nodule to check. If there are no nodules, inoculate reliably the following year, and this year add a small amount of urine diluted 10× |
| Leaf edges dry brown and curl inward | Potassium deficiency | Scatter wood ash between plants and scratch it lightly into the soil |
| Growth slow, leaves tinged purple | Phosphorus deficiency | Bone meal, roasted and ground fish bones, wood ash. Adzuki beans have a large phosphorus demand |
| Flowers open but pods fail to set | Boron deficiency, or heat and drought during flowering | Compost made from varied materials. For the current year, lower temperature with irrigation and mulch |
| Veins green but the tissue between them yellow | Iron or magnesium deficiency. Soil tipped toward alkaline | Stop using ash. Increase organic matter with leaf mold and fully rotted compost |
| New leaves small and misshapen, growing point dies | Calcium or boron deficiency | Roasted, ground eggshells; ground shellfish shells |
| Leaves lush and dark green, but few pods | Nitrogen excess | Stop supplying nitrogen |
| Plant overgrown tall, lower pods fail to ripen | Nitrogen excess or overcrowding | Reduce fertilizer rate and planting density the following year |
| Lower leaves yellow, roots rotted brown | Excess moisture. Not a fertilizer problem | Clear the furrows immediately to drain water |
| Brown blotches spread on lower leaves and they drop in large numbers | Leaf-drop disease. Not a nutrient problem | Remove diseased leaves and rotate 3 years or more |
| Many pods but seeds small and shriveled | Water or potassium shortage during pod-filling | Irrigate; wood ash |
| Seed coat red is pale and blotchy | Incomplete ripening | Sow earlier. Pigment builds up in the later part of ripening |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Yellowing leaves in adzuki beans can have as many as four causes: nitrogen deficiency, excess moisture, leaf-drop disease, and ordinary senescence at ripening. Because the response to each is completely different, telling them apart matters. The way to tell them apart is to combine the root check covered in Section 5 with the soil check from Section 4. Dig up the roots: no nodules means nitrogen deficiency; nodules present but roots rotted brown means excess moisture; roots intact but brown blotches spreading on lower leaves means leaf-drop disease. If it is the ripening stage, nothing needs to be done at all.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Phosphorus deficiency deserves particular attention. The adzuki bean's fertilizer standard calls for 4 to 6kg of phosphorus, more than soybean, because its nodules are smaller and fewer, so phosphorus has to prop up nitrogen-fixation efficiency. In a phosphorus-poor field, even nodules that do form fail to turn red inside, and nitrogen-deficiency symptoms follow as a result. In this case the answer is to give phosphorus, not nitrogen. Without chemical fertilizer, the realistic sources of phosphorus are roasted, ground bone meal and fish bones, and wood ash.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Trace-element deficiencies are hard to address one by one. Without chemical fertilizer, there is essentially no way to obtain molybdenum or cobalt. Instead, compost made by rotting together vegetable scraps, fruit peels, eggshells, fish bones, fallen leaves, weeds, and ash contains a broad spread of trace elements. The practical strategy is the principle of putting everything into the compost pile.

## 11. Intercropping and Crop Rotation - Continuous-Cropping Damage

### 11-1. Compatibility

| Compatibility | Crop | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Good | Maize, sorghum, foxtail millet | Intercropping is possible. Neighboring plants share in the nitrogen the adzuki bean fixes |
| Good | Garlic, onion, barley (preceding crop) | Adzuki beans are sown where these are harvested in June. Fits exactly with the adzuki bean's sowing time |
| Good | Potato (preceding crop) | Adzuki beans are sown where potatoes are harvested in late June |
| Good | Marigold | Root exudates suppress nematodes |
| Neutral | Napa cabbage, cabbage, and related crops | Planted the year after adzuki beans, they use the leftover nitrogen |
| Bad | Tomato, chili pepper, eggplant | Share pests and diseases |
| Bad | Soybean, mung bean, cowpea, peanut, and other legumes | The same pathogens and nematodes build up |
| Bad | Continuous adzuki bean cropping | Leaf-drop disease and viruses build up in the soil |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Its relationship with preceding crops is the adzuki bean's greatest advantage. Garlic, onion, barley, and potato are all harvested in June, and the adzuki bean's optimal sowing time is mid-to-late June. The timing fits exactly, giving two harvests from one field in a year. What's more, these preceding crops share almost no pests or diseases with adzuki beans, since garlic and onion belong to the allium group, barley to the grass family, and potato to the nightshade family — all distant from the adzuki bean's lineage. There is an observation, though, that exudates from garlic and onion suppress rhizobia, so when adzuki beans follow them, inoculation should be done with particular care.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Intercropping with maize needs caution. Adzuki bean yield drops by half at 40% shade, and at only 30-50cm tall it falls completely into maize's shadow — worse off than soybean in this respect. If intercropping anyway, space maize rows generously apart running north-south and fit only about two rows of adzuki beans between them; if the field allows it, keeping them separate is better.

### 11-2. Continuous-Cropping Damage and Rotation

| Item | Standard |
|---|---|
| Break period | 3 years or more. Long even among legumes |
| Ideal cycle | 4 years |
| Cause of continuous-cropping damage | Leaf-drop disease, viruses, root rot, and nematodes build up in the soil |
| Relationship with other legumes | Planting soybean or mung bean after adzuki beans still counts as continuous cropping |

> **The adzuki bean's continuous-cropping damage comes from leaf-drop fungus and viruses that build up in the soil. It cannot be cured with chemicals — only time with the field left empty solves it. Harvesting adzuki beans every year requires four sections of field.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Let's pin down what the 3-year break condition actually means in practice. Split a field into four sections and plant adzuki beans in only one each year: each section rests 3 years and receives adzuki beans again in the 4th year. This way, adzuki beans can be harvested every year, but the area assigned to them is fixed at 25% of the total. Given that the adzuki bean's calories per area is D, this is an acceptable share — if anything, giving adzuki beans 25% of the field may be too much.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;One thing to watch is that other legumes count within the break period too. Planting soybean, mung bean, or cowpea the year after adzuki beans, from the point of view of soil pathogens and nematodes, is still continuous cropping. Mung bean and cowpea in particular, being in the same genus, share nearly identical diseases and should be avoided most of all. As a rule, legumes should be managed together as one group within the rotation.

| Sample 4-Year Rotation |
|---|
| Year 1: Adzuki beans after garlic/onion (preceding crop) |
| Year 2: Grass-family grains such as maize, sorghum, foxtail millet |
| Year 3: Leafy vegetables and root vegetables such as napa cabbage, cabbage, radish |
| Year 4: Potato or sweet potato |
| Back to adzuki beans |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;If the field is too small to split into four sections, there are two compromises. First, do not plant adzuki beans every year — plant only once every 2 or 3 years. Harvest generously in the year they are grown and store the surplus; since adzuki beans keep for 3 years, there is no need to plant every year, and the SS storability grade backs up this strategy. Second, shift the row positions within the field: plant maize this year where adzuki beans stood last year, and adzuki beans this year where maize stood last year. This is not full rotation, but it slows pathogen buildup considerably.

## 12. Harvest and Threshing

### 12-1. Judging Harvest Time

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Because adzuki bean flowers keep opening from the bottom of the stem upward as it grows, ripening spreads out over time. When the lower pods have turned fully brown, the upper part of the plant can still be carrying green pods. This makes judging harvest time trickier than for other legumes.

| Criterion | Details |
|---|---|
| Pods | Harvest all at once when 70-80% have turned brown |
| Days since flowering | 50-55 days after flowering |
| Seed | Bite it and your teeth do not sink in — it snaps apart. Seed coat is a deep red |
| Leaves | Lower leaves turn yellow and drop |
| Timing (central/northern South Korea) | Early-to-mid October |
| Timing (southern South Korea) | Mid-to-late October |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The standard of harvesting at 70-80% ripe is the result of a trade-off. Wait longer and the lower pods split open, dropping seed on the ground; harvest earlier and the upper pods' seeds are underfilled and shriveled. The point where these two losses combine to the smallest sum is 70-80%. Picking ripe pods in several passes is also possible, but takes several times the labor, and under disaster conditions harvesting all at once is usually the more sensible choice.

> **When a ripe adzuki bean pod dries, it splits open on its own. This is called shattering, and a delayed harvest can lose up to 30% of the crop to it. Once the lower pods start to pop open when twisted by hand, do not wait any longer.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;For small-scale self-sufficiency, picking in stages is worth considering. Hand-pick only the ripe pods, come back a week later and pick again, and after about three rounds, losses are nearly zero and total yield goes up too. Adzuki bean pods are long and easy to spot, so picking them is not difficult. Where there is labor to spare and the area is small, this method brings in the most.

### 12-2. Harvest Technique

| Step | Details |
|---|---|
| 1 | Harvest on a clear, dry day, after 10 a.m. once the dew has dried |
| 2 | Cut the stem base with a sickle. Leave the roots in the ground to keep the nodules' nitrogen there |
| 3 | Move it onto a mat or cloth. Dragging it across the field pops open ripe pods and drops the seed |
| 4 | Spread and dry for 4-6 days. Bring it in right away if it rains |
| 5 | Thresh once the pods are bone-dry and pop open when twisted |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The moment of greatest loss in harvesting adzuki beans is cutting and carrying them. Ripe pods are already dry, so brushing against them or shaking them pops them open. It is best to spread a mat or old cloth in the field beforehand and lay the cut plants straight onto it; dragging them out after cutting is effectively scattering seed along the way. Carrying them in sacks also works — any seed that pops open inside the sack simply stays there, so nothing is lost.

### 12-3. Threshing - Methods Without Tools

| Method | Tool | Procedure | Efficiency |
|---|---|---|---|
| Treading in a sack | Thick burlap sack | Fill a sack halfway with dry adzuki bean stalks, tie the opening, and tread on it. Tread about ten times, shake to shift the position, and repeat | The easiest method for small amounts, with almost no loss |
| Flail | A tool with a striking rod hung from a long handle | Spread the crop thin on a mat and strike down on it. Turn it over two or three times | Suited to medium quantities |
| Beating with a stick | Wooden rod | Put it in a sack or spread on a mat and beat it | Used in place of a flail |
| Rubbing with feet | None | Spread on a mat and grind with a shod foot | Suited to small amounts |
| Striking against a board | A board or a bin | Hold a bundle of stalks and strike it against the inside wall of a bin | Seeds collect inside the bin, so loss is low |
| Shelling by hand | None | Press pods open by hand | Used for small amounts kept for seed. The seed is not damaged at all |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Because adzuki bean seeds are small and hard, threshing is easier than for soybean: pods open readily and the seeds do not crack. But being small, the seeds also scatter easily in every direction, so working inside a sack has an advantage. Fill a thick burlap sack halfway with dry stalks, tie the opening firmly, and tread on it — the pods open and the seeds collect at the bottom of the sack. It is done once the stalks are breaking into short pieces.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Success in threshing depends on drying. No amount of beating opens the pods on stalks that are not dry enough. The right time is when a dry pod pops open at a twist of the hand. Doing it on a sunny afternoon, when the stalks are at their driest, makes the work far easier.

### 12-4. Cleaning - Sorting with Wind

| Method | Principle | Procedure |
|---|---|---|
| Winnowing | Seeds are heavy; husks and debris are light | On a windy day, hold a winnowing basket or bowl high and let a thin stream pour down. The seeds drop straight down while the chaff blows away |
| Basket tossing | Shake up and down while tossing forward | The heavier seeds stay at the back |
| Fanning | One person pours while another fans a breeze | Used on a windless day |
| Sieving | Separates by hole size | A coarse sieve removes stalk fragments, a fine sieve removes soil and small seed |
| Floating in water | Empty husks and weevil-eaten seed are light | Pour into a bucket of water and skim off anything that floats. Dry the recovered beans again the same day |
| Rolling down an inclined board | Round seeds roll well; debris catches | Set a board on a slant and roll the mixture down it |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There are three tricks to winnowing. Choose a time and spot with a steady breeze. Pour in a thin stream rather than all at once. Repeat two or three times. Because adzuki bean seeds are small and light, they blow away more easily than soybean's, so avoid days with strong wind and pour from a lower height.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Floating seed in water is especially good. Since weevil-eaten seed is hollow inside and floats, sorting this way substantially cuts storage losses. Floating the dried beans once right after harvest, skimming off what rises, and drying them right away before storage reduces from the start how many weevils enter the storehouse. Beans that have been soaked, though, must be spread thin and dried completely again the same day; the seed coat releases a little red color, but the nutrient loss is not significant. Do not use this method for seed stock — soaking starts the germination process, and drying it in that state reduces germination ability.

## 13. Seed Saving

| Item | Method |
|---|---|
| Selecting plants for seed | Mark plants in the field ahead of time that show no disease symptoms, carry many pods, and ripen evenly |
| Harvest time | Harvest after pods have turned fully brown — a little later than for eating |
| Separation | Shell by hand or thresh separately. Keep it from mixing with the food-grade crop |
| Sorting | Discard seeds with pale or blotchy seed coat, wrinkled seeds, and seeds with weevil holes |
| Drying | Moisture 12% or below. Snaps apart when bitten |
| Insect control | Always take weevil precautions before storage. See Section 15 |
| Storage | Put in a sealed container and keep somewhere cool and dark, below 12°C |
| Germination retained | 3-4 years |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The trait most worth watching for in adzuki bean seed saving is even ripening. Since the biggest harvest loss in adzuki beans comes from spread-out ripening, selecting and carrying forward seed from plants whose pods ripen together at around the same time, year after year, makes harvest markedly easier after a few years. The method is simple: walk the field in late September and tie a thread or cloth strip onto plants where the upper and lower pods are the same color, with no mix of green and brown pods. Look together for plants with many pods and no leaf-drop symptoms.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;It is better to shell seed-stock adzuki beans by hand. This is practical since the quantity is not large, and it avoids the tiny nicks that threshing causes, keeping germination ability intact and reducing mold during storage. Pressing a dry pod open with your fingers lets the seeds come straight out.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The most dangerous stage in saving adzuki bean seed is storage. The adzuki bean weevil is the surest way to ruin seed stock: a seed it has hollowed out will not germinate, and one only partly eaten sprouts weak even if it does come up. So seed-stock adzuki beans should be sealed more strictly than food-grade beans — fill a glass jar as full as possible, seal it, and keep it somewhere cool. Do not coat seed stock in oil: an oil film blocks respiration and lowers germination ability. Coating with wood ash, however, can also be used on seed stock.

| Seed Storage Time and Germination Rate |
|---|
| Year of harvest: 90% or higher |
| After 1 year: 85-90% |
| After 2 years: 70-85% |
| After 3 years: 50-70% |
| After year 4: Drops sharply |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The adzuki bean's seed longevity is grade A, 3 years sealed at room temperature, and because it has almost no fat, its germination rate falls more slowly than other legumes'. Even so, the practical approach is to save new seed every year and keep old seed as a backup. Whenever old seed has to be sown, always run a germination test first. Since adzuki beans need warmth to sprout, the test must be run in a warm spot too — testing it somewhere cool and concluding it will not sprout leads to the wrong conclusion.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Repeated self-saving of seed lets seed-borne disease accumulate. Anthracnose and viral disease pass to the next generation along with the seed. Blotches are hard to see against a red seed coat, so check under bright light, and every 3 to 4 years, get some seed from a neighboring village or a different valley and mix in a portion.
