## 13. Seed Saving

| Item | Method |
|---|---|
| Selecting plants for seed | Mark plants in the field ahead of time that show no disease symptoms, carry many pods, and came through winter well |
| Isolation | Keep different varieties several hundred meters apart, or grow only one variety per year |
| Harvest time | Collect only after the pods have dried fully black. Harvest later than for eating |
| Separation | Thresh it separately. Cracking pods open by hand keeps the seeds undamaged |
| Selection | Discard anything spotted, shriveled, discolored, or undersized. Keep only the plump, glossy seeds |
| Drying | 12% moisture. It should crack with a snap when bitten, without the tooth sinking in |
| Storage | In an airtight container, in a cool, dark place. A separate container from the ones eaten |
| Storage duration | 3 years, airtight, at room temperature |

> **Fava beans are naturally self-pollinating, but bee-mediated cross-pollination reaches 30-50%. Plant several varieties close together, and the following year's seed comes up with mixed traits. Isolation is necessary to keep a pure line.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This tendency toward cross-pollination is an important difference that sets fava beans apart from peas. Pea petals fully enclose the pistil and stamens, keeping outside pollen out, but fava bean flowers are large and strongly scented, drawing bees in. Plant several varieties side by side, and hybrids result. In a disaster situation, if you are growing only one line anyway, this problem solves itself. If you want to keep more than one line, alternate saving seed from one variety a year, or place the fields far apart.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Marking plants for seed in the field ahead of time changes the outcome considerably. Selecting only by looking at the seed after harvest selects only for size and shape. Selecting in the field lets you select at the same time for traits that actually matter — winter survival, pod count, resistance to spot disease, resistance to lodging. This matters especially for fava beans, where picking out plants that came through winter well is valuable. Since there is essentially no widely available variety in South Korea, selecting over several years is effectively how you create your own field's variety.

| Seed storage duration and germination rate |
|---|
| Year of harvest: 90% or higher |
| After 1 year: 80-90% |
| After 2 years: 60-80% |
| After 3 years: 40-60% |
| After 4 years: drops sharply |

## 14. Toxicity and Precautions — Favism

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This section is the most important one in this document. Fava beans are a safe, highly nutritious food for most people, but for people with a certain condition, they can be life-threatening. This risk is not removed by cooking, and it has nothing to do with how thoroughly the beans are cooked.

> **In a person with G6PD enzyme deficiency, eating fava beans triggers acute hemolysis — the rapid destruction of red blood cells. This is called favism. It can be fatal in children, and cases have even been reported of an attack brought on simply by inhaling fava bean pollen.**

### 14-1. What Is Favism

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Human red blood cells rely on an enzyme called G6PD (glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase) to protect themselves from oxidative stress. This enzyme continuously regenerates antioxidant compounds inside the red blood cell. A person who is genetically deficient in this enzyme shows no symptoms ordinarily, but when a strong oxidizing substance enters the body, their red blood cells cannot withstand it and break apart.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Fava beans contain glycosides called vicine and convicine. Once broken down in the gut, these become strong oxidizing compounds called divicine and isouramil. In a person with normal G6PD, red blood cells handle these without trouble, but in a deficient person's red blood cells, this causes them to be destroyed en masse within hours. Hemoglobin released from the destroyed red blood cells turns the urine cola-colored, and the shortage of red blood cells left to carry oxygen brings on severe anemia and jaundice.

| Item | Details |
|---|---|
| Causative substances | Fava beans' vicine and convicine, and their metabolites divicine and isouramil |
| Susceptible condition | G6PD deficiency. X-linked inheritance, so symptoms appear more often in males |
| Common ancestry | Mediterranean, Middle Eastern, African, Southeast Asian |
| Onset | A few hours to two days after eating |
| Route of exposure | Eating it, and, rarely, inhaling pollen during flowering |
| Removed by cooking | No. The risk remains whether boiled or roasted |
| Highest-risk group | Children. A larger dose relative to body weight leads to more severe cases |

### 14-2. What Symptoms Appear

| Timing | Symptoms |
|---|---|
| A few hours later | Chills, fever, headache, nausea, pain in the abdomen and back |
| Half a day to a day | Urine turns dark brown or cola-colored |
| One to two days | Jaundice — a yellow tinge to the face and eyes — severe paleness, shortness of breath, rapid pulse |
| Severe cases | Too weak to stand, clouded consciousness. Urine output drops to almost nothing |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The easiest sign to notice is the color of the urine. If urine turns dark brown or cola-colored after eating fava beans, that means hemolysis is underway — stop feeding them fava beans at once, and get medical help if at all possible. What can be done where medical care is out of reach is limited, but it includes: giving plenty of water so the kidneys can clear out the debris from destroyed red blood cells, keeping the person fully at rest, and never feeding that person fava beans again.

### 14-3. How to Prepare for This

| Principle | Detail |
|---|---|
| Always start with a tiny amount | Give only five or six beans the first time, and watch for a day or two. If nothing happens, increase gradually |
| Extra caution with children | Start a child on just two or three beans the first time |
| Check family history | If anyone in the bloodline has developed jaundice and anemia after eating fava beans or certain medications, that family should avoid them |
| Access to the field during flowering | Keep anyone with a confirmed deficiency away from the fava bean field while it is flowering |
| Removing the seed coat | Vicine and convicine are relatively concentrated in the seed coat. Removing it lowers exposure, but the risk does not disappear |
| Soaking and boiling | Discarding the soaking water and the cooking water removes some of it. Again, the risk does not disappear |
| An alternative crop | A household with a deficient member is safer growing peas or cowpeas instead of fava beans |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There is no way to test for G6PD deficiency in a disaster situation. That makes the small-amount trial the only real safeguard available. Apply this procedure without exception to anyone newly joining the group, to children, and to anyone who has never eaten fava beans before. A person who has eaten them once without trouble is generally safe afterward as well.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Whether fava beans are worth growing despite this risk can only be answered conditionally. The frequency of G6PD deficiency in the South Korean population is low compared with the Mediterranean or Southeast Asia. And the benefits fava beans offer — using the winter field, giving the earliest harvest of spring, and leaving nitrogen behind in the soil — are hard to replace with another crop. But that judgment must be made knowing the people who will be eating them, and the small-amount trial step must never be skipped.

### 14-4. Other Antinutrients

| Compound | Effect | How to remove it |
|---|---|---|
| Trypsin inhibitor | Blocks the action of protein-digesting enzymes | Soak 8 hours, then boil for 1 hour |
| Lectin | Binds to the gut lining and hinders absorption | Destroyed by heat. Not as toxic as the lectin in kidney beans |
| Phytic acid | Binds iron, zinc, and calcium and blocks their absorption | Soaking, sprouting, and fermentation reduce it by 60-80% |
| Oligosaccharides | Indigestible to humans; fermented by gut bacteria | Discard the soaking water after soaking |
| L-DOPA (levodopa) | A precursor to a neurotransmitter | Concentrated in young pods and sprouts. Eating a lot can cause dizziness |
| Mycotoxins | Produced by mold during storage | Moldy fava beans are not made safe by washing or cooking. Discard them |

## 15. Storage Without Refrigeration

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Fava beans' storability is rated SS. Dry seed lasts three years at room temperature, and with fat content under 2%, rancidity develops slowly. But if conditions go wrong, a single weevil species can turn an entire stored batch to shells.

> **Beans are not lost to rot — they are lost to insects eating them. The technique of storage is, in essence, the technique of keeping insects out.**

### 15-1. Drying Comes First

| Stage | Method | Check |
|---|---|---|
| Preliminary drying | Hang whole stems upside down under the eaves for three or four days | The pods turn black and dry through |
| Post-threshing drying | Spread thin on a mat or drying sheet in the sun for 3-5 days | Takes longer than soybeans, since the seeds are large. Turn often |
| Target moisture | 12% for both eating and seed | It should crack with a snap when bitten, without the tooth sinking in |
| Salt test | Shake dry salt in a jar together with the beans | If salt sticks to the jar's wall, it is still damp |
| Cooling | Bring in from the sun and let it cool fully before storing | Sealing it warm creates condensation |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Because fava bean seeds are large, they easily end up dry on the outside while still damp inside. Store them going by appearance alone, and mold appears inside the container within weeks. Get in the habit of splitting a seed in half to check that the color is uniformly dry all the way through, and allow a day or two more sun-drying time than for other legumes.

### 15-2. Controlling Bean Weevils in Storage

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The fava bean weevil (Bruchus rufimanus) is the greatest threat to stored fava beans. The frightening part is that it begins in the field before storage even starts. Adult weevils lay eggs on pods still on the plant, and those eggs hatch inside storage and bore into the beans. The infestation is already built in by the time the beans come indoors, and within half a year, a whole sack can be reduced to riddled shells.

| Method | Principle | Application | Effect |
|---|---|---|---|
| Full airtight sealing | Cuts off oxygen and suffocates them | Fill a glass jar or metal container completely and seal it | Most reliable. Kills even eggs and larvae within 3-4 weeks |
| Mixing in wood ash | Fine particles scratch the waxy exoskeleton, causing dehydration | Mix in 2-5% of the fava bean's volume | Rinse with water before eating |
| Diatomaceous earth | The silica shell damages the exoskeleton | 1-3g per 1kg of fava beans | More effective than ash. Works only when dry |
| Oil coating | An oil film blocks egg attachment and hatching | Mix 2-5mL of cooking oil into 1kg of fava beans by hand | Since the beans are large, less oil is needed per surface area. Not used on seed stock |
| Cold treatment | Sub-zero temperatures kill eggs and larvae | Set outside for a few days in the depth of winter, then bring back in | Be careful of condensation when bringing it back inside |
| Heat treatment | Eggs and larvae die at 60 degrees | Spread thin in the sun for half a day | Not used on seed stock |
| Dried hot pepper, Sichuan pepper, mugwort | Essential-oil compounds suppress egg-laying | Layer them in | A supplementary measure |
| Charcoal, roasted salt | Absorbs moisture | Wrap in cloth and add | Doubles as mold prevention |
| Burying in ash | Combines sealing with physical control | Layer ash and fava beans in a jar | Widely used traditionally |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Full airtight sealing matters most. Insects also breathe. Inside a sealed container, both the beans and the insects consume the available oxygen, which falls while CO2 rises, and the insects die. The beans, being dormant, tolerate this fine. The fuller the container and the smaller the empty space, the faster it works. Several small containers packed tight beat one large one, and this also means that if one container spoils, you do not lose everything at once.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Oil coating has long been used by smallholders in India and Africa. Mix 2-3ml of cooking oil into 1kg of fava beans and rub it by hand so every bean gets a thin film. Because the beans are large, their surface area is relatively small, so this uses less oil than it would for smaller beans. Use it only on beans you plan to eat within six months, never on seed stock — the oil film interferes with respiration and lowers germination. Sieve wood ash to keep only the fine part, and mix in 2-5% by volume. Insects cannot develop resistance to it, and it is harmless to people.

### 15-3. Storage Containers and Location

| Container | Airtightness | Duration | Advantage | Disadvantage |
|---|:---:|---|---|---|
| Glass jar | Very good | 3 years or more | Condition can be checked by eye | Small capacity, does not block light |
| Metal container | Very good | 3 years or more | Fully blocks rats and insects | Condensation spoils the spot where it forms |
| Ceramic jar | Good | 2-3 years | Slow temperature change, rats cannot gnaw through | Heavy and breakable |
| Thick plastic | Fair | 1-2 years | Light and cheap | Rats gnaw through it |
| Underground storage pit | Good | 2-3 years | Constant, low temperature | Losing the whole batch if water gets in |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;For checking, the most reliable senses are hearing and smell. A rustling sound when you put your ear to the container means insects are active; a musty smell means mold has started. Small round holes on the surface mean the weevils have already gone through one generation. Even at that point you can save what remains: take everything out, dry it in the sun, float it in water to skim off the hollow seeds, mix in ash or oil, and reseal it. Checking once every two months is a reasonable standard.

## 16. Cooking and Processing

### 16-1. Removing the Seed Coat and Soaking

| Treatment | Time/temperature | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Soaking | 8 hours or more in room-temperature water. 12 hours for large seeds | Takes longer than other beans, since the seeds are large |
| Discarding the soaking water | Always | Removes some of the oligosaccharides and vicine/convicine along with it |
| Removing the seed coat | Push it off by hand after soaking | Shortens cooking time, eases digestion, and reduces the antinutrient load |
| Boiling | 1 hour in boiling water | Down to 30-40 minutes once the seed coat is removed |
| Checking doneness | Mashes easily under finger pressure | If a firm core remains, it is not fully cooked |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Fava beans have a thick, tough seed coat, so removing it is worthwhile in several ways. Soak the beans in water, then press a seed between your fingers and the coat slips right off. Removing it cuts cooking time by nearly half, saving fuel, eases digestion, and reduces the intake of vicine and convicine, which are relatively concentrated in the coat. Do not discard the removed coats — save them for livestock feed or compost.

### 16-2. Fuel-Saving Cooking

| Method | Fuel | Procedure |
|---|:---:|---|
| Boiling with the coat removed | Little | Soak, remove the coat, and it softens in 30-40 minutes |
| Pressure cooking | Least | Pressure-cook the soaked beans for 15 minutes |
| Residual-heat cooking | Very little | Boil for 15 minutes, then turn off the heat and wrap it in a straw- or husk-filled box or blankets for 2-3 hours |
| Roasted fava beans | Little | Roast soaked, dried fava beans with no oil. They can be eaten as they are |
| Fava bean flour | Almost none | Roast, grind, and store; mix with water to eat |
| Green fava beans | Little | Remove the coat and blanch — it takes only a few minutes |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Roasted fava beans are eaten widely across the Mediterranean and Middle East. Soak them, dry them, then roast with no oil, and the surface cracks open with a nutty flavor. Roast a batch once and it can be eaten as is afterward at no further fuel cost, and sealed airtight it keeps for a month or two. However, this method does not remove vicine and convicine, so for anyone at risk of favism, it remains dangerous regardless of how it is cooked.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Residual-heat cooking works well for fava beans too. Boil for just 15 minutes, then set it in an insulated box, and the internal temperature stays high for more than two hours while cooking continues. A wooden box packed with straw or husks, or a thick blanket wrapped fully around the pot, serves as that insulated box. This method fits fava beans especially well, since their large seeds take time to cook through to the center.

### 16-3. Processing for Storage

| Preparation | Summary | Storage | Value |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fava bean flour | Roast, then grind | Airtight, 3-6 months | Needs no fuel to prepare |
| Pickled young shoots | Salt the shoots taken from pinching | Several months | Carries spring greens through to winter |
| Blanched and dried green fava beans | Blanch, drain, and dry thoroughly | Several months | Add to winter soups and stews |
| Fava bean doenjang | Boil, mash, ferment like meju, then submerge in brine | Several years | An alternative when soybeans are scarce |
| Roasted fava beans | Soak, dry, then roast with no oil | Airtight, 1-2 months | A snack and an emergency food |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Making a fermented paste from fava beans is a long-standing practice in East Asia. Boiled fava beans are inoculated like meju, left to ferment, then aged in brine with salt and chili. Fermentation breaks down the antinutrients and raises digestibility, and with salt it keeps for years. In a year when soybeans are unavailable but fava beans are plentiful, this is well worth trying. However, fermentation, too, does not fully eliminate vicine and convicine.

## 17. Annual Work Calendar

| Month | Task | Note |
|---|---|---|
| January | Check stored fava beans, walk the overwintering field, press down frost-heaved plants | Step on any plants the frost has lifted, to press them back down |
| February | Spring sowing (mid-to-late month), field planning, lime application | Sow as soon as the soil thaws. Sow late and the heat will get to them |
| March | Hill the overwintered plants, weed, check the drainage ditches | Keep spring rain from pooling |
| April | Flowering begins, pinching, aphid management, check root nodules | Pinching is the single most essential task in growing fava beans |
| May | Pod fill, focused irrigation, start harvesting green fava beans (overwintered crop) | Water at this stage is what makes the yield |
| June | Harvest dry seed (overwintered crop), harvest green fava beans (spring-sown), hang to dry | If rain is forecast, do not wait for full ripeness |
| July | Threshing, winnowing, drying, storage, return residue to the field | Do not burn the stems — cut and lay them down instead |
| August | Manage the following crop, check stored beans | The summer crop planted where the fava beans grew uses the leftover nitrogen |
| September | Prepare the autumn field, apply lime, add compost | Apply lime 3 weeks before sowing |
| October | Build the beds, dig drainage ditches, select seed | Winter drainage decides whether overwintering succeeds |
| November | Autumn sowing (around the start of winter, in southern South Korea) | Set the sowing date 40-50 days ahead of the first frost |
| December | Cover with straw or fallen leaves, guard against animals, check storage | If a fava bean field is the only green thing in the winter landscape, water deer will come |

### 17-1. Five Things Not to Forget

| Item | Details |
|---|---|
| Favism | Can be fatal to someone with a G6PD deficiency. Anyone eating it for the first time must always test with five or six beans and watch for a day or two |
| Pinching | Pinch off the growing tip once flowering starts. It raises yield, cuts aphids, and yields a vegetable besides |
| Overwintering sowing date | 40-50 days ahead of the first frost. A fava bean sown early and grown large dies first |
| Drainage | Thick roots rot the moment they sit in water. Beds and drainage ditches come first |
| Residue | Fail to return the stems, leaves, and roots to the field, and fava beans become a crop that strips the land instead of feeding it |
