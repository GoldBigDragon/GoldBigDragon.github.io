## 5. Field Preparation and Fertilizer - Nitrogen Balance and Fertilizer Application

### 5-4. The Pitfall of Nitrogen Fixation

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The claim that planting beans improves the soil has hardened into farming common sense. It is half right and half wrong. Without understanding this point precisely, a field planted with legumes for several years running can end up growing more depleted instead.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Let's work through the numbers. When fava bean grows well, 1,000m² yields 300kg of dried seed. Fava bean seed is 26.1% protein, and dividing protein by 6.25 gives the nitrogen content. 26.1 ÷ 6.25 is 4.18, meaning about 4.2% of the seed's weight is pure nitrogen. The nitrogen contained in 300kg is about 12.5kg, which converts to 125kg per hectare.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Meanwhile, the nitrogen that fava bean's root nodules fix runs 150-200kg per hectare under good conditions — among the highest of any legume, and the basis for fava bean's SS grade in fertilizer demand. Under poor conditions it drops to just 50kg. Laid side by side, what leaves and what comes in look like this:

| Item | Nitrogen per Hectare | Direction |
|---|---|---|
| Root-nodule fixation (good conditions) | 150-200kg | In |
| Root-nodule fixation (poor conditions) | 40-80kg | In |
| Removed with harvested seed | 110-140kg | Out |
| Nitrogen remaining in stems and leaves | 40-60kg | Stays if returned to the field; leaves if burned or hauled away |
| Nitrogen remaining in roots and nodules | 15-30kg | Usually stays in the field |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The table shows why fava bean is used as a green manure crop. Fixation is clearly greater than what is removed, so nitrogen stays in the field even if only the stems and leaves are returned. Few legumes have this kind of margin. But conditions change the picture: in a field where inoculation failed and fixation was only 40kg, if 130kg of seed is hauled away and the stems are collected too, the nitrogen balance turns clearly negative.

> **Legumes revive the soil not because they fix nitrogen, but because part of the nitrogen they fix stays in the field, held in the stems and leaves. Haul those stems and leaves away, and the legume becomes a crop that depletes the soil.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Fava bean offers one more option that flips this calculation: giving up the seed harvest and turning the whole plant into the soil at flowering. In that case, removal drops to zero and all the fixed nitrogen stays in the field. 150-200kg of nitrogen per hectare is an amount that cannot be produced by any other method in a world without chemical fertilizer. Using the whole field this way feels wasteful, but reviving one visibly depleted plot — worn down by years of continuous cropping — with fava bean is a perfectly reasonable strategy.

| Principle | Details |
|---|---|
| Stems and leaves always go back to the field | Return all the stems, leaves, and pod hulls left after threshing to the field |
| Do not burn them | Burning releases the nitrogen as gas, leaving only potassium and phosphorus behind in the ash |
| Do not dig up the roots | Fava bean's thick roots and nodules hold a substantial amount of nitrogen |
| Cut into 20-30cm pieces and cover the soil | Fava bean stems are thick and moist, so cutting and covering makes them break down quickly |
| Choosing green manure | Turning it into the soil at flowering leaves 150-200kg of nitrogen per hectare in the field |
| If fed to livestock, return the manure | If fava bean stems were fed to livestock, be sure to put that animal's manure back on the same field |

### 5-5. Field Preparation

| Item | Method | Under Disaster Conditions |
|---|---|---|
| Tilling | Till to a depth of 20-30cm and level evenly | Turn it over with a spade or dig with a hoe. Since the roots go deep, deeper tilling is better |
| Raised bed | Height 20cm or more | The key to preventing waterlogging |
| Drainage ditch | Depth 30-50cm | Winter drainage matters especially for overwintering cultivation |
| pH correction | pH 6.0-7.0 | Done 3 weeks before sowing |
| Organic matter | Well-rotted compost 2,000-3,000kg/1,000m² | Fava bean's need for organic matter is on the high side among legumes |
| Lime | 150-200kg/1,000m² for acidic soil | Substitute with baked and crushed shells/eggshells, or with wood ash |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Lime must go in ahead of time because the reaction takes time. Applied right before sowing, it creates locally strong alkalinity that damages the seed and young roots, while the reaction is still incomplete right when it's actually needed. Also, if lime and nitrogen fertilizer go into the same spot together, ammonia escapes as gas and nitrogen is lost. Keep the order: lime first, fertilizer two weeks later. If there is no lime, bake shells and eggshells over a fire until they turn brittle, then crush them in a mortar and use that instead.

### 5-6. Fertilizer Amounts

| Fertilizer | Per 1,000m² | Converted per m² | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Nitrogen | 3-5kg | 3-5g | Apply only the minimum; this is for the early stage before the root nodules start working |
| Phosphorus | 10-15kg | 10-15g | Apply the full amount as base fertilizer. Needed for the energy metabolism of nitrogen fixation |
| Potassium | 10-15kg | 10-15g | Apply the full amount as base fertilizer. Involved in seed filling and stem strength |
| Compost | 2,000-3,000kg | 2-3kg | Use fully rotted compost |
| Lime | 150-200kg (acidic soil) | 150-200g | 3 weeks before sowing |

> **Going easy on nitrogen for fava bean is not frugality — it is technique. Give it too much, and the stems and leaves grow lush and lodge, aphids swarm in, and pod set actually goes down.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;2,000-3,000kg of compost is a large amount compared with soybean's 1,000kg. This is why fava bean is called a heavy feeder, but the "feeding" meant here is organic matter, not nitrogen. Because fava bean has thick, deep roots and a large frame, it needs a soil structure that holds water and nutrients well. Adding plenty of well-rotted compost makes the soil crumbly, which helps the thick roots spread and improves water retention. Nitrogen itself still only needs to be the minimum amount.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In principle, top-dressing is not needed. However, if the leaves turn pale yellow overall around flowering and growth is clearly lagging, that is a sign the root nodules are not working properly. Only then should a small amount be supplemented. In a disaster, the easiest option is to dilute urine tenfold with water and pour it onto the soil between plants. Keep it off the leaves, and apply it diluted and often rather than concentrated and all at once.

### 5-7. Disaster Substitute Fertilizers

| Substitute Resource | Nutrients Supplied | How to Use | Caution |
|---|---|---|---|
| Wood ash | Potassium, phosphorus, calcium | Scatter 100-200g per m² and mix in | Alkaline. Fava bean tolerates up to pH 8.0, so it has some margin |
| Well-rotted compost | Comprehensive, organic matter | Apply 2-3kg per m² as base fertilizer | The single most important resource for fava bean |
| Diluted urine | Nitrogen, potassium | Dilute tenfold with water and pour onto the soil between plants | Keep off the leaves. Use only a small amount |
| Bone meal | Phosphorus, calcium | Bake, crush, and mix into compost | Effect appears slowly |
| Fish bones and fish guts | Phosphorus, nitrogen | Bury and let them break down before use | Animals will dig them up if buried as is |
| Shells and eggshells | Calcium | Bake, crush, and scatter | A substitute for lime. Reacts slowly |
| Leaf mold | Comprehensive, organic matter | Mix into the soil | Improves water retention |
| Rice husks and rice straw | Potassium, silica | Cover as mulch and till in the following year | Also serves as cold-protection mulch for overwintering cultivation |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Boron is also worth remembering. Fava bean has a boron requirement, and when boron is lacking, flowers drop and pods fail to set. Since fava bean already has a naturally high flower-drop rate, a boron deficiency on top of that can cause yield to collapse badly. When chemical borax is unavailable, the most realistic alternative is compost made with a mix of vegetable scraps, fruit peels, and fallen leaves together. Compost made from only one kind of material ends up skewed in trace elements.

## 6. Sowing

### 6-1. Sowing Time

| Cultivation Type | Sowing Time | Harvest Time | Applicable Region |
|---|---|---|---|
| Autumn/overwintering | Around ipdong, early November | Mid-May to mid-June the following year | Southern coast, Jeju Island |
| Spring cultivation | Mid-to-late February to early March | Late May to late June | Central and northern South Korea |
| Green-manure cultivation | Autumn or early spring | Turned into the soil at flowering | Across South Korea |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There is a rule for calculating the sowing time for overwintering cultivation. It withstands winter best if it reaches three or four true leaves and a height of 10-15cm before winter arrives. Larger than that, it freezes to death; smaller, and it cannot root properly. Counting back 40-50 days from the expected first frost date gives the sowing date. That early November, around ipdong, becomes the benchmark in southern South Korea is a result of this calculation.

> **Whether fava bean survives the winter comes down to the sowing date. A fava bean sown early and grown large dies before a smaller one sown later.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;For spring cultivation, mid-to-late February is the right time. The rule is to sow as soon as the soil thaws; push past mid-March and flowering runs into the heat, dropping yield sharply. Most failures with spring-sown fava bean come from sowing too late. While you delay out of fear of frost, the heat creeps closer, and once days above 25°C start stringing together, the flowers simply drop. Since fava bean withstands -10°C, there is no reason to worry about frost in February.

### 6-2. Seed Preparation

| Item | Method | Under Disaster Conditions |
|---|---|---|
| Sorting | Discard seeds with spots, discoloration, wrinkling, or insect holes | Sort by hand, one seed at a time. Being large, they are easy to sort |
| Sizing | Choose the thick, heavy seeds | Float them in water and remove any that float |
| Soaking | Soak in water for about a day | The thick seed coat means soaking has a strong effect. Do not exceed 24 hours |
| Hot-water disinfection | Soak in 50-55°C water for 10-15 minutes | Exceeding this temperature reduces germination ability |
| Ash treatment | Coat with fine wood ash | Helps disinfect and dry the seed, and has a repellent effect on birds and rats |
| Root-nodule inoculation | Coat with soil from a field that grew a legume with the same bacterial strain | See section 5-3. Do not combine with disinfection |
| Germination test | Wrap 20 seeds in a wet cloth, leave for a few days, and count how many sprout | Always check old seed first |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Because fava bean has a thick seed coat and large seeds, soaking has a bigger effect than for other legumes. Soak it in water for a day and the seed visibly swells and germinates several days sooner. However, past 24 hours, oxygen runs short, the inside turns soft, and it rots in the soil. A good rhythm is to soak it in the evening and sow it the following evening. Use at least three times the seed's volume in water, and change the water once partway through.

### 6-3. Sowing Method

| Item | Standard | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Sowing depth | 5-7cm | Larger seeds are sown deeper than other beans. Deeper still for overwintering cultivation |
| Seeds per hole | 1-2 | Large seeds have a high germination rate |
| Row spacing | 45-90cm | Ensures airflow and an access path for management |
| Plant spacing | 15-30cm | Wider for large-seeded types, closer for small-seeded types |
| Plants per area | 4-8 plants per m² | Sparser than other legumes because of its large size |
| Sowing orientation | On an angle, with the eye facing down | Lets the root extend straight downward |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Fava bean seeds have a black or white band-shaped mark at one end. This is the eye, and this is where the root emerges. Planting it on an angle with this mark facing down means the root doesn't have to work to find its direction, and emergence comes a day or two sooner. Because fava bean's seeds are large and easy to orient, this trick is practical only for a crop like this.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The biggest threat right after sowing is rats and birds. Fava bean seeds are large and starchy, so rats can pinpoint them underground precisely. In overwintering cultivation, the damage is worse because the seeds sit exposed all winter. Keeping to the proper sowing depth and coating the seed with fine wood ash reduces this significantly. Covering the ground with straw or netting until germination also helps, and in overwintering cultivation, that straw doubles as cold protection.

## 7. Water Management

| Stage | Water Management | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Sowing to germination | Keep the soil from drying out completely | Being large, the seed also needs to absorb more water |
| Early growth | Rely on natural rainfall | Dryness at this stage actually encourages the roots to go deeper |
| Winter (overwintering type) | Manage drainage only | Water pooling during freeze-thaw cycles is the greatest danger |
| Flowering to pod filling | Water generously enough that the soil does not dry out | The single most reliable way to lower the flower-drop rate |
| Maturity | Stop irrigation | Otherwise the pods fail to dry out and mold develops |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;For fava bean, water management is drainage first, irrigation second. Thick roots rot as soon as they are submerged, and root-nodule bacteria die without oxygen too. This is especially true in overwintering cultivation, where drainage ditches often collapse as the ground repeatedly freezes and thaws over winter, so the field should be checked occasionally even in the depths of winter to clear the water channels.

| Symptom | Cause | Action |
|---|---|---|
| Leaves droop at midday and recover by evening | Temporary water shortage. Within the normal range | Leave it as is |
| Leaves are still drooping in the morning | The roots cannot draw up water | Water generously |
| Flowers drop in large numbers | Fava bean naturally has a high flower-drop rate. It worsens when dryness and heat combine | Irrigate and mulch. Pinch the tip to concentrate nutrients |
| Pods have set but the seeds are small | Water shortage during pod filling | Irrigation at this stage directly determines yield |
| Lower leaves yellow and roots are black | Root damage from excess moisture | Clear the drainage ditch and mound up the soil |
| All leaves are pale yellow but not wilting | Nitrogen shortage because the root nodules are not working | Dig up a root and check the nodules |
| Plants heave up in winter, exposing the roots | The ground has heaved from frost | Press the soil back down by foot and cover with straw |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Frost-heave damage is a problem that appears only in overwintering cultivation. As water in the ground freezes, it expands and pushes the soil upward, and in the process young fava bean plants get lifted up, roots and all. Exposed roots dry out or freeze to death. Checking the field occasionally through the winter, pressing heaved plants back down by foot, and covering the ground with 5-10cm of straw or fallen leaves greatly reduces this damage. If this mulch is left in place rather than cleared away in spring, it goes on to suppress weeds and conserve moisture.
