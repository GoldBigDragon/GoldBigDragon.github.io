## 3. Growing Conditions

### 3-1. Temperature and Light

| Condition | Limit | Optimum | Outside This Range |
|---|---|---|---|
| Germination temperature | 5°C or above | 15-20°C | Germinates slowly even at 8-10°C |
| Growing temperature | 5-28°C | 10-20°C | Above 25°C, growth stops and disease increases |
| Fruiting temperature | 10-24°C | 13-20°C | A range of 14°C, wide for a legume |
| Heat during flowering | 25°C+ for several days | - | Flowers and young pods drop |
| Cold hardiness | Down to -10°C | - | The highest among legumes; overwinters in the field |
| Overwintering limit | Dies if -15°C or below persists | - | Overwintering is difficult in the inland central regions of South Korea |
| Sunlight | 6+ hours a day | 8+ hours a day | Yield drops by half at 40% shade |

> **The one number that sets fava bean apart from every other legume is -10°C. Because of this single figure, fava bean can occupy the winter field and be the first to offer food in spring.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Fava bean's cold hardiness comes with a condition: if it grows too large before winter sets in, it will actually freeze to death. It withstands cold best at the stage of three or four true leaves and a height of 10-15cm. This is why autumn sowing is timed 40-50 days ahead of the first frost — the reason ipdong, around early November, is the benchmark in southern South Korea. Sow in September and the plant grows knee-high before winter and freezes; sow in December and it gets locked in frozen ground before it can root.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In the inland central regions of South Korea, overwintering generally fails. Where -15°C persists for several days, even fava bean dies. In those areas, go with spring sowing in mid-to-late February instead. Overwintering cultivation is reliable along the southern coast and on Jeju Island, while the central west coast and the southern inland areas vary from year to year. If you are not sure, split the planting between an autumn batch and a spring batch to spread the risk.

### 3-2. Soil and Water

| Condition | Limit | Optimum | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Soil texture | Sandy to clayey | Well-drained sandy loam | Waterlogged ground is worst |
| pH | 5.5-8.0 | 6.0-7.0 | Root nodules decrease in acidic soil |
| Effective soil depth | 40cm or more | 60cm or more | The taproot goes in deep |
| Seasonal evapotranspiration | - | about 450mm | Middling among legumes |
| Flooding | Roots are damaged if submerged for a day or more | - | Very susceptible to waterlogging |
| Water table | 50cm or deeper | 80cm or deeper | Water management in the winter field matters |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Fava bean is extremely intolerant of waterlogging. Its thick roots go deep, and those thick roots rot as soon as they are submerged. What's more, root-nodule bacteria are aerobic and need oxygen, so if the field is flooded for even a single day, the nodules lose function before the roots themselves do. This problem is even trickier in overwintering cultivation, because freezing and thawing over winter heaves the soil and blocks drainage channels. Raising the bed at least 20cm and digging deep drainage ditches is, for fava bean, not optional but a precondition.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Fava bean is more tolerant of soil acidity than pea. It tolerates pH 5.5 to 8.0, with 6.0-7.0 as the optimum. This range, though, reflects the needs of the root-nodule bacteria more than the plant itself. Below pH 5.5, fava bean can still grow somehow, but the bacteria cannot survive, nitrogen fixation stops, and the leaves turn pale yellow. Because fields in South Korea receive heavy rainfall and tend to skew acidic, liming comes before fertilizing as the first task.

## 4. Varieties

### 4-1. Classification by Use

| Type | Seed Size | Characteristics | Suited To |
|---|---|---|---|
| Large-seeded food type | 2g or more per seed | Large seeds, high yield | The staple choice for food and storage |
| Small-seeded landrace | 0.7-1.5g per seed | Small seeds, strong against cold and poor soil | Poor fields or cold winters |
| Green-manure/forage type | Not relevant | High stem and leaf production | When the goal is to revive the soil |
| Early-maturing type | Not relevant | Short growing period | When spring sowing must beat the heat |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Fava bean has essentially no formally registered variety in South Korea. What remains are landrace lines that were grown in some regions into the early 1980s, plus a small number of varieties bred in South Korea that have appeared recently. Radiation-bred lines such as Wonjam 1 are known, but their seed distribution is limited. What is available on the market is mostly imported seed, or small quantities sold for home gardens.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;So if fava bean is to be part of the plan, the only realistic path is to secure seed in advance and keep it going through self-seed-saving. Fortunately, because the seeds are large, the weight of seed collected from a single plant is substantial, and it keeps for 3 years sealed at room temperature. In the first few years, it is reasonable to focus on multiplying seed rather than expecting a real harvest. Selecting and continuing plants that made it through the local winter will, within a few years, build a line suited to that particular land.

### 4-2. Selection Criteria by Situation

| Situation | Priority | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Winters are cold | Small-seeded landrace | Smaller seeds tend to overwinter better |
| Soil needs reviving | Green-manure type, or any line | Turned whole into the soil at flowering |
| Seed is scarce | Small-seeded type | The same weight of seed covers a much larger area |
| Stored food is needed | Large-seeded food type | The same labor harvests more weight |
| Can only sow in spring | Early-maturing type | Must finish before the heat arrives |
| Just obtained seed for the first time | Any type | Focus on seed saving for the first two or three years |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Fava bean's seed size has a major effect on how much seed sowing requires. To sow 100m² with a large-seeded type at 2g per seed takes two to three times the weight needed for a small-seeded type. In the first year, when seed is scarce, a small- or medium-seeded type covers a far larger area.

## 5. Field Preparation and Fertilizer - Root-Nodule Bacteria (Rhizobia)

### 5-1. How Nitrogen Fixation Happens

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;78% of the air is nitrogen. Even though a virtually limitless supply of nitrogen floats above every head, plants cannot use it. Nitrogen in the air is a molecule in which two nitrogen atoms are locked together by a triple bond, and breaking that bond takes an enormous amount of energy. Breaking it industrially to make ammonia is the Haber-Bosch process, and the world's nitrogen fertilizer comes from there — it requires high temperature, high pressure, a catalyst, and vast amounts of fossil fuel.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Root-nodule bacteria pull off the same feat at room temperature and normal pressure. Using an enzyme called nitrogenase, they break the triple bond and convert nitrogen into ammonia. But there is a cost: this reaction takes a great deal of energy, and the bacteria cannot photosynthesize on their own. So they live inside the roots of legumes, receive the sugar the plant makes through photosynthesis, and hand over the nitrogen they fix in exchange. It is a trade relationship.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The bacterium that partners with fava bean is Rhizobium leguminosarum bv. viciae. This is the same organism that partners with pea, lentil, and wild vetch, and it is a different species from soybean's Bradyrhizobium japonicum. This point matters in practice: rubbing soil from a soybean field onto fava bean seed has no effect. Soil from a field that grew pea, lentil, or vetch, on the other hand, works directly as an inoculant for fava bean.

| Stage | What Happens |
|---|---|
| 1. Signal exchange | Fava bean roots release flavonoids, and bacteria in the soil respond to the signal by releasing Nod factors |
| 2. Infection | Root hairs curl inward to enclose the bacteria, which then travel into the root along an infection thread |
| 3. Nodule formation | Cells inside the root divide, the nodule swells, and the bacteria settle inside it |
| 4. Nitrogen fixation | Nitrogenase acts inside the nodule, converting atmospheric nitrogen into ammonia |
| 5. Distribution | Ammonia is converted into amino acids and transported to the stems, leaves, and pods |
| 6. Senescence | At the ripening stage, the nodule loses function and returns to the soil along with the root |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Nitrogenase is extremely sensitive to oxygen and loses function the instant oxygen reaches it. So the inside of the root nodule is filled with a red pigment protein called leghemoglobin, which binds oxygen and keeps the oxygen concentration around the enzyme extremely low. It works on the same principle as why human blood is red, and this pigment is what makes the inside of a nodule look red or pink when you split it open. This fact matters enormously in practice, because that red color is visible proof that the nodule is fixing nitrogen right now.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Fava bean's root nodules are larger and easier to spot than those of other legumes. It is common for several bean-sized nodules to cluster together on the upper part of the thick taproot, making them easy to check. It is no coincidence that fava bean earns an SS for fertilizer demand and is rated the top legume for nitrogen fixation — this structural trait is part of the reason.

### 5-2. How to Check Whether Root Nodules Have Formed

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Whether or not you inoculated, you must check whether root nodules actually formed. Without checking, you cannot know the cause if nitrogen turns out to be lacking, and you will repeat the same mistake the following year.

| Step | Details |
|---|---|
| 1 | The best time is around when flowering begins, about 8 weeks after sowing |
| 2 | Choose two or three plants that look healthy |
| 3 | Do not pull it out — dig a wide area around the plant with a spade and lift it out with the soil attached. Pulling it out leaves the nodules on the fine roots behind in the soil |
| 4 | Put it in a bucket of water and gently rinse the soil away |
| 5 | Count the nodules on the taproot and the thick lateral roots |
| 6 | Split a few nodules open with a fingernail and check the color inside |

| Observation | Meaning | Action |
|---|---|---|
| 20+ nodules per plant, especially clustered on the upper taproot | Inoculation succeeded | Leave as is |
| Split interior is red or pink | Fixing nitrogen | Good condition |
| Split interior is white or pale green | Nodule formed but is not fixing nitrogen | Check pH, waterlogging, and phosphorus deficiency |
| Split interior is brown or black | Already senesced or dead | Normal if at the ripening stage; a problem if at flowering |
| 5 or fewer nodules | Inoculation failed | If leaves are yellow, supplement nitrogen |
| Many small nodules only at the tips of fine roots | Infected by native bacteria, but efficiency is low | Change the inoculant source next year |

> **This one habit — digging up a root, splitting a nodule open, and checking for red — is the single most reliable signal of whether your bean crop will succeed in a world with no nitrogen fertilizer.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The position of the nodules also carries information. Thick nodules on the upper taproot, within 10cm of the soil surface, are the most efficient — this is the position typical of a good inoculation. Conversely, if there are none on the taproot and only small nodules are scattered at the tips of the fine roots, it is often a case of native bacteria already present in the soil infecting late, and fixation efficiency is lower.

### 5-3. Where to Find an Inoculant Source

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Commercial inoculants are products that carry live bacteria in peat or clay. These cannot be obtained in a disaster. However, root-nodule bacteria are common soil microbes, and soil from a field that grew a legume sharing the same symbiotic bacterium is itself an inoculant source. Because fava bean has little history of cultivation in South Korea, many fields lack the native bacteria, so inoculation matters even more here than for other legumes.

| Method | Procedure | Effect | Caution |
|---|---|---|---|
| Coating seed with soil from a field with the same bacterial strain | Scoop soil from a depth of 5-15cm from a field where fava bean, pea, lentil, or vetch grew well and formed good nodules. Lightly moisten the seed, coat it with this soil, and sow it right away | The most reliable and free method | Soil from a diseased field also carries pathogens along with it |
| Wild vetch roots | In spring, dig up vetch from fields and field edges and check the root nodules. If the nodules are red, use that soil and those roots together | The most realistic option in an area with no history of growing fava bean | Fixation efficiency may be lower than a strain adapted to a cultivated crop |
| Using the nodules themselves | Collect nodules from dug-up roots, crush them, mix into water, then dip the seed in that water and take it out | Gives the highest bacterial density | Must be used the same day the nodules are dug up; the bacteria die if it dries out |
| Scattering soil over the field | Scatter the collected soil along the sowing rows and till it in together | Spreads the bacteria through the soil | About one shovelful per 10m² is the standard |
| Leaving root residue behind | At harvest, cut only the stems and leave the roots in the field | The bacteria remain in the soil until the following year | The lowest-effort way to maintain them |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There is something you must always observe when inoculating. Root-nodule bacteria are very sensitive to direct sunlight, drying out, and heat. A few minutes of UV exposure kills a large share of them, and drying kills them even faster. Do the work of coating the seed with soil in the shade, and sow the coated seed right away rather than laying it out in the sun. Sowing within 30 minutes is best, and it should be finished within a few hours at the latest. One more thing: seed disinfection and inoculation cannot be done together. If you coat seed that has fungicide on it with soil, the bacteria die. If you disinfected with a hot-water treatment, let the seed cool completely before inoculating.

| Conditions That Cause Inoculation to Fail | Reason |
|---|---|
| Soil pH 5.5 or below | Bacteria cannot survive |
| A field given plenty of nitrogen fertilizer | The plant feels no need to trade, and cuts back nodule formation on its own |
| A waterlogged field | Being aerobic, the bacteria die without oxygen |
| A field lacking phosphorus | Nitrogen fixation needs a lot of energy, and phosphorus is used in that metabolism |
| Deficiency of molybdenum, iron, or cobalt | These are components of nitrogenase |
| Drying inoculated seed in the sun | UV and drying kill the bacteria |
| Using soil from a soybean field | A different bacterial species, it does not infect fava bean |
