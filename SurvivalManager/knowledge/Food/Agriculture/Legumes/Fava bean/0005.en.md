## 8. Crop Management — Pinching

### 8-1. Why Pinching Is Essential

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;If you had to pick the single most efficient task in growing fava beans, it would be pinching. Around when flowers begin opening at the lower nodes, the tender growing tip at the top of the stem is pinched off by hand. This one action accomplishes three things at once.

| Effect | Mechanism |
|---|---|
| Higher yield | Upward vegetative growth stops, and nutrients shift to the flowers and pods that have already formed. Fava beans drop a high share of their flowers, so this effect is especially strong |
| Aphid control | Fava bean aphids cluster on the youngest growing point. Pinching alone removes a large share of the population |
| A vegetable, besides | The pinched tips are blanched and eaten as a namul side — a welcome addition when fresh spring greens are scarce |

> **It is rare, without pesticides, to get pest control, a harvest, and a yield boost from a single task. Pinching fava beans is that rare case. It is also the basis for the B rating given to pest-control self-reliance in the evaluation table.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Timing matters. The right moment is when the lower nodes are flowering and buds still remain higher up. Pinch too early, and the plant loses nodes where flowers could have formed; pinch too late, and aphids have already spread downward. Simply pinch off about 5-10cm by hand — no tool is needed. Eat the pinched tips right away after blanching, or, if there are plenty, blanch and dry them for winter use.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;That said, pinching is not always beneficial. Pinch a fava plant that is already struggling on poor soil, and it lacks the strength to recover, so yield actually drops. Walk the field first: pinch plants that have reached waist height with dark leaves, and leave alone those still stalled at knee height with pale leaves.

### 8-2. Thinning and Weeding

| Task | Timing | Method |
|---|---|---|
| 1st thinning | 2-3 true leaves | Clear out only the most crowded spots |
| 2nd thinning | 4-5 true leaves | Keep only the healthy plants |
| 1st weeding | 3-4 weeks after sowing | Scrape shallowly. Do not damage the roots |
| 2nd weeding | Before flowering | Combine with hilling |
| Hilling | Around 30cm tall | Mound soil at the base to prevent lodging |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Hilling is especially valuable for fava beans. The plant grows up to 120cm tall and carries heavy pods, so it lodges easily in wind; mounding soil at the base gives it support, and new roots grow from the buried stem. At the same time, it deepens the furrows between plants for better drainage and buries young weeds. In overwintered crops, one round of hilling as growth resumes in spring also has the effect of pressing back down any plants heaved up by frost.

### 8-3. Managing Lodging

| Cause | Response |
|---|---|
| Excess nitrogen | Stick to the recommended fertilizer rate. This is the root cause |
| Overcrowding | Keep proper plant spacing. Fava beans are large plants, so space them out |
| Skipping hilling | Always hill when plants reach about 30cm |
| A windy site | Drive stakes around the field and run a line at waist height |
| Weight of the pods | Picking ripe pods from the bottom as they mature reduces the load |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Fava beans stand upright rather than climbing, so unlike peas they need no stakes or netting. Driving stakes around the field and running one or two lines of string at waist height is usually enough, and in a sheltered spot even that is unnecessary. Where bamboo and string are scarce, this is a clear advantage fava beans have over peas.

## 9. Pests and Diseases

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Fava beans' pest-and-disease pressure is rated C. The estimated yield loss without control, 48.9%, is the average value assigned across legumes as a whole. But in fava beans, a large share of the actual damage is concentrated in a single aphid species, and that aphid can be substantially controlled through pinching. That is the basis for its B rating in pest-control self-reliance.

### 9-1. Major Diseases

| Disease | Symptoms | Conditions | Non-chemical response |
|---|---|---|---|
| Chocolate spot | Small round reddish-brown spots appear on the leaves; in severe cases the spots enlarge and the leaves wither | Humid, cool weather. Fava beans' most damaging disease | Ensure airflow, drainage, and proper spacing. Worsens with a potassium deficiency, so apply wood ash |
| Rust | Orange powdery clumps form on the leaves | Humid conditions late in the growing season | Remove affected leaves, ensure airflow. Avoid it by harvesting early |
| Anthracnose | Black cankers form on stems and pods | Fungal. Humid conditions | Seed selection and crop rotation are key |
| Wilt and root rot | Lower leaves wilt first, roots turn brown and rot | Waterlogging, continuous cropping | Rotate for 2+ years, ensure drainage, raise the beds |
| Viral disease | Leaves mottle in a mosaic pattern and curl | Spread by aphids | No cure. Pull and burn affected plants, reduce aphids |
| Sclerotinia rot | Stems turn soft, white cottony mycelium appears | Cool, humid conditions | Dig out affected plants with their soil and burn them |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Chocolate spot is fava beans' most common and most damaging disease. As the name suggests, chocolate-colored spots appear on the leaves, and in a humid year it can spread across an entire field within days, withering the leaves. Without chemicals, there are three responses. First, plant them with enough space for airflow. Second, dig drainage channels deep to shorten how long the leaves stay wet. Third, keep up potassium — this disease grows noticeably worse in potassium-deficient fava beans, so scattering wood ash between plants is a practical preventive measure.

### 9-2. Major Pests

| Pest | Damage | Non-chemical control |
|---|---|---|
| Fava bean aphid (black bean aphid) | Dense black aphids cover new shoots and flower stalks, sucking sap and spreading viruses. Fava beans' worst pest | Pinching is the most effective step. Spray soapy water (5g soap per 1L water) on the undersides of leaves. Protect ladybugs and lacewings |
| Mites | Suck sap from the undersides of leaves, mottling them yellow | Increase in dry conditions. Spray leaves firmly with water |
| Bean weevil | Lays eggs on pods in the field; larvae eat into the seeds during storage | Full drying followed by airtight storage is key. See Section 15 |
| Leaf miners | Tunnel inside the leaves, leaving white streaks | Pick off and remove affected leaves |
| Rats | Dig up and eat the sown seed underground | Keep to the proper sowing depth and bury wood ash with the seed |
| Birds | Peck at young seedlings | Cover with straw or netting until they emerge |
| Water deer, roe deer | Graze on overwintering fava beans when nothing else is available in winter | Fencing or netting. A real threat for overwintered crops |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Water deer damage to overwintered crops is worse than it sounds. In the depth of winter, a fava bean field can be the only green thing in the whole landscape. Practical responses are to ring the field with netting or line, hang up something with a strong smell, and have people pass by often. Given this risk, it is safer to place an overwintered planting close to the house.

### 9-3. Non-Chemical Control Materials

| Material | How to make it | Target | Caution |
|---|---|---|---|
| Soapy water | 5g unscented soap per 1L water | Aphids, mites | Spraying in the hot midday sun burns the leaves |
| Bordeaux mixture | Dissolve 400g copper sulfate and 400g quicklime separately in 100L water and combine | Chocolate spot, rust | Requires copper to make. Use it right after mixing |
| Wood ash dusting | Dust fine ash on leaves on a dewy morning | Suppresses fungus, strengthens leaves | Also supplies potassium, helping against spot disease |
| Wood ash water | Soak 1.8L of ash in 10L water overnight, use the water on top | Suppresses fungus | Alkaline — dilute well |
| Wood vinegar | Dilute 300-500× | Repellent, growth promotion | The concentrate is strongly acidic — always dilute |
| Hot pepper/garlic water | Grind, steep in water, strain | Repellent | Effect fades quickly — apply often |
| Diluted milk | Dilute milk 5-10× with water | Powdery mildew, mites | Leftovers spoil and smell |

### 9-4. Cultural Control

| Method | Principle |
|---|---|
| Pinching | Physically removes the habitat of fava beans' worst pest, the aphid |
| Crop rotation | Leave legumes out for 2+ years. Wilt and anthracnose decline in the soil |
| Wide spacing | A large plant. Planting it close together lets chocolate spot explode |
| Drainage | Most fava bean disease is tied to excess moisture |
| Maintaining potassium | A potassium deficiency makes plants much more vulnerable to spot disease |
| Seed selection | Anthracnose is seed-borne. Cull spotted or discolored seed |
| No field work after rain | Brushing against wet leaves spreads pathogens |
| Handling residue | Burn or bury diseased residue deep. Return only healthy residue to the field |

## 10. Nutrient Deficiency and Excess

| Symptom | Cause | Natural-material response |
|---|---|---|
| Whole plant pale yellow from the lower leaves up, small plants | Nitrogen deficiency. In fava beans, poor nodulation is the most common cause | First dig up a root to check the nodules. If there are none, ensure proper inoculation next year; for this season, give a small amount of urine diluted 10× |
| Leaf edges brown and dry, chocolate spot severe | Potassium deficiency | Scatter wood ash between plants and lightly work it in |
| Slow growth, leaves tinged purple | Phosphorus deficiency | Bone meal, roasted ground fish bones, wood ash |
| Unusually heavy flower drop, pods barely setting | Boron deficiency, or heat and dryness during flowering | Compost blended from varied materials. For this season, respond with irrigation and pinching |
| Veins green while the tissue between them is yellow | Iron or manganese deficiency. Common in soil that has drifted alkaline | Stop applying ash. Add organic matter through leaf mold and well-rotted compost |
| New leaves small and malformed, growing point dies | Calcium or boron deficiency | Roasted, ground eggshell; ground shell powder |
| Lush stems and leaves but few pods | Excess nitrogen | Stop nitrogen inputs immediately. Redirect nutrients through pinching |
| Overly tall growth that lodges | Excess nitrogen or overcrowding | Hilling and staking with line. Reduce fertilizer next year |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;When you see nitrogen-deficiency symptoms in fava beans, the first move is not to apply fertilizer but to dig up a root. A fava bean's nitrogen supply comes from its root nodules, not the soil. If the nodules are plentiful and pink inside when cut open, yet the leaves are still yellow, the problem is something other than nitrogen. If there are no nodules, or they are white inside, that is the cause — and even then, applying plenty of nitrogen is not the fix, because more nitrogen in the soil only makes the remaining nodules work less.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Potassium is an element that needs particular attention in fava beans. A deficiency not only dries the leaf edges directly but also weakens resistance to chocolate spot at the same time. Wood ash is the easiest source of potassium, and the most reliable cycle is burning what came off a field and returning the ash to that same field. However, adding too much ash pushes pH above 8 and blocks the uptake of iron and manganese, so cap it at 200g per m².

## 11. Intercropping and Crop Rotation

### 11-1. Compatibility

| Compatibility | Crop | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Good | Wheat, barley | Same winter growing season, so they can be grown together. The grain shares the nitrogen the fava beans fix |
| Good | Potato | Plant it where fava beans were just harvested. Diseases don't overlap |
| Good | Cabbage, napa cabbage group | Planted after fava beans, they use the leftover nitrogen |
| Good | Spinach, lettuce | Grows between fava bean rows, sharing the space |
| Fair | Maize | Plant in summer after harvesting fava beans. Hard to grow at the same time |
| Poor | Garlic, onion group | Reported to release compounds that suppress root-nodule bacteria |
| Poor | Other legumes | Pathogens and nematodes build up |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Intercropping fava beans with grain over winter is a long-used practice in Europe. Plant fava beans between rows of wheat or barley, and the two crops winter together and grow up together in spring. Some of the nitrogen the fava beans fix flows to the grain through the roots, and the grain's upright stalks help keep the fava beans from lodging in wind. However, fava bean yield drops by half under 40% shade, so leave the rows wide enough that it does not sit too deep in the grain's shade.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Harvesting fava beans in May-June creates a real advantage for planning the field. That timing lines up exactly with the sowing time for summer crops. Harvest the fava beans, cut and lay down the residue, then plant sweet potato, maize, or a late soybean in that spot, and you effectively get two harvests from one year — with the second crop using the nitrogen the fava beans left behind.

### 11-2. Crop Rotation

| Item | Standard |
|---|---|
| Rest period | 2+ years. RDA's 2-year break group |
| Ideal cycle | 3-4 years |
| Continuous-cropping damage | Wilt, anthracnose, and nematodes build up |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Fava beans' A rating for continuous-cropping tolerance is good among legumes. It has more room than peas' 5-7 years or kidney beans' and chickpeas' 3 years. But tolerating it is not the same as it being fine to replant year after year. Push continuous cropping past 3 years, and wilt builds up until plants dying at flowering becomes common.

| A sample 4-year rotation |
|---|
| Year 1: Fava beans (overwintered), then sweet potato or maize (summer) |
| Year 2: Cabbage/napa cabbage group, or leafy greens |
| Year 3: Potato or a grain crop |
| Year 4: A root vegetable or green manure |
| Back to fava beans |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;For a field too small to leave room for rotation, here is a compromise. The simplest approach is dividing the field into four plots and moving the fava bean planting one plot over each year. If the field is too small even to divide, shift at least the position of the fava bean row each year. Simply planting potato where fava beans grew last year, and fava beans where potato grew, slows the buildup of pathogens considerably.

## 12. Harvest and Threshing

### 12-1. Judging Harvest Time

| Use | Harvest time | Criterion |
|---|---|---|
| Young shoots (namul) | At flowering, during pinching | The tender 5-10cm at the stem tip |
| Green fava beans (as a vegetable) | 70-90 days after sowing | While the pods are plump, glossy, and still green |
| Dry seed | 100-120 days after sowing | Once the pods have dried black and the seeds have hardened |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;A ripe fava bean pod turns black rather than brown. This differs from other legumes, and a first-time grower can easily mistake it for disease. The pods at the lower nodes blacken first, so harvest proceeds from the bottom up in sequence.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There is a trick to timing green fava beans right. Press a pod with your fingers — the right moment is when you can clearly feel the outline of the seeds while the pod still has some spring to it. Split one open: if the white, cottony membrane wrapping the seeds is still moist, it is the right time; once that membrane has begun to dry and yellow, it is already too late. Peeling a green fava bean reveals pale green flesh inside, which is blanched or fried to eat.

> **If rain is forecast, harvest rather than wait for full ripeness. A slightly underripe fava bean can still be dried and used, but a pod that has been wetted and dried repeatedly splits and drops its seeds, and mold takes hold in what remains.**

### 12-2. Harvesting Technique

| Order | Detail |
|---|---|
| 1 | Do this on a clear, dry day, after 10am once the dew has burned off |
| 2 | Twist off green fava bean pods by hand. Pulling damages the stem |
| 3 | For dry seed, cut the stem base with a sickle. Leave the roots in the field |
| 4 | Tie into bundles and hang upside down under the eaves or in a shed to dry |
| 5 | Thresh once the pods are fully dry and rattle when shaken |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Cutting the stem and leaving the root behind is the right call in a disaster situation. Fava bean roots are thick and carry large nodules, holding as much as 15-30kg of nitrogen per hectare. Pull the plant out, and that nitrogen leaves the field with it; leave it, and it rots down into nutrients for the next crop.

### 12-3. Threshing Without Tools

| Method | Tool | Procedure | Efficiency |
|---|---|---|---|
| Stomping in a sack | A thick burlap or cloth sack | Put dried stems in a sack, tie the opening, and stomp on it | Most convenient for a small amount. Seeds don't scatter |
| Flail | A tool with striking rods hung from a long handle | Spread a mat, lay the stems thin, and strike from above | Suits a medium scale |
| Beating with a stick | A wooden stick | Put in a sack or spread on a mat and beat it | Substitutes when there is no flail |
| Cracking open by hand | None | Twist each pod open one at a time | Much faster than other beans, since the seeds are large |
| Rubbing underfoot | None | Spread on a mat and rub, rolling with a shod foot | Suits a small amount |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Because fava bean seeds are so large, cracking pods open by hand is far more efficient than with other legumes. A dry pod holds only two or three seeds and the thick pod splits easily, so a small operation has little need to bother with a flail. However, a pod that thick resists opening if it isn't fully dry, so whether you beat it or crack it by hand, wait until it is completely dried through.

### 12-4. Winnowing — Sorting by Wind

| Method | Principle | Procedure |
|---|---|---|
| Winnowing (tossing in the wind) | Fava beans are heavy; the pods and debris are light | On a windy day, load a winnowing basket or wide dish, lift it high, and pour slowly |
| Sieve-tossing | Shake up and down, tossing forward | Heavy seeds stay back, light material is carried forward |
| Fanning | One person pours while another creates a breeze with a fan | Used on a windless day |
| Screening | Sorted by hole size | Fava beans, being large, sort especially easily by screen |
| Floating in water | Empty and insect-eaten seeds are light | Pour into a tub of water and skim off what floats. Always re-dry what is recovered |
| Hand-sorting | By eye | With seeds this large, hand-sorting is the most accurate method |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Fava beans, being large-seeded, are the easiest of all legumes to winnow. One pass of winnowing clears out most of the chaff, and what is left can simply be picked over by eye and by hand. There are three tricks to winnowing: choose early morning or dusk, when the wind blows steadily; pour in a thin stream rather than all at once; and repeat it two or three times. Floating in water sorts and selects at the same time, but a fava bean that has been soaked must be fully re-dried the same day, and is never used for seed.
