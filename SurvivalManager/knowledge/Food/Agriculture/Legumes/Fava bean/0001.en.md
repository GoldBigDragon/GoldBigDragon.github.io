## Introduction
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Fava bean (Vicia faba), also called the silkworm bean, faba bean, or broad bean, is an annual or biennial legume that came from the eastern Mediterranean and southwest Asia. Its defining trait is cold tolerance S: it is the only legume that withstands -10℃ and overwinters in the field. Sown around the start of winter in the south, it is harvested the following year from mid May to June, the first protein to come out of the field that year, filling the food gap after old grain runs out and before summer crops ripen. Its overall grade is A (50.2 points, 15th of 183 valid species), with short term A (48.6), long term S (51.7), and supplementary food SS (65.9 points), and protein density, fertilization, and storability are SS. Calories per area are C, at 1,023 kcal per ㎡, only 0.39 of the self-sufficiency line, so it cannot be a staple, and domestic cultivation spread is B, so outside 3-4 provinces seed is hard to get. There is one risk not in the grade table: favism, acute hemolysis in people with G6PD deficiency, which cooking cannot remove, so read section 10 first before deciding to grow it.

> **The value of fava bean lies not in yield but in when and where it is grown. It holds the winter field where nothing else grows, gives the first food in spring, and leaves nitrogen for the crop that follows.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Continuous-cropping tolerance A is a 2-year rest group, more relaxed than the 5-7 years of pea. Cooking and processing D is a daily cost, since the thick seed coat means soaking 8 hours and boiling 1 hour. Pest-control self-reliance B is because the damage is concentrated in one aphid species that pinching out the tips controls. The grade for each item is in the Crop Evaluation Results document.

## 2. Plant Overview
| Item | Content |
|:---|:---|
| Classification | Genus Vicia of the legume family. Mainly self-pollinating, but cross-pollination by bees is 30-50% |
| Leaves, stem | Pinnately compound leaves with 2-6 pairs of leaflets and no tendrils. The stem is upright and square in section, 80-120 cm tall, so stakes are not required |
| Roots, flowers | A straight taproot goes deep and thick nodules cluster on its upper part. Flowers are butterfly-shaped, white with black spots, strongly scented, and bees visit them readily |
| Pods, seeds | Thick and long, 15-30 cm, with a cottony white membrane inside. The seed is flat and large, 2 g each, the largest among legumes; when ripe the pod dries black, not brown |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Most of the flowers drop by themselves and only about one in ten becomes a pod; water, temperature, and bee visits at flowering decide that ratio. Per 100 g of dried seed: 341 kcal, protein 26.12 g (30.6% of calories), fat 1.5-2 g, carbohydrate about 58 g, dietary fiber about 25 g, iron 6-7 mg, calcium about 100 mg, magnesium about 190 mg, and it is very rich in folate, and being low in fat it goes rancid slowly. Young pods and sprouts are rich in levodopa (L-dopa), and eating a lot can cause dizziness or nausea.

## 3. Growing Environment and Varieties
| Condition | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Temperature | Germination 5℃ or above (optimum 15-20℃). Growth 5-28℃ (optimum 10-20℃), and growth stops above 25℃. Seed set 10-24℃. If 25℃ or above lasts a few days at flowering, flowers and young pods drop |
| Cold tolerance | Down to -10℃, and it dies if -15℃ or below continues. If it grows too large before winter it freezes to death instead, so 3-4 true leaves and 10-15 cm tall endure best |
| Water, soil | Well-drained sandy loam (adapts from sandy to clay soil), pH 5.5-8.0 (optimum 6.0-7.0, and below 5.5 nitrogen fixation stops), effective soil depth 40 cm or more (optimum 60 cm). If submerged for a day or more the roots are damaged and the nodules lose function first |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In the inland central region, -15℃ lasts for many days and overwintering mostly fails, so go with spring cultivation. Overwintering is stable on the southern coast and Jeju, and the areas between vary by year, so spread the risk by planting both autumn and spring batches.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The large-seeded food type, 2 g or more per seed, yields more and is the basis for food and storage, while the small-seeded landrace, 0.7-1.5 g per seed, is tough against cold and poor ground and suits poor fields, cold winters, or short seed (the same weight plants a much wider area). Since there are practically no formally registered varieties in Korea, securing seed beforehand and carrying it on by saving your own is the only way; for the first two or three years concentrate on multiplying seed rather than yield, and keep selecting plants that came through the winter in that locality.

## 4. Field Preparation and Sowing
| Item | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Making the field | Plow 20-30 cm deep or more, beds 20 cm or higher, drainage ditches 30-50 cm deep. Fully matured compost 2,000-3,000 kg per 1,000㎡ |
| Fertilization (1,000㎡) | Nitrogen 3-5 kg (a minimum for the start, and more makes it lodge, draws aphids, and reduces pods), phosphorus 10-15 kg, potassium 10-15 kg (all as basal fertilizer), lime 150-200 kg on acidic soil (3 weeks before sowing). Substitute resources are wood ash (100-200 g per ㎡), bone meal, and roasted, ground shells and eggshells (in place of lime) |
| Nodule inoculation | Coat seed lightly moistened with water with topsoil 5-15 cm deep from a field that grew fava bean, pea, lentil, or vetch, in shade, and plant within 30 minutes (the bacteria are weak to direct sun, drying, and heat, and inoculation is not done together with disinfection). Soil from a soybean field is useless because the bacterial species differs |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Around flowering, lift with a spade with the soil, wash, and split the nodules. With 20 or more clustered on the upper taproot and red inside, it went well; 5 or fewer is failure; and tiny nodules only at the root tips mean infection by native bacteria with low efficiency, so change the inoculum source the next year.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Fixation per hectare is 150-200 kg under good conditions (40-80 kg if poor), 110-140 kg leaves with the seed, and 40-60 kg remains in the stalks and leaves. Cut the stalks and leaves into 20-30 cm lengths and cover with them, do not dig up the roots, and if you fed the stalks to livestock return the manure to that field so as not to plunder the soil. A parched plot can be revived by giving up the seed and plowing the crop under at flowering, which leaves 150-200 kg of nitrogen.

| Sowing | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Timing | Autumn and overwintering cultivation is 40-50 days before the expected first frost, around the start of winter (early November in the south, harvest from mid May to mid June the next year). Planted in September it grows too large before winter and freezes to death, and planted in December it is caught in frozen ground before rooting. Spring cultivation is as soon as the soil thaws, late February-early March (harvest late May-late June), and past mid March flowering meets the heat and yield falls sharply |
| Seed preparation | Discard seeds with spots, discoloration, wrinkles, or insect holes. The seed coat is thick so soaking works well: soak for 24 hours (beyond that the inside turns soft and rots), disinfect in 50-55℃ water for 10-15 minutes, and coat with fine wood ash to keep off rats and birds |
| Method | Depth 5-7 cm (deeper for overwintering), 1-2 seeds per hill, row spacing 45-90 cm, plant spacing 15-30 cm (wider for large seed), 4-8 plants per ㎡. Cover with straw or netting until germination |

## 5. Water Management and Cultivation Management
| Period | Water management |
|:---|:---|
| Sowing to germination | Keep the soil from drying out completely |
| Early growth | Leave it to natural rainfall (dryness makes the roots spread deep) |
| Winter (overwintering) | Manage only drainage (water standing as ground freezes and thaws is the most dangerous, so open the water channels now and then even in midwinter) |
| Flowering to pod filling | Water enough that the soil does not dry (the surest way to lower flower drop) |
| Maturity | Stop irrigation (pods do not dry and mold grows) |

| Management | Method |
|:---|:---|
| Pinching out the tips | When flowers begin to open on the lower nodes and buds remain above, pick off the top 5-10 cm of the stem by hand. Nutrients are pushed to the flowers and pods already open so yield rises, the aphids crowded on the youngest growing point are removed, and the picked tips are blanched and eaten as greens. Too early reduces the nodes where flowers can set, and too late the aphids have already spread downward. Do not do it on poor plants whose growth is stunted (stopped at knee height with pale leaves) |
| Thinning, weeding | Thin at 2-3 true leaves only where overcrowded, and at 4-5 true leaves leave only healthy plants. Weed 3-4 weeks after sowing by scraping shallowly, and once more before flowering together with hilling |
| Hilling, lodging | Mounding soil at the base when the plant is about 30 cm gives support, new roots form on the buried stem, and the furrows deepen so water drains. Lodging comes from excess nitrogen and dense planting, and in windy places drive stakes around the field and run a line at waist height |
| Frost heave, animals | Plants lifted in winter are pressed down with the foot and covered with 5-10 cm of straw or leaves. If the fava bean is the only green thing in the winter field, water deer come, so fence it or net it and keep it in a field near the house |

## 6. Pests, Diseases, and Nutrient Problems
| Pest or disease | Symptoms and conditions | Control without chemicals |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Chocolate spot | Small round reddish-brown spots on leaves that grow until the leaves dry and die. The worst disease of fava bean, spreading when humid and cool | Plant sparsely for airflow and dig drainage ditches deep. It worsens when potassium is short, so give wood ash |
| Rust, anthracnose | Orange powdery clumps on leaves (humid late growth) / black cankers on stems and pods (seed-borne) | Remove diseased leaves, secure airflow, harvest early / seed selection and rotation |
| Fusarium wilt, root rot, virus diseases | Wilting from the lower leaves and roots rotting brown (excess moisture, continuous cropping) / leaves mottled and crinkled (spread by aphids) | Rotation of 2 years or more, drainage, raising the beds / no cure, so pull and burn and reduce aphids |
| Black bean aphid | Black aphids packed on new shoots and flower stalks suck sap and carry viruses, the worst pest | Pinching out the tips is the most effective. Spray soapy water (5 g of soap per 1 L of water) on the underside of leaves and protect ladybugs and lacewings |
| Broad bean weevil | Lays eggs on pods in the field and eats the seed in storage | Dry completely and seal (see section 9) |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Bordeaux mixture is made by dissolving 400 g of copper sulfate and 400 g of quicklime separately in 100 L of water and mixing, and is used at once on chocolate spot and rust. Fine ash dusted on leaves on a dewy morning suppresses mold and also supplies potassium. Brushing wet leaves carries pathogens, so do not enter the field after rain, burn or bury deeply diseased residue, and return only healthy residue.

| Symptom | Cause and remedy |
|:---|:---|
| Whole plant pale yellow from the lower leaves and small | Nitrogen deficiency (poor nodules are the most common cause). Dig up the roots and check the nodules before applying fertilizer, and if there are none, make inoculation certain the next year and this year give a little urine diluted 10 times |
| Leaf margins dry brown and chocolate spot is severe | Potassium deficiency. Scatter wood ash between plants and rake it in lightly, up to 200 g per ㎡ (more blocks iron and manganese uptake) |
| Many flowers drop and few pods set | Boron deficiency, or heat and dryness at flowering. Compost of mixed materials, and this year water and pinch out the tips |

## 7. Intercropping and Crop Rotation
| Compatibility | Companions |
|:---|:---|
| Good | Wheat, barley (same winter season, and the grain shares the nitrogen of fava bean while its straight stems prevent lodging, but yield falls in shade so space the rows widely), potato (planted in the place after the fava bean is harvested), cabbage and Chinese cabbage (use the leftover nitrogen) |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;If fava bean is harvested in May-June, the place meshes with the sowing time of summer crops: after cutting the stalks and leaves and covering with them, sweet potato, maize, or late soybean can be planted, giving two crops in a year and the second using the leftover nitrogen. The rest period is 2 years or more, and continuing for more than 3 years accumulates Fusarium wilt, anthracnose, and nematodes. An example rotation is year 1 fava bean (overwintered) followed by sweet potato or maize (summer) → year 2 Chinese cabbage and cabbage group or leafy vegetables → year 3 potato or grain → fava bean again. If the field is small, divide it into four plots and move one plot a year, or at least change the position of the rows every year.

## 8. Harvest and Seed Saving
| Harvest | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Timing | Green beans 70-90 days after sowing, when pods are plump and glossy, still green, and the white membrane inside is moist. Dry seed 100-120 days after sowing, when pods have dried black and the seeds are hard |
| Method | Twist green pods off (pulling damages the stem). Dry pods blacken from the lower nodes, so harvest from bottom to top, cut the stem base with a sickle and leave the roots in the field. Tie in bundles and hang upside down under eaves or in a shed to dry. If rain is forecast, do not wait for full ripeness and harvest (pods that get wet and dry again repeatedly burst and drop the seed) |
| Threshing, winnowing | After the pods are completely dry, beat with a flail or shell by hand (shelling by hand is efficient because the seeds are large). On a windy day pour slowly to blow off the husks, sort with a sieve, skim off floating seeds in water, and dry them again that same day (not for seed) |

| Seed saving | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Selection, harvest | Mark in the field in advance plants with no disease symptoms, many pods, and good survival through winter. Harvest after the pods have dried completely black, later than for food, thresh separately and shell by hand |
| Isolation | Cross-pollination by bees is 30-50%, so keep other varieties several hundred meters away or grow only one variety a year |
| Sorting, drying | Discard spots, wrinkles, discoloration, and small seeds, leaving only large, glossy seeds. Moisture 12% so that a bite leaves no dent and the bean cracks crisply. Seed lasts 3 years sealed at room temperature |

## 9. Storage
| Step | Method |
|:---|:---|
| Drying | Hang the stalks upside down under eaves for 3-4 days, and after threshing spread thin in the sun for 3-5 days (the seeds are large so it takes longer than soybean, and turn them often). Moisture 12%, and since the outside dries first, split a bean to check the inside |
| Broad bean weevil control | Eggs come in already stuck on in the field, so in half a year a sack becomes a hollow shell with holes. Complete sealing matters most (oxygen falls and the insects die): fill the container to leave no empty space and use several small containers rather than one big one. Mix in sifted wood ash at 2-5% of the volume (rinse before eating), diatomaceous earth 1-3 g per 1 kg (only when dry), and oil coating 2-5 mL of cooking oil per 1 kg (not for seed) |
| Containers, checking | Glass jars and metal tins last 3 years or more, clay jars and underground storage pits 2-3 years, and thick plastic 1-2 years (rats chew through). Put an ear to the container: rustling means insects, a musty smell is mold, and small round holes on the surface mean the weevils have passed a generation. Check once every two months |

## 10. Cooking and Precautions
> **If a person deficient in the G6PD enzyme eats fava bean, red blood cells are destroyed rapidly in acute hemolysis. This is called favism. It can be fatal to children, and attacks have been reported from merely breathing in fava bean pollen.**

| Favism | Content |
|:---|:---|
| Cause | Vicine and convicine in fava bean become strong oxidants (divicine, isouramil) in the gut and destroy red blood cells lacking G6PD in large numbers within hours |
| Who | G6PD deficiency (more common in males because of X-chromosome inheritance, and common among people of Mediterranean, Middle Eastern, African, and Southeast Asian descent). Children are at greatest risk |
| Symptoms | Hours later chills, fever, headache, and pain in the belly and back → urine dark brown or cola-colored → jaundice, severe pallor, and shortness of breath → in severe cases unable to stand, clouded consciousness, and almost no urine |
| Removal | Boiling, roasting, and fermenting do not remove the risk (peeling and soaking reduce it only partly) |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In a disaster there is no way to test for G6PD deficiency, so a small trial is the only safeguard. Give anyone newly arrived, children, and anyone who has never eaten fava bean, without exception, only five or six beans (two or three for children) and watch for a day or two. If the urine turns dark brown or cola-colored, stop at once, keep the person at rest, and never feed it to them again. If a family member has had jaundice and anemia after eating fava bean or certain drugs, or if anyone is deficient, grow pea or cowpea instead of fava bean, and keep the deficient person away from the field at flowering.

| Component | What it does | How to remove |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Trypsin inhibitors, lectins | Block protein-digesting enzymes and stick to the gut lining and hinder absorption | Soak 8 hours, then heat 1 hour in boiling water |
| Phytic acid, oligosaccharides, mold toxins | Block absorption of iron, zinc, and calcium / gut fermentation causes gas / made by mold during storage | Soaking, sprouting, and fermenting cut it by 60-80% / discard the soaking water / moldy beans are not made safe by washing or cooking, so discard them |

| Treatment | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Soaking, peeling, boiling | Soak 8 hours or more in room-temperature water (12 hours for large seeds) and always discard the soaking water. Pressing a soaked bean with a finger slips the skin off, which cuts cooking time nearly in half and makes it easier to digest, and boil in boiling water for 1 hour (30-40 minutes if peeled) until it mashes easily |
| Saving fuel | Pressure cooking is 15 minutes under pressure for soaked beans. Retained-heat cooking is boiling 15 minutes, turning off the fire, and wrapping the pot in a straw or rice-hull box or bedding for 2-3 hours. Roasted fava beans are soaked, dried, and roasted without oil and eaten as they are |
| Green beans, shoots | Peel green beans and blanch a few minutes. Shoots are blanched and eaten as greens, or salted, or blanched and dried for winter |
| Preserved products | Fava bean flour (3-6 months sealed), pickled young shoots (a few months), blanched and dried green beans (a few months), and fava bean doenjang made by boiling, mashing, and fermenting (an alternative when soybeans are short, though fermentation does not remove vicine and convicine) |

## 11. Annual Work Schedule
| Month | Work |
|:---|:---|
| Jan-Feb | Check storage, tour the overwintering field and press down frost-heaved plants. In late February, spring sowing, field planning, and liming |
| Mar-Apr | In March, hilling of overwintered plants, weeding, and checking drainage ditches. In April, flowering begins, pinching out the tips, aphid management, and checking nodules |
| May-June | In May, pod filling, concentrated watering, and harvesting green beans from the overwintered crop. In June, harvesting dry seed from the overwintered crop, green beans from the spring crop, and hanging to dry |
| July-Aug | In July, thresh, winnow, dry, store, and return stalks and leaves. In August, manage the following crop and check storage |
| Sep-Oct | In September, prepare the autumn field, apply lime (3 weeks before sowing), and add compost. In October, make beds, dig drainage ditches, and select seed |
| Nov-Dec | In November, autumn sowing. In December, cover with straw and leaves, keep animals off, and check storage |

> **If you first test for favism with a small trial, time sowing 40-50 days before the first frost, provide drainage, pinch out the tips at flowering, and return the stalks, leaves, and roots to the field, fava bean is a crop that gives protein first in spring from the winter field.**
