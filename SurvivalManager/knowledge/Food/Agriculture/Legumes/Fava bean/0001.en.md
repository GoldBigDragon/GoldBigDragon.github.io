## Introduction

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Fava bean (Vicia faba) is also called the broad bean or horse bean. It originated in the eastern Mediterranean and southwest Asia and, like the pea, is one of the oldest crops ever domesticated by humans. It has been found in Egyptian tombs and Bronze Age European sites, and in medieval Europe it was the protein source that fed the poor until the potato arrived. It reached the Korean Peninsula in the late Joseon period and was grown in some regions into the early 1980s before nearly disappearing.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;From a disaster-preparedness standpoint, fava bean's place is clear. Its cold hardiness is grade S: it withstands -10°C and overwinters in the field. It is the most cold-tolerant of all the legume crops. Sown in southern South Korea around ipdong (early November, the traditional start of winter), it is harvested the following May-June, making it the first protein crop to come off the field that year. Few crops fill this gap — the period often called borigogae (the "barley hump," the hunger gap in spring when the previous year's grain has run out but the summer crops have not yet ripened) — as well as fava bean does. This is where the overall grade of A, 50.2 points, comes from.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In numbers: 100g of dried fava beans contains 341kcal and 26.12g of protein. The protein share of calories is 30.6%, second only to soybean, and this criterion is graded SS. A fertilizer-demand grade of SS means its nitrogen-fixing ability is at the very top among legumes — in fact, Europe grows fava bean purely as a green-manure crop. It also scored high across the board: temperature range A, water use A, storability SS, and continuous-cropping tolerance A. It is an easy, resilient crop to grow.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;However, fava bean carries a risk called favism. In people with a G6PD (glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase) deficiency, eating fava beans triggers acute hemolysis — the rapid breakdown of red blood cells. This is common among people of Mediterranean, Middle Eastern, African, and Southeast Asian ancestry, and it can be fatal in children. Cases have even been reported where merely inhaling the pollen triggered an attack. This risk cannot be removed by cooking. This is why Section 14 must be read before deciding to grow and eat fava beans.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There is also a common misconception about legumes in general — the idea that planting beans automatically improves the soil. That is only half true. Fava bean, too, partners with root-nodule bacteria (rhizobia) to draw nitrogen from the air, but once the seed is harvested and carried off the field, more nitrogen can leave the field than was fixed. Whether a fava bean field enriches the soil or depletes it comes down to how the stems and leaves are handled. This document works through that fork in the road with numbers in Section 5.
