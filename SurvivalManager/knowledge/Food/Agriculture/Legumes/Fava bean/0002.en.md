### 1-3. How to Read the Strengths

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Four criteria carry an SS: protein density, fertilizer demand, infrastructure needs, and storability. Above that, an S goes to cold hardiness and taste. An A goes to micronutrients, water use, temperature range, continuous-cropping tolerance, yield retention without synthetic inputs, and yield stability. Notably, there is not a single E. Fava bean has no criterion where it falls apart.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Cold hardiness at S is the centerpiece of this table. Fava bean is the only legume that withstands -10°C and overwinters in the field. Pea and lentil each withstand only -5°C — fava bean endures twice that. This single fact makes fava bean's growing calendar completely different from every other legume. Sown in southern South Korea in early November to overwinter, it is harvested the following year from mid-May to early June — the first protein to come off the field that year, earlier even than potato. In a disaster, the spring food gap is not a mere inconvenience but a period when people genuinely go hungry. The simple fact that something ripens during that gap is worth more than any other number here.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The fertilizer-demand grade of SS is exceptional even among legumes. Fava bean's nitrogen-fixing ability is the highest of any legume, fixing up to 150-200kg per hectare under good conditions — more than soybean's 100-150kg. This is why Europe grows fava bean not to eat it but to revive the soil: sown in winter and turned whole into the soil in spring, it leaves nitrogen in the field for the next crop to use. In a world where fertilizer plants have stopped running, this ability can be worth more than the grain yield itself.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Temperature range A is also worth noting. Fava bean's viable growing range is 10-24°C, a span of 14°C — much wider than soybean, mung bean, and kidney bean, which all have a span of only 10°C. This means fava bean is less rattled by the mood swings of spring weather, and unlike summer legumes that can lose an entire crop to a single late frost, fava bean can ride that out.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Continuous-cropping tolerance A also matters in practice. By RDA standards it is in the 2-year break group, more forgiving than pea's 5-7 years or kidney bean and chickpea's 3 years. Where field space is tight, this difference greatly increases the freedom to plan crop layout.

> **Fava bean's value lies not in its yield but in its timing and its effect on the soil. It holds the winter field when nothing else grows, is the first to offer food in spring, and leaves nitrogen behind for the crop that follows.**

### 1-4. How to Read the Weaknesses

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Three criteria carry a D: perenniality, sunlight response, and cooking & processing. C goes to calories per area, pest & disease pressure, tillage energy, and non-food value. B goes to speed to harvest, pest-control self-reliance, and prevalence in South Korea.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Prevalence in South Korea at B is, in practice, the first wall you run into. Fava bean was grown in some regions into the early 1980s before nearly disappearing, and today it is grown only on a small scale in 3-4 provinces. Outside its main production areas, seed is not easy to find. Getting seed after a disaster has already struck is close to impossible, so anyone planning to include fava bean must secure seed in advance. Fortunately, it can be self-seed-saved and the seed keeps for 3 years, so once you have secured it, you can keep it going on your own.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Calories per area at C is the same story as other legumes: 1,023kcal per m², or 0.39× the self-sufficiency line. Even so, it ranks near the top among legumes. Compared with soybean's 0.38×, pea's 0.34×, and mung bean's 0.16×, fava bean's productivity per area is on the higher side for a legume, because its large seeds pack more weight into each pod. Even so, it cannot serve as a staple on its own, so it should be paired with a cereal or a tuber crop (tubers/storage roots).

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Cooking & processing at D is a cost paid every single day. Dried fava beans need 8 hours of soaking and an hour of boiling, and because the seed coat is thick, it is better to remove it. The larger the seed, the longer it takes for heat to reach the center. Where fuel is scarce, this burden is not small. Section 16 covers ways to cut fuel use separately.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There is also one risk that does not show up as a criterion in this grading table: favism. This condition, which triggers acute hemolysis in people with a G6PD deficiency, is a deciding factor in whether to choose fava bean at all — independent of its yield or how easy it is to grow. If anyone in the family has this condition, it is safer not to bring fava bean into the field. Section 14 covers this in detail.

## 2. What Kind of Plant Is This?

### 2-1. Taxonomy and Origin

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Fava bean is an annual or biennial herb in the genus Vicia, family Fabaceae (legumes). Unlike most other edible legumes, which mostly belong to different genera, fava bean shares its genus with wild vetch — the same wild vetch commonly found in fields and along field edges across South Korea. This fact matters in practice: the root-nodule bacteria that partner with fava bean are the same species as those for pea, lentil, and vetch, so soil from a field that has grown any of these can be used as an inoculant source.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Its origin is thought to be the region stretching from the eastern Mediterranean to southwest Asia, but it is one of the rare crops whose wild ancestor has still not been clearly identified, because domesticated forms already appear at Neolithic sites. Wherever it originated, fava bean's physiology is tuned to a Mediterranean climate: it grows through cool, rainy winters and sets seed before the heat arrives. This is why, in South Korea, fava bean is a spring and autumn crop, and midsummer is the season it cannot tolerate.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;One more point: fava bean self-pollinates, but not as completely as pea does. Its flowers are large and fragrant, so bees visit often, and depending on conditions anywhere from 30% up to nearly half of pollination can be cross-pollination. So if two varieties are planted close together, the seed the following year can show mixed traits. To keep a pure line, the safest approach is to separate varieties by several hundred meters, or to grow only one variety per year. This is why seed saving is graded S and yet isolation is still recommended.

### 2-2. Appearance

| Organ | Characteristics |
|---|---|
| Leaves | Pinnately compound. Bears 2-6 pairs of leaflets that are thick and grayish-green. No tendrils |
| Stem | Upright, square in cross-section. 80-120cm tall, branching from the base |
| Roots | A straight taproot goes deep, bearing thick root nodules |
| Flowers | Butterfly-shaped, white with black spots. Strongly fragrant, attracting bees readily |
| Pods | Thick and long, reaching 15-30cm. Lined inside with a cottony white membrane |
| Seeds | Flat and large. A single seed reaches 2g, the largest of any legume |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Having no tendrils is the most practical difference between fava bean and pea. Fava bean's stem is upright and sturdy enough to stand on its own. Staking is not essential, which is a real advantage where bamboo and string are unavailable. Even so, since it can grow up to 120cm tall and the pods are heavy, it will lodge in a windy spot. Driving stakes around the field edge and running a line at waist height is usually enough.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The cottony white membrane lining the inside of the pod is another distinctive feature. Split open a young pod and the seeds are wrapped in this soft, cushioning lining, which protects them and keeps them moist. Because of this membrane, fava bean pods are much thicker and heavier than other beans' pods. This is why the pods themselves are not eaten — only the seeds inside are taken out and eaten.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The large seed size brings both an advantage and a drawback. At 2g a seed, harvesting the same number of seeds yields more weight, and shelling is easier. On the other hand, sowing takes more seed, and it takes longer for heat to reach the center when cooking, which uses more fuel.

### 2-3. Growth Stages

| Stage | Timing | Key Physiology | Key Nutrients |
|---|---|---|---|
| Germination & seedling | 0-4 weeks after sowing | Seed germinates and root nodules begin forming | Phosphorus, a small amount of nitrogen |
| Vegetative growth | 4-8 weeks | Stem stands up and branches. In the overwintering type, the plant spends winter in this state | Nitrogen supplied by root nodules |
| Flowering | 8-10 weeks | Flowers open in sequence starting from the lower nodes | Phosphorus, boron |
| Pod filling | 10-12 weeks | Pods lengthen and seeds thicken | Potassium, phosphorus |
| Maturity | 12-14 weeks | Pods dry and blacken from the bottom up | Irrigation stopped |

> **Fava bean sheds most of its own flowers. A single plant produces hundreds of flowers, but only about one in ten becomes a pod. Water, temperature, and bee visits during flowering determine that ratio.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Knowing in advance that the flower-drop rate is high keeps you from being needlessly alarmed when you look at the field. Flowers falling in showers is normal, not disease. Still, there are ways to raise that ratio somewhat. First, keep the soil from drying out during flowering. Second, pinch the growing tip so nutrients concentrate on the remaining flowers. Third, leave flowering plants around the edge of the field so bees visit more often. Fava bean flowers are strongly fragrant and draw bees well, which is also why the crop has value as a nectar source.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There is also something worth noting about early nitrogen. Root nodules do not work right from the moment of sowing. It takes three to four weeks for the seed to germinate, the roots to extend, the bacteria to infect them, and the nodules to form and start supplying nitrogen. In the meantime, the young fava bean plant lives on the nutrients stored in the seed. Because fava bean's seed is large, it has ample stored nutrients and gets through this stretch better than other beans. Even so, if the soil has no nitrogen at all, early growth will be slow, so a small amount should go into the base fertilizer. It must be a small amount, though: when the soil already has plenty of nitrogen, the plant feels no need to strike a deal with the bacteria, and it cuts back nodule formation on its own.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Fava bean contains one compound not found in other legumes: levodopa, or L-DOPA. It is especially abundant in young pods and sprouts, and it is the same substance that is the main ingredient in Parkinson's disease medication. In small amounts it causes no problem, but because of this compound, eating a lot of fava beans can cause dizziness or nausea in some people. There is also room for interaction in people taking certain medications. The advice to start with a small amount the first time you eat it is due not only to favism but to this compound as well.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The ample iron and folate are worth noting. When meat and dairy are gone, anemia is a real threat that creeps in slowly over months. Fava bean has 6-7mg of iron per 100g, and its folate is on the high side even among legumes. However, phytic acid interferes with iron absorption, so soaking, sprouting, and fermenting to reduce phytic acid also carry real nutritional value.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Having only 1.5-2% fat is an advantage for storage. Soybean, with 15-20% fat, turns rancid within months once ground into flour, but fava bean flour keeps for much longer. It trades a lower calorie density for easier storage.
