## 8. Crop Management

### 8-1. Thinning and Weed Control

| Task | Timing | Method |
|---|---|---|
| First thinning | 1-2 true leaves | Leave 2 plants per hole |
| Second thinning | 3-4 true leaves | Remove diseased plants |
| First weeding | 2 weeks after sowing | Scrape the surface shallowly |
| Second weeding | 4 weeks after sowing, before flowering | Combine with hilling |
| After flowering | No weeding | Roots are damaged and flowers drop |

> **No herbicide is registered for mung bean, and herbicides used on other beans injure it. Even in a world with agrochemicals, mung bean is a crop that has to be weeded by hand.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;So weed control in mung bean has to rely on physical means from the start. The most effective is mulching. Covering the ground between the rows with straw, rice hulls, or fallen leaves right after sowing cuts weeds sharply, and it also prevents waterlogging damage and soil splash. Without mulch, weed twice, at 2 weeks and 4 weeks after sowing, and stop once flowering begins. Mung bean roots spread shallowly, so putting a hoe in after flowering cuts the roots and actually lowers the yield.

### 8-2. Preventing Lodging - Stakes

| Item | Method |
|---|---|
| Timing | When plants have grown to 30-40cm |
| Method | Drive sticks 60-80cm long into the ground at both ends of the row and tie a cord between them to hold the plants up |
| Spacing | One stake every 2-3m |
| Height | A single line 30-40cm above the ground is usually enough |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Mung bean stems are weak and fall over easily. When rain makes the pods heavy, the plants simply lie flat. Lodged mung bean causes three kinds of loss. Pods touch the soil and rot, blocked airflow lets disease spread, and when you go in several times to pick, you cannot see which pods are ripe. In practice the third is the biggest loss.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;If there is no bamboo or other stakes, it is enough to drive wooden stakes on both sides of the row and run one line of saekki (twisted straw rope) around them at knee height. Unlike peas, mung bean does not need supports for vines to climb; it only needs something to keep it from falling sideways. Hilling at the same time buries the base of the plants in soil, which adds support and doubles the effect.

## 9. Pests and Diseases

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Mung bean's Pest & disease pressure is C, but its Pest-control self-reliance is A. This means a good part of the trouble can be managed without chemicals, through cultural practices and natural materials, and it is the second-highest value among legumes after cowpea. Most mung bean diseases are tied to excess moisture, so drainage management alone reduces several problems at once.

### 9-1. Main Diseases

| Disease | Symptoms | Conditions | Response without chemicals |
|---|---|---|---|
| Root rot | The base of the stem and the roots turn brown and soft, and the plant wilts | Excess moisture. Appears throughout the whole growing period | Raise the beds, dig drainage channels, correct acidity with lime |
| Brown spot | Brown spots form on the leaves and the spots fall out, leaving holes | High humidity at flowering and pod enlargement | Ensure airflow, remove diseased leaves, Bordeaux mixture |
| Anthracnose | Black cankers form on pods and seeds | High humidity. Also spread by seed | Select seed, rotate crops, Bordeaux mixture |
| Powdery mildew | A white powder covers the leaf surface | Dry weather with a large day-night temperature swing | Ensure airflow, milk diluted 5-10 times |
| Viral disease | Leaves become mottled in a mosaic pattern and crinkle | Carried by aphids | Cannot be treated. Pull up and burn the plants and reduce aphids |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Water is always at the center of mung bean disease. Root rot is the most common and causes the greatest loss, and brown spot and anthracnose also get worse in wet years. A single measure, raising the beds by at least 20cm and cutting drainage channels properly, reduces all three diseases at once. It is a rare case where moving soil with a shovel is more effective than making or spraying any chemical.

### 9-2. Main Pests

| Pest | Damage | Control without chemicals |
|---|---|---|
| Stink bugs | Pierce the pods and suck the sap, leaving empty, unfilled seeds | Pick them by hand in the early morning while the dew is on and drop them into a bucket of water |
| Aphids | Cluster on the flower stalks at flowering, suck the sap, make the flowers drop, and spread viruses | Spray soapy water (5g of soap per 1L of water) on the undersides of the leaves at the first sign of infestation |
| Tobacco cutworm | Chews leaves and stems | Pick by hand. If you find an egg mass, remove it together with the leaf |
| Adzuki bean weevil | Lays eggs on the pods in the field and eats out the seeds in storage | Storage management is the key. See Section 15 |
| Birds (pigeons, pheasants) | Peck off the cotyledons right after sowing | Cover with straw or netting until germination |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Aphids at flowering are a particular problem for mung bean. Mung bean keeps flowering throughout the latter part of its growth, and when aphids cluster on the flower stalks, the flowers simply drop and no pods set. Catching them early matters, and once flowering starts it is important to get into the habit of checking the undersides of the leaves and the flower stalks every three or four days.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The adzuki bean weevil looks like a storage problem, but it actually starts in the field. Adults glue their eggs onto pods still hanging in the field, and those eggs hatch in the storeroom. So the first step in cutting storage loss is not to leave ripe pods in the field for long but to pick them on time. Diligent staged harvesting protects both yield and storability at once.

### 9-3. Pest-Control Materials That Need No Chemicals

| Material | How to make it | Target | Caution |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bordeaux mixture | Dissolve 400g of copper sulfate and 400g of quicklime separately in 100L of water, then mix | Brown spot, anthracnose | Can be made only if copper is available. Use immediately after mixing |
| Soapy water | 5g of unscented soap per 1L of water | Aphids, mites | Spraying in the midday heat scorches the leaves |
| Dry ash dusting | Dust fine ash onto the leaves on a dewy morning | Leaf-eating insects, mold | Washes off when it rains |
| Wood ash water | Soak 1 doe (about 1.8 L) of ash in 10L of water overnight and use the clear liquid on top | Suppressing mold | Alkaline, so use it diluted |
| Wood vinegar | Dilute the liquid released when making charcoal 300-500 times | Repellent, growth promotion | The undiluted liquid is strongly acidic, so always dilute it |
| Hot pepper and garlic water | Grind, steep in water, and strain | Repellent | The effect does not last, so spray often |
| Diluted milk | Dilute milk 5-10 times with water | Powdery mildew | Leftover milk spoils and smells |

### 9-4. Cultural Control

| Method | Principle |
|---|---|
| Drainage and raised beds | More than half of mung bean diseases are linked to excess moisture. This is the single most effective measure |
| Mulching | Keeps splashed soil off the pods and suppresses weeds |
| Adjusting sowing time | Sowing late can sidestep the peak of pest outbreaks |
| Timely picking | Ripe pods left in the field for long collect weevil eggs |
| Crop rotation | Keep legumes out of the field for at least 3 years |
| Seed selection | Anthracnose is carried by seed |
| Adequate plant spacing | With good airflow, leaves dry quickly and mold cannot gain a foothold |
| Residue disposal | Burn diseased residue and return only healthy residue to the field |

## 10. Nutrient Deficiency and Excess

| Symptom | Cause | Remedy with natural materials |
|---|---|---|
| Whole leaves pale yellow, plants small | Nitrogen deficiency. In mung bean, the most common cause is nodules killed by waterlogging | First dig up a root and check the nodules. Fix drainage and apply a small amount of urine diluted 10 times |
| Leaf edges dry out brown | Potassium deficiency | Scatter wood ash between the plants and rake it in lightly |
| Slow growth, leaves take on a purple tint | Phosphorus deficiency | Bone meal, roasted fish-bone powder, wood ash |
| Flowers open but pods do not set | Boron deficiency, or aphid damage | Compost made from mixed materials. Check the flower stalks for aphids |
| Yellowing between the leaf veins | Iron or manganese deficiency. Common in soil leaning alkaline | Stop using ash. Raise organic matter with leaf mold and well-rotted compost |
| Stems grow long but few pods form | Nitrogen excess or excess moisture | Cut off nitrogen and fix drainage |
| Plants fall over | Nitrogen excess, dense planting, no stakes | Hilling and supporting the row. Adjust fertilizing and plant spacing the next year |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;When mung bean leaves turn yellow, the first thing to suspect is not a shortage of fertilizer but water. If the whole field has turned yellow after the monsoon season (jangma, late June to July in South Korea), it is almost certainly waterlogging: the nodules have died from lack of oxygen and the nitrogen supply has been cut off. Giving nitrogen at this point does not bring recovery. First clear the drainage channels, drain the water from the furrows, and let the soil dry; if recovery is still slow after that, then add a small amount of nitrogen.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Trace-element deficiencies are hard to correct one by one. Without chemical fertilizer there is practically no way to obtain molybdenum or cobalt. Instead, compost made by rotting vegetable scraps, fruit peels, eggshells, fish bones, fallen leaves, weeds, and ash together contains trace elements in even proportions. The rule of putting anything and everything into the compost pile is the practical strategy for managing trace elements.

## 11. Intercropping and Crop Rotation

### 11-1. Compatibility

| Compatibility | Crop | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Good | Maize, sorghum | Different heights let them share light, and they take up the nitrogen the mung bean has fixed right alongside |
| Good | Barley, wheat, garlic, onion, potato | As the preceding crop, they leave the field empty in June (in South Korea), and mung bean is planted there as the following crop |
| Good | Napa cabbage, radish | Planted after mung bean, they use the leftover nitrogen |
| Good | Marigold | Root exudates suppress nematodes |
| Fair | Sweet potato | Keep a distance so the vines do not smother the mung bean |
| Poor | Adzuki bean, cowpea | Same genus, so they share pathogens and weevils directly |
| Poor | Continuous cropping with other legumes | Pathogens and nematodes accumulate |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The most important principle in placing mung bean is to link it to what comes before and after. Mung bean's calories per area are low, so it cannot be the main crop of a field, but it is ideal for filling the two and a half months between a preceding crop and a following crop. In South Korea, harvest barley in June, plant mung bean and harvest it in September, then plant napa cabbage in the same spot, and one field gives three crops in a year. Along the way, the napa cabbage uses the nitrogen the mung bean left behind, and the napa cabbage residue goes back into the soil.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;It is best not to place adzuki bean and cowpea next to mung bean. Being in the same genus (Vigna), they share pathogens, and above all the adzuki bean weevil moves among the three crops. Growing adzuki bean and mung bean together in one field doubles weevil damage in storage.

### 11-2. Crop Rotation

| Item | Standard |
|---|---|
| Rotation break | 3 years or more |
| Ideal cycle | 3-4 years |
| Continuous-cropping injury | Root rot and nematodes accumulate |

| Example of a 4-year rotation |
|---|
| Year 1: barley (winter), then mung bean (summer) |
| Year 2: maize or sorghum |
| Year 3: potato or sweet potato |
| Year 4: napa cabbage and radish group, or leafy vegetables |
| Then mung bean again |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Here is a compromise for when the field is too small to rotate. Mung bean does not need a large planting area. If the main purpose is to keep up the seed for mung bean sprouts (sukju namul), a few m² is enough, and an area that small can be moved to a new spot every year. Also, since mung bean is often planted as a following crop, fitting it onto the rotation plan of the preceding crop so that its location shifts naturally means no separate management is needed.

## 12. Harvest and Threshing - Staged Harvesting

### 12-1. Judging When to Harvest

| Criterion | Details |
|---|---|
| Pod color | When the pods have turned completely from green to black |
| Days after flowering | 40-50 days |
| Days after sowing | About 80-90 days |
| Number of harvests | Divided into 3-4 pickings |
| Interval | Go around the field every 5-7 days and pick the black pods |

> **Mung bean pods twist and burst open on their own within a few days of turning black. If you see a black pod and leave it, those seeds fall to the ground and are lost. Missing the harvest time is the most common loss in mung bean.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Mung bean flowers and matures throughout the latter part of its growth, so it cannot be harvested all at once. A single plant carries black pods, green pods, and open flowers at the same time. The rule is to go around the field every 5 to 7 days and pick only the pods that have turned black, and the job is usually finished in three or four rounds. Green pods left at the end can be cut with the stems and dried in the shade, and they will fill out to some extent.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The judgment changes in the monsoon season or in rainy periods. If you wait for the pods to turn fully black and they get rained on, the seeds sprout or rot inside the pods. When rain is forecast, it is better to pick even the pods that have just begun to turn black and finish drying them indoors or under the eaves. Slightly underripe mung beans can be dried and used, but sprouted mung beans can be neither eaten nor planted.

### 12-2. Harvesting Tips

| Step | Details |
|---|---|
| 1 | Do it on a clear, dry day, after the dew has dried |
| 2 | Twist off only the black pods by hand. Pulling damages the stems |
| 3 | Put the picked pods in a breathable net or basket |
| 4 | Put them in onion nets or napa cabbage nets and spread them in the sun to dry |
| 5 | When bone dry, beat the net with a club to thresh |
| 6 | After the last harvest, lay the remaining stalks down in the field or dig them in |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Drying in nets is especially useful for mung bean. Many pods burst open by themselves as they dry, and inside a net the seeds that fly out collect at the bottom of the net. If you spread the net out on a sunny day and shake it now and then, half of the threshing is already done by itself. Without a net, a sack or a large container covered with cloth will also do.

### 12-3. Threshing - Methods That Need No Equipment

| Method | Tool | Procedure | Efficiency |
|---|---|---|---|
| Beating inside a net | Onion net or napa cabbage net | Put the dry pods in the net and beat with a club | Best suited to mung bean. No seeds leak out |
| Trampling in a sack | Thick burlap or a sack | Put the dry pods in the sack and tread on it | Convenient for small amounts |
| Flail | A flail (dorigae): rods tied to a long handle | Spread a straw mat (meongseok), lay out the pods, and strike | The seeds are small and scatter in all directions. Fence off the edges of the mat |
| Rubbing by hand | None | Rub the dry pods between both hands | Very small amounts; suitable for seed |
| Leaving in the sun | None | Spread thinly on cloth and the pods burst open on their own | The least laborious method |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Mung bean pods burst easily, so threshing is very easy. Well-dried pods spread on cloth will burst about halfway on their own. The problem is that the seeds are small and fly in every direction. Trap the flying seeds by surrounding the edge of the mat with cloth, working inside a large container, or beating the pods inside a net. The seeds lost may look few, but because the total yield of this crop is small to begin with, the loss ratio is by no means small.

### 12-4. Cleaning - Separating by Wind

| Method | Principle | Procedure |
|---|---|---|
| Wind sorting (winnowing) | Seeds are heavy, while husks and debris are light | On a windy day, put the material in a winnowing basket (ki) or a wide bowl, hold it high, and pour it out slowly |
| Basket winnowing | Shake up and down while tossing forward | Heavy seeds stay behind and light material is pushed forward and out |
| Fanning | One person pours and another makes wind with a fan | Used on days without wind |
| Sieving | Separates by hole size | Seeds are small, so a fine sieve is needed |
| Floating in water | Empty and insect-eaten seeds are light | Pour into a bucket of water and skim off what floats. Dry again the same day |
| Rolling down a slope | Round seeds roll well | Tilt a board and roll them down |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Winnowing in the wind comes down to three points. Choose early morning or dusk, when the wind blows steadily, pour in a thin stream rather than all at once, and repeat two or three times. Mung bean seeds are small and light, so a strong wind blows the seeds away too. Test with a small amount first to gauge the wind strength, then do it in earnest. Floating in water is especially effective for picking out seeds eaten by the adzuki bean weevil and greatly cuts storage loss. However, soaked mung beans must be dried completely again the same day and are not used for seed.
