## 3. Growing Conditions

### 3-1. Temperature and Light

| Condition | Limit | Optimum | When outside it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Germination temperature | 15°C or above | 25-30°C | Below 15°C, germination stops and the seed rots |
| Growth temperature | 15-40°C | 28-30°C | The most heat-tolerant of the legumes |
| Seed-setting temperature | 20-30°C | 25-30°C | The range is 10°C |
| High-temperature limit | Tolerates even above 35°C | - | The legume most resistant to extreme heat |
| Cold hardiness | None | - | A single frost kills it |
| Sunshine | 6 hours or more a day | 8 hours or more a day | At 40% shade, yield drops by half |
| Accumulated temperature | 1,600°C or more | - | Requires little, to suit the short growing period |

> **Mung bean is almost the only legume that endures extreme heat. Even in summers when 35°C persists and soybean flowers drop off in droves, mung bean keeps on flowering. The hotter the climate gets, the higher mung bean's relative standing rises.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This heat tolerance widens mung bean's sowing window. In South Korea, it can be sown from early April to late July, at any time the temperature is above 15°C. The last sowing date, however, must be worked out backward from frost. Counting back 90 days from the expected date of the first frost gives the last sowing date for that region, which is usually mid-July in central South Korea and late July in southern South Korea.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Double cropping is also possible thanks to this wide sowing window. In southern South Korea, the first crop can be sown in late April and harvested in early July, and the second sown right away and harvested in late September. However, there is a risk that the pod-filling period of the first crop overlaps with the monsoon season (jangma, late June to July in South Korea), so in practice it is more common and safer to sow once, as a succeeding crop, in a June field from which barley, potatoes or garlic have been harvested.

### 3-2. Soil and Water

| Condition | Limit | Optimum | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Soil texture | Sandy soil to loam | Well-drained sandy loam to loam | Ground where water stands is the worst |
| pH | 5.5-7.5 | 6.0-7.0 | Nodules decrease in acid soil |
| Effective soil depth | 30cm or more | 50cm or more | |
| Seasonal evapotranspiration | - | About 250mm | Among the lowest of the legumes |
| Flooding | Roots are damaged by even one day under water | - | The legume most vulnerable to excess moisture damage |
| Bed height | 20cm or more | 25cm | The key to preventing excess moisture damage |
| Water table | 50cm or deeper | 80cm or deeper | |

> **The greatest enemy of growing mung bean is excess moisture damage. And mung bean's growing period in South Korea overlaps exactly with the monsoon. Raising the bed by 20cm or more and cutting deep drainage ditches comes before any other management.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The effects of raising beds and mulching have been confirmed by measurement. Raising the bed and mulching together increases emergence by 16% and yield by 54%. Without plastic film, covering with straw, rice hulls or fallen leaves gives much the same effect. Three benefits come together: it keeps rain from pounding the soil directly and crusting the surface, keeps splashed soil off the pods, and suppresses weeds.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The knack with drainage ditches is to make a sure path for water to leave toward the lower side of the field. If only the bed is raised and the end of the furrow is blocked, water pools in the furrow and things get worse. Before the monsoon, it is good to pour a dipperful of water along the furrow and check that it runs all the way out.

## 4. Varieties

### 4-1. Varieties Bred in South Korea

| Variety | Features | Suited use |
|---|---|---|
| Nampyeongnokdu | Small seeds and many pods give a high yield | Dual use: seed and mung bean sprouts |
| Geumseongnokdu | An upright type whose pods do not curve, and resistant to disease | For seed |
| Soseonnokdu | A small-seeded variety best suited to growing sprouts | Sprouts only |
| Jangannokdu | Resistant to disease and weevils | For long-term storage |
| Dahyeonnokdu | Resistant to lodging and disease, with many pods per plant | Low-labor growing |
| Chaeheun | Fairly even maturity and high sprout yield | Sprouts; one-pass harvest |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Several varieties of mung bean have been bred in South Korea and the crop is grown across South Korea, so securing seed is not difficult. This is a great advantage compared with chickpea or lentil. In a disaster, however, what matters more than which variety you can get is which traits you select and carry on.

### 4-2. Traits That Matter in a Disaster

| Trait | Why it matters | How to select |
|---|---|---|
| Uniform maturity | Reduces the number of staged harvests and saves labor | Select plants whose pods turn black all at once and carry them on |
| Pods that do not burst easily | Losses are small even if the harvest time is missed | Save seed from pods that have not burst even several days after turning black |
| Weevil resistance | Reduces storage losses | The Jangannokdu line. Seed coat thick and hard |
| Lodging resistance | Cuts harvest labor several-fold | Plants with thick, straight stems |
| Sprout yield | More sprouts from the same weight | Small-seeded varieties are generally higher |
| Earliness | Finishes before frost and makes double cropping possible | Plants that flower first and ripen first |

> **In choosing a mung bean variety, the priority in a disaster is not yield but uniform maturity and pods that do not burst. When hands are short, you cannot go out to pick three or four times, and the pods you could not pick burst and vanish.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;It is also good to be sure to secure some small-seeded varieties for sprouts. The smaller the seed, the less effort it spends in germinating, and the sprouts grow straight and plump. If sprouts are grown from a large-seeded variety, they come out bent and go soft. Having only a seed-type variety leaves the winter vegetable problem unsolved.

## 5. Field Preparation and Fertilizer - Root-Nodule Bacteria

### 5-1. How Nitrogen Fixation Works

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Nitrogen makes up 78% of the air. Although unlimited nitrogen floats above people's heads, plants cannot use it. Atmospheric nitrogen is a molecule in which two nitrogen atoms are bound tightly by a triple bond, and breaking this bond takes enormous energy. The industrial process that breaks this bond to make ammonia is the Haber-Bosch process, and the world's nitrogen fertilizer comes from it. It needs high temperature, high pressure, a catalyst and enormous amounts of fossil fuel.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Root-nodule bacteria (rhizobia) do this at room temperature and normal pressure. With an enzyme called nitrogenase they break the triple bond and turn nitrogen into ammonia. There is a price, however. This reaction needs a great deal of energy, and the bacteria cannot photosynthesize on their own. So they live inside the roots of legume plants, receive the sugars the plant makes by photosynthesis, and in return hand over the nitrogen they have fixed. It is a trading relationship.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The bacteria that partner with mung bean are in the Bradyrhizobium group. They are compatible with the strains used by adzuki bean and cowpea, and partly compatible with those of soybean. In practice, soil from a field where adzuki bean was grown is the most reliable inoculum. Adzuki bean is widely grown in South Korea, so an inoculum is not hard to find.

| Stage | What happens |
|---|---|
| 1. Signal exchange | The mung bean root secretes flavonoids, and bacteria in the soil respond to the signal by sending out Nod factors |
| 2. Infection | Root hairs curl inward and enclose the bacteria, and the bacteria enter the root along an infection thread |
| 3. Nodule formation | Cells inside the root divide and the nodule swells, and the bacteria settle inside it |
| 4. Nitrogen fixation | Inside the nodule, nitrogenase works and turns atmospheric nitrogen into ammonia |
| 5. Distribution | The ammonia is converted into amino acids and carried to the stems, leaves and pods |
| 6. Senescence | In the pod-filling period the nodule loses its function and returns to the soil along with the root |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Nitrogenase is extremely sensitive to oxygen and loses function the moment oxygen touches it. So the inside of the nodule is filled with a red pigment protein called leghemoglobin, which holds on to oxygen and keeps the oxygen concentration around the enzyme extremely low. It is the same principle that makes human blood red, and the red or pink color inside a nodule when it is split open is this pigment. This fact is of great practical importance. The red color is visible proof that the nodule is fixing nitrogen right now.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There is a reason why this structure is especially vulnerable in mung bean. Root-nodule bacteria are aerobic bacteria that require oxygen, so when a field is flooded they die before the roots do. This is where the fact that the mung bean growing period overlaps with the monsoon and the fact that mung bean is the most vulnerable to excess moisture damage meet. The yellowing of the leaves in a mung bean field that has been under water for two days is due not only to root damage but to the cutting off of the nitrogen supply.

### 5-2. How to Check Whether Nodules Have Formed

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Whether or not you inoculated, you must check that nodules have actually formed. Because mung bean's growing period is short, the time for checking is also early.

| Step | Details |
|---|---|
| 1 | Around the start of flowering, 5-6 weeks after sowing, is the best time |
| 2 | Choose two or three healthy-looking plants |
| 3 | Do not pull them up; dig a wide area around the plant with a spade and lift it out with the soil |
| 4 | Put it in a bucket of water and gently wash off the soil |
| 5 | Count the nodules on the taproot and thick lateral roots |
| 6 | Split a few nodules with your fingernail and look at the color inside |

| Observation | Meaning | Action |
|---|---|---|
| 15 or more nodules per plant | Inoculation worked well | Leave as is |
| Inside is red or pink when split | It is fixing nitrogen | A good condition |
| Inside is white or pale green when split | Nodules have formed but are not fixing | Check acidity, excess moisture damage and phosphorus deficiency |
| Inside is brown or black when split | Already senescent or dead | After the monsoon, excess moisture damage is the likely cause |
| 5 nodules or fewer | Inoculation failed | If the leaves are yellow, add a small amount of nitrogen |

> **The one habit of digging up roots, splitting nodules and checking for red is the most reliable signal of the success or failure of bean farming in a world without nitrogen fertilizer.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;With mung bean, it is good to check twice, before and after the monsoon. If nodules that were red before the monsoon are dead and black after it, excess moisture damage is the cause. In that case, clear the drainage ditches, and if the leaves are yellow, give a very small amount of diluted urine to carry the crop through the rest of the period. And the next year, raise the bed higher.

### 5-3. Where to Get an Inoculum

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Commercial inoculants are products that sell living bacteria carried in peat or clay. In a disaster they cannot be obtained. But the bacteria that mung bean uses are common ones shared with adzuki bean and cowpea, so an inoculum is relatively easy to obtain.

| Method | Procedure | Effect | Caution |
|---|---|---|---|
| Coating seed with soil from a field where adzuki bean, mung bean or cowpea grew | Dig up soil from 5-15cm below the surface in a field where these were planted last year and nodules formed well. Lightly moisten the seed with water, coat it with this soil, and plant right away | The most reliable and costs nothing | Soil from a diseased field carries pathogens along with it |
| Using the nodules themselves | Collect the nodules from dug-up roots, crush them and stir them into water, then soak the seed in that water and take it out | The highest bacterial density | Must be used on the same day the nodules are dug |
| Scattering soil in the field | Scatter the soil you brought along the rows to be sown and work it in | The bacteria spread through the soil | About one shovelful per 10m² is the guideline |
| Leaving root residue | At harvest, cut only the stems and leave the roots in the field | Bacteria remain in the soil until next year | The lowest-labor way to maintain it |
| Roots of wild saepat (a wild relative of the adzuki bean) | Collect nodules from the roots of wild saepat in fields and on field banks | Same genus, so the bacteria are compatible | Fixation efficiency may be lower than that of strains for cultivated varieties |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There is something you must observe when inoculating. Root-nodule bacteria are extremely sensitive to direct sunlight, drying and heat. A few minutes of ultraviolet light kills a good many of them, and drying kills them faster. Do the work of coating seed with soil in the shade, and plant the coated seed at once without spreading it out in the sun. It is best to plant within 30 minutes, and in any case finish within a few hours. One more thing: seed disinfection and inoculation cannot be done together. If you disinfected with a hot-water treatment, inoculate after it has cooled completely.

| Conditions that cause inoculation failure | Reason |
|---|---|
| Soil pH 5.5 or below | The bacteria cannot live |
| A field given plenty of nitrogen fertilizer | The plant feels no need for the trade and reduces nodule formation on its own |
| A field where water stands | Aerobic bacteria die without oxygen. The most common cause of failure in mung bean |
| A field short of phosphorus | Nitrogen fixation takes a lot of energy, and phosphorus is used in that metabolism |
| Molybdenum, iron or cobalt deficiency | They are components of nitrogenase |
| Drying inoculated seed in the sun | Ultraviolet light and drying kill the bacteria |
| Sowing in the scorching midsummer sun | The soil surface temperature is high and the bacteria on the seed die. Plant in the morning or evening |
