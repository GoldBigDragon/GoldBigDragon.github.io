## Introduction

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Mung bean (Vigna radiata) comes from India. It belongs to the genus Vigna, which also includes the adzuki bean and the cowpea, and it reached South Korea very long ago, where it became established as an ingredient of bindaetteok (mung bean pancakes), cheongpomuk (mung bean starch jelly) and sukjunamul (seasoned mung bean sprouts). It is a short, fast crop, sown in midsummer and harvested in early autumn in South Korea, and that shortness creates almost all of its value.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;It takes 60-90 days from sowing to harvest, the shortest among the legumes in this crop table. Soybean needs 120-150 days and peanut 130-150 days, so mung bean takes about half as long. Its grade of A for Speed to harvest comes from this. This shortness makes three things possible. First, it can be sown in the same spot right after another crop has been harvested: in South Korea, the empty field of June left after a barley, potato or garlic harvest is mung bean's place. Second, in southern South Korea it can be planted twice in one year. Third, the field can be used again when another crop has failed. Even in mid-July, when summer crops have been wrecked by the monsoon season (jangma, late June to July in South Korea), mung bean is close to being the only grain crop that can still be planted.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In addition, a handful of mung beans, water and a dark place are all it takes to produce a fresh vegetable, mung bean sprouts, in three to five days. No field, sunlight or soil is needed, and vitamin C, which the dry beans did not contain, is newly formed during germination. When there are no fresh vegetables at all in winter, this is a cheap and reliable way to prevent scurvy, and the whole of Section 16 of this document is devoted to it.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;However, the Overall grade of mung bean is C, with a score of 42.0. Calories per area is graded D, and 1,000m² yields only 120kg. At 422kcal per m², that is 0.16 times the self-sufficiency line and the lowest among the legumes in this crop table. Filling a field with mung bean is a definite loss in terms of calories. On top of that, the plant is killed outright by frost, is the weakest of the legumes against waterlogging, and has to be harvested in three or four rounds because it keeps flowering and the pods split open as soon as they ripen. It is a labor-intensive crop.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Mung bean is not the main crop of a field but a crop that fills gaps, and a crop that solves the winter vegetable problem. In those roles, almost no other crop can take its place.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Finally, there is one misunderstanding common to all legume crops: the saying that planting beans improves the soil. That is only half true. Mung bean also lives in symbiosis with root-nodule bacteria (rhizobia) and draws nitrogen from the air, but if the seeds are harvested and carried out of the field, more nitrogen can leave the field than was fixed. Section 5 untangles that fork in the road with numbers.
