## Introduction
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Mung bean (Vigna radiata) is an annual legume native to India, a short, fast crop sown in midsummer and harvested in early autumn. From sowing to harvest it takes 60-90 days, the shortest among legumes, which earned it a harvest speed grade of A. That lets it be sown right after barley, potatoes, or garlic have been harvested, in the empty fields of June, and it is almost the only seed crop that can still be sown in mid-July, when summer crops have been wiped out by the rainy season. With a handful of dry mung beans, water, and a dark place, sprouts are ready in 3-5 days, and the vitamin C newly formed during germination prevents scurvy in winters without fresh vegetables. The overall grade is C (42.0 points, 86th of 183 valid species), with short term C (40.7), long term C (43.3), and supplementary SS (63.7 points). Calories per area is D: 120 kg per 1,000 ㎡ and 422 kcal per ㎡, only 0.16 times the self-sufficiency line and the lowest among legumes, so filling a field with mung beans is a loss. On top of that, it dies in frost, is the weakest of the legumes against waterlogging, and its pods burst as they ripen, so it must be harvested in three or four passes. Mung bean is not the master of the field but a crop that fills the gaps and solves the winter vegetable problem.

> **In a disaster, plans always go wrong. Even if seedlings are lost to a late frost or the field is flooded by the rainy season, mung bean is almost the only legume that can still be sown until late July.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Calories per area D is less than half that of soybean, and land-preparation and digging energy D follows from it (the calories recovered are only 2.6 times the energy spent tilling). So it is not a main crop but is placed in ready-made spots, such as an empty field after the preceding crop or a corner of the field. Cold tolerance E means a single frost kills it, and the date counted back 90 days from the expected first frost is the last sowing date (mid-July in the central region, late July in the southern region). The weakness not in the grade table is waterlogging: it is the weakest of the legumes against standing water, and its growing season overlaps the rainy season. ## 2. Plant Overview
| Item | Details |
|:---|:---|
| Classification | Annual legume of the genus Vigna in the family Fabaceae. Native to India, self-pollinating |
| Leaves, stem | Trifoliate leaves with fine hairs. Height 60-80 cm; it branches, but the stem is thin and soft and falls over easily |
| Roots, flowers | Straight taproot with fine roots and nodules. Small yellow flowers bloom throughout the latter half of growth |
| Pods, seeds | Slender cylindrical pods 5-10 cm that turn black when ripe. Seeds are small, round, and dark green |

> **Mung bean pods turn black, not brown, when ripe, and twist open by themselves within a few days of blackening. Pick black pods that day or the next.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;A single plant carries black pods, green pods, and flowers at the same time, so it must be picked in three or four passes, and cutting everything at once loses more than half. Germination takes 4-5 days, among the fastest of the legumes. Management in the first 3-4 weeks after sowing nearly decides the yield. Nodules take 3 weeks to start handing over nitrogen, more than a quarter of the growing period, so putting a small amount of nitrogen in the base fertilizer is meaningful. Per 100 g: 352 kcal, protein 24.51 g (27.9% of calories), about 1 g fat, about 6 mg iron, about 1,200 mg potassium, and 0 g sugars. The protein is rich in lysine and slightly short of methionine, so eaten with grains, which are the opposite, the two complement each other. Fat is the lowest among legumes, so it hardly goes rancid and keeps well even as flour, and starch can be extracted to make cheongpomuk (mung bean starch jelly). Dry mung beans contain no vitamin C, but sprouting creates it anew, so dry mung beans and mung bean sprouts are effectively different foods.

## 3. Growing Environment and Varieties
| Condition | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Temperature | Germination from 15 °C (optimum 25-30 °C; below 15 °C the seed rots). Growth 15-40 °C (optimum 28-30 °C). Fruit set 20-30 °C. Tolerates temperatures above 35 °C |
| Soil, water | Well-drained sandy loam to loam (waterlogged ground is the worst), water table 50 cm or deeper |

> **Mung bean is almost the only legume that endures extreme heat, and it keeps flowering at 35 °C, when soybean flowers drop. Its greatest enemy is waterlogging, and raising ridges to 20 cm or more and digging deep drainage ditches come before any other management.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The sowing window runs four months, from early April to late July, incomparable with soybean's little over a month. Raising ridges together with mulching increases emergence by 16% and yield by 54%. Dig drainage ditches so that water drains all the way to the low end, and check by pouring water into the furrows before the rainy season.

| Variety | Features | Use |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Nampyeong | Small seeds, many pods, high yield | Both seed and sprouts |
| Sosun | Small-seeded type | Sprouts only |
| Jangan | Resistant to disease and weevils | Long-term storage |
| Dahyeon | Resistant to lodging and disease, many pods | Low-labor cultivation |
| Chaeheun | Even ripening, high sprout yield | Sprouts, harvest all at once |

> **In a disaster, the priority in variety choice is not yield but even ripening and pods that do not burst. If there is no one to go and pick, the pods left unpicked burst and are lost.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Next come weevil resistance (the Jangan line), lodging resistance, and early maturity. Always keep some small-seeded type separately for sprouts. The smaller the seed, the straighter and plumper the sprouts grow, while large-seeded types give bent, soft sprouts.

## 4. Field Preparation and Sowing
| Item | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Field preparation | A ridge height of 20-25 cm decides success. On acidic soil, apply 100-200 kg of lime per 1,000 ㎡ 3-4 weeks before sowing (roasted and crushed shells, eggshells, or wood ash can substitute) |
| Fertilization (1,000 ㎡) | Nitrogen 2-4 kg, phosphorus 4-7 kg, potassium 4-6 kg (all as basal fertilizer), and 1,000 kg of fully decomposed compost. In a disaster, use 100-150 g of wood ash per ㎡ (above that, pH exceeds 7) and cover the soil with residues of the preceding crop (barley straw, garlic stalks, potato stems) as fertilizer. Saving nitrogen is the skill: too much makes plants grow leggy and fall over, which makes the multi-pass harvest several times harder. Top dressing is unnecessary, but if growth is clearly poor at flowering, give a very small amount of urine diluted 10 times |
| Nodule inoculation | Coat seeds lightly moistened with water with soil from 5-15 cm depth of a field where adzuki bean, mung bean, or cowpea grew and formed nodules well, and sow within 30 minutes in the shade (do not dry them in the sun or combine this with seed disinfection). Causes of failure: pH 5.5 or below, excess nitrogen, standing water (the most common cause), phosphorus deficiency, sowing in the scorching midsummer sun |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Check nodules around flowering (5-6 weeks after sowing) and after the rainy season. Lift a plant with a spade together with its soil, wash it, and split the nodules. With 15 or more per plant and a red or pink interior, they are fixing nitrogen; white or pale green means they are not fixing; brown or black means dead. If nodules that were red before the rainy season have died afterward, waterlogging is the cause.

| Sowing | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Timing | Central region single crop: early to mid June. Southern region single crop: mid to late June. Succession crop (after the preceding crop): mid June to early July. Double cropping in the southern region (late April to early May and early to mid July) risks the first crop's ripening overlapping the rainy season, so a succession crop is safer |
| Method | Discard seeds with insect holes, discoloration, or wrinkles, and coat with fine wood ash. Depth 2-4 cm, 2-4 seeds per hole (thin to the 2 best plants at 1-2 true leaves), row spacing 60 cm (40-60 cm for succession crops), plant spacing 10-15 cm, seed rate 1.5-2 kg per 1,000 ㎡ (2-3 kg for succession crops). Leave row spacing generous, since it is the path for going out to pick pods. Pigeons and pheasants peck the cotyledons right after sowing, so cover with straw or netting |

## 5. Water Management and Cultivation Management
| Stage | Water management |
|:---|:---|
| Sowing to germination | Keep the surface layer from drying out |
| After true leaves unfold | Keep somewhat dry |
| Flowering and podding | Irrigate if drought is severe (water shortage at this stage reduces yield the most) |
| Rainy season, ripening | Dig drainage ditches and stop irrigation during ripening |

> **Water management for mung bean is the art of draining, not of giving. If ripe pods are rained on, the seeds sprout inside and can be neither eaten nor planted, so when rain is forecast, pick pods even if not yet fully black and dry them indoors.**

| Care | Method |
|:---|:---|
| Weeding | Scrape shallowly 2 and 4 weeks after sowing (before flowering, combined with hilling). Do not weed after flowering, since roots are cut and flowers drop. No herbicide is registered for mung bean, and herbicides for other beans cause injury |
| Mulching | Right after sowing, cover between rows with straw, rice hulls, or fallen leaves about 5 cm thick to block weeds, waterlogging, and soil splash together (in the rainy season, pull it back from the shoulder of the ridge) |
| Stake support | When plants are 30-40 cm tall, drive 60-80 cm sticks or stakes at both ends of the row and every 2-3 m, and run one line of straw rope at 30-40 cm above the ground to prevent lodging (fallen plants rot their pods, and ripe pods become hard to see) |

## 6. Pests, Diseases, and Nutrient Problems
| Pest or disease | Symptoms and conditions | Control without chemicals |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Root rot | Stem base and roots turn brown and soft, and the plant wilts. Excess moisture | Raise ridges, dig drainage ditches, correct acidity with lime |
| Brown spot, anthracnose | Brown spots and holes on leaves / black cankers on pods and seeds (seed-borne). Humid conditions | Secure airflow, remove diseased leaves, select seeds, rotate crops |
| Aphids | Cluster on flower stalks at flowering, suck sap, cause flowers to drop, and no pods set | Check the undersides of leaves and the flower stalks every three or four days, and spray soapy water (5 g of soap per 1 L of water) early |
| Adzuki bean weevil | Lays eggs on pods in the field and eats the seeds in storage | Do not leave ripe pods long and pick on time (see Section 9) |

> **Water is always at the center of mung bean disease. Digging with a spade to raise ridges and make drainage ditches does more than making any chemical.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Bordeaux mixture is made by dissolving 400 g of copper sulfate and 400 g of quicklime separately in 100 L of water, mixing them, and using it immediately against brown spot and anthracnose.

| Symptom | Cause and action |
|:---|:---|
| Whole leaf pale yellow, small plant | Nitrogen deficiency. After the rainy season, nodules have died from waterlogging, so nitrogen will not restore it; first dig drainage ditches and let the soil dry, and if recovery is still slow, give a small amount of urine diluted 10 times |
| Flowers bloom but no pods set | Boron deficiency, drought at flowering, or aphid damage. Compost made from many materials, irrigation, and checking flower stalks |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The saying "planting beans improves the soil" is only half true. 120 kg of seed contains about 4.7 kg of nitrogen (47 kg per hectare), while nodules fix 50-80 kg per hectare under good conditions but only 10-20 kg when they die from waterlogging, so in a waterlogged year simply carrying the seed away makes the field lose nitrogen. What improves the soil is the nitrogen left in stems and leaves, so after picking the pods, lay the stems down where they stand or plow them in.

## 7. Intercropping and Crop Rotation
| Compatibility | Companions |
|:---|:---|
| Good | Maize and sorghum (share light through the height difference and receive fixed nitrogen alongside), barley, wheat, garlic, onion, and potato (as the preceding crop, leaving the empty field in June for a succession crop), Chinese cabbage and radish (planted after mung bean, they use the leftover nitrogen) |
| Poor | Adzuki bean and cowpea (same genus, so they share pathogens and weevils; do not plant them nearby) |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Arrange mung bean by linking what comes before and after it. Harvest barley in June, sow mung bean, harvest it in September, and plant Chinese cabbage, and one field yields three crops in a year, with the cabbage using the nitrogen mung bean leaves behind. Continuous cropping accumulates root rot and nematodes, so keep legumes out for at least 3 years. A 4-year rotation example is year 1 barley followed by mung bean, year 2 maize or sorghum, year 3 potato or sweet potato, and year 4 Chinese cabbage and radish or leafy vegetables. If the field is small, move the few ㎡ used to carry on seed for sprouts to a new spot every year.

## 8. Harvest and Seed Saving
| Harvest | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Timing | When pods have turned from green to completely black (40-50 days after flowering, 80-90 days after sowing). Walk the field every 5-7 days and pick only the black ones, in 3-4 passes. Cut the last green pods with their stems and dry them in the shade |
| Technique | On a clear day after the dew has dried, twist off only black pods (pulling damages the stem). Put them in onion or cabbage mesh bags and dry in the sun; shaking them now and then collects burst seeds at the bottom of the bag and finishes half the threshing |
| Threshing, cleaning | Beat dry pods with a club inside the mesh bag, tread them in a sack, or spread them on cloth to burst by themselves. Flailing scatters seeds in all directions, so wall off the edge of the mat. Sieve with a fine sieve and sort by rolling down a tilted board. Winnow in the early morning or at dusk, pouring in a thin stream two or three times. Floating them in water separates seeds eaten by weevils (dry them again that day; not for seed use) |

| Seed saving | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Selection | Mark in the field beforehand plants whose pods ripen together, do not burst easily even after turning black, and have not fallen over. The non-bursting trait is heritable, so selecting for just a few years produces a line that loses little even if the harvest is slightly late. It is self-pollinating, so isolation is unnecessary, but keep harvest and storage separate from adzuki bean and cowpea |
| Harvest, storage | Pick right after pods turn completely black. Discard seeds with insect holes, discoloration, wrinkles, or small size, dry to 12% moisture or below, and seal. Germination is 95% or more in the harvest year, 90% or more after 1 year, 80-90% after 2, 70-80% after 3, and 50-70% after 4-5 years, and drops sharply after 6 years. Test old seed for germination, and if only half germinate, double the seed rate |

## 9. Storage
| Step | Method |
|:---|:---|
| Drying | Put picked pods in mesh bags in the sun for 2-3 days, and after threshing spread thinly on a mat for 1-2 days |
| Weevil control | Complete sealing matters most (oxygen runs out and the insects die): fill containers completely to reduce empty space, and use several small containers rather than one large one. Mix in sifted wood ash at 2-5% of the volume (rinse before eating) or rub in 3-5 mL of cooking oil per 1 kg of mung bean. Hot-water treatment means dipping briefly in boiling water, cooling in cold water, and drying thoroughly again. Do not use hot water or oil on seed |
| Containers | Glass jars and metal cans: 3 years or more; clay jars and underground storage pits: 2-3 years; thick plastic: 1-2 years. Watch for condensation in metal cans, flooding in underground pits, and rat damage and leaking small seeds in plastic |
| Inspection | Once every two months. Put an ear to the container and check for rustling (insects), a musty smell (mold), and small round holes (weevils). If found, take everything out, dry it in the sun, float it in water to skim off empty seeds, mix in ash, and seal again |

> **Beans are not lost to rot but eaten by insects. The art of storage is the art of keeping insects out.**

## 10. Cooking and Precautions
| Compound | Effect | Removal |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Trypsin inhibitors | Block protein-digesting enzymes, causing abdominal discomfort and lower protein utilization | Soak 4 hours, then boil 30 minutes to 1 hour (far less than in soybean) |
| Phytic acid | Blocks absorption of iron, zinc, and calcium | Reduced by 60-80% by soaking and sprouting |

> **What harms people in mung bean is not the bean itself but beans that were stored badly and molded. If there is a musty smell or a bluish powder on the surface, throw them out without regret.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;It has neither strong lectins nor hemolytic compounds, so it has the lowest toxicity risk among legumes, and it digests well and is safe even for the sick, the elderly, and children.

| Cooking | Method |
|:---|:---|
| Soaking, boiling | Soak in room-temperature water for 4 hours, then boil 30 minutes to 1 hour. Hulled and split beans take 20 minutes and keep 1-2 months sealed |
| Mung bean porridge | Boil mashed cooked mung beans with rice (food for the sick) |
| Starch, cheongpomuk | Soak, grind, stir into water, and dry the settled sediment to get starch flour (keeps for several months); mix with water and boil to get cheongpomuk (it sets well because the fat is low) |
| Other processing | Roasted and ground flour keeps 3-6 months sealed and needs no cooking fuel. The hulls are used as animal feed and compost |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Dry mung beans contain no vitamin C, so in winters without fresh vegetables scurvy (bleeding gums, wounds that do not heal) quietly advances over months. 100 g of dry mung beans becomes 500-700 g of sprouts; the added weight is mostly water, so calories do not increase, but vitamin C that was not there is created and phytic acid and inhibitors decrease.

| Sprouts | Method |
|:---|:---|
| 1. Selecting, soaking | From a small-seeded type, discard cracked or insect-eaten seeds (they rot and spoil the whole batch) and seeds that float when rinsed (usually dead), then soak in lukewarm water for 6-8 hours until doubled in size |
| 2. Filling, darkness | Put in a container with drainage or a perforated sprouting pot (siru), cover with a damp cloth, block light completely, and keep at 20-25 °C (if light gets in, they turn green, tough, and bitter) |
| 3. Watering | Pour water over generously 3-4 times a day and let it drain completely, skimming off the floating green hulls each time (left alone they rot and spoil all the sprouts). Many fine roots signal too little water |
| 4. Pressing, harvest | Place a light board or stone on top to thicken the stems, and harvest after 3-5 days at 4-7 cm, before the roots turn brown |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Above 30 °C they become soft and spoil quickly, and below 15 °C they grow very slowly. Without a sprouting pot, put a drainage tray under a jar, bucket, or sieve with holes in the bottom and cover with a black cloth; in winter, find a spot of around 20 °C, such as beside the kitchen hearth or at the end of a heated-floor room farthest from the firebox. They are safe to eat raw, but blanching briefly in boiling water and rinsing in cold water is better, and long blanching washes out vitamin C.

## 11. Annual Work Schedule
| Month | Work |
|:---|:---|
| Jan-Feb | Check storage, test seed germination, and grow sprouts (winter vitamin C). In February, turn the compost and decide the layout with the preceding crop |
| Mar-Apr | In March, spread lime (3-4 weeks before sowing). In late April, sow the first crop of southern double cropping and prepare mulching materials |
| May-June | In May, prepare ridges (20 cm or higher) and drainage ditches. In June, main sowing (early month in the central region, mid to late month in the southern region), mulching, and bird protection. Sow right after the preceding crop is harvested |
| July-Aug | In July, weeding and stakes (finish weeding before flowering) and the late-sowing deadline (replant failed fields up to this point). In August, aphid control, checking drainage ditches, and checking nodules |
| Sep-Oct | In September, start split harvesting and dry in mesh bags. In October, finish harvesting, thresh, clean, and return stems and leaves to the field |
| Nov-Dec | In November, drying, storage, processing, and managing the autumn crop planted where mung bean grew. In December, check storage, grow sprouts, and plan next year |

> **Mung bean earns its keep when you keep drainage with 20 cm ridges, pick black pods in split passes every 5-7 days, fill failed fields by late sowing, get winter vitamin C from sprouts, and return the stems to the field after harvest.**
