## 5. Field Preparation and Fertilizer - Nitrogen Balance and Fertilization

### 5-4. The Nitrogen Fixation Trap

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The saying that planting beans improves the soil has hardened into common wisdom in farming. Half of it is right and half is wrong. Without understanding exactly where the line falls, a field planted with legumes year after year can end up becoming depleted instead.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Let us work it out in numbers. When mung bean grows well, 120kg of dry seed is harvested from 1,000m². The protein of mung bean seed is 24.5%, and dividing protein by 6.25 gives the amount of nitrogen. 24.5 ÷ 6.25 is 3.92, that is, about 3.9% of the seed weight is pure nitrogen. The nitrogen contained in 120kg is about 4.7kg, which is 47kg when converted to a hectare.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Meanwhile, the nitrogen fixed by mung bean nodules is 50kg to 80kg per hectare when conditions are good. Because the growing period is short, the absolute amount is smaller than for soybean or fava bean. Putting what goes out next to what comes in gives the following.

| Item | Nitrogen per hectare | Direction |
|---|---|---|
| Nodule fixation (good conditions) | 50-80kg | In |
| Nodule fixation (nodules dead from excess moisture damage) | 10-20kg | In |
| Removed with the seed | 40-50kg | Out |
| Nitrogen left in the stems and leaves (haulm) | 20-30kg | Stays if returned; leaves if burned or carried away |
| Nitrogen left in the roots and nodules | 8-15kg | Usually stays in the field |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There is a reason this calculation is special for mung bean. Mung bean is a staged-harvest crop: only the pods are picked off in several rounds, and the season ends with the stems and leaves still standing in the field. Structurally, it is the bean for which returning the stems and leaves is easiest. After harvest, all that is needed is to lay the stems down where they stand or work them in, and because the stems are thin and soft, they rot away within days. This is why the original sources describe mung bean stubble as helpful for improving the soil.

> **Legumes restore the land not because they fix nitrogen, but because they leave part of the fixed nitrogen in the field, held in their stems and leaves. If those stems and leaves are carried away, the bean becomes a crop that plunders the soil.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In a year when the nodules die from excess moisture damage, however, this relationship reverses. If only 15kg is fixed and 45kg is carried out as seed, the field takes a definite loss. A mung bean field submerged in the monsoon season (jangma, late June to July in South Korea) loses not only yield but also nitrogen. Drainage management is, in effect, soil fertility management as well.

| Principle | Details |
|---|---|
| Stems and leaves always go back to the field | After the pods are picked, lay the remaining stems and leaves down in the field as they are, or work them in |
| Do not burn | Burning sends the nitrogen off as gas and leaves only potassium and phosphate behind as ash |
| Do not dig up the roots | The nitrogen in the roots and nodules is the easiest share to leave behind |
| Keep up drainage | If the nodules die of excess moisture, the fixation itself disappears |
| Plant the next crop right after | The next crop uses the nitrogen mung bean left behind right away |
| If you fed livestock, return the dung | If you fed the stems and leaves to animals, always put those animals' manure into that field |

### 5-5. Field Preparation

| Item | Method | In disaster conditions |
|---|---|---|
| Tillage | Plow to 20-30cm deep and level evenly | If the field is where a previous crop was harvested, just digging and turning the rows is enough |
| Bed | Height 20-25cm | The condition that decides the success or failure of growing mung bean |
| Drainage ditch | 30cm or more deep, so that water drains all the way out | Pour water to check before the monsoon |
| Acidity correction | pH 6.0-7.0 | Do it ahead of time, 3-4 weeks before sowing |
| Lime | 100-200kg/1,000m² on acid soil | Substitute burned and crushed shells or eggshells, or wood ash |
| Mulching | Straw, rice hulls, fallen leaves, plastic film | Effect: emergence up 16%, yield up 54% |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The effect of mulching is especially large in mung bean because three things overlap. First, it keeps monsoon rain from pounding the soil surface and crusting it. Mung bean is sown shallow, at 2-4cm, so if the surface hardens, young shoots cannot break through. Second, it suppresses weeds. Mung bean is weak against weeds and hard to enter once it has flowered, so early weed management is everything. Third, it keeps splashed soil off the pods and so reduces disease. Even without plastic film, much of the same effect is obtained with straw, rice hulls or fallen leaves.

### 5-6. Fertilizer Rates

| Fertilizer | Per 1,000m² | Per m² | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Nitrogen | 2-4kg | 2-4g | Minimum only. Too much makes plants leggy and they fall over |
| Phosphate | 4-7kg | 4-7g | All as base fertilizer |
| Potassium | 4-6kg | 4-6g | All as base fertilizer |
| Compost | 1,000kg | 1kg | Use fully rotted compost |
| Lime | 100-200kg (acid soil) | 100-200g | 3-4 weeks before sowing |

> **Holding back nitrogen in mung bean is not thrift but technique. The stem is weak to begin with, so with too much nitrogen the plant grows leggy and falls over, and once it has fallen, a harvest that must be done in several rounds becomes several times harder.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Top-dressing is, in principle, not needed. However, because the growing period is short, there is no time to catch up if the early stage goes badly. If growth is clearly poor at flowering and 15 days after flowering, supplement nitrogen in a very small amount, around 2kg per 1,000m². In a disaster, the alternative is to dilute urine 10 times with water and pour it on the soil between plants, taking care that it does not touch the leaves.

### 5-7. Disaster Substitute Fertilizers

| Substitute resource | Nutrients supplied | How to use | Caution |
|---|---|---|---|
| Wood ash | Potassium, phosphate, calcium | Spread 100-150g per m² and mix in | Alkaline, so a lot of it pushes the pH above 7 |
| Well-rotted compost | All-round | Apply 1kg per m² as base fertilizer | Poorly rotted compost burns roots and carries disease |
| Diluted urine | Nitrogen, potassium | Dilute 10 times with water and pour on the soil between plants | Very small amounts only. Watch for leggy growth |
| Bone meal | Phosphate, calcium | Burn and crush, and mix into compost | The effect appears slowly |
| Fish bones and fish guts | Phosphate, nitrogen | Bury and let them decompose before use | If buried as they are, animals dig them up |
| Shells and eggshells | Calcium | Burn, crush and scatter | A substitute for lime. The reaction is slow |
| Residue of the previous crop | Organic matter | Cover with barley straw, garlic stalks or potato stems as mulch and fertilizer at once | Mung bean is often planted as a succeeding crop, so this is highly useful |
| Rice hulls and rice straw | Potassium, silica | Cover as mulch | The mulching effect is especially large in mung bean |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Because mung bean is often planted right after the previous crop is harvested, the most natural thing is to use the previous crop's residue as it is. On a field where barley was harvested, spread barley straw; where garlic was harvested, spread garlic stalks, and use them as mulch right there. As this residue decays it becomes organic matter, mung bean grows on top of it and fixes nitrogen, and after the mung bean is harvested its stems and leaves remain once again. It is an arrangement in which the cycle continues inside the field without bringing anything in from outside.

## 6. Sowing - Double Cropping and Late Sowing

### 6-1. Sowing Time

| Region / cropping type | Best sowing time | Latest sowing time | Harvest time |
|---|---|---|---|
| Central South Korea, single crop | Early to mid June | Mid-July | Early to mid September |
| Southern South Korea, single crop | Mid to late June | Late July | Mid to late September |
| Succeeding crop (after a previous crop) | Mid June to early July | Mid-July | Late September to early October |
| Southern South Korea, double cropping, 1st crop | Late April to early May | - | Early July |
| Southern South Korea, double cropping, 2nd crop | Early to mid July | Late July | Late September |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Mung bean's sowing window runs for four months, from early April to late July (in South Korea). It is the widest of the legumes, and this is the practical value of mung bean. The limit line, however, is set by frost. Counting back 90 days from the expected date of the first frost gives the last sowing date for that region.

> **Mung bean's strength shows when farming does not go as planned. When spring crops die of cold damage, or when a field flooded in the monsoon has to be replanted, there is hardly any grain crop other than mung bean that can still be planted in mid-July.**

### 6-2. Planning Double Cropping

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In southern South Korea, double cropping of mung bean is theoretically possible, but in practice conditions attach to it. If the first crop is sown in late April, the pod-filling period falls from late June to early July, that is, at the leading edge of the monsoon. Ripe mung bean pods that are rained on sprout or rot inside, so this period is risky. Therefore the following two arrangements are more realistic than pure double cropping.

| Arrangement | Details | Advantage |
|---|---|---|
| Succeeding crop after a previous crop | Mung bean on the spot after barley, wheat, garlic, onion or potato harvested in June | Uses the field twice while avoiding monsoon risk |
| Autumn crop after mung bean | After harvesting mung bean in September, napa cabbage, radish or spinach | The autumn crop uses the nitrogen mung bean left behind |
| Mung bean double cropping | Sowing in late April, then re-sowing in early July | South Korea's southern coast only. Risk that the first crop's pod-filling overlaps with the monsoon |
| For recovering from failure | Sowing up to July where another crop has collapsed | Keeps the year from being lost completely |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;A succeeding crop after a previous crop is the most stable and most widely used arrangement. A June field from which barley, garlic or potatoes have been harvested has already been tilled and still has some fertilizer left in it. Simply re-forming the beds and clearing the drainage ditches lets you plant mung bean right away. The energy of turning the field over is shared with the previous crop, so the weakness of the D for Tillage & digging energy is also offset to a considerable degree.

### 6-3. Seed Preparation and Sowing Method

| Item | Method | In disaster conditions |
|---|---|---|
| Sorting | Discard seed with insect holes, discoloration or wrinkles | The seeds are small and sorting is tedious, but always do it |
| Sinking in water | Soak in water for 3-4 hours and use only those that sink | Those that float are hollow or insect-eaten |
| Hot-water disinfection | Soak in 50-55°C water for 10-15 minutes | Kills seed-borne pathogens |
| Ash treatment | Coat with fine wood ash | Helps disinfection and drying, and repels birds |
| Nodule inoculation | Coat with soil from a field where adzuki bean or mung bean was grown | See Section 5-3. Not done together with disinfection |
| Germination test | Wrap 20 seeds in a damp cloth, leave for a few days and count | Germination is fast, so results come in three or four days |

| Item | Standard | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Sowing depth | 2-4cm | Seeds are small and cannot emerge if sown deep |
| Seeds per hole | 2-4 seeds | Later thinned to leave 2 plants |
| Row spacing (single crop) | 60cm | Secures ventilation and a path for the repeated picking rounds |
| Row spacing (succeeding crop) | 40-60cm | Plants grow small, so rows can be narrowed |
| Plant spacing | 10-15cm | If crowded, plants grow leggy and fall over |
| Seeding rate (single crop) | 1.5-2kg/1,000m² | The lowest among legumes |
| Seeding rate (succeeding crop) | 2-3kg/1,000m² | The later the sowing, the denser |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The reason for leaving generous row spacing is special in mung bean. You must go into the field three or four times to pick ripe pods, and if the rows are narrow, people passing through break the stems and knock off pods that are not yet ripe. Securing harvest paths in advance ends up protecting the yield.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The threat right after sowing is birds. Mung bean germinates quickly, in four to five days, so the exposure period is short, but the cotyledons are large and tender, and pigeons and pheasants peck at them. Cover the sown rows with a thin layer of straw or put netting or an old window screen over them, and walk around the field morning and evening for the first few days of germination.

## 7. Water Management

| Period | Water management | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Sowing to germination | Do not let the soil dry out completely | Seeds are buried shallow, so if the surface layer dries, germination stops |
| After true leaves unfold | Keep somewhat dry | Too much moisture makes plants leggy and rots roots |
| Flowering and pod-setting stage | Irrigate if drought is severe | Water shortage at this time cuts yield the most |
| Monsoon season | Clear the drainage ditches | The most dangerous stretch in growing mung bean |
| Pod-filling stage | Stop irrigating | Ripe pods that get wet with rain sprout inside |

> **Water management in mung bean is not the art of giving water but the art of draining it. Among legumes it is the most vulnerable to excess moisture damage, and its growing period overlaps exactly with the monsoon.**

| Symptom | Cause | Action |
|---|---|---|
| Leaves droop at midday and recover in the evening | Temporary water shortage. Within the normal range | Leave as is |
| Leaves still droop in the morning | Severe dryness | Irrigate |
| Lower leaves turn yellow and roots are black | Root rot from excess moisture | Clear the drainage ditches and hill up soil |
| Entire leaf is pale yellow but not wilting | Nodules died from excess moisture and nitrogen was cut off | Dig up a root to check, then give a little diluted urine |
| Flowers bloom but pods do not set | Dryness during flowering, or aphid damage | Irrigate and check the undersides of leaves |
| Only stems grow long and fall over | Too much nitrogen or too much moisture | Reduce water and reduce fertilizer the next year |
| Seeds have sprouted inside ripe pods | Rained on during pod-filling | The only remedy is to pick as soon as they turn black |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The last item is the most frustrating loss in growing mung bean. When fully ripe pods are rained on, the seeds begin to germinate inside, and can be neither eaten nor planted. The only way to prevent this is to pick as soon as the pods turn black. If there are ripening pods during a stretch of continuing monsoon rain, it is better to pick them even slightly before they are fully black and dry them indoors.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Mulching is also of great value in water management. It reduces surface evaporation to prepare for drought, and at the same time keeps rain from splashing soil, reducing disease. If it is laid on thick and stays constantly damp, however, root rot increases instead, so cover to about 5cm and, in the monsoon season, pull back the mulch a little at the shoulder of the bed so the soil can dry.
