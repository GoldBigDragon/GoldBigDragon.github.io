## 1. Disaster-Preparedness Evaluation

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Mung bean's Overall grade is C, with a score of 42.0. Broken down by scenario, its Short-term ability to get through the early phase of a disaster is C (40.7), its Long-term ability to sustain itself over several years is C (43.3), and its Supplementary value as a side-dish resource beyond the staple is SS (63.7). The fact that only the Supplementary grade is unusually high sums up the character of mung bean. It cannot become the staple meal, but it is a crop that fills the empty places on the table.

### 1-3. How to Read the Strengths

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Four criteria carry an SS: Protein density, Fertilizer demand, Infrastructure needs and Storability. S went to Micronutrients, Water use, Taste, Prevalence and Seed saving, and A went to Speed to harvest, Pest-control self-reliance, Yield retention without synthetic inputs, Yield stability and Seed longevity.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The A for Speed to harvest is the identity of mung bean. It is harvested in 80-90 days, or in 60 days when conditions are good. What this shortness creates is not simply speed but generous room in the sowing date. It can be sown at any time from early April to late July, so the sowing window runs to four months. Compared with soybean, whose window is a little over a month, from early to late June, it is a different order of magnitude.

> **In a disaster, plans always go wrong. Seedlings are lost to a late frost, fields are flooded in the monsoon season (jangma, late June to July in South Korea), and what has been planted collapses to disease. Whether there is a crop that can be planted again then decides the year. Mung bean is almost the only legume crop that can still be replanted as late as late July.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The SS for Protein density is also worth noting. A P:E of 27.9% ranks next after soybean and fava bean, and is higher than kidney bean, chickpea and cowpea. It means that even if the grains are small and the yield is low, the quality of the protein inside them is high. The Supplementary grade of SS, 63.7 points, comes from here.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The S for Water use and the A for Pest-control self-reliance should be read together. With a total evapotranspiration of 250mm it uses little water, and without chemicals it can be kept largely under control by cultural means. It is a labor-intensive crop, but that labor is work, not materials. In conditions where nothing comes in from outside, a crop whose needs can be replaced by labor is better than a crop that demands materials.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The Prevalence S and the Seed longevity A are also of great value. Mung bean is grown across South Korea, so seed is easy to find, and dry seed lasts three to four years at room temperature. With good care, germination remains for five to six years. Securing seed and carrying it on is free of the difficulties of chickpea or lentil.

### 1-4. How to Read the Weaknesses

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Only one criterion carries an E: Cold hardiness. D went to Calories per area, Perenniality, the sunlight response, Cooking & processing, Tillage energy and Non-food value, and C went to Temperature range and Pest & disease pressure.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The D for Calories per area weighs the most. At 422kcal per m², it is 0.16 times the self-sufficiency line. It is last among the legumes in this crop table and falls short of even half of soybean. The D for Tillage & digging energy also derives from this. The energy recovered as calories is 2.6 times the energy spent on working the field, which means that ploughing a field to plant mung bean leaves almost nothing in the energy account. Mung bean should not be made the main crop of a field. The right way is to lay it onto spots that are already prepared, such as an empty field after another crop is harvested, a corner of the field, or the space between raised beds.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The E for Cold hardiness is a weakness common to summer legumes. A single frost kills it. In regions where the first frost arrives from late September to early October, mid-July is the sowing limit. Because the advantage of late sowing and the weakness of dying to frost interlock, working out the last sowing date becomes the key judgment in growing mung bean.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The note beside Perenniality D, that maturity is uneven and the crop is harvested in three or four rounds, is both a strength and a weakness. As a strength, the harvest is spread out instead of being lost all at once, so there is something to eat from time to time. As a weakness, the labor is split into several rounds, and if ripe pods are not picked in time they burst open by themselves and drop the seeds on the ground. Where hands are short, this is a real constraint.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;And there is one more weakness that the grade table does not capture: excess moisture damage. Mung bean is the legume most vulnerable to standing water, and mung bean's growing period in South Korea overlaps exactly with the monsoon. Raising the bed by 20cm or more and cutting deep drainage ditches is not an option for mung bean but a precondition. A trial has indeed found that raising the bed and mulching increased emergence by 16% and yield by 54%.

## 2. What Kind of Plant Is This?

### 2-1. Classification and Origin

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Mung bean is an annual of the genus Vigna in the legume family (Fabaceae). It is in the same genus as the adzuki bean and the cowpea, and is much closer to them than to soybean or pea. This fact matters in practice. The root-nodule bacteria that partner with mung bean belong to the Bradyrhizobium group and are compatible with the strains used by adzuki bean and cowpea. This means that soil from a field where adzuki bean or cowpea was grown can be used as an inoculum, and because adzuki bean is commonly grown in South Korea, securing an inoculum is not difficult.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Its origin is India and the surrounding region. As a plant that grew up in the tropical and subtropical monsoon climate, it likes heat and withstands hot weather. Its optimum growth temperature is 28-30°C, the highest among legumes, and it tolerates temperatures above 35°C. On the other hand, frost kills it outright, and growth stops below 15°C. This is not the opposite of soybean's nature, but a nature tilted one step further toward the warm side than soybean.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Mung bean is self-pollinating. The flowers are yellow and small, and the petals enclose the male and female parts, so outside pollen has difficulty getting in. Even when several varieties are planted side by side, their traits do not mix, and seed that is saved and planted again gives plants identical to the parent.

### 2-2. Appearance

| Organ | Characteristics |
|---|---|
| Leaves | Trifoliate, with three leaflets in a set. Close to heart-shaped, with fine hairs |
| Stem | Stands upright but is weak. 60-80cm tall and branching |
| Roots | A straight taproot with fine roots spreading out, and nodules attached |
| Flowers | Yellow and small. They keep blooming throughout the later part of growth |
| Pods | Slender, long and cylindrical, 5-10cm. They turn black when ripe |
| Seeds | Small, round and dark green. A single seed weighs about 0.05g, among the smallest of the legumes |

> **Mung bean pods turn black, not brown, when ripe. And within a few days of turning black they twist and burst open on their own. When you see a black pod, pick it that day or the next.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;What most sets mung bean apart from other legumes is that flowering and maturing go on continuously. Soybean turns yellow across the whole field at once and is cut in a single pass, but mung bean keeps flowering from the bottom upward, so a single plant carries black pods, green pods and flowers at the same time. Because of this, the pods must be picked in three or four rounds, and cutting everything down and laying it out in one pass loses more than half.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The weak stem also matters in practice. Mung bean grows 60-80cm tall, but the stem is thin and soft and falls over easily, especially after rain or when it has grown leggy from too much fertilizer. When it falls, the pods touch the soil and rot, and the harvest, which must be done in several rounds, becomes much harder.

### 2-3. Growth Stages

| Stage | Timing | Key physiology | Important nutrients |
|---|---|---|---|
| Germination and seedling stage | 4-5 days from sowing | Germination is very fast, among the fastest of the legumes | Phosphorus, a small amount of nitrogen |
| Vegetative growth stage | Weeks 3-6 | The stem rises and branches | Nitrogen is supplied by the nodules |
| Flowering and pod-setting stage | Weeks 6-8 | Flowers begin to open and continue throughout the later part of growth | Phosphorus, potassium |
| Pod-filling and maturity stage | Weeks 8-12 | Pods ripen to black starting from the bottom. The staged harvest period | Potassium, stop irrigation |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Germination in four to five days is very fast, another advantage of mung bean. Compared with chickpea at ten to fifteen days and soybean at five to seven days, it is clearly faster. The result is known within days of planting, so if it fails, the crop can be replanted right away. Together with the short growing period, this quick germination makes mung bean a crop for recovering from failure.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Because the growing period is short, early management almost decides the yield. If the crop is overwhelmed by weeds, submerged in water, or short of nutrients during the 3-4 weeks after sowing, there is no time to recover. Conversely, if it gets through this period well, it needs little labor afterward.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There is a point about early nitrogen worth noting. The nodules do not work right after sowing. It takes 3 weeks for the bacteria to infect, for nodules to form, and for nitrogen to be handed over. In mung bean, with a growing period of 80 days, these 3 weeks are more than 25% of the total. That is why putting a small amount of nitrogen into the base fertilizer makes more sense than for other beans. It must be only a small amount, however. When the soil has plenty of nitrogen, the plant reduces nodule formation by itself, and only the stems grow leggy and fall over.

| Nutrient | Content | Notes |

| Fat | About 1g | Very little. Almost no rancidity during storage |

| Iron | About 6mg | An important source when there is no meat |
| Potassium | About 1,200mg | |
| Folate | Abundant | Involved in anemia and fetal development |
| Vitamin C (in sprouts) | Newly formed | Absent from dry mung beans, but made on germination |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Dry mung beans and mung bean sprouts (sukjunamul) are in fact different foods. Dry mung beans contain no vitamin C, but when sprouted it is newly synthesized during germination. At the same time, the weight grows five- to sixfold, and water makes up most of it, so the energy density falls sharply. Dry mung beans are a store of protein and starch, and the sprouts are a source of vitamins and water. In a disaster, both need to be used.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The amino acid composition needs to be looked at separately. Mung bean protein is rich in lysine and leucine and a little short in methionine. Cereals are exactly the opposite. Eaten together with rice or bread, they fill in each other's gaps, and protein utilization rises noticeably.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The fat content of 1%, the lowest among legumes, is an advantage in storage. Almost no rancidity occurs, so even ground into flour it keeps for a long time. The tradition of extracting mung bean starch to make cheongpomuk is also possible thanks to this low fat content. In exchange for low energy density, it became the bean that is easiest to keep.
