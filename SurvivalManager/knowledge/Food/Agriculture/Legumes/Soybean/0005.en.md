## 9. Pests and Diseases - Pests and Control

### 9-2. Major Pests

| Pest | Damage | Control without chemicals |
|---|---|---|
| Stink bugs (including the bean bug) | Pierce the pods and suck the sap, leaving empty pods. The biggest pest of soybean | Their movement is sluggish in the early morning when dew is present, so catch them by hand and drop them in a bucket of water. Plant a trap crop around the field to drive them there and catch them |
| Aphids | Suck sap and transmit viral diseases | Spray soapy water (5g of soap in 1 L of water) on the undersides of the leaves. Protect ladybugs and lacewings |
| Soybean pod borer | Larvae enter the pods and eat the seeds | Avoid the outbreak period by early sowing and early harvest. Clear away residue to remove overwintering sites |
| Tobacco cutworm | Devours leaves and pods | Catch by hand. If you find an egg mass, remove it together with the leaf |
| Spider mites | Suck sap from the undersides of the leaves, leaving the leaves mottled yellow | Increase when it is dry. Spray water hard on the leaves |
| Birds (pigeons, magpies, pheasants) | Peck the cotyledons right after sowing | Cover with straw or netting until germination |
| Korean water deer and roe deer | Eat young leaves and stems | Put up a fence or netting |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Stink bugs are soybean's biggest pest. They pierce the pod wall and suck the young seeds inside; on the outside the pod looks normal, but when opened the seeds are shriveled or missing altogether. There are three ways to deal with stink bugs without insecticide.

| Method | Details | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Catching in the early morning | Stink bugs do not fly well in the cool early morning. Search under the shaded pods, shake the bugs off by hand, and catch them in a bucket of water | The most reliable. Done for several days in a row, the population drops noticeably |
| Trap crops | Plant a crop the stink bugs like better (sesame, foxtail millet, sorghum) around the field to gather them, then catch them intensively there | Reduces damage in the main field |
| Adjusting harvest time | Plant early-maturing varieties so that grain filling finishes before late summer, when stink bugs increase | An avoidance strategy. Decided at the stage of choosing a variety |

### 9-3. Control Materials Without Chemicals

| Material | How to make it | Target | Caution |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bordeaux mixture | Dissolve 400g of copper sulfate and 400g of quicklime separately, then mix them into 100 L of water | Bacterial and fungal diseases | It can be made only if you have copper. Use immediately after mixing |
| Soapy water | 5g of unscented soap per 1 L of water | Aphids, spider mites | Sprayed in the heat of midday, it burns the leaves |
| Wood ash water | Soak 1 doe (about 1.8 L) of ash in 10 L of water overnight and use the clear liquid on top | Suppresses mold, strengthens leaves | It is alkaline and can damage leaves, so use it diluted |
| Dusting with dry ash | Sprinkle fine ash on the leaves in the morning when dew is present | Soft rot, leaf-eating insects | Rain washes it off |
| Wood vinegar | Collect the smoke released when making charcoal, cool it into a liquid, and dilute it 300-500 times | Repellent, growth promotion | The undiluted liquid is strongly acidic, so always dilute it |
| Tobacco leaf infusion | Steep dried tobacco leaves in water and use | Aphids, moth caterpillars | It is toxic to people too, so do not use it right before harvest |
| Hot pepper and garlic water | Grind hot peppers and garlic, steep in water, strain, and use | Repellent | The effect does not last long, so it must be sprayed often |
| Diluted milk | Dilute milk 5-10 times with water | Powdery mildew, spider mites | Leftover milk spoils and smells |

### 9-4. Cultural Control - The Strongest Tool

| Method | Principle |
|---|---|
| Seed sorting | A good many soybean diseases are seed-borne. Picking out spotted and discolored seeds alone greatly reduces outbreaks |
| Crop rotation | Pathogens slowly die in the soil without a host. Keep legumes out of the field for 2-3 years |
| Adjusting sowing time | Shift it so that the peak pest outbreak and the plant's vulnerable stage do not overlap |
| Securing planting distance | With good ventilation, leaves dry quickly and mold cannot take hold |
| Drainage | More than half of soybean diseases are related to excess moisture |
| Residue disposal | Burn or deeply bury diseased residue. Return only healthy residue to the field |
| No entering the field after rain | If a person brushes wet leaves, pathogens are carried over |
| Resistant varieties | Landraces that have survived in a region have most likely already adapted to that region's diseases |

> **Where there are no chemicals, the center of gravity of control shifts from spraying to avoiding. Choosing seed, rotating fields, draining water, and widening spacing - these four are more effective than any spraying.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Separating diseased residue from healthy residue is a point that conflicts with the nitrogen balance in section 5. To keep nitrogen, the stems and leaves must be returned to the field, but returning diseased stems and leaves leaves the pathogens behind. If the leaves have only a few spots, they can still be chopped and used as cover. Only plants with Sclerotinia stem rot, in which the stem turns soft, anthracnose, with its black cankers, or mosaic disease should be collected separately and burned or left to rot for a long time outside the field.

## 10. Nutrient Deficiency and Excess

| Symptom | Cause | Remedy with natural materials |
|---|---|---|
| Entire plant pale yellow starting from the lower leaves, plants small | Nitrogen deficiency. In soybean, poor nodulation is the most common cause | First dig up the roots and check the nodules. If there are none, make sure to inoculate next year, and this year give a small amount of urine diluted 10 times |
| Leaf margins dry brown and curl inward | Potassium deficiency | Scatter wood ash between the plants and lightly rake it in |
| Growth is slow and the leaves take on a purplish tint | Phosphorus deficiency | Bone meal, powder of burned fish bones, wood ash |
| Flowers open but pods do not set | Boron deficiency, or high temperature and dryness at flowering | Compost made from mixed materials. This year, lower the temperature with irrigation and mulch |
| Veins stay green but the tissue between them is yellow | Iron or magnesium deficiency. Common in soil tilted toward alkaline | Stop using ash. Increase organic matter with leaf mold and fully matured compost |
| New leaves are small and deformed, and the growing point dies | Calcium or boron deficiency | Powder of burned eggshells, shell powder |
| Only the leaves are dark green and lush, with few pods | Nitrogen excess | Stop the nitrogen supply immediately. Induce side branches by pinching |
| Plants grow leggy and fall over | Nitrogen excess or dense planting | Hilling and supporting the rows. Reduce the fertilizer rate next year |
| Lower leaves turn yellow and roots are brown | Root damage from excess moisture | Dig drainage ditches. It is not a fertilizer problem |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;When you see symptoms of nitrogen deficiency in soybean, the first thing to do is not to apply fertilizer but to dig up the roots and look. The nitrogen source of soybean is the nodules, not the soil. If the nodules are well formed and the inside of a split one is red but the leaves are yellow, the problem is not nitrogen but something else. If there are no nodules or the inside is white, that is the cause, and even then giving plenty of nitrogen is not a solution, because when soil nitrogen increases, even the remaining nodules reduce their function. The right order is to get by with the minimum this year and fix the root cause next year with inoculation and acidity correction.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Trace-element deficiencies are hard to treat one by one. Without chemical fertilizer there is practically no way to obtain molybdenum or cobalt. Instead, compost made by rotting vegetable scraps, fruit peels, eggshells, fish bones, fallen leaves, weeds, and ash together contains trace elements in a balanced way. The principle of putting everything into the compost pile is the practical strategy for managing trace elements.

## 11. Intercropping and Crop Rotation

### 11-1. Compatibility

| Compatibility | Crop | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Good | Maize, sorghum | They share, from alongside, part of the nitrogen the soybean fixes. The height difference lets them share light |
| Good | Marigold | Root exudates suppress nematodes |
| Good | Potato | Their diseases do not overlap and their nutrient demands differ |
| Good | Cabbage and napa cabbage group | Planted after soybean, they use the leftover nitrogen |
| Fair | Cucumber and squash group | Keep a distance so the vines do not smother the soybean |
| Poor | Tomato, chili pepper, eggplant | They share pests and diseases in the same field |
| Poor | Garlic and onion group | There are reports that their exudates suppress root-nodule bacteria |
| Poor | Continuous cropping with other legumes | Pathogens and nematodes accumulate |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Planting maize and soybean mixed together is a combination long in use. Maize uses the light above and soybean the light below, and while maize demands a lot of nitrogen, soybean makes its own. However, soybean is a crop whose yield falls by half under 40% shade, so if it sits deep in the maize shade it falls sharply. Alternate two rows of maize with two rows of soybean, and orient the maize rows north-south so that shade does not stay on one side for long. If soybeans are sown scattered between maize plants, almost no soybeans are harvested.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The relationship with the Allium group is a point of debate. Observations that the sulfur compounds of garlic and onion suppress the activity of root-nodule bacteria have been reported in several places. It has not been established as conclusive evidence, but since nitrogen fixation is the core mechanism of soybean cultivation, there is no reason to take the risk, so place them apart.

### 11-2. Crop Rotation

| Item | Standard |
|---|---|
| Rotation break | 1 year or more. Do not plant legumes in the same field in succession |
| Ideal cycle | 3-4 years |
| Continuous-cropping injury | Soybean tolerates continuous cropping better than most legumes, but nematodes and root rot accumulate |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Soybean's Continuous-cropping tolerance is S, good among legumes, and it belongs to the RDA 1-year break group. Compared with adzuki bean at 3 years and peanut at 4 to 5 years, there is some leeway. But this means it can tolerate it, not that it is fine to plant repeatedly. If continuous cropping goes on for more than 3 years, soybean cyst nematodes and root rot accumulate and the yield falls.

| Example of a 4-year rotation |
|---|
| Year 1: Soybean |
| Year 2: Leafy vegetables or Allium group (they use the nitrogen the soybean left) |
| Year 3: Cereals or tuber crops (tubers/storage roots) (change the soil layer used, with crops of different rooting depth) |
| Year 4: Root vegetables or green manure (let the field rest) |
| Then soybean again |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Here is a compromise for when the field is too small to rotate. The simplest approach is to divide the field into four plots and plant beans in one plot at a time, moving on each year. If the field is a small one that cannot be divided, at least move the position of the soybean row every year. Just planting maize this year in last year's soybean row and soybeans this year in last year's maize row slows nematode buildup considerably. After soybean, the napa cabbage and cabbage group, which demand a lot of nitrogen, and maize suit best.

## 12. Harvest and Threshing

### 12-1. Judging the Harvest Time

| Criterion | Details |
|---|---|
| Leaves | The leaves have turned yellow and nearly all have fallen |
| Pods | 80% or more of all pods have turned brown |
| Sound | When shaken in the field, the seeds rattle inside the pods |
| Seeds | When bitten, the teeth do not sink in and the seed cracks crisply |
| Moisture | Seed moisture of 13-15% |
| Timing | 7-14 days after all the leaves have fallen |

| Region | Harvest time |
|---|---|
| Central South Korea | Early to mid October |
| Southern South Korea | Mid to late October |
| Medium-late varieties for meju (fermented soybean blocks) | Late October-mid November |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Missing the harvest time causes loss in two directions. If too early, the seeds are not filled and are shriveled, and they spoil easily in storage. If too late, the pods dry and burst on their own, dropping the seeds on the ground; this is called shattering, and in a late harvest nearly 30% can be lost. In particular, when dry pods get rained on and dry again repeatedly, shattering increases sharply.

> **If rain is forecast, do not wait for full maturity; harvest. Slightly underripe beans can be dried and used, but beans that have fallen on the ground cannot be harvested.**

### 12-2. Harvesting Method

| Step | Procedure |
|---|---|
| 1 | Choose a clear, dry day. After 10 a.m., when the dew has dried, is best |
| 2 | Cut the base of the stem with a sickle; to keep the nodules, cut only the stems and leave the roots |
| 3 | Spread in the field for one to two days for preliminary drying. If rain comes, bring it in immediately |
| 4 | Tie into bundles and finish drying by standing or hanging them under the eaves or in a shed |
| 5 | Thresh when the pods are bone dry and rattle when shaken |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In conventional practice the whole plant is sometimes pulled up, but under disaster conditions it is better to cut only the stems with a sickle and leave the roots in the field. The nitrogen in the roots and nodules comes to 10 to 20kg per hectare, and when those roots rot, they become organic matter for the next crop.

### 12-3. Threshing - Methods Without Machinery

| Method | Tool | Procedure | Efficiency |
|---|---|---|---|
| Trampling in a sack | A thick burlap sack or bag | Put dry bean stalks in a sack, tie the mouth, and tread on it with the feet or rub it by hand | The easiest for small amounts. The seeds do not fly out |
| Flail (dorikkae) | A long handle with a swinging beater of switches attached | Spread out a straw mat (meongseok) or cloth, lay the bean stalks thinly on it, and strike from above | Suited to medium amounts. Turn them over two or three times as you beat |
| Beating with a club | A wooden stick | Put them in a sack or spread them on a mat and beat | Used when you have no flail. Takes more effort |
| Rubbing with the feet | None | Spread on a mat and rub and turn with feet wearing shoes | Suited to small amounts |
| Striking against a board | A plank or tub | Hold a bundle of bean stalks and strike it against the inner wall of the board | The seeds collect in the tub. Little loss |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The success of threshing depends on drying. Bean stalks that are not dry enough will not open the pods no matter how much you beat them, and beans that are too dry break when beaten. The right moment is when pods pop open when pressed by hand, and doing it in the afternoon on a sunny day makes it much easier.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Trampling in a sack is the most sensible method for small-scale self-sufficiency. It needs no tools, the seeds do not fly in all directions so there is almost no loss, and it takes less effort. Fill a thick burlap sack halfway with dry bean stalks, tie the mouth, lay it on the ground, and tread on it evenly with the feet. Tread about ten times, shake the sack to change the position, and tread again, repeating this three or four times; by the time the bean stalks are broken into short pieces, it is done.

### 12-4. Cleaning - Separating with the Wind

| Method | Principle | Procedure |
|---|---|---|
| Wind winnowing (letting the wind clear it) | Bean seeds are heavy; husks and debris are light | On a windy day, put the beans in a winnowing basket or wide vessel, hold it high, and pour slowly. The seeds fall straight down and the chaff blows away |
| Basket winnowing | Shake up and down and toss forward | The heavy seeds stay behind and the lighter material is pushed forward |
| Fanning | One person pours and one person makes wind with a fan | Used on days with no wind |
| Sieving | Separates by hole size | Use a coarse sieve to strain out pieces of bean stalk, and a fine sieve to drop out soil and small seeds |
| Floating in water | Empty husks and insect-eaten seeds are light | Pour into a bucket of water and skim off those that float. Beans taken out must be dried again |
| Rolling down an inclined board | Round seeds roll well and debris catches | Set a plank at a slant and roll the seeds down it |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Wind winnowing is a method that has been used since people began handling grain, and even now there is no simpler manual cleaning method. There are three knacks. First, choose a time and place where the wind blows steadily. Wind in the early morning or at dusk is good. Second, do not pour everything at once; let it trickle down in a thin stream. If poured thick, chaff drops mixed in among the seeds. Third, repeat two or three times.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Floating in water does cleaning and sorting at once. Seeds that bean weevils have eaten out are hollow and float, and empty husks float too, so sorting this way greatly reduces storage losses. However, beans soaked in water must be spread thinly and fully dried again within the same day. If stored wet, mold appears within days. Do not use it for seed beans. Soaking starts the physiology of germination, and drying them in that state lowers germination ability.

## 13. Seed Saving

| Item | Method |
|---|---|
| Selecting seed plants | Mark in advance, in the field, plants that show no disease symptoms, carry many pods, and have not lodged |
| Harvest time | Harvest after the pods have turned completely brown. Harvest slightly later than for food |
| Separation | Thresh separately. Do not let it mix with food beans |
| Sorting | Discard all spotted, wrinkled, discolored, and small seeds. Keep only plump, glossy seeds |
| Drying | Moisture of 12-13%. When bitten, the teeth do not sink in and the seed cracks crisply |
| Storage | Put in an airtight container and keep in a cool, dark place. A container separate from the food beans |
| Storage period | 3 years airtight at room temperature. The germination rate falls each year |
| Renewal | Every 3-4 years, obtain seed from somewhere else and mix some in |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Soybean is a self-pollinating crop, so seed saving is very easy. Traits do not scatter even when different varieties are planted mixed together, and unlike maize, no isolation distance is needed. It means that even if seed companies and distribution networks disappear, beans continue on their own, and this is where the S grade for Seed saving comes from.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;But there is something to watch when continuing over several years. Repeated seed saving quietly accumulates seed-borne diseases. Purple seed stain, anthracnose, and mosaic disease all pass to the next generation through the seed. Picking out visible spots can prevent much of it, but latent infections with no visible symptoms are not filtered out. So every 3-4 years it is good to obtain beans from a neighboring village or another valley and mix some in.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The habit of marking seed beans in the field in advance changes the results greatly. If you select by looking only at the seeds after harvest, you select only for size and shape. If you select in the field, you can select together for traits that really matter, such as the number of pods, lodging resistance, disease resistance, and the height of the first pod. If you tie a string on good plants during flowering and grain filling and harvest only those plants separately at harvest, after a few years you will have a line that has improved bit by bit to suit the conditions of that field.

| Seed storage period and germination rate |
|---|
| Year of harvest: 90% or more |
| After 1 year: 80-90% |
| After 2 years: 60-80% |
| After 3 years: 40-60% |
| After 4 years: drops sharply |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The Seed longevity grade of soybean is A, 3 years airtight at room temperature. But the germination rate in the third year falls to half, so the right approach is to take fresh seed every year and keep the old seed as a reserve. Always run a germination test first on old seed. If the germination rate is half, double the seeding rate to compensate; if it is below 30%, it is better to eat it than use it as seed.
