## 3. Growing Conditions

### 3-1. Temperature and Light

| Condition | Limit | Optimum | If it is missed |
|---|---|---|---|
| Germination temperature | 10°C or higher | 25-30°C | At 10°C or lower, germination nearly stops and the seed rots |
| Growth temperature | 15-35°C | 20-25°C | Growth stops at 15°C or lower, and flowers drop above 35°C |
| Seed-set temperature | 18-32°C | 20-30°C | A narrow range, only 10°C wide |
| High temperature at flowering | 35°C or higher for several days | - | Flowers and young pods simply drop off |
| Accumulated temperature | 2,500°C or more | 2,500-3,000°C | If it falls short, frost arrives before grain filling is finished |
| Frost | Dies when the temperature falls below freezing | - | It has no cold hardiness. Finish the harvest before the first frost |
| Sunshine | 6 hours or more a day | 8 hours or more a day | Under 40% shade, yield drops by half |
| Day length | Short-day plant | Flowers as days shorten | Where light falls at night, flowering is delayed |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The narrowest gate for soybean is the temperature for seed set. It is 20-30°C, only 10°C wide. Below this, pods do not set; above it, flowers that have already formed drop. If days above 35°C continue for several days in a South Korean summer, the flowers that open during that time are mostly wasted. It is normal physiology for soybean to shed more than half of the flowers it opens, but when high temperature and dryness coincide, the flower drop rate can exceed 80%.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The practical response to this vulnerability is to stagger the sowing dates. Instead of sowing everything at once, sow two or three times at 2-week intervals, and the flowering periods are spread out. One of them will miss the heat-wave period. This strategy works even when seed is not plentiful. Not staking the whole year's harvest on a single climatic condition is the basic principle of cultivation for disaster preparedness.

### 3-2. Soil and Water

| Condition | Limit | Optimum | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Soil texture | From clay to sandy soil | Well-drained sandy loam to loam | Poorly drained ground is the worst |
| pH | 5.5-7.5 | 6.0-6.5 | In acid soil, nodule formation drops sharply |
| Effective soil depth | 30cm or more | 50cm or more | The taproot goes deep |
| Evapotranspiration over the season | - | 450-700mm | Among legumes, it is a rather heavy water user |
| Flooding | Dies if submerged for two days or more | - | The nodules die first |
| Water table | 50cm or deeper | 80cm or deeper | The most important condition when planting in a paddy field |

> **Soybean's pH requirement is really the requirement of the root-nodule bacteria rather than of the plant itself. Below pH 5.5 the beans manage to grow somehow, but the bacteria cannot live and nitrogen fixation stops.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This is why soil acidity matters so much for soybean. The root-nodule bacteria (Bradyrhizobium japonicum) lose survival and infectivity sharply in acid soil. If soybeans are planted in a field of pH 5.0, they appear to grow, but when you dig up the roots there are almost no nodules, and the leaves are pale yellow. The plants are starving because they cannot make their own nitrogen. Because fields in South Korea receive heavy rainfall and therefore tend toward acidity, liming comes before fertilizer in a soybean field.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The vulnerability to waterlogging must also be stressed. Root-nodule bacteria are aerobic bacteria that require oxygen. When a field floods, the oxygen in the soil disappears, and the nodules lose function before the roots do. A soybean field that has been under water for two days still shows yellowing leaves after the water drains, not only because the roots are damaged but because the nitrogen supply has been cut off. When planting beans in a paddy field, digging drainage ditches deep, to 50-80cm, and raising the beds by 20cm or more is not optional but essential.

## 4. Varieties

### 4-1. Classification by Maturity

| Type | Growing days | Sowing time (South Korea) | Harvest time (South Korea) | Characteristics |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Early-maturing (olkong, hadaedu: summer soybeans) | 100-120 days | April-May | July-August | Harvested early. Yield is low |
| Mid-maturing | 120-140 days | Late May-early June | Early to mid October | Well balanced |
| Medium-late (gaeulkong: autumn soybeans) | 140-180 days | Late May-late June | Late October-November | Highest yield. Carries a frost risk |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In a disaster, choosing maturity is a matter of spreading risk. Early-maturing varieties put food on the table in July or August. That is the season when old grain has run out and the autumn harvest is still far off, the stretch commonly called boritgogae (the "barley hump," the lean period when stored grain runs out before the next harvest), and early soybeans play a large part in filling the gap. Even with a low yield, the very fact that they come at that time is valuable. Conversely, medium-late varieties give a large total yield, but in years when frost comes early, grain filling is not completed. If seed is plentiful, it is safer to sow early and medium-late varieties separately and spread out the harvest period.

### 4-2. Classification by Use

| Use | Seed size | Characteristics | Known varieties |
|---|---|---|---|
| For doenjang (Korean soybean paste), soy sauce, and tofu | Large-seeded | High in protein and high-yielding | Daepung 2-ho, Daechan, Seonpung |
| For bean sprouts | Small-seeded | Small seeds sprout well and the sprouts come out straight | Small-seeded landrace lines |
| For mixing into rice | Medium to large | When boiled, the skin is soft and the beans are slightly sweet | Black soybean lines, seoritae (black soybean with green cotyledons) |
| For oil | Medium | High fat content | Oil-specific lines |
| For machine harvesting | Medium to large | The first pod is set at 16cm or higher, and the plant resists lodging and pod shattering | Cheongja 5-ho, Taecheong |
| For disaster preparedness | Any | Landraces from which you can save your own seed come first | Local landraces |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;For disaster preparedness, the first criterion in choosing a variety is whether you can save your own seed. Seed of first-generation hybrid (F1) varieties scatters in traits when planted the next year, and yield collapses. Fortunately, most cultivated soybean varieties are fixed lines and self-pollinating, so the seed you harvest can be planted as it is; but if the seed packet says hybrid, do not expect anything the next year. A landrace that has been grown in a region for a long time yields less than an improved variety, but it is already adapted to that region's climate and diseases, and you can keep saving its seed and carry it on. Rather than putting everything into one improved variety, securing landraces alongside is advantageous in the long run.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The second criterion is the balance of seed sizes. If you secure only a large-seeded variety, you can make soy sauce, doenjang, and tofu, but bean sprouts do not turn out well. Large seeds use a lot of strength when germinating, so the sprouts come out bent and mushy. Setting aside one small-seeded variety exclusively for sprouts is the key means of solving the winter vegetable problem, and this is covered in detail in section 16.

### 4-3. Selection Criteria by Situation

| Situation | Priority | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Need to make doenjang, soy sauce, and tofu | Large-seeded, medium-late varieties | High in protein and high-yielding |
| No winter vegetables | Always include some small-seeded varieties | For bean sprouts. They become a source of vitamin C |
| Region with an early first frost | Early- or mid-maturing varieties | If grain filling is not completed, the whole crop ends up as empty, shriveled seeds |
| Poor field | Landraces | Improved varieties have been bred to respond to fertilizer |
| Labor is short | Lodging-resistant varieties | Lodged beans multiply the harvest labor several times over |
| Seed is short | Small-seeded varieties | The same weight of seed can plant a much larger area |

## 5. Field Preparation and Fertilizer - Root-Nodule Bacteria

### 5-1. How Nitrogen Fixation Happens

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;78% of the air is nitrogen. An unlimited supply of nitrogen floats above people's heads, yet plants cannot use it. Nitrogen in the air is a molecule (N2) in which two nitrogen atoms are tightly bound by a triple bond, and breaking this bond takes an enormous amount of energy. The industrial process that breaks this bond to make ammonia is the Haber-Bosch process, and the world's nitrogen fertilizer comes from it. It is a process that requires high temperature, high pressure, a catalyst, and huge amounts of fossil fuel.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Root-nodule bacteria do this job at ordinary temperature and pressure. With an enzyme called nitrogenase, they break the triple bond and convert nitrogen into ammonia. But there is a price. This reaction requires a great deal of energy, and the bacteria cannot photosynthesize themselves. So they live inside the roots of legumes, receive the sugars the plant makes by photosynthesis, and in return pass the fixed nitrogen to the plant. It is a trade relationship.

| Stage | What happens |
|---|---|
| 1. Signal exchange | The soybean root secretes flavonoids, and bacteria in the soil respond to the signal by releasing Nod factors |
| 2. Infection | Root hairs curl inward and enclose the bacteria, and the bacteria travel along an infection thread into the root |
| 3. Nodule formation | Cells inside the root divide, the nodule swells, and the bacteria settle inside it |
| 4. Nitrogen fixation | Inside the nodule, nitrogenase goes to work and converts nitrogen from the air into ammonia |
| 5. Distribution | Ammonia is converted into amino acids and carried to the stems, leaves, and pods |
| 6. Senescence | At the grain-filling stage, the nodules lose function and return to the soil together with the roots |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Nitrogenase is extremely sensitive to oxygen. It loses function immediately on contact with oxygen. So the inside of the nodule is filled with a red pigment protein called leghemoglobin, which holds on to oxygen and keeps the oxygen concentration around the enzyme extremely low. It works on the same principle that makes human blood red, and the red or pink color inside a nodule when you split it is this pigment. This fact is enormously important in practice, because the red color is visible proof that the nodule is fixing nitrogen right now.

### 5-2. How to Check Whether Nodules Have Formed

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Whether or not you inoculated, you must check whether nodules have actually formed. Without checking, you cannot tell why nitrogen is short, and you will repeat the same mistake the next year.

| Step | Procedure |
|---|---|
| 1 | Do it around the time flowering begins; about 6-8 weeks after sowing is the best time |
| 2 | Choose two or three healthy-looking plants |
| 3 | Do not pull them up; dig a wide area around each plant with a spade and lift it with the soil. If you pull, the nodules on the fine roots stay behind in the soil |
| 4 | Put it in a bucket and gently wash off the soil |
| 5 | Count the nodules on the taproot and the thick lateral roots |
| 6 | Split a few nodules with a fingernail and look at the color inside |

| Observation | Meaning | Action |
|---|---|---|
| 20 or more nodules per plant, concentrated especially on the upper part of the taproot | Inoculation worked well | Leave as is |
| The inside of the split nodule is red or pink | It is fixing nitrogen | Good condition |
| The inside of the split nodule is white or pale green | Nodules formed but are not fixing | Check acidity, waterlogging damage, and phosphorus deficiency |
| The inside of the split nodule is brown or black | Already senescent or dead | Normal at grain filling; a problem at flowering |
| 5 or fewer nodules | Inoculation failed | If the leaves are yellow, supplement nitrogen |
| Many tiny nodules only at the tips of the fine roots | Infected by native bacteria, but with low efficiency | Change the inoculant source next year |

> **This one habit of digging up the roots, splitting a nodule, and checking for the red color is the surest signal of success or failure in growing soybeans in a world without nitrogen fertilizer.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The position of the nodules also gives information. Large nodules on the upper part of the taproot, within 10cm of the soil surface, are the most efficient ones. This is where they form when inoculation has gone well. Conversely, if there are none on the taproot and only small nodules scattered at the tips of the fine roots, the cause is often a late infection by native bacteria that were already in the soil. Native bacteria are not strains that have lived in long symbiosis with soybean, so their fixation efficiency is lower.

### 5-3. Where to Get the Inoculant

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;A commercial inoculant is a product that sells live bacteria packed in peat or clay. In a disaster, you cannot get it. But root-nodule bacteria are common soil microbes, and the soil of a field where soybeans were grown is itself the inoculant. This fact is enormously important for disaster preparedness.

| Method | Procedure | Effect | Caution |
|---|---|---|---|
| Coating seed with soil from a field that grew soybeans | From a field where soybeans were planted last year and nodules formed well, bring topsoil from a depth of 5-15cm. Lightly moisten the seed with water, coat it evenly with this soil, and plant right away | The most reliable method, and it costs nothing | Soil from a diseased field also carries pathogens |
| Spreading the soil on the field | Scatter the soil brought in along the rows to be sown and work it in together | Needs more soil than coating the seed, but the bacteria spread through the soil | The standard is about one spadeful of soil per 10m² |
| Using the nodules themselves | Collect nodules from pulled soybean roots, crush them, dissolve them in water, and soak the seed in that water, then lift it out | The highest bacterial density | Must be used on the same day the nodules are dug up. If they dry, the bacteria die |
| Plowing in soybean root residue | Do not dig up the roots of harvested soybeans; leave them in the field as they are | The bacteria remain in the soil until the following year | The least labor-intensive way to maintain them |
| Roots of wild soybean | Collect nodules from the roots of wild soybean (Glycine soja) growing in fields and on field banks | Same genus as soybean, so the bacteria are compatible | Fixation efficiency may be lower than that of strains for cultivated soybean |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There is something you must observe when inoculating. Root-nodule bacteria are very sensitive to direct sunlight, drying, and heat. A few minutes of ultraviolet light kills a good many of them, and drying kills them even faster. So coat the seed with soil in the shade, and plant the coated seed straight away without leaving it out in the sun. Planting within 30 minutes is best, and in any case finish within a few hours. If coated seed is left for more than a day before planting, the inoculation effect nearly disappears.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;One more thing: seed disinfection and inoculation cannot be done together. If soil is applied to seed treated with a fungicide, the bacteria die. If the seed was disinfected by hot-water treatment, inoculate only after it has cooled completely. For seed whose diseases are a concern, it is sensible to choose disinfection and do the inoculation separately by spreading the soil directly on the field.

| Conditions that cause inoculation to fail | Reason |
|---|---|
| Soil pH of 5.5 or lower | The bacteria cannot live |
| A field given plenty of nitrogen fertilizer | The plant feels no need for the trade and cuts back nodule formation itself |
| A field where water collects | They are aerobic bacteria and die without oxygen |
| A field short of phosphorus | Nitrogen fixation needs a lot of energy, and phosphorus is used in that energy metabolism |
| Deficiency of molybdenum, iron, or cobalt | These are components of nitrogenase |
| Drying inoculated seed in the sun | Ultraviolet light and drying kill the bacteria |
| Inoculating fungicide-treated seed | The bacteria die |
| A field where legumes have not been grown for a long time | Almost no native bacteria exist, so inoculation is essential |

### 5-4. The Nitrogen-Fixation Trap

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The saying that planting beans improves the soil has hardened into common sense in farming. It is half right and half wrong. If you do not understand exactly where the line falls, a field planted with beans for several years in a row can end up becoming depleted instead.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Let us work it out in numbers. When soybeans grow well, 250kg of seed is harvested from 1,000m². The protein in soybean seed is 38%, and dividing protein by 6.25 gives the amount of nitrogen. 38 ÷ 6.25 is 6.08, so about 6% of the seed weight is pure nitrogen. The nitrogen contained in 250kg is about 15kg. Converted to a hectare, it comes to 150kg.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Meanwhile, the nitrogen fixed by nodules is 100-150kg per hectare when conditions are good. This is the upper limit, reached when inoculation has worked well, the acidity is right, and the water is adequate. When conditions are poor, it stops at 50kg. Putting what goes out next to what comes in gives the following.

| Item | Nitrogen per hectare | Direction |
|---|---|---|
| Fixed by nodules (good conditions) | 100-150kg | In |
| Fixed by nodules (poor conditions) | 30-60kg | In |
| Removed in the seed | 130-160kg | Out |
| Nitrogen remaining in the stems and leaves | 25-40kg | Stays if returned; leaves if burned or hauled away |
| Nitrogen remaining in the roots and nodules | 10-20kg | Mostly stays in the field |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The table makes it clear. If you take away only the seed and return the stems and leaves to the field, fixation and removal are about even, or leave a small surplus. But if you cut the bean stalks too and use them for fuel, burn them, or haul them off as animal feed, the nitrogen balance turns firmly negative. That is, more nitrogen leaves the soybean field than enters it.

> **Soybeans restore the land not because they fix nitrogen, but because they put part of the fixed nitrogen into stems and leaves and leave it in the field. If those stems and leaves are hauled away, the soybean becomes a crop that plunders the land.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Here is one more comparison. About 6% of the weight of soybean seed is nitrogen, while maize seed is about 1.4%. When the same 1kg of seed is carried out of the field, soybean takes away 4.5 times as much nitrogen as maize. The price of using soybeans as a protein factory is the removal of nitrogen, and this is an accounting that cannot physically be avoided. So the principles of soybean growing in a disaster are clear.

| Principle | Details |
|---|---|
| Stems and leaves always go back to the field | Return all the bean stalks, leaves, and pod husks left after threshing to the field |
| Do not burn them | When burned, the nitrogen escapes as gas and only potassium and phosphorus remain in the ash |
| Do not dig up the roots | The nitrogen in roots and nodules is the easiest share to leave behind |
| Do not rake away fallen leaves | The leaves that drop at grain filling also hold a considerable amount of nitrogen |
| Cut the bean stalks and spread them as cover | Cut into 20-30cm lengths and spread over the field, they serve as mulch and fertilizer at once |
| If fed to livestock, return the manure | If bean stalks were fed to animals, always put those animals' manure on that same field |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Dry bean stalks burn well, and the temptation to use them as fuel is strong. The compromise is to always return the ash to that field. When burned, the nitrogen is lost, but potassium, phosphorus, and calcium remain in the ash. However, burning several years in a row visibly worsens the nitrogen balance, so it is better to alternate: burn in some years and spread as cover in others.
