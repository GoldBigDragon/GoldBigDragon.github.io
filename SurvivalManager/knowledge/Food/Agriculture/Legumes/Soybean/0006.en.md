## 14. Toxicity and Antinutritional Compounds

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Soybeans are safe when cooked and dangerous raw. Legume seeds contain chemical defense compounds so that they will not be eaten, and those compounds interfere with human digestion. Fortunately most are heat-labile and are destroyed by sufficient heating, but the problem is that the standard for "sufficient" is higher than you might expect.

### 14-1. What the Antinutritional Compounds Are

| Compound | What it does | Symptoms | How to remove it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Trypsin inhibitor | Blocks the action of trypsin, a protein-digesting enzyme | Abdominal pain, vomiting, diarrhea, lower protein utilization, strain on the pancreas | Soak for 8 hours, then heat at 100°C for 30-60 minutes or more |
| Lectin (hemagglutinin) | Binds to sugar chains on the intestinal lining, hindering absorption, and clumps red blood cells | Severe abdominal pain, vomiting, diarrhea | Soak, then heat at 100°C for 10-30 minutes or more |
| Phytic acid | Binds iron, zinc, calcium, and magnesium and blocks their absorption | Anemia and zinc deficiency with long-term consumption | Reduced by 60-80% through soaking, germination, and fermentation |
| Saponin | Foams and irritates the stomach and intestines | Harmless in small amounts; upsets the stomach in large amounts | Soak, then skim off the foam while boiling |
| Oligosaccharides (raffinose and stachyose) | People cannot digest them, so gut bacteria ferment them | Gas in the abdomen | Soak and discard the soaking water. Greatly reduced by fermentation |
| Goitrogens | Interfere with the use of iodine | Strain on the thyroid in diets short of iodine | Reduced by heating. Eating seaweed alongside offsets them |

> **Undercooked beans can be more dangerous than raw ones. A light blanching breaks down the cell walls so that absorption is good, but the inhibitors and lectins remain. When cooking, do not stop halfway; cook all the way through.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The danger of lectins differs greatly by bean type. Soybean lectin is less toxic than the lectin of kidney beans (Phaseolus). Eating just four or five raw kidney beans brings severe vomiting and diarrhea within a few hours, and food poisoning from undercooked kidney beans has been reported many times. In particular, kidney beans have a range in which heating at a low temperature for a long time actually raises lectin activity, so cooking that keeps them in lukewarm water for a long time is the most dangerous. Soybean is not that extreme, but the principle is the same. Rolling boiling water for a sufficient time is the only safe method.

### 14-2. Standards for Soaking and Heating

| Treatment | Time and temperature | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Soaking | 8 hours in room-temperature water (6 hours in summer, with the water changed) | Heat penetrates to the center. Some of the oligosaccharides and saponins leach into the water |
| Discarding the soaking water | Always | The oligosaccharides and antinutritional compounds that leached into the water are thrown away with it |
| Boiling | 60 minutes or more in rolling boiling water | Trypsin inhibitor and lectin are inactivated |
| Steaming | 60-90 minutes in 100°C steam | Less nutrient loss than boiling |
| Pressure cooking | 120°C for 15-20 minutes | Uses the least fuel |
| Checking doneness | Beans mash easily when pressed between thumb and forefinger | If a hard core remains, they are undercooked |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Dry beans are hard on the outside, so heat does not easily reach the inside. Boiled without soaking, only the outside cooks and the inside stays undercooked, and this state is the most dangerous. It also takes more than twice the fuel. Do not drink or cook with the soaking water, but giving it to the field or using it for washing dishes is not a waste.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In summer, the high temperature spoils the water within 8 hours and the beans turn slimy. Change the water every 3 to 4 hours, or shift the soaking to the night by soaking in the evening and draining in the morning.

### 14-3. Germination and Fermentation

| Method | Compounds reduced | Extent |
|---|---|---|
| Germination (bean sprouts) | Phytic acid, trypsin inhibitor, oligosaccharides | Phytic acid down 50-70%, inhibitor down 30-40% |
| Fermentation (doenjang, a Korean soybean paste; soy sauce; cheonggukjang, a quick-fermented soybean paste) | Phytic acid, trypsin inhibitor, oligosaccharides, lectin | Mostly broken down, and absorption rises |
| Soaking only | Oligosaccharides, some phytic acid | Down 20-30% |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Fermentation is the most fundamental way to solve the antinutrient problem. Microorganisms break proteins such as inhibitors and lectins into amino acids and release enzymes that degrade phytic acid. As a result, the antinutritional compounds disappear, and absorption rises even above that of boiled beans. East Asia developed ways of fermenting soybeans to eat not only because of taste but for this physiological benefit.

## 15. Storage Without Refrigeration

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Soybean's Storability grade is SS. The median storage-stage loss rate in the FAO food loss index is 0.25%, among the lowest figures in this crop table, and dry beans last 3 years at room temperature. But when conditions slip, a single species of bean weevil can turn all the stored beans into empty husks.

> **Beans are lost not to rot but to being eaten by insects. The art of storage is the art of keeping insects out.**

### 15-1. Drying Comes First

| Stage | Method | Check |
|---|---|---|
| Preliminary drying | One to two days in the field or under the eaves, still on the stalks | The pods become bone dry |
| Drying after threshing | Spread thinly on a straw mat (meongseok) or a woven mat and dry in the sun for 2-3 days | Turn often to dry evenly |
| Target moisture | 12-13% for food, 12% for seed | When bitten, the teeth do not sink in and the bean cracks crisply |
| Salt test | Put dry salt in a jar with beans and shake | If the salt sticks to the jar wall, the beans are still damp |
| Cooling | After bringing them in from the sun, let them cool completely before packing | Sealing them while warm causes condensation |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;If drying is insufficient, every later measure collapses. Above 15% moisture, mold grows, and above 13% bean weevils multiply faster. Conversely, if it falls below 10%, the seed coat cracks and the beans break in storage, and for seed the germination ability drops.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Checking by biting is the most practical method. If the teeth sink in even a little when biting with the front teeth, the bean is not dry enough; if it splits into two pieces with a snap, it is fully dry. The salt test is also simple. Put a handful of beans and a spoonful of roasted salt in a dry jar, shake it, and leave it for a few minutes; if the salt clings to the jar wall, dry the beans more.

### 15-2. Controlling Bean Weevils in Storage

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The bean weevil (the Callosobruchus group, which includes the adzuki bean weevil and the mung bean weevil) is the greatest threat to stored legumes. What makes it frightening is that it starts already in the field. Adults lay eggs on pods still hanging in the field, and the eggs hatch inside the storehouse and bore into the beans. Eggs come in with the beans on arrival, one generation takes less than a month, and in half a year a single sack is filled with holed shells and powder.

| Method | Principle | How to apply | Effect |
|---|---|---|---|
| Full airtight sealing | Cuts off oxygen and suffocates the insects | Fill glass jars or metal tins to the brim and seal them | The most reliable. In 3-4 weeks even eggs and larvae die |
| Mixing in wood ash | Fine particles scratch the wax layer of the exoskeleton and dehydrate the insects | Mix in 2-5% of the bean volume | Rinse it off with water before eating |
| Diatomaceous earth | Silica shells damage the exoskeleton | 1-3g per 1kg of beans | More effective than ash. Works only when dry |
| Oil coating | The oil film prevents eggs from attaching and hatching | Add 2-5mL of cooking oil per 1kg of beans and rub in | Very effective on legumes. Not used for seed |
| Cold treatment | Eggs and larvae die below freezing | Put the beans outside for a few days in midwinter, then bring them in | Watch for condensation when bringing them back in |
| Heat treatment | Eggs and larvae die at 60°C | Spread thinly in the sun and leave for half a day | Not used for seed |
| Dried chili peppers, sancho (Korean prickly ash), mugwort | The essential oils suppress egg-laying | Layer them in | A supplementary measure |
| Charcoal, roasted salt | Absorb moisture | Wrap in cloth and put in | Also prevents mold |
| Burying in ash | Gives airtight sealing and physical control together | Layer ash and beans in an earthenware jar (onggi) | Widely used traditionally |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The most important is full airtight sealing. Insects breathe too. When the beans and insects use oxygen inside a sealed container, the oxygen falls and carbon dioxide rises, and the insects die. Beans are dormant and tolerate these conditions. The more fully you fill the container and the less empty space it holds, the faster the effect. Packing tightly into several small containers is better than one large one, which also has the advantage that if one container spoils, you do not lose everything.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Oil coating suits legume storage especially well. Add 2-3ml of cooking oil per 1kg of beans, about half a small spoonful, and rub by hand so that a thin film forms on every seed. The oil film makes it hard for adults to attach eggs, and blocks the breathing pores of eggs already attached, hindering hatching. It is a method long used by small farms in India and Africa. However, when the oil goes rancid it smells, so use it only on the portion that will be eaten within half a year, and do not use it on seed. The oil film interferes with respiration and lowers germination ability.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;For wood ash, choose only fine ash. Sift out coarse pieces of charcoal and mix only the fine portion into 2-5% of the bean volume so that every seed gets a thin coating. Rinsing three or four times in water when eating makes the beans clean. Insects cannot develop resistance to it, and it is harmless to people.

### 15-3. Storage Containers and Places

| Container | Airtightness | Duration | Advantages | Drawbacks |
|---|:---:|---|---|---|
| Glass jar | Very good | 3 years or more | The condition of the beans can be checked by eye | Small capacity, and it does not block light |
| Metal tin | Very good | 3 years or more | Completely blocks rodents and insects | Spoils at any spot where condensation forms |
| Earthenware jar | Good | 2-3 years | Temperature changes slowly, and rodents cannot gnaw through it | Heavy and breakable |
| Thick plastic bag | Fair | 1-2 years | Light and cheap | Rodents chew through it |
| Underground storage pit | Good | 2-3 years | Temperature is constant and low | If water seeps in, everything is lost |

| Storage place conditions | Target |
|---|---|
| Temperature and humidity | 15°C or lower, air humidity 65% or lower |
| Light | Completely blocked |
| Location | A basement or north-facing room, on a stand so it does not touch the ground |
| Inspection | Once every two months |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The most reliable senses in inspection are hearing and smell. If you put your ear to the container and hear a rustling sound, insects are active, and if there is a damp smell, mold has started. If you see small round holes on the bean surface, the bean weevils have gone through a generation. Even then, if you take out all the beans, dry them in the sun, float them in water to skim off the empty seeds, mix in ash or oil, and seal again, you can protect what remains.

## 16. Cooking and Processing

### 16-1. Bean Sprouts - Vitamin C for Winter

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Dry beans contain practically no vitamin C. But when they sprout, vitamin C that was not there is produced. This is because the plant newly synthesizes it as the physiological shift of germination takes place. In winter, when there are no fresh vegetables at all, a handful of dry beans, water, and a dark place yield fresh vegetables in three or four days. No field, sun, or soil is needed.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Vitamin C deficiency progresses quietly over months, then shows up as bleeding gums, wounds that do not heal, and loss of energy. It is the disease that sailors suffered in the Age of Sail, and it appeared again and again in societies that got through long winters on stored food alone. Bean sprouts are a cheap and sure answer to this problem.

| Step | Procedure | Key point |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Choose the beans | Small-seeded varieties are best. Discard cracked or insect-eaten seeds | Cracked seeds rot and spoil the whole batch |
| 2. Wash | Rinse several times and skim off those that float | Floating seeds are usually dead |
| 3. Soak | Soak in lukewarm water for 6-8 hours | The beans should swell to twice their size |
| 4. Fill the vessel | Put them in a vessel that drains, or a siru (perforated earthenware sprouting pot), and cover with a damp cloth | If water collects, they rot |
| 5. Keep dark | Block light completely. 20-25°C is good | If light gets in, they turn green and tough |
| 6. Water | Pour water generously from above 3-4 times a day | Serves both temperature control and removal of waste |
| 7. Harvest | 4-7 days, length 5-10cm | Harvest before the roots turn brown |

| Condition | Result |
|---|---|
| Light gets in | The heads of the sprouts turn green and the stems become tough |
| Too little water | The roots put out many fine rootlets and become tough |
| Water collects | They rot from the bottom and smell |
| Temperature goes above 30°C | They grow fast but turn soft and spoil easily |
| Temperature below 15°C | They grow very slowly |
| A weight is placed on top | The stems become thick and plump |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Watering is almost everything in growing bean sprouts. Water supplies moisture, cools the heat produced during germination, and washes away accumulated waste. Pour generously from above three to four times a day and let it drain completely; increase the frequency in summer and use lukewarm water in winter. Many fine rootlets are a sign that water is short.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;If you have no siru, use a jar or tub with holes made in the bottom, a fine-meshed sieve basket, or a washbasin with holes punched in it. Put a water catcher below and cover the top with black cloth, and that is your siru. All that matters is two things: a structure that lets the water drain, and blocking of light.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;100g of dry beans become 400g to 500g of bean sprouts. Most of the added weight is water, so calories do not increase, but vitamin C is newly created, phytic acid and trypsin inhibitor decrease, and part of the protein is broken down into a more digestible form. Bean sprouts too are not eaten raw but blanched or stir-fried, because of the inhibitor that remains.

### 16-2. Making Jang (Fermented Soybean Sauces and Pastes) - Storage That Pays the Fuel Up Front

| Processing | Procedure summary | Storage | Value |
|---|---|---|---|
| Meju (fermented soybean blocks) | Boil the beans, mash them, shape into blocks, wrap in straw, and ferment for a month or more | Dried, all winter long | The raw material for doenjang and soy sauce |
| Doenjang | Break up the fermented meju, mix with brine, and age for several months | Several years | Protein storage and a seasoning at once |
| Soy sauce | Soak meju in strong brine, leave for several months, and boil down the extracted liquid | Several years | A source of amino acids |
| Cheonggukjang | Wrap boiled beans in straw and keep at around 40°C for 2-3 days | A few days; several months if dried | The fastest fermented beans |
| Tofu | Grind soaked beans, boil, strain, and set with bittern (gansu) | A few days | The easiest to digest |
| Soybean oil | Roast, pound, and press | Several months | The extraction rate is low |
| Roasted soybean flour | Roast and pound | Airtight, 2-3 months | No cooking fuel needed |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In a disaster, making jang is valuable in three layers. First, the antinutritional compounds are completely broken down and absorption rises. Second, once it is made, almost no cooking fuel is needed afterward. It is as if you pay the fuel for the hour of boiling beans only once for a whole year's worth. Third, being stored with salt, it keeps for several years.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Cheonggukjang is the fastest fermented bean to obtain. If you wrap boiled beans in straw and leave them in a warm place for two or three days, the bacteria on the rice straw multiply and produce sticky threads. You do not have to wait a month as with meju, and no salt is needed. If the smell is sour and savory, it has succeeded; if it smells rotten or shows black mold, throw it out.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Tofu is the easiest form to digest. Fiber and oligosaccharides are filtered out, so it does not cause gas, and it is good for the elderly, children, and the sick. Do not throw away the leftover pulp (kongbiji, or okara) either; eat it in a jjigae (stew) or dry it and keep it for later. The bittern needed for setting is the bitter liquid that drains out when sun-dried sea salt is piled up; if you have none, you can boil down seawater or use a little vinegar.

### 16-3. Cooking That Saves Fuel

| Method | Fuel | Procedure |
|---|:---:|---|
| Pressure cooking | Lowest | Pressurize soaked beans for 15-20 minutes |
| Residual-heat cooking | Very low | Boil for 10-15 minutes, remove from the heat, wrap in a box of straw or rice hulls or in a quilt, and leave for 2-3 hours |
| Roasted soybean flour | Almost none | Roast and pound in advance, then stir into water to eat |
| Roasted beans | Low | Roast dry beans over low heat. In 20-30 minutes the inhibitor drops considerably |
| Fermentation (jang) | Paid up front | Once made, no fuel is needed afterward |
| Bean sprouts | None | Germination needs no heat |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Residual-heat cooking is especially effective with soybean. Boil for only 10-15 minutes and put the pot in an insulated box, and the internal temperature stays above 80°C for over two hours while cooking continues. However, 80 degrees may not be enough to destroy lectin and trypsin inhibitor completely, so secure an initial boiling time of 15 minutes or more, and check on removal that the beans mash easily with a finger. If a core remains, boil again.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Roasted beans are the answer when you are not in a position to cook. Put dry beans in a heated pot and roast over low heat for 20-30 minutes, stirring, until a nutty aroma comes; this dry-heat treatment reduces trypsin inhibitor considerably, so the beans can be eaten as they are. However, it is not as complete as boiling, so do not eat them as a staple for long. Roasted and pounded soybean flour can be eaten immediately when stirred into water, and mixed with cereal flour it completes the amino acid composition.

## 17. Annual Work Calendar

| Month | Work | Key points |
|---|---|---|
| January | Inspect stored beans, run germination tests on seed, maintain tools | Put an ear to the container to listen for insects |
| February | Plan the field, turn the compost, prepare for making jang | Check the rotation order and the germination rate of old seed |
| March | Spread lime, finish the compost, ferment meju | Apply lime 3-4 weeks before sowing |
| April | Plow the field, make raised beds, dig drainage ditches, apply base fertilizer | Dig the drainage ditches deep with the monsoon season (jangma, late June to July in South Korea) in mind |
| May | Sort seed, hot-water disinfection, sow early-maturing varieties, secure soil for inoculant | Dig up soil from a field that grew soybeans ahead of time |
| June | Main sowing (early June in central South Korea, mid to late June in southern South Korea), bird protection, first weeding | Sow in batches 2 weeks apart to spread out the flowering periods |
| July | Thinning, weeding, hilling, pinching, check the nodules | Pinch before flowering, and dig up roots and split a nodule to look |
| August | Flowering and pod filling, concentrated irrigation, catching stink bugs | Water makes the yield at this stage. Catch stink bugs in the early morning |
| September | Grain-filling management, stop irrigation, mark seed plants | Tie a string on good plants |
| October | Harvest, preliminary drying, hanging to dry, threshing | If rain is forecast, do not wait for full maturity |
| November | Cleaning, drying, storage, returning stems and leaves, cooking beans for meju | Chop the bean stalks and spread them over the field. Do not burn them |
| December | Making jang, storage inspection, growing bean sprouts | Bean sprouts give vitamin C for winter |

### 17-1. Five Things Not to Forget

| Item | Details |
|---|---|
| Nitrogen | Giving a lot will always reduce the yield. Do not exceed 3kg/1,000m² |
| Nodules | At flowering, dig up the roots, split a nodule, and check for the red color. It is the instrument panel of soybean growing |
| Stems and leaves | If the bean stalks, leaves, and roots are not returned to the field, soybean becomes a crop that plunders the land |
| Water | The 4 weeks from flowering to pod filling decide 80% of the yield |
| Heating | Soak for 8 hours and boil for an hour or more. Undercooked beans can be more dangerous |
