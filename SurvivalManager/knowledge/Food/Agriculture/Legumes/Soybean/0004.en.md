## 5. Field Preparation and Fertilizer - Fertilizer Application

### 5-5. Field Preparation

| Item | Method | Under disaster conditions |
|---|---|---|
| Plowing | Plow 20-30cm deep and level evenly | Turn the soil with a spade or dig it up with a hoe. If plowing the whole surface is hard, dig and turn only the rows to be planted |
| Raised bed | Height of 20cm or more | The key to preventing waterlogging damage. 30cm or more when planting in a paddy field |
| Drainage ditch | Depth of 50-80cm | In paddy-field cultivation, drainage ditches come before raised beds |
| Acidity correction | pH 6.0-6.5 | Do it ahead of time, 3-4 weeks before sowing |
| Lime | For acid soil, slaked lime or dolomitic lime at 200kg/1,000m² | In a disaster, substitute shells or eggshells that have been burned and ground, or wood ash |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The reason lime must be applied in advance is that the reaction takes time. If it is applied right before sowing, the alkalinity becomes strong locally and damages the seeds and young roots, and at the very time it is needed the reaction has not progressed far enough. Also, if lime and nitrogen manure are put in the same place together, ammonia escapes as gas and nitrogen is lost. Keep to the order: lime first, manure two weeks later. If you have no lime, use clamshells and eggshells. Both are calcium carbonate, so the principle is the same, but as they are the reaction is very slow, so burn them in a fire to make them brittle, then pound them in a mortar into powder before use. Wood ash is also alkaline, but its main component is potassium, so its effect in supplying calcium is weaker than that of lime.

### 5-6. Fertilizer Rates

| Fertilizer | Per 1,000m² | Converted to m² | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Nitrogen | 3kg | 3g | Apply only the minimum. For the early stage, before the nodules start working |
| Phosphorus | 3.4kg | 3.4g | All as base fertilizer. Needed for the energy metabolism of nitrogen fixation |
| Potassium | 3.4kg | 3.4g | All as base fertilizer. Involved in seed enlargement |
| Compost | 1,000kg | 1kg | Use fully rotted compost |
| Lime | 200kg (acid soil) | 200g | 3-4 weeks before sowing |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;3kg of nitrogen is a surprisingly small amount compared with other crops. Compared with the 20kg or more put on maize, it is about one-seventh. This is the reason soybean received an SS for Fertilizer demand, and it is also an upper limit that must be respected. Giving more nitrogen causes two things. First, nodule formation decreases. When the soil has plenty of nitrogen, the plant stops the trade of giving up sugars in return for nitrogen. Second, only the stems and leaves grow lush, and fewer pods set. This is called leggy growth, and leggy beans fall over easily, ventilation worsens, and diseases increase.

> **In soybean, holding back nitrogen is not thrift but technique. Giving a lot will always reduce the yield.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Top-dressing is in principle unnecessary. However, if about ten days after flowering the leaves turn pale yellow overall and growth is clearly poor, it is a sign that the nodules are not working properly. Only then, supplement a small amount of nitrogen. In conventional farming 4-6kg of urea is used, and in a disaster, diluted urine is the easiest alternative.

### 5-7. Substitute Fertilizers for Disasters

| Substitute resource | Nutrients supplied | How to use | Caution |
|---|---|---|---|
| Wood ash | Potassium, phosphorus, calcium | Scatter 100-200g per m² and mix in | It is alkaline, so too much pushes the pH above 7 |
| Well-rotted compost | All-round | Apply 1kg per m² as base fertilizer | Half-rotted compost burns roots and spreads disease |
| Diluted urine | Nitrogen, potassium | Dilute 10 times with water and pour it on the soil between plants | Keep it off the leaves. Only a small amount for soybean |
| Bone meal | Phosphorus, calcium | Burn and pound it, then mix it into compost | The effect appears slowly |
| Fish bones and fish guts | Phosphorus, nitrogen | Bury them and let them decompose before use | If buried as they are, animals dig them up |
| Shells and eggshells | Calcium | Burn, pound, and scatter | A substitute for lime. The reaction is slow |
| Leaf mold from fallen leaves | All-round, organic matter | Mix into the soil | Improves water retention |
| Rice hulls and rice straw | Potassium, silica | Spread as mulch and plow in the following year | Plowing in poorly decomposed material robs the soil of nitrogen |
| Legume green manure | Nitrogen | Grow cowpea or hairy vetch and plow it in | Avoided as a preceding crop for a soybean field because of disease buildup |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;What actually matters in fertilizing soybean is not nitrogen but phosphorus and potassium. Phosphorus is a key element in the energy metabolism of nitrogen fixation, so in a field short of phosphorus, the amount fixed falls even when nodules form. Potassium is involved in seed enlargement and stem strength, and when it is lacking, leaf margins dry out brown and lodging increases. Without chemical fertilizer, the most realistic sources are bone meal and fish bones for phosphorus, and wood ash for potassium.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There is something to watch when using wood ash. Scattering ash on a soybean field raises the pH, which creates favorable conditions for the root-nodule bacteria, but if overdone, the pH goes above 7 and the uptake of iron, zinc, and manganese is blocked. If yellowing between the leaf veins appears, it is a signal that the soil has tilted toward alkaline, so stop the ash. 100-200g per m² is the upper limit. Boron is also worth remembering. Soybean has a boron requirement, and when it is lacking, flowers drop and pods do not set. When chemical borax is unavailable, compost made from vegetable scraps, fruit peels, and fallen leaves together is the most realistic alternative. Compost made from a single material is skewed in trace elements.

## 6. Sowing

### 6-1. Sowing Time

| Region and cropping system | Best sowing time | Latest sowing time | Harvest time |
|---|---|---|---|
| Central South Korea, single crop per year | Early May-early June | Late June | Early to mid October |
| Central South Korea, double cropping (in paddy fields) | Mid to late June | Early July | Late October |
| Southern South Korea, single crop per year | Early to mid June | Mid July | Mid to late October |
| Southern South Korea, double cropping | Late June-early July | Late July | Late October-November |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The reason soybean sowing time is divided so finely is photoperiod response. Soybean is a short-day plant that flowers on the signal of shortening days, so after the summer solstice, when days begin to shorten, it prepares to flower. If sown too early, the vegetative growth period before flowering becomes long, and the plants only grow tall and fall over; if sown too late, the flowering signal arrives before they have grown enough, so they flower in a small body and the yield is low.

> **Soybeans sown after mid to late July (in South Korea) yield much less. The days available for sowing are fewer than you think, and if you miss that window you lose only the seed.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;If you sow in two or three batches at 2-week intervals within the best sowing period, the flowering periods are spread out, and a heat wave or drought cannot take everything at once. There is also the advantage of spreading out labor. Soybean is a crop that takes much work at sowing and harvest, so if everything is sown at once it must all be harvested at once, which becomes hard to manage where people are short.

### 6-2. Seed Preparation

| Item | Method | Under disaster conditions |
|---|---|---|
| Sorting | Discard seeds with spots, discoloration, wrinkles, or insect holes | Pick them out by hand, one seed at a time. The cheapest and most effective task |
| Size selection | Choose and use plump, heavy seeds | Float them in water and skim off those that float |
| Hot-water disinfection | Soak in 50-55°C water for 10-15 minutes to kill pathogens on the surface | If the temperature is exceeded, germination ability drops. Check with a hand and keep the water at a hot level |
| Ash treatment | Coat the seed with fine wood ash | Helps disinfection and drying, and repels birds and insects |
| Nodule inoculation | Coat the seed with soil from a field that grew soybeans | See section 5-3. Do not combine with disinfection |
| Germination test | Wrap 20 seeds in a damp cloth, leave for a few days, and count how many sprout | Always check old seed first |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Sorting is the most important. A good many of soybean's major diseases are seed-borne. Seeds infected with purple seed stain have purple blotches on the surface, and seeds infected with anthracnose have black spots. A single such seed can spread disease through an entire field the next year. Where there are no agrochemicals, seed sorting is the most powerful means of disease control, and all it requires is bright light, eyes, and time.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There is a knack to managing the temperature of hot-water treatment. If you have no thermometer, the range of 50-55°C is the heat at which it is hard to keep a hand in for 5 seconds. Use at least ten times as much water as seed, so the temperature does not drop suddenly when the seed goes in. When done, cool the seed immediately in cold water, dry off the moisture, and plant. Above 60°C germination ability is damaged, so leaving the seed a long time in hot water is the most dangerous mistake.

### 6-3. Sowing Method

| Item | Standard | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Sowing depth | 3-5cm | Within 2cm it dries out easily and birds peck it. Beyond 7cm it cannot come up |
| Seeds per hole | 2-3 seeds | Allows for the germination rate. Later, thin to leave 2 plants |
| Row spacing | 60-80cm | Secures ventilation and a path for management |
| Plant spacing | 15-20cm (single crop), 10-15cm (double crop) | The later the sowing, the denser the planting |
| Seeding rate (small-seeded) | 3-4kg/1,000m² | |
| Seeding rate (medium-seeded) | 5-6kg/1,000m² | |
| Seeding rate (large-seeded) | 6-7kg/1,000m² | |

| Sowing time (South Korea) | Planting distance |
|---|---|
| Early June | 70-80cm × 20cm |
| Mid June | 70-80cm × 15-20cm |
| Late June | 60-70cm × 15cm |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The reason for planting more densely the later you sow is that the individual plants grow smaller. Beans sown in late June have small bodies, so at the same spacing there would be unused ground. The number of plants is increased by as much as the size of each plant is reduced, to maintain the yield per area.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The biggest threat right after sowing is birds. Pigeons, magpies, and pheasants peck at the cotyledons of newly sprouted beans, and since soybean stores its nutrients in the cotyledons, a plant that loses them usually dies. Cover the sowing row thinly with twigs or straw, cover it with netting or an old window screen until germination, or string glittering cords crosswise. The most reliable measure is to walk around the field morning and evening for the first ten days after germination.

## 7. Water Management

| Stage | Water management | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Sowing to germination | Do not let the soil dry out completely | Soybean seed must absorb water equal to its own weight to germinate |
| Early growth | Leave it to natural rainfall | Dryness at this stage actually makes the roots reach deep |
| Flowering to pod filling | Water generously so the soil does not dry out | 80% of the water used over the whole growing period is used at this stage |
| Grain filling | Reduce the water | If it is too wet, the pods do not dry |
| Maturity | Stop irrigation | Irrigating at this stage causes pod shattering and early leaf drop |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;It is important to tell the symptoms of water shortage from those of excess. Both make the leaves wilt, but the causes are opposite, so the remedies are opposite too.

| Symptom | Cause | Action |
|---|---|---|
| Leaves droop at midday and recover in the evening | Temporary water shortage. Within the normal range | Leave as is |
| Leaves are still drooping in the morning | The roots cannot lift water | Water generously |
| Leaves curl inward and darken | Severe dryness | Irrigate immediately |
| Flowers drop in large numbers | Dryness or high temperature during flowering | Irrigate and lower the soil temperature with mulch |
| Pods have set but the seeds are small | Water shortage at pod filling | Irrigation at this stage directly decides the yield |
| Lower leaves turn yellow and roots are black | Root damage from excess moisture | Clear the drainage ditches and raise the soil by hilling |
| Whole leaves are pale yellow but do not wilt | Nodules are not working, so nitrogen is short | Dig up the roots and check the nodules |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Where water is scarce, the irrigation strategy is selection and concentration. If you try to supply it evenly through the whole growing period, it is never enough at any stage. It is better to hold back at first and concentrate water in the 4 weeks from the start of flowering until the pods fill out, which yields far more beans from the same water. Rather than watering often in small amounts, giving enough at once to wet the soil to a depth of 20cm and spacing out the intervals to the next irrigation is advantageous because it makes the roots deep.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Mulching is the cheapest way to save water. Even without plastic film, covering 5-10cm with straw, rice hulls, fallen leaves, cut weeds, or sawdust cuts surface evaporation to half or less and moderates swings in soil temperature. Because soybean is vulnerable to high temperature during flowering, it also gains the benefit of a cooler soil. However, in the monsoon season (jangma, late June to July in South Korea), it is better for disease prevention to pull back some of the mulch to secure ventilation.

## 8. Crop Management

### 8-1. Thinning and Weeding

| Task | Timing | Method |
|---|---|---|
| Thinning | At 1-2 true leaves | Leave 2 plants per hole and pull out the weak ones |
| First weeding | 2-3 weeks after sowing | Scrape shallowly. Take care not to damage the roots |
| Second weeding | 5-6 weeks after sowing | Combine with hilling |
| Third weeding | Before flowering | After this, do not enter the field |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Weed losses in soybean are larger than you might think. In Oerke's data, 37% of the 60% potential loss without pest control was due to weeds. This means weeds are a bigger threat than pests and diseases. Once the soybean leaves touch each other and cover the ground, weeds do not increase much after that, so it is enough to weed diligently only in the first month.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;After flowering, the principle is not to enter the field. People passing through knock flowers off, and walking through in the morning before the dew has dried carries disease across the whole field. It is better to clear the weeds before flowering and cover the ground with mulch.

### 8-2. Hilling

| Timing | Method | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| 30-40 days after sowing, up to the node of the first true leaf | Scrape soil together and pile it around the base of each plant | Prevents lodging, promotes root development, improves drainage, buries weeds |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Hilling is the single most efficient task in soybean growing. Four things happen at once. The base of the stem is buried in soil and gains support, so lodging decreases; new roots emerge from the buried stem and the absorbing area increases; the furrows between plants deepen so water drains well; and in the process young weeds are buried. Burying up to the node of the first true leaf is about right; burying higher than that lets the lower pods touch the soil and rot.

### 8-3. Pinching

| Timing | Method | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| At 5-7 true leaves, always before flowering | Pinch off the growing point at the tip of the stem by hand | Side branches increase and the number of pods rises |

> **Pinching after flowers have opened actually reduces yield. After flowering, the growing point is no longer a place that adds height but the place where new pods set.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Pinching is not good in every situation. It is very effective under conditions that invite leggy growth, such as plentiful fertilizer and heavy rain, but pinching beans that are already growing poorly in a poor field leaves them without the strength to recover, and the yield falls further. Look over the field: if the plants have grown past knee height, the leaf color is dark, and the nodes are spaced far apart, pinch; if they have stopped at shin height and the leaf color is pale, leave them alone.

### 8-4. Lodging Management

| Cause | Response |
|---|---|
| Excess nitrogen | Keep to the fertilizer rate. This is the root of the matter |
| Dense planting | Keep to the planting distance |
| Hilling not done | Always do it at 30-40 days |
| Typhoon or heavy rain | Tie lines around the field to support the plants |
| Tall variety | Choose a lodging-resistant variety |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Lodged beans multiply harvest labor several times over, pods touching the ground rot, and blocked ventilation spreads disease. If they have already lodged, it is better to leave them as they are than to try forcibly to stand them up. In the process of lifting them, roots are cut and pods fall. However, for parts lying completely flat on the ground, propping them slightly up with twigs can reduce rotting. Driving stakes around the field and tying a line around it at waist height is very effective if done in advance, before a typhoon is forecast.

## 9. Pests and Diseases

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The Pest & disease pressure grade of soybean is A. The estimated yield loss without pest control is low at 23%, and a catastrophe in which a field disappears entirely, as with potato late blight, is rare. Many soybean diseases are seed-borne, which also means there is much room to respond without chemicals.

### 9-1. Major Diseases

| Disease | Symptoms | Conditions for occurrence | Response without chemicals |
|---|---|---|---|
| Soybean mosaic | Leaves are mottled in a mosaic pattern and crinkled. Brown spots form on the seeds | Spread by aphids | No cure. Pull up and burn affected plants as soon as they are found, and reduce aphids. Do not use spotted seed for planting |
| Bacterial pustule (bacterial spot) | Angular brown spots form on the leaves, ringed by a yellow border | Bacterial. Spread by wind and rain | Spray Bordeaux mixture. Do not enter the field right after rain |
| Anthracnose | Black cankers form on stems and pods | Fungal. High humidity | Seed sorting and crop rotation are the key. Bordeaux mixture helps as a supplement |
| Purple seed stain | The seed surface is mottled purple | Fungal. Seed-borne | Thoroughly pick out stained seeds. Hot-water disinfection is effective |
| Downy mildew | Yellow spots on the upper leaf surface, grayish-white mold on the underside | High humidity and dense planting | Keep the planting distance, drain well, use Bordeaux mixture |
| Sclerotinia stem rot (white mold) | Stems turn soft, and white cottony mycelium and black sclerotia form | Cool, wet conditions | Rotate crops for 4 years or more. Dig up diseased plants with the soil and burn them |
| Brown spot | Brown spots start on the lower leaves, followed by early leaf drop | Late monsoon season | Secure ventilation, remove crop residue |
| Root rot | Lower leaves turn yellow and roots rot brown | Excess moisture, continuous cropping | Drain, raise the beds, rotate crops |
| Soybean root-lesion nematode | Growth is poor and gall-like deformations form on the roots | Sandy soil, continuous cropping | Intercropping with marigold, crop rotation |
