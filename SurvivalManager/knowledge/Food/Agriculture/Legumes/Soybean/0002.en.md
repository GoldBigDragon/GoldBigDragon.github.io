## 1. Disaster-Preparedness Evaluation

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Soybean's Overall grade is A, with a score of 49.9 points. Broken down by scenario, its Short-term ability to get through the early phase of a disaster is B (47.3), its Long-term ability for self-reliance over many years is S (52.8), and its Supplementary value as a side-dish resource beyond the staple is SS (65.1). A Supplementary score of 65.1 puts it in the top tier of this crop table. The fact that Short-term is 5.5 points lower than Long-term sums up soybean's character. You have to plant it and wait four months, and it does not fill the stomach, but it is a crop whose relative standing rises as the years go by.

### 1-3. How to Read the Strengths

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Four criteria carry an SS: Protein density, Fertilizer demand, Infrastructure needs and Storability. On top of that, S goes to Micronutrients, Continuous-cropping tolerance, Yield retention without synthetic inputs, Taste and Prevalence in South Korea. What this pattern says is that soybean is not a crop that excels explosively under particular conditions, but a crop that never collapses decisively on any single point.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The SS for Protein density is the center of this table. A P:E of 37.9% is among the best figures of any plant food, and it shows that the saying that a handful of beans stands in for a piece of meat is no exaggeration. The nutrient people lose first in a disaster is not calories but high-quality protein. Calories can somehow be made up with tuber crops (tubers/storage roots) and grains, but protein has almost no alternative route. This is why a few rows of beans in one corner of a field change the character of the whole diet. The Supplementary grade of SS, 65.1 points, comes from the same source.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The SS for Fertilizer demand and the S for Yield retention without synthetic inputs go hand in hand. Soybean effectively does not need nitrogen fertilizer, because root-nodule bacteria fix nitrogen from the air and hand it over to the plant. The nodules do not cover the entire requirement, but the plant produces on its own anywhere from half to 75% of the nitrogen it needs. Where fertilizer plants have stopped and distribution networks are cut, nitrogen is the first resource to disappear, and the ability to take that resource from the air is worth more than any other criterion. The DOK trial's 63.4%, the highest among legumes, is also the result of low pest and disease pressure being added to this.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The A for Pest & disease pressure is also worth noting. The estimated yield loss without pest control of 23% is fairly low in this crop table. It means that even with no pesticides at all, the share lost stays at around 25%, which is far more bearable than potato late blight or the diseases of napa cabbage and its relatives. Most soybean diseases can be largely avoided by cultural means: seed selection, crop rotation and adjusting the sowing time.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The SS for Storability, the S for Seed saving and the A for Seed longevity complete the loop of the cycle. The harvested beans can be kept for more than 3 years, part of them can be used as seed the next year just as they are, and because the plant is self-pollinating, varietal traits do not get scrambled. There is no need to keep living tubers alive all winter, as with seed potatoes. Dry beans put in an earthenware jar are at once food, seed and reserve.

> **Soybean's value lies not in the amount that comes out of the field but in the quality of the protein contained in that amount, and in a structure whose cycle does not break even when nothing comes in from outside.**

### 1-4. How to Read the Weaknesses

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Only one criterion carries an E, Cold hardiness, but D goes to Perenniality, Sunlight and Cooking & processing, and C goes to Calories per area, Speed to harvest, Temperature range, Tillage & digging energy and Non-food value.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The C for Calories per area is the most substantive constraint. It is 1,002kcal per 1m², 0.38 times the self-sufficiency line. For one adult to meet 2,100kcal a day from soybeans alone, he or she would have to eat 524g every day, which is close to three cups of dried beans and is hard to eat in practice. The burden on digestion is heavy too, and the fuel is hard to afford. That is why soybean must always be placed as a partner to a staple crop. The structure is to build the calories with potatoes, sweet potatoes or grains and to put the protein from soybeans on top. When planning the allocation of field area, giving soybean 20-30% of the total is usually reasonable.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The D for Sunlight is heavy as well. At 40% shade, relative yield falls to 50%. That means a field half in shade yields half the harvest, so the shade of fences, spots under large trees and north-facing slopes must be avoided. Soybean is also a short-day plant that is sensitive to day length, so its flowering is regulated not only by light intensity but by changes in the length of the day. Spots lit by artificial light at night delay flowering and spoil the seed set, so these too are avoided.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The D for Cooking & processing is a cost that has to be paid every day. Dried soybeans must be soaked for 8 hours and boiled for more than an hour. This is a matter of safety, not taste. Raw and undercooked soybeans contain trypsin inhibitors and lectins, which interfere with digestion and cause abdominal pain and diarrhea. Where fuel is scarce, an hour of heating is a considerable burden, which is why Section 16 deals separately with ways to cut fuel use. It also looks at how putting the beans up as fermented jang (soybean paste and sauce) resolves this burden in one stroke.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The E for Cold hardiness and the C for Speed to harvest together narrow the growing window. A single frost kills the plant outright, and it needs 120-150 days. This is why early June is the best sowing time in central South Korea and why late June is taken as the latest limit. If planted late, the ripening period is caught by frost and only empty, shriveled seeds are left.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Nitrogen fixation is included in the C for Non-food value, but a trap lies hidden here. Fixing nitrogen and leaving nitrogen in the soil are different things. The nitrogen content of soybean seed is about 6.5% of its weight, which, compared with 1.4% for maize seed, is 4.5 times as much. The very act of harvesting the beans and carrying them away is an act of removing nitrogen from the field. Section 5 works through this problem in numbers.

## 2. What Kind of Plant Is This?

### 2-1. Taxonomy and Origin

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Soybean is an annual herb of the legume family (Fabaceae), genus Glycine. It belongs to the same family as adzuki bean, mung bean, cowpea, peanut, pea and kidney bean. This fact means two things. One is that it shares the nitrogen-fixing ability that comes from living in symbiosis with root-nodule bacteria; the other is that it shares the diseases and pests common to the legume family, so planting legumes one after another builds up pathogens.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Its region of origin is the temperate zone of East Asia, from northeastern China to the Korean Peninsula. Its wild ancestor, the wild soybean (dolkong, Glycine soja), still grows in the fields and on the field banks of South Korea. This origin explains two things. First, soybean is a crop already adapted to South Korea's climate, so it grows in the open field without special structures or protection. Second, its growth cycle is tuned to the rhythm of South Korea's climate, with rain concentrated in summer and frost in winter. Conversely, the year-round heat of the tropics or the short summers of cold regions are conditions soybean has never experienced.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;One more thing: soybean is a short-day plant, sensitive to day length. It flowers when it receives the signal that days are beginning to shorten. So if you move far in latitude, the flowering time goes awry, and if you miss the sowing time, the plant grows only leaves and sets no pods. If you have obtained seed from far away, you need to check which latitude that variety is adapted to.

### 2-2. Appearance

| Organ | Characteristics |
|---|---|
| Leaf | A trifoliate leaf, in which three leaflets form a set. The leaflets are close to heart-shaped and covered with fine hairs |
| Stem | Grows upright, 60-100cm tall. It branches, and leaves and flowers attach at every node |
| Root | A straight taproot with fine roots spreading from it. Nodules formed by root-nodule bacteria hang on the fine roots like beads |
| Flower | White or pale purple. Small and inconspicuous, blooming in July-August |
| Pod | Brown and covered with coarse hairs. Each pod holds 2-4 seeds |
| Seed | Round or flattened-round in shape. Yellowish white is the basic color, but there are also black, brown and green ones |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The fine hairs are soybean's defensive structure. The hairs covering the leaves and pods physically obstruct small pests and slow water evaporation. The hairs are coarse, so handling the pods bare-handed for long chafes the hands, which is why cotton work gloves are worn at harvest.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;That the flowers are small and self-pollinating is an important advantage in disaster preparedness. The plant sets seed even if no bees come, and its traits do not get scrambled even when it is planted mixed with other varieties. Unlike maize, it does not need an isolation distance, and yields do not fall even in years when pollinating insects decline. However, if high temperatures continue after flowering or the water runs out, the flowers simply drop off. Soybean is a crop that drops more than half of its open flowers on its own, so managing the flowering period is the same as managing yield.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The root nodules are this crop's key structure. The roots of a single well-grown soybean plant carry from several dozen to more than a hundred nodules the size of peas. How to check for them and how to get them to form well is the subject of Section 5.

### 2-3. Growth Stages

| Stage | Timing | Key physiology | Important nutrients |
|---|---|---|---|
| Germination and seedling stage | From sowing to 2-3 true leaves | The seed germinates and nodules begin to form | Phosphorus, a small amount of nitrogen |
| Vegetative growth stage | From true-leaf expansion to branching | Stems and leaves increase and the nodules become active | Nitrogen supplied by the nodules |
| Flowering stage | From first flower to full bloom | Flowers open and pollination takes place | Phosphorus, boron |
| Pod-swelling stage | Pod formation and swelling | The seeds enlarge and draw in nutrients | Potassium, phosphorus |
| Ripening and maturity stage | From seed filling to yellowing | Leaves turn yellow and nutrients move into the seeds | Stop irrigation |

> **Soybean takes up about 80% of all the water it uses over the whole growing season between flowering and pod swelling. If the water runs out during these 4 weeks, yield collapses far more than at any other time.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;One point about early nitrogen supply is worth stressing. The nodules do not start working right after sowing. It takes 3-4 weeks from the seed germinating and the roots extending, to bacteria infecting them, nodules forming and nitrogen being handed over. In the meantime, the young soybean gets by on the nutrients stored in the seed and the nitrogen in the soil. That is why putting in a small amount of nitrogen as base fertilizer makes sense. It must, however, be a small amount. When the soil has plenty of nitrogen, the plant feels no need to bother trading with bacteria and reduces nodule formation on its own. This is why the roots in a soybean field given a lot of nitrogen fertilizer have almost no nodules.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Stopping irrigation at maturity is also important. Once the leaves begin to turn yellow, water is cut off. If there is a lot of water at this point, the pods do not dry well, and when pods that have already dried absorb water and dry again repeatedly, they split and the seeds fall to the ground. This is called shattering, and rain at harvest time eats into yield in this way.

### 2-4. Nutrition per 100g

| Nutrient | Content | Notes |
|---|:---:|---|
| Energy | 401kcal | For dried beans. Higher than grains because of the fat |
| Protein | 38.02g | 37.9% of calories. Among the highest of any plant food |
| Fat | 15-20g | Mostly unsaturated fatty acids. A content high enough to press oil |
| Carbohydrate | 30-35g | Less than in grains, and includes a considerable amount of oligosaccharides |
| Dietary fiber | About 20g | On the high side. Eating a lot suddenly fills the belly with gas |
| Calcium | About 240mg | On the high side among plant foods |
| Iron | About 7mg | An important source when there is no meat |
| Potassium | About 1,800mg | Provides balance when salt intake rises |
| Folate | Abundant | Relevant to anemia and fetal development |
| Isoflavones and saponins | Present | Antioxidant substances |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The amino acid composition needs to be looked at separately. Soybean protein is rich in lysine. Grains such as rice, barley, wheat, sorghum and foxtail millet are all deficient in lysine, while soybean is slightly short of methionine. The two fill each other's gaps precisely. When rice and beans are eaten in the same meal, protein utilization rises noticeably compared with eating each separately. The Korean diet of kongbap (rice cooked with beans), kongguksu (noodles in chilled soybean soup) and doenjang-guk (soup made with doenjang, Korean soybean paste) served with rice stands on this principle.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Iron and calcium are also worth noting. Where meat and dairy have disappeared, anemia and declining bone density are real threats that arrive gradually over several months. Iron at 7mg and calcium at 240mg per 100g of soybeans are high values for plant foods. However, phytic acid interferes with the absorption of both, so treatments that reduce phytic acid by soaking, sprouting and fermentation are meaningful from a nutritional standpoint too. Section 14 deals with this issue.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Fat at 15-20% is the highest among legumes after peanut. It raises the calories and at the same time is a risk factor for rancidity in storage. Dried soybeans lasting 3 years holds only when the seed coat is intact and the moisture is low; once ground into flour, they develop a rancid smell within a few months. The rule is to grind only as much flour as will be eaten.
