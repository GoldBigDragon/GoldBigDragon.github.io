## 3. Growing Conditions

### 3-1. Temperature and Light

| Factor | Limit | Optimum | Outside this range |
|---|---|---|---|
| Germination temperature | 10°C or above | 20-22°C | Below 10°C, germination stops and the seed rots |
| Growing temperature | 15-35°C | 20-23°C | Prefers cooler conditions than soybean |
| Fruit-set temperature | 18-32°C | 20-30°C | A narrow 10°C range |
| Grain-filling temperature | 15°C or above | 18-25°C | Below 15°C, grain-filling essentially stops |
| Accumulated temperature | 2,800°C or above | 2,800-3,200°C | Needs 300°C or more beyond soybean |
| Frost | Leaves and stems die below freezing | - | Seeds in already-ripened pods are not harmed |
| Sunlight | 6+ hours a day | 8+ hours a day | At 40% shade, yield is halved |
| Day length | Short-day plant | Flowers as days shorten | Late-maturing types respond to day length later |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Accumulated temperature is the gatekeeper for growing black soybean. The sum of daily average temperatures from sowing to harvest must exceed 2,800°C for the seeds to fill properly. Compared with soybean, which reaches this at 2,500°C, black soybean must accumulate 300°C or more beyond that — equivalent to needing a growing season about a month longer. In central South Korea, sowing in late May and harvesting in early November roughly fills this requirement. If sowing is delayed by ten days, the late grain-filling period falls into a colder stretch, so the loss that comes back is not ten days but more than half a month.

> **In black soybean, the price of sowing late is not a late harvest but empty pods. Sow late, and the result is not a later harvest — it is nothing to harvest.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Black soybean is difficult to grow in high-altitude areas and the northern highlands of South Korea. Where the first frost comes in early October, the required accumulated temperature cannot be reached, and in such places it is more sensible to choose early soybean or another legume instead. Southern South Korea, by contrast, has more room to spare because frost comes late, so sowing can continue as late as late June. The surest way to fix a sowing date is to know the average first-frost date for your own area and count back 170 days from it.

### 3-2. Soil and Water

| Factor | Limit | Optimum | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Soil texture | From clay to sandy soil | Moisture-retentive, well-drained sandy loam to loam | Since it stands longer, moisture retention matters more than for soybean |
| pH | 5.5-7.5 | 6.0-7.0 | Closer to neutral favors the nodules |
| Effective soil depth | 30cm or more | 50cm or more | |
| Seasonal evapotranspiration | - | about 450mm | Mid-range among legumes |
| Flooding | Dies if submerged 2 days or more | - | The nodules die first |
| Raised-bed height | 20cm or more | Rounded bed | Preventing waterlogging during the monsoon season (jangma, late June to July in South Korea) is key |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Where black soybean's soil requirements diverge from soybean's is moisture retention. Black soybean stands in one spot for more than five months, through the monsoon, through the summer heat, and into autumn. Over that span there is a good chance of hitting at least one drought, and how well the soil holds water at that point decides the yield. Sandy soil that drains too fast needs frequent irrigation, while heavy clay soil that holds standing water lets the nodules die during the monsoon. The ideal is soil that holds moisture without waterlogging — a sandy loam rich in organic matter. Working in well-rotted compost and leaf mold pays off especially well with black soybean.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;A pH of 6.0 to 7.0 — a little higher than for soybean — is recommended. Root-nodule bacteria are most active near neutral, and because black soybean depends on its nodules for longer, this difference accumulates over the season. Farmland in South Korea tends to run acidic, so 3-4 weeks before sowing, correct it by adding 200kg of dolomitic lime per 1,000m². If lime is not available, use burnt, crushed shells and eggshells instead.

## 4. Varieties

| Cultivar | Characteristics | Yield (kg/1,000m²) | Suited to |
|---|---|:---:|---|
| Native black soybean | Traditional cultivar. Good flavor and locally adapted | about 160-260 | Continuing to save your own seed |
| Cheongja 5 | A newer cultivar. Resistant to disease and lodging, with pods that resist shattering | about 343 | When yield and stability matter most |
| Heuktae types | Large-seeded. Suited to jang and tofu | varies by cultivar | When you need to make jang |
| Seomoktae (jwinunikong) | Smallest-seeded. Suited to bean sprouts and medicinal use | low | When seed is scarce, or bean sprouts are needed |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The difference between Cheongja 5 and native black soybean is worth thinking through from a disaster-preparedness standpoint. Cheongja 5 yields 30-70% more than the native cultivar, loses less at harvest because its pods resist shattering, and is more resistant to lodging and disease. Looking at the numbers alone, there would seem to be no real choice. But two things need to be weighed together. First, improved cultivars are generally bred to respond to fertilizer, so their advantage shrinks under zero-input conditions. Second, the native cultivar is already adapted to the local climate and diseases, and its seed can be saved and carried forward indefinitely.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Fortunately, both are self-pollinating, true-breeding types of black soybean, so seed can be saved from either and replanted. The ideal choice, then, is not to pick one but to plant both, split between them. Use Cheongja 5 to secure yield, and the native cultivar to preserve local adaptation and genetic diversity. In a year when disease spreads widely or the weather turns wrong, one of the two is likely to survive. Never staking everything on a single cultivar is a basic principle of securing your seed supply.

| Situation | Choice | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Areas where the first frost comes earlier than mid-October | Avoid black soybean; choose early soybean | Insufficient accumulated temperature |
| Areas with many stink bugs | Cheongja 5 | Its tougher pods suffer less damage |
| Need to keep saving your own seed long-term | Native black soybean | It is locally adapted |
| Need to make jang | Heuktae | Large seeds and high yield |
| Seed is scarce | Seomoktae | The same weight of seed plants a much larger area |
| No winter vegetables | Some seomoktae | It sprouts well as bean sprouts |

## 5. Field Preparation and Fertilizer — Rhizobia and the Nitrogen Budget

### 5-1. Root-Nodule Bacteria — Condensed Summary

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Black soybean's mechanism for obtaining nitrogen is identical to soybean's. Root-nodule bacteria (Bradyrhizobium japonicum) form nodules on the roots, and inside them an enzyme called nitrogenase breaks the triple bond of atmospheric nitrogen molecules (N2) and converts it into ammonia. Since the bacteria cannot photosynthesize, they receive sugar from the plant and hand over the fixed nitrogen in exchange — it is a trade relationship. The enzyme is highly sensitive to oxygen, so the inside of the nodule is filled with a red pigment protein called leghemoglobin that holds oxygen in check.

| Check | Details |
|---|---|
| Timing | Around the start of flowering, 8-10 weeks after sowing |
| Method | Don't pull the plant up — lift it out with the soil using a spade, then wash the soil off in water |
| Count | 20 or more per plant; it's a good sign if they cluster on the upper taproot |
| Color check | Split a nodule with a fingernail and check the color inside |

| Color inside | Meaning | Action |
|---|---|---|
| Red or pink | It is fixing nitrogen | A good sign |
| White or pale green | The nodule formed but is not fixing nitrogen | Check pH, waterlogging, and phosphorus deficiency |
| Brown or black | Aged or dead | Normal during grain-filling; a problem during flowering |

> **The single habit of digging up a root, splitting a nodule, and checking for red is the surest signal of success or failure in growing beans in a world without nitrogen fertilizer.**

### 5-2. Securing an Inoculant Source

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;When a commercial inoculant cannot be obtained, the source of inoculant is soil. Topsoil from a field where beans grew well-nodulated last year is a living inoculant in itself.

| Method | Procedure | Caution |
|---|---|---|
| Coating with soil from a bean field | Scoop soil from the top 5-15cm, coat it evenly onto seeds lightly dampened with water, and sow at once | Work in the shade and sow within 30 minutes. Drying in the sun kills the bacteria |
| Scattering soil along the sowing row | Scatter the soil along the row and till it in together. One shovelful per 10m² | Uses more soil, but the bacteria spread through it |
| Crushing nodules | Collect nodules from dug-up bean roots, crush them into water, then soak the seeds in that water and remove | Must be used the same day |
| Leaving the roots | At harvest, leave the roots in the field instead of digging them up | The least labor-intensive way to maintain it |
| Wild dolkong roots | Collect nodules from the roots of wild dolkong (wild soybean, Glycine soja) growing on field edges | Same genus, so the bacteria are compatible |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There is a reason inoculation matters especially for black soybean. Because its growing period runs over five months, the total nitrogen the nodules must supply is greater than for soybean. If inoculation is weak, the plant manages early on, but nitrogen runs short during the pod-filling stage in August and September, causing leaves to yellow early and seeds to fill poorly. Nitrogen cutting out late in grain-filling is a common cause of poor black soybean yields, and its root cause traces back to inoculation at sowing time.

| Conditions That Cause Inoculation Failure |
|---|
| Soil pH 5.5 or below |
| Fields given plenty of nitrogen fertilizer |
| Fields where water pools |
| Fields low in phosphorus |
| Inoculated seed dried in the sun |
| Inoculating fungicide-treated seed |

### 5-3. The Trap in Nitrogen Fixation

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The claim that planting beans improves the soil is true only with conditions attached. Let's check it with numbers. The protein content of black soybean seed is 38%, and dividing protein by 6.25 gives the nitrogen content. About 6% of seed weight is pure nitrogen. Harvesting 230kg from 1,000m² means about 14kg of nitrogen leaves with it, which comes to 140kg per hectare.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Nitrogen fixed by the nodules, meanwhile, comes to 100-150kg per hectare under good conditions, and as little as 50kg under poor ones.

| Item | Nitrogen per hectare | Direction |
|---|---|---|
| Nodule fixation (good conditions) | 100-150kg | In |
| Nodule fixation (poor conditions) | 30-60kg | In |
| Removed in seed | 130-145kg | Out |
| Remaining in stems and leaves | 25-40kg | Stays if returned to the field; leaves if burned or carried away |
| Remaining in roots and nodules | 10-20kg | Usually stays in the field |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;If only the seed is harvested and the stems and leaves are returned to the field, the nitrogen budget comes out roughly even, or slightly positive. But if the bean stalks are cut for firewood, burned, or carried off as livestock feed, the nitrogen budget turns clearly negative. Soybean seed is about 6% nitrogen by weight, while maize kernels are about 1.4%, so for every 1kg carried off the field, beans take away 4.5 times as much nitrogen as maize does.

> **Beans do not enrich the soil because they fix nitrogen — they enrich it because part of the nitrogen they fix stays behind in the stems and leaves left in the field. Carry those stems and leaves away, and beans become a crop that strips the soil instead.**

| Principle | Details |
|---|---|
| Stems and leaves must go back to the field | Return all bean stalks, leaves, and pod husks. Cut into 20-30cm pieces and spread as cover |
| Do not burn | Burning releases the nitrogen as gas, leaving only potassium and phosphorus |
| Do not dig up the roots | Cut only the stalks and leave the roots in place |
| Do not rake away the fallen leaves | Since frost drops black soybean's leaves all at once, that leaf litter is itself fertilizer |
| If you do burn, return the ash | Nitrogen is lost, but potassium and phosphorus are recovered |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There is one circumstance that makes it easy to follow this principle with black soybean: because harvest falls in November, there is rarely an immediate need to reuse the field. If the bean stalks are cut and left spread over the plot, they break down over the winter and are in good shape to till in the following spring. This also keeps the bare soil covered through winter, reducing topsoil loss. There is almost no reason to carry the stalks off a black soybean field.

### 5-4. Fertilizer Rates and Disaster Substitutes

| Fertilizer | Per 1,000m² | Per m² | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Nitrogen | 3kg | 3g | Minimal amount only, for early use before the nodules become active |
| Phosphorus | 3kg | 3g | All as base fertilizer; needed for the energy metabolism of nitrogen fixation |
| Potassium | 3.4kg | 3.4g | All as base fertilizer; involved in seed enlargement |
| Compost | 1,000kg | 1kg | Fully rotted; improves moisture retention |
| Lime | 200kg (acidic soil) | 200g | 3-4 weeks before sowing |

> **Adding more nitrogen only makes the leaves lush while fewer pods set. Black soybean is already a tall bean prone to lodging, so the harm from excess nitrogen is greater than it is for soybean.**

| Substitute | Nutrients supplied | How to use | Caution |
|---|---|---|---|
| Wood ash | Potassium, phosphorus, calcium | Scatter 100-200g per m² and mix in | Too much pushes pH above 7 and blocks iron and zinc uptake |
| Well-rotted compost | All-round, organic matter | 1kg per m² | Under-rotted compost burns roots and spreads disease |
| Diluted urine | Nitrogen, potassium | Dilute 10x with water and pour onto the soil between plants | Small amounts only; keep it off the leaves |
| Bone meal / fish bones | Phosphorus, calcium | Burn, crush, and mix into compost | Effects appear slowly |
| Shells / eggshells | Calcium | Burn, crush, and scatter | Used in place of lime |
| Leaf mold | All-round, organic matter | Mix into the soil | Improves moisture retention; especially beneficial for black soybean |
| Rice hulls / rice straw | Potassium, silica | Use as mulch cover, then till in the following year | Tilling in under-decomposed material robs the soil of nitrogen |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In principle, top-dressing is not needed, but black soybean's long growing period can create an exception. If, 1-2 weeks after flowering, the leaves turn pale yellow all over and growth is clearly lagging, that is a sign the nodules are not working properly. Only then should a small supplement be given. Conventional practice uses 10-20kg of a nitrogen-potassium compound fertilizer; under disaster conditions, dilute urine tenfold with water and pour it onto the soil between plants. Apply it about 20cm away from the plant itself, since direct contact damages the roots.
