## 14. Toxicity and Antinutrient Compounds

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Black soybean is the same species as ordinary soybean, so it has the same antinutritional compounds. Cooked, it is safe; raw, it is dangerous. Legume seeds carry chemical defenses so they will not be eaten, and those substances interfere with human digestion. Most are heat-sensitive and are destroyed by thorough cooking, but the problem is that the bar for "thorough" is higher than you would think.

### 14-1. What the Antinutritional Compounds Are

| Compound | What it does | Symptoms | How to remove it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Trypsin inhibitors | Block the action of trypsin, a protein-digesting enzyme | Stomach pain, vomiting, diarrhea, reduced protein utilization | Soak 8 hours, then heat at 100°C for 30-60 minutes or more |
| Lectins (hemagglutinin) | Bind to the intestinal lining, hindering absorption, and clump red blood cells | Severe stomach pain, vomiting, diarrhea | Soak, then heat at 100°C for 10-30 minutes or more |
| Phytic acid | Binds iron, zinc, and calcium, blocking their absorption | Anemia, zinc deficiency with long-term intake | Reduced by 60-80% through soaking, sprouting, and fermentation |
| Saponins | Foam and irritate the stomach | Discomfort if intake is high | Skim off the foam while boiling |
| Oligosaccharides | Not digestible by humans; fermented by gut bacteria instead | Bloating and gas | Discard the soaking water. Greatly reduced by fermentation |

> **There are cases where an underdone bean is more dangerous than a raw one. A light blanching breaks down the cell walls so absorption improves, but the inhibitors and lectins are still there. Do not stop halfway — cook it all the way through.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The danger from lectins varies greatly by type of bean. The lectins in soybean and black soybean are less toxic than those in kidney beans (the genus Phaseolus). Eating just four or five raw kidney beans can bring on severe vomiting and diarrhea within a few hours, and food poisoning from undercooked kidney beans has been reported many times. Kidney-bean-family lectins in particular have a range where prolonged heating at a low temperature actually raises lectin activity, so cooking that leaves them in lukewarm water for a long time is the most dangerous. Black soybean is not as extreme, but the principle is the same. Enough time in briskly boiling water is the only safe method.

### 14-2. Standards for Soaking and Heating

| Treatment | Time/temperature | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Soaking | 8 hours in room-temperature water | Heat penetrates to the core. Some oligosaccharides and saponins leach into the water |
| Discarding the first soaking water | Mandatory | It holds dissolved oligosaccharides and antinutritional compounds |
| Boiling | 60 minutes or more in briskly boiling water | Trypsin inhibitors and lectins are inactivated |
| Steaming | 60-90 minutes in 100°C steam | Less loss of nutrients and pigment than boiling |
| Residual-heat cooking | Boil 15 minutes, then 2-3 hours in an insulated box | Fuel use drops to less than half |
| Checking doneness | Presses easily to mush with a finger | If a hard core remains, it is underdone |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There is one color-related judgment call in soaking black soybean. The soaking water turns dark red — that is anthocyanin. Discarding all of that water means discarding the pigment along with it, which is wasteful. The compromise is to discard only the first water and keep the water after that. The water from the first two or three hours has oligosaccharides and antinutritional compounds concentrated in it, so it must be discarded, but after that, change the water, soak for the rest of the time, and use that water for cooking rice or boiling. Do not discard the dark red water that comes out during boiling either — use it directly in cooking.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Steaming preserves pigment better than boiling. Since the beans are not submerged in water, the anthocyanin is not washed away. It takes a little more fuel, but all you need is a steamer basket or a colander, and the flavor is also richer.

### 14-3. Sprouting and Fermentation

| Method | Compound reduced | Degree |
|---|---|---|
| Sprouting (soybean sprouts) | Phytic acid, trypsin inhibitors, oligosaccharides | Phytic acid down 50-70%, inhibitors down 30-40% |
| Fermentation (doenjang — Korean soybean paste; soy sauce; cheonggukjang — quick-fermented soybean paste) | Phytic acid, trypsin inhibitors, oligosaccharides, lectins | Mostly broken down, with absorption rate rising |
| Soaking alone | Oligosaccharides, some phytic acid | Down 20-30% |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Fermentation is the most fundamental way to solve the antinutritional-compound problem. Microorganisms break proteins such as inhibitors and lectins down into amino acids and release enzymes that break down phytic acid. What's more, once it has been set to ferment, almost no cooking fuel is needed afterward, so in a disaster where fuel is precious, fermented soy paste becomes a form of storage that pays its cooking cost up front.

## 15. Storage Without Refrigeration

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Black soybean's storability is SS. Dried seed lasts 3 years at room temperature. But that holds only when conditions are right — if conditions go wrong, a single species of bean weevil can turn an entire stored batch into empty husks.

> **Beans are not lost to rot but to being eaten by insects. The technique of storage is, in essence, the technique of keeping insects out.**

### 15-1. Drying Comes First

| Stage | Method | Check |
|---|---|---|
| Preliminary drying | Stand the whole bean stalks under the eaves, hanging, to dry in the wind | In November, wind matters more than sun |
| Drying after threshing | Spread thinly on a straw mat in the sun for 3-4 days | Winter sun is weak, so it takes a day or two longer than for ordinary soybean |
| Target moisture | 12-13% for food use, 12% for seed | It splits with a snap when bitten, without leaving a tooth mark |
| Salt test | Put beans and roasted salt together in a dry bottle and shake it | If the salt sticks to the bottle wall, it needs more drying |
| Cooling | Let it cool completely before storing | Sealing it while still warm causes condensation |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Drying a November harvest is trickier than for ordinary soybean. The days are short, the temperature is low, and the air is humid, so sun-drying is slow. Two things help with this. One is wind: spreading it thinly under the eaves or in a passage where wind blows through is more effective than weak sunlight. The other is dry indoor air: spreading it on the warm side of a heated room's floor, or near a firebox, dries it quickly. However, if it is placed somewhere too hot, the seed coat cracks, so choose a spot that feels only lukewarm to the touch.

### 15-2. Controlling Bean Weevils in Storage

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Bean weevils (the Callosobruchus group, including the adzuki bean weevil and the mung bean weevil) are the biggest threat to stored legumes. What makes them frightening is that the infestation already starts in the field. Adult weevils attach eggs to the pods still on the plant, and those eggs hatch inside storage and bore into the beans. The eggs are already riding along when the beans come in, and since one generation takes less than a month, within half a year a single sack can turn into holed husks and powder.

| Method | Principle | Application | Effect |
|---|---|---|---|
| Full airtight sealing | Cuts off oxygen, suffocating them | Fill a glass jar or metal container completely and seal it | The most reliable method. In 3-4 weeks even the eggs and larvae die |
| Mixing in wood ash | The fine particles scrape the exoskeleton's waxy layer, dehydrating them | Mix in 2-5% of the beans' volume | Rinse with water before eating |
| Diatomaceous earth | Its silica shells damage the exoskeleton | 1-3g per 1kg of beans | More effective than ash. Works only in dry conditions |
| Oil coating | An oil film keeps eggs from sticking and from hatching | Add 2-5mL of cooking oil per 1kg of beans and rub it in | Very effective for legumes. Do not use on seed beans |
| Cold treatment | Eggs and larvae die below freezing | Set the sealed container outside for a few days in midwinter, then bring it back in | Harvesting in November gives the advantage that winter follows right away |
| Heat treatment | Eggs and larvae die at 60°C | Spread thinly in the sun for half a day | Do not use on seed beans |
| Dried chili pepper, Sichuan pepper, mugwort | Essential-oil compounds suppress egg-laying | Layer them in | A supplementary measure |
| Charcoal, roasted salt | Absorbs moisture | Wrap in cloth and place inside | Also helps prevent mold |
| Burying in ash | Combines sealing with physical control | Layer ash and beans in a jar | Widely used traditionally |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The most important thing is a complete seal. Insects breathe too. Inside a sealed container, as the beans and insects use up the oxygen, oxygen falls and carbon dioxide rises, and the insects die. The beans, being dormant, tolerate this condition. The fuller the container and the less empty space inside, the faster it works, and several small containers packed tight are better than one large one — if one container spoils, you do not lose everything.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Black soybean has circumstances that favor cold treatment. Since it is harvested in November, deep winter follows soon after threshing and drying finish. Setting a sealed container outside for a few days to expose it to below-freezing temperatures kills the eggs and larvae. When bringing it back in, do not open the container right away — leave it indoors for half a day to reach room temperature before opening. Opening a cold container immediately causes condensation inside, which seeds mold.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Oil coating suits legume storage especially well. Add 2-3ml of cooking oil per 1kg of beans and rub it in by hand so a thin film forms on every bean. The oil film makes it hard for adult weevils to attach eggs and blocks the breathing pores of eggs already attached. However, if the oil turns rancid it produces an odor, so use this only for the portion you will eat within half a year, and do not use it on seed beans.

### 15-3. Storage Containers and Locations

| Container | Seal | Duration | Notes |
|---|:---:|---|---|
| Glass jar | Very good | 3+ years | You can check condition by eye. Does not block light |
| Metal container | Very good | 3+ years | Completely blocks rodents and insects. Watch for condensation |
| Earthenware jar | Good | 2-3 years | Temperature changes slowly, and rodents cannot get through |
| Thick plastic | Fair | 1-2 years | Rodents can chew through |
| Underground storage pit | Good | 2-3 years | The whole batch is lost if water seeps in |

| Storage condition | Target |
|---|---|
| Temperature/humidity | 15°C or below, air humidity 65% or below |
| Light | Fully blocked. Anthocyanin breaks down slowly in light |
| Location | Underground or a north-facing room, on a stand off the ground |
| Checks | Once every two months |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Blocking light matters a bit more for black soybean. Anthocyanin breaks down slowly under light and heat, so keeping it in a clear jar in a bright spot fades the seed-coat color within a few months. The calories and protein stay the same, but the pigment's benefit is lost. If you use a glass jar, wrap it in cloth or keep it somewhere dark.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;For checks, the most reliable senses are hearing and smell. If you put your ear to the container and hear a rustling sound, insects are active, and a musty smell means mold has started. Small round holes on the beans' surface mean a bean weevil has already completed a generation. Even then, you can protect what remains by taking the whole batch out, drying it in the sun, removing the hollow beans, mixing in ash or oil, and sealing it again.

## 16. Cooking and Processing

### 16-1. Soybean Sprouts – Winter's Vitamin C

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Dried beans have practically no vitamin C. But sprouting produces vitamin C that was not there before, because the plant newly synthesizes it as it undergoes the physiological shift of germination. When there are no fresh vegetables at all in winter, a handful of dried beans plus water and a dark spot are enough to get fresh vegetables in three or four days. No field, sunlight, or soil is needed.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Black soybean can be sprouted too, but its large seeds tend to produce sprouts that are bent and soft. Among the black bean lines, the one best suited to sprouting is seomoktae (rat's-eye bean), which has small seeds. Even if you mainly grow black soybean, planting about one row of seomoktae alongside it and keeping it aside for sprouts is a practical way to solve the winter-vegetable problem.

| Step | Details | Key point |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Choosing beans | Seomoktae or small-seeded black beans. Discard any cracked or insect-damaged seeds | A cracked seed rots and spoils the whole batch |
| 2. Washing | Rinse several times and skim off anything that floats | What floats is usually a dead seed |
| 3. Soaking | Soak in lukewarm water for 6-8 hours | It should swell to twice its size |
| 4. Setting up | Place in a draining container or steamer basket and cover with a damp cloth | Standing water causes rot |
| 5. Keeping it dark | Block light completely. 20-25°C | Light turns the sprouts green and tough |
| 6. Watering | Pour water thoroughly over the top 3-4 times a day | This both regulates temperature and washes away waste |
| 7. Harvest | 4-7 days, 5-10cm long | Harvest before the roots turn brown |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Watering is nearly everything for soybean sprouts. Water supplies moisture, cools the heat generated by germination, and washes away the waste that builds up. Lots of fine roots forming is a sign of too little water. Without a steamer basket, use a container with holes in the bottom, a fine-mesh strainer, or a basin with holes punched in it. Put a drip tray underneath and cover the top with a black cloth, and that becomes your steamer basket. All you need is a structure that drains water and something that blocks light. Soybean sprouts are also not eaten raw — they are blanched or stir-fried, because of the inhibitors that remain.

### 16-2. Cooking and Processing Black soybean

| Product | Procedure | Storage | Value |
|---|---|---|---|
| Kongjaban (soybeans braised in soy sauce) | Boil soaked beans, then braise them down with soy sauce | 2-3 weeks in a cool place | Sweet enough to need no added sugar. Black soybean's signature dish |
| Bean rice | Cook soaked beans together with rice | Made fresh each time | The amino acids of grain and bean complement each other |
| Black soybean tea | Roast, then boil in water | Roasted beans keep 2-3 months airtight | Anthocyanin infuses into the water. Uses little fuel |
| Black soybean soy milk | Grind soaked beans, boil, and strain | A few days | Easy to digest. The pulp is used separately |
| Meju (fermented soybean blocks), doenjang, soy sauce | Boil, mash, let ferment, then age in brine | Several years | The black color makes the paste darker. Heugtae (plain black soybean) suits this better |
| Cheonggukjang | Wrap boiled beans in straw and ferment around 40°C for 2-3 days | A few days; months if dried | The fastest-fermenting bean product |
| Roasted soybean flour | Roast, then grind | 2-3 months airtight | Ready to eat as soon as it is mixed with water |
| Soybean oil | Roast, grind, and press | Several months | The pressing yield is low |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Kongjaban makes the best use of black soybean's character. Black soybean's sugar content, at 3.28g, is higher than ordinary soybean's, so it tastes sweet even without added sugar. With sweeteners gone, this is no small advantage. The trick is to first boil the fully soaked beans in plain water until soft, and only then add soy sauce and braise them down. Adding soy sauce from the start makes the beans tough from the salt and they do not cook through well. The dark red pigment in the boiling water also goes into the braise, so do not discard it.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Black soybean tea is an efficient use of fuel. Roast the dried beans over low heat for 20 to 30 minutes, and after that all you need is a few minutes of boiling in water. Roasting also considerably reduces the trypsin inhibitors. Do not throw away the beans after brewing the tea — eat them or feed them to livestock. In a situation where you must boil water to drink anyway, this tastes better than plain boiled water and brings in pigment and minerals at the same time, so it doubles as both disinfecting your drinking water and getting nutrition.

### 16-3. Fuel-Saving Cooking

| Method | Fuel | Procedure |
|---|:---:|---|
| Residual-heat cooking | Very little | Boil soaked beans for 15 minutes, turn off the heat, wrap in a box of straw or rice hulls or in a blanket, and leave for 2-3 hours |
| Pressure cooking | The least | Pressure-cook soaked beans for 15-20 minutes |
| Roasted soybean flour | Almost none | Roast and grind ahead of time, then mix with water to eat |
| Black soybean tea | Little | Boil pre-roasted beans in water for a few minutes |
| Fermentation (soy paste) | Paid up front | Once it is set to ferment, no fuel is needed afterward |
| Soybean sprouts | None | Germination needs no heat |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There is one precaution when using residual-heat cooking. The temperature inside an insulated box stays around 80°C, which may not be enough to fully destroy lectins and trypsin inhibitors. So make sure the initial boiling time is a full 15 minutes or more, and when you take the beans out, check that they mash easily with a finger. If a hard core remains, boil them again.

## 17. Annual Work Calendar

| Month | Work | Key point |
|---|---|---|
| January | Check stored beans, test seed germination, grow soybean sprouts | Setting the sealed container outside for a few days doubles as cold treatment |
| February | Plan the field, turn the compost, make soy paste | Check the rotation order and the germination rate of old seed |
| March | Apply lime, finish the compost | Work it in 3-4 weeks before sowing |
| April | Till the field, build the raised beds, dig drainage channels, apply base fertilizer | Dig drainage channels deep with the monsoon in mind |
| May | Sort seed, hot-water disinfection, secure inoculant soil, begin sowing in late month | Scoop up soil from a field that grew beans beforehand |
| June | Finish sowing (by mid-month in central South Korea), guard against birds, thin, first hilling | Past mid-month, seed fill runs into frost |
| July | Second and third hilling, weeding, pinching, mulching | Finish hilling and pinching before flowering |
| August | Flowering, check root nodules, irrigate, watch for stink bugs, stake the rows | Tie up the rows before typhoon season |
| September | Pod fill, intensive irrigation, catching stink bugs by hand | The 3 weeks up to late month decide the yield |
| October | Seed fill, stop irrigation, mark seed-saving plants, begin harvest in late month | Tie thread on the plants that ripen first |
| November | Finish harvest, hang to dry, thresh, clean, return stalks and leaves to the field | Cut the stalks and spread them over the field. Do not burn them |
| December | Final drying, storage, make soy paste, soybean sprouts | Use cold treatment to control bean weevils |

### 17-1. Five Things Not to Forget

| Item | Details |
|---|---|
| Sowing window | Mid-June is the limit in central South Korea. Sowing late does not just mean a later harvest — it can mean nothing to harvest at all |
| Stink bugs | The 3 weeks from late August to mid-September decide the outcome. Neglecting control can cost you half the crop |
| Hilling and pinching | Always finish before flowering. Doing them after flowering reduces yield |
| Stalks and leaves | If the stalks, leaves, and roots are not returned to the field, beans become a crop that strips the soil |
| Heating | Soak for 8 hours and boil for at least an hour. An underdone bean can be more dangerous |
