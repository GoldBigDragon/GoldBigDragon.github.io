## 6. Sowing

### 6-1. Sowing Time — Black soybean's First Gate

| Region | Optimal sowing time | Sowing deadline | Harvest time |
|---|---|---|---|
| Central South Korea | late May-mid-June | late June | late October-mid-November |
| Southern South Korea | early June-late June | early July | early November-late November |
| High-altitude areas and the northern highlands | Not suitable for cultivation | - | Insufficient accumulated temperature |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The way to set a sowing date is to count backward. Find the average first-frost date for your area, and subtracting 170 days from it gives the sowing deadline. Add a ten-day margin to that, and you have the end of the optimal window. Since the first frost in central South Korea usually falls in late October, mid-June becomes the deadline. In the south, the first frost comes in mid-November, so there is room until late June.

> **Following potatoes or garlic with black soybean in the same spot leaves the timing too tight. Past late June, it is safer to choose a different legume than to attempt double-cropping. Mung bean or cowpea, which need only 80-90 days, are far better suited as a follow-on crop.**

### 6-2. Seed Preparation and Sowing

| Item | Method | Under disaster conditions |
|---|---|---|
| Sorting | Discard seeds with spots, discoloration, wrinkling, or insect holes | Sort by hand, one seed at a time. This is the cheapest and most effective step |
| Checking for purple seed stain | Pick out seeds with purple stains on the seed coat | On a black seed coat the stain is hard to see, so check under bright light |
| Hot-water treatment | Soak in 50-55°C water for 10-15 minutes | Water hot enough that you can barely hold your hand in it for 5 seconds. Above 60°C damages germination |
| Ash treatment | Coat with fine wood ash | Helps disinfect and dry the seed, and repels birds and insects |
| Nodule-bacteria inoculation | Coat with soil from a field where beans were grown | Not done together with disinfection. Do it only after the hot-water treatment has fully cooled |
| Germination test | Wrap 20 seeds in a damp cloth, leave for a few days, and count how many sprout | Always check old seed first |

| Item | Standard | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Sowing depth | 3-5cm | Within 2cm, the seed dries out and birds eat it. Beyond 5cm, emergence suffers |
| Seeds per hole | 2-3 seeds | Thin to 2 plants later |
| Row spacing | 70cm (single-row planting recommended) | Ensures airflow and sunlight, and prevents waterlogging damage during the monsoon |
| Plant spacing | 20cm | Black soybean grows larger, so space it wider than soybean |
| Raised-bed height | 20cm or more, rounded bed | Drainage and lodging prevention |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Planting spacing is set wider than for soybean because black soybean grows for five months and becomes a bigger plant. Planted at soybean's spacing, the leaves are already overlapping by August, blocking airflow, and disease spreads and plants lodge as they go through the monsoon and typhoons. 70cm by 20cm looks generous at first, but by September it fits exactly. The same reasoning is why single-row planting on each bed is recommended.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The threat right after sowing is birds. Pigeons, magpies, and pheasants peck the cotyledons off newly sprouted beans, and a plant that loses its cotyledons usually dies. Cover the sowing row thinly with twigs or straw, drape netting or an old window screen over it until germination, or crisscross shiny string above it. The surest method is simply walking the field morning and evening for the first ten days after emergence.

## 7. Water Management

| Timing | Water management | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Sowing to germination | Keep the soil from drying out completely | The seed must absorb water equal to its own weight to germinate |
| Early growth (June-July) | Rely on natural rainfall | Dryness at this stage encourages deep rooting. Overwatering is more dangerous |
| The monsoon season | Focus on drainage | The task here is removing water, not adding it |
| Flowering to pod-filling (August-September) | Water generously so the soil does not dry out | 80% of the plant's total water use over its life happens in this period |
| Grain-filling (October) | Reduce watering | Overwatering keeps the pods from drying |
| Ripening | Stop irrigation | Causes pod-shattering and premature leaf drop |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;What sets black soybean's water management apart from soybean's is that it also has to account for autumn drought. Soybean has already finished grain-filling by September, but black soybean is still filling its pods into late September. In a year with no rain in September, irrigation at this stage directly determines the yield. Conversely, if rain is frequent in October, the pods fail to dry, delaying harvest and letting mold develop in the seed.

| Symptom | Cause | Action |
|---|---|---|
| Leaves droop at midday and recover by evening | Temporary water shortage. Normal | Leave it as is |
| Leaves are still drooping in the morning | The roots cannot draw up enough water | Water thoroughly |
| Large numbers of flowers drop | Drought or high heat during flowering | Water and mulch to lower soil temperature |
| Pods have set but the seeds are small | Water shortage during pod-filling | Irrigation at this stage directly determines yield |
| In September, the lower leaves suddenly turn yellow first | Nitrogen or water shortage late in grain-filling | Check the nodules and water |
| Lower leaves are yellow and the roots are black | Root damage from overwatering | Dig drainage channels and raise the soil with hilling |
| The whole leaf is pale yellow but not wilting | Nitrogen shortage from poor nodulation | Dig up a root and split a nodule to check |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Mulching matters especially for growing black soybean. Because the growing period is long, the period of competing with weeds is also long, and the crop goes through both summer drought and rising soil temperature. Even without plastic film, covering the soil with 5-10cm of straw, rice hulls, fallen leaves, or cut weeds cuts surface evaporation by more than half and reduces weeding labor. However, since hilling must be done several times, it fits the work sequence better to lay the mulch after hilling is finished, from July onward.

## 8. Crop Management — Hilling and Pinching

### 8-1. Hilling — Three Rounds

| Round | Timing | Method |
|---|---|---|
| Round 1 | 2-3 true leaves | Rake soil up until it covers the cotyledons |
| Round 2 | 4-5 true leaves | Raise the soil up to the first true leaves |
| Round 3 | 6-7 true leaves | Raise it to the node of the first true leaf. Finish 10 days before flowering |

> **Hilling must always be finished before flowering. Going into the field to turn the soil after the flowers have opened knocks blossoms off and severs fine roots, reducing yield.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The reason hilling for black soybean is split into three rounds is its size and its long growing period. Black soybean grows tall and branches heavily, so its risk of lodging from August onward is higher than soybean's. Burying a lot of soil at once damages the stem, so soil is added a little at a time, matching the plant's growth rate, to build a sturdy base. Hilling brings four benefits at once: the buried base gains support, new roots grow from the buried stem and expand the absorption area, the furrow between plants deepens so monsoon water drains away, and young weeds are buried.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Under disaster conditions, these three rounds of hilling are not a small amount of labor. If people are short, do at least the first and third rounds. The first helps young plants establish, and the third acts directly to prevent lodging. If even that is not possible, it is best to do at least the round timed for the third stage.

### 8-2. Pinching

| Round | Timing | Method | Effect |
|---|---|---|---|
| Round 1 | 5-7 true leaves, always before flowering | Pinch off the growing tip at the end of the stem by hand | More side branches form, giving more sites for pods |
| Round 2 (hard pinch) | Mid-growth, when the risk of lodging is high | Cut back, leaving only 20-25cm from the base | Forces the plant's height down |
| Do not do this | Flowering, from August onward | Do not do it | Cutting at this stage sharply reduces yield |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The second, hard pinch is a technique unique to black soybean, and it must be applied selectively. In a year with heavy compost application or a lot of rain, if black soybean grows taller than an adult's waist, a single typhoon can flatten the whole field. When that sign appears, cutting it back decisively to leave only 20-25cm from the base lets new, low, sturdy side branches grow that carry pods without lodging. In a plot that received very heavy compost, leave only 20cm.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Conversely, applying the hard pinch to black soybean that is already growing poorly on infertile soil leaves it without the strength to recover, and yield drops sharply. Here is the rule for deciding: cut it back if it is well above knee height, the leaves are dark green, and the nodes are spaced far apart. Leave it alone if growth has stalled at around shin height and the leaves are pale. Since a shortage of fertilizer is the norm under disaster conditions, the default is to do only the first pinch and skip the second.

### 8-3. Weeds and Lodging

| Task | Timing | Method |
|---|---|---|
| Thinning | 1-2 true leaves | Leave 2 plants per hole |
| Weeding | Focus from 30-40 days after sowing | Combine with hilling. Scrape shallowly |
| Mulching | After the third hilling | Cover with straw, rice hulls, fallen leaves, or cut weeds |
| Staking and tying | August, before typhoon forecasts | Drive stakes around the field and tie a line around it at waist height |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Since black soybean stays in the field for five months, the period of competing with weeds is also long. Fortunately, by August the leaves fully cover the ground, so weeds stop increasing much, which means the weeding period that really matters is June and July. These two months of weeding are what make the yield. Combining it with hilling cuts the labor in half, and covering with mulch after the final hilling leaves almost nothing to do by hand after that.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Preparing against lodging needs to be done ahead of time, in August. Trying to prop plants back up after they have already gone down breaks roots and drops pods, causing more damage. Driving stakes around the field and tying a line around it once at waist height lets the plants inside lean on each other and hold up. In a large field, add another crosswise line every few rows. For sections that are already completely flattened, prop them up slightly with branches so the pods do not touch the ground.

## 9. Pests and Diseases

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Black soybean's pest & disease pressure grade is C. The estimated yield loss without pest control is 47.4%, meaning with no control at all you lose nearly half the crop. The cause is the growing period. September and October are when stink-bug and soybean-pod-borer populations peak, and by then every other bean has already been harvested, so all the pests in the area converge on the black soybean still standing in the field.

### 9-1. Major Diseases

| Disease | Timing | Symptoms | Response without chemicals |
|---|---|---|---|
| Anthracnose | After the monsoon through the pod stage | Black cankers on stems and pods | Seed sorting and crop rotation. Resistant cultivars (Cheongja 5). Bordeaux mixture as a supplement |
| Bacterial spot (wildfire disease) | After the monsoon through the pod stage | Angular brown leaf spots with yellow halos | Seed disinfection, ensuring airflow, staying out of the field right after rain |
| Black root rot | Throughout the growing season | Roots brown and rot, and the lower leaves turn yellow | Improve drainage, raise the beds, disinfect seed, rotate crops |
| Brown spot | Late July-early August | Brown spots starting on lower leaves, premature leaf drop | Ensure airflow, remove crop debris, Bordeaux mixture |
| Downy mildew | Late July-early August | Yellow spots on the leaf's upper side, grayish-white mold underneath | Adequate spacing, drainage, Bordeaux mixture |
| Soybean mosaic virus | Throughout the growing season | Leaves develop a mottled mosaic pattern and curl | No cure. Pull and burn immediately, and reduce aphids |
| Purple seed stain | Grain-filling | Purple stains on the seed coat | Hard to spot on a black seed coat. Sort under bright light |
| Sclerotinia stem rot | In cool, humid conditions | Stems soften, with white mycelium and black sclerotia | Rotate crops for 4 years or more. Dig up diseased plants with the soil and burn them |

### 9-2. Major Pests

| Pest | Peak occurrence | Damage | Control without chemicals |
|---|---|---|---|
| Stink bugs | Late August-early September | Pierce pods and suck the sap, producing empty seeds. Neglecting control can cut yield by up to 58% | Early-morning hand-picking, trap crops, insect netting |
| Soybean pod borer | Late August-early September | Larvae feed on the seeds inside the pods | Remove crop debris to eliminate overwintering sites. Pick off damaged pods |
| Aphids | Late July-early August | Suck sap and spread viral disease | Spray soapy water (5g soap per 1L water) on the undersides of leaves |
| Tobacco cutworm | August | Voraciously devours leaves and pods | Hand-pick them. Remove egg masses along with the leaf |
| Mites | Dry conditions in August | Sap-sucking on leaf undersides mottles the leaves yellow | Spray leaves forcefully with water |
| Birds | Right after sowing | Peck off the cotyledons | Cover with straw or netting until germination |
| Water deer, roe deer | Summer | Eat the leaves and stems | Put up fencing or netting |

> **In growing black soybean, stink-bug control is not a side task — it is the key work that determines yield. The three weeks from late August to mid-September are where it is won or lost.**

| Stink-bug response | Details | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Early-morning hand-picking | At low pre-dawn temperatures they cannot fly well. Search under the pods, knock them off by hand into a container of water | The most reliable method. Doing it every other day for three weeks clearly reduces the population |
| Trap crops | Plant sesame, foxtail millet, or sorghum around the field's edge to draw them in, then focus catching there | Reduces damage to the main plot |
| Covering with insect netting | For a small plot, cover with garden fleece or insect netting after flowering | Since black soybean self-pollinates, it sets fruit even without bees |
| Clearing weeds | Cut down legume weeds on field edges ahead of time | Removes the stink bugs' alternate host plants |
| Cultivar choice | Choose Cheongja 5, which has tougher pods | Reduces piercing damage |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Covering with insect netting is especially useful for black soybean. Most crops need insects to help with pollination, so covering them with netting during flowering hurts fruit set, but black soybean self-pollinates, so it suffers no such loss. For small-scale self-sufficiency, setting up a frame around the time flowering begins and covering it with garden fleece blocks stink bugs and the pod borer almost completely. If netting cannot be found, an old mosquito net, torn insect screen, or tightly woven cloth can also be used.

### 9-3. Chemical-Free Control Materials and Cultural Controls

| Material | How to make | Target |
|---|---|---|
| Bordeaux mixture | Dissolve 400g copper sulfate and 400g quicklime separately in 100L of water, then mix | Bacterial and fungal diseases |
| Soapy water | 5g unscented soap in 1L water | Aphids, mites |
| Wood-ash water | Soak 1 doe (about 1.8L) of ash in 10L water overnight and use the water on top | Fungal suppression |
| Dusting with dry ash | On a dewy morning, dust fine ash onto the leaves | Leaf-eating insects |
| Wood vinegar | Dilute 300-500x | Repellent |
| Hot pepper / garlic water | Grind, steep in water, strain, and use | Repellent |

| Cultural control | Principle |
|---|---|
| Seed sorting | Anthracnose and purple seed stain spread through seed |
| Crop rotation | Keep legumes out for 2-3 years. Pathogens die without a host |
| Plant spacing | Keep to 70cm × 20cm to ensure airflow |
| Drainage | More than half of black soybean's diseases are related to excess moisture |
| Debris management | Burn diseased debris; return only healthy debris to the field |
| No entering the field after rain | Brushing wet leaves spreads pathogens |
| Clearing field edges | Legume weeds serve as alternate hosts for stink bugs |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Telling diseased debris apart from healthy debris is where this collides with the nitrogen budget from Section 5. To retain nitrogen, the stems and leaves must be returned, but returning diseased ones leaves the pathogen behind. If the leaves have only a few spots, it is fine to cut and spread them as cover. Only plants with stems softened by sclerotinia stem rot, plants with black anthracnose cankers, or plants infected with mosaic virus should be gathered separately and either burned or composted for a long time away from the field.
