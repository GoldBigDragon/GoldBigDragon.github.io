## 10. Nutrient Deficiency and Excess

| Symptom | Cause | Natural-material remedy |
|---|---|---|
| The whole leaf is pale yellow and the plant is small | Nitrogen deficiency. In black soybean (black soybean with green cotyledons), poor root nodulation is the most common cause | First dig up a root and split a nodule open to check. If there are no nodules, be sure to inoculate next year, and for now give a small amount of urine diluted 1:10 |
| In September the lower leaves suddenly turn yellow and drop, and the seeds fill poorly | Nitrogen shortage late in seed fill | This symptom is specific to black soybean. The root nodules often senesce early. Next year, strengthen phosphorus application and inoculation |
| Leaf edges dry brown and curl inward | Potassium deficiency | Scatter wood ash between the plants and lightly rake it in |
| Growth is slow and the leaves turn purplish | Phosphorus deficiency | Bone meal, roasted and ground fish bones, wood ash |
| Flowers bloom but pods fail to set | Boron deficiency, or high heat and dryness during flowering | Compost made from varied materials. For now, lower the temperature with watering and mulching |
| The veins are green but the tissue between them is yellow | Iron or magnesium deficiency; soil that has turned alkaline | Stop using ash. Increase organic matter with leaf mold and well-rotted compost |
| New leaves are small and malformed, and the growing point dies | Calcium or boron deficiency | Roasted and ground eggshell, ground shell powder |
| Only the leaves grow lush and dark green, with few pods | Nitrogen excess | Stop applying nitrogen. Lower the plant's height with hard pinching |
| Plants grow leggy and lodge | Nitrogen excess or overly dense planting | A second round of hard pinching, and staking with string. Reduce fertilizer next year |
| The seed coat's black color is pale and blotchy | Insufficient seed fill, or early frost | Sow earlier. The pigment accumulates late in seed fill |
| Lower leaves are yellow and the roots are brown | Root damage from excess moisture | Dig drainage channels. This is not a fertilizer problem |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There are two symptoms that need to be looked at specifically in black soybean. One is the lower leaves dropping suddenly in September. The root nodules' lifespan is tuned to ordinary soybean, so they sometimes cannot last the full five months and lose function late in seed fill. When the soil then has no nitrogen, the plant breaks down protein from its lower leaves and sends it to the seeds, which is why the leaves drop quickly. This is a normal form of aging to some degree, but if it progresses sharply from early September it becomes a problem because the seeds do not fill fully. The fundamental fix is to apply plenty of phosphorus to support the root nodules' energy metabolism, and to make sure you have a reliable inoculant.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The other is the seed coat's color turning pale. Anthocyanins accumulate late in seed fill, so if seed fill is not sufficient, the black color is pale and blotchy. This is a sign of insufficient time, not a nutrient deficiency. It means that sowing was late that year, or frost came early, and the next year's sowing should be moved earlier.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Micronutrient deficiencies are hard to address one at a time. Without chemical fertilizer, there is practically no way to obtain molybdenum or cobalt. Instead, compost made by mixing and rotting down vegetable scraps, fruit peels, eggshells, fish bones, fallen leaves, weeds, and ash together contains a broad range of micronutrients. The practical strategy is the principle of putting everything into the compost heap.

## 11. Intercropping and Crop Rotation

| Compatibility | Crop | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Good | Maize, sorghum | They share in the nitrogen the beans fix nearby. The height difference lets them share light |
| Good | Marigold | Its root exudates suppress nematodes |
| Good | Potato | Their diseases do not overlap, and their nutrient needs differ. Since harvesting potato in June leaves the timing tight for sowing black soybean, place them in a neighboring bed instead |
| Good | Napa cabbage and cabbage family | Planting them the year after black soybean makes use of the leftover nitrogen |
| Bad | Tomato, chili pepper, eggplant | They share pests and diseases |
| Bad | Garlic and onion family | Their exudates are reported to suppress root-nodule bacteria |
| Bad | Continuous cropping with other legumes | Pathogens and nematodes build up |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There is one constraint on intercropping with black soybean: the field is not free until November. Fall-sown crops such as garlic, onion, and barley cannot follow black soybean on schedule. By the time black soybean is harvested it is already mid-November, and if garlic is planted then, the ground freezes before it can root and it will not survive the winter. The black soybean field should therefore be planned as a field that rests until the following spring. Cutting the bean stalks and spreading them over the bed keeps that period from being wasted.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Mixed planting with maize calls for more caution with black soybean. Maize is harvested in August, but black soybean still has two and a half more months to grow after that. Spending the summer in maize's shade stunts black soybean's growth, and it only gets full light after the maize is harvested, which delays seed fill. Given the figure that legume yield drops by half under 40% shade, it is better to keep black soybean separate from maize rather than mix them. If they are mixed, divide them generously into two rows of maize to two rows of black soybean and lay them out in a north-south orientation.

| Item | Standard |
|---|---|
| Rotation break | 1 year or more |
| Ideal cycle | 3-4 years |
| Continuous-cropping damage | Nematodes, black root rot, and anthracnose build up |

| Example 4-year rotation |
|---|
| Year 1: Black soybean |
| Year 2: Spring potato or spring leafy vegetables (using the nitrogen left by black soybean) |
| Year 3: Cereals such as maize, sorghum, or foxtail millet |
| Year 4: Root vegetables or green manure |
| Back to black soybean |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;When the field is too small to allow for crop rotation, the simplest approach is to divide it into four plots and shift by one plot each year. If it cannot be divided into plots, at least move the position of the planting rows every year. Simply planting maize this year where beans grew last year, and beans this year where maize grew last year, considerably delays the buildup of nematodes.

## 12. Harvest and Threshing

### 12-1. Judging Harvest Timing

| Criterion | Details |
|---|---|
| Leaves | They have turned yellow and almost all dropped. A frost brings them all down at once |
| Pods | More than 80% of them have turned brown |
| Sound | Shaking makes the seeds rattle inside the pods |
| Seed | Biting it does not leave a tooth mark; it splits with a snap. The seed coat is a deep black |
| Moisture | Seed moisture around 15% |
| Timing | 7-14 days after the leaves have all dropped |

| Region | Harvest time |
|---|---|
| Central South Korea | Late October-mid-November |
| Southern South Korea | Early November-late November |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The name black soybean — literally "frost bean" — points to its harvest timing. It is harvested after the first frost. Once frost falls, the leaves all drop within a day or two, leaving just the pods, which actually makes harvesting easier. By this point the seeds have already filled out fully, so the frost does no harm. Being hit by frost and being fully filled out are two different things, though. Open a few pods to check that the seeds are plump and the seed coat is dark before harvesting. If the seeds are shriveled or the seed coat is pale, seed fill is not complete, which means sowing was late that year.

> **November rain and snow are black soybean's last enemy. If dry pods get wet and dry out over and over, they split open and the seeds fall to the ground. If the weather looks like it is turning bad, cut and bring the crop in rather than waiting for full ripeness.**

### 12-2. Harvesting Technique

| Step | Details |
|---|---|
| 1 | Harvest on a clear, dry day, after 10 a.m. once the dew has dried |
| 2 | Cut the base of the stalks with a sickle. Leave the roots in the ground to retain the nitrogen from the root nodules |
| 3 | Spread it out in the field for one to two days for preliminary drying. Bring it in right away if it rains |
| 4 | Tie it into bundles and stand them hanging under the eaves or in a shed to finish drying |
| 5 | Thresh once the pods are bone dry and rattle when shaken |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The difficulty with a November harvest is drying. The days are short, the temperature is low, and the humidity is high, so sun-drying is slow. For that reason, it is better to tie the crop into bundles and hang them under the eaves or in a well-ventilated shed rather than leaving it spread out in the field for a long time. Airflow matters more than sunlight. When tying black soybean into bundles, do not make them too thick. If the inside does not dry, mold develops. It is better to make several bundles each about as thick as one hand can grip.

### 12-3. Threshing – Methods Without Tools

| Method | Tool | Procedure | Efficiency |
|---|---|---|---|
| Treading in a sack | A thick burlap sack | Fill a sack halfway with dry bean stalks, tie the mouth shut, and tread on it with your feet. Tread about ten times, then shake it to shift the stalks' position, and repeat | The easiest method for a small amount, with almost no loss |
| Flail | A tool with switches tied to a long handle | Spread the bean stalks thinly on a straw mat and strike down on them from above. Turn them over two or three times | Suited to a medium scale |
| Beating with a stick | A wooden stick | Put the stalks in a sack, or spread them on a mat, and beat them | Substitutes for a flail |
| Rubbing with your feet | None | Spread the stalks on a mat and grind them by twisting your shod feet over them | Suited to a small amount |
| Striking against a board | A board or a barrel | Hold a bundle of bean stalks and strike it against the inner wall of a barrel | The seeds collect inside the barrel, so loss is low |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Whether threshing succeeds depends on drying. However much you beat stalks that are not dry enough, the pods will not open, while beans that are too dry crack. The right moment is when pressing a pod with your hand makes it pop open with a snap, and doing it at midday on a sunny winter day makes it much easier. Black soybean's large seeds tend to resist cracking, but striking them hard with a flail splits the seed coat and lowers their market quality and storability. Treading in a sack is the safest method.

### 12-4. Cleaning – Sorting by Wind

| Method | Principle | Procedure |
|---|---|---|
| Winnowing (raising a breeze) | The seeds are heavy while the husks and debris are light | On a windy day, put the mix in a winnowing tray or bowl, hold it up high, and pour it down in a thin stream. The seeds drop straight down while the chaff blows away |
| Tray winnowing | Shake the tray up and down while flicking it forward | The heavy seeds stay at the back |
| Fanning | One person pours while another fans a breeze | Used on days with no wind |
| Sieving | Sorts by hole size | A coarse sieve filters out stalk fragments, and a fine sieve filters out soil and small seeds |
| Floating in water | Empty husks and insect-damaged seeds are light | Pour the beans into a tub of water and skim off what floats. Dry the recovered beans again that same day |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There are three tricks to winnowing. Choose a time and spot where the wind blows steadily. Pour it down in a thin stream rather than all at once. Repeat two or three times. Since black soybean's seed coat is black and stands out in color from the chaff, it is relatively easy to check the result by eye as you go.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The float-in-water method needs one precaution with black soybean. Anthocyanins leach out of the black seed coat into the water, turning it red and fading the color slightly. The nutrient loss is not large, but soaking it too long is wasteful. Soak it only briefly, quickly skim off what floats, and take the rest out and dry it right away. Do not use this method for beans meant for seed. Soaking in water starts the physiology of germination, and drying it in that state lowers germination capacity.

## 13. Seed Saving

| Item | Method |
|---|---|
| Selecting seed-saving plants | Mark plants in the field in advance that show no disease symptoms, carry many pods, have not lodged, and filled their seed early |
| Harvest timing | Harvest after the pods have turned fully brown and the seed coat has become a deep black |
| Separation | Thresh it separately. Keep it from mixing with the food-use crop |
| Sorting | Discard seeds with a pale or blotchy seed coat, wrinkled seeds, and small seeds |
| Drying | Moisture 12-13%. It splits with a snap when bitten |
| Storage | In an airtight container, in a cool, dark place. Use a separate container from the food-use beans |
| Storage period | 3 years airtight at room temperature. Germination rate drops with each year |
| Renewal | Every 3-4 years, obtain seed from another area and mix in some of it |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The trait especially worth watching for when saving black soybean seed is speed of seed fill. Black soybean's biggest risk is failing to finish filling its seeds before frost, so if you select seed from the plants in the same field that ripen earlier than the others and keep doing this, after a few years you will have produced a line tuned to your area's frost timing. This is the same work that seed companies do as breeding, and the longer a disaster lasts, the more this accumulated work pays off.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The method is simple. In mid-October, walk the field and tie a thread or piece of cloth around any plant whose leaves turn yellow and whose pods turn brown before the others. At the same time, also look for plants with many pods that have not lodged. At harvest, gather only the marked plants separately, thresh them separately, and store them separately. Repeating this every year makes the line a little earlier each time.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Use seed-coat color as the standard for sorting. Seeds with a pale or blotchy color have not finished filling, so their germination capacity is lower and there is a chance the trait will be passed on. A deep, glossy black, and a vivid green when split open inside, are the marks of good black soybean seed.

| Seed storage duration and germination rate |
|---|
| Harvest year: 90% or higher |
| After 1 year: 80-90% |
| After 2 years: 60-80% |
| After 3 years: 40-60% |
| After year 4: Drops sharply |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Repeating self-saved seed lets seed-borne diseases quietly accumulate. Anthracnose, purple seed stain, and mosaic virus all carry over to the next generation through the seed. Culling seeds with visible blotches can prevent a good deal of this, but the difficulty is that blotches are hard to see against a black seed coat. Check them under bright light, and if you are still uneasy, obtain seed from a neighboring village or a different valley every 3-4 years and mix in some of it.
