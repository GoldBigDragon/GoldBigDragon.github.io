## Introduction
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Lentil (Lens culinaris) is an annual legume whose seed is a convex disc shaped like a lens, native to the Fertile Crescent of southwest Asia, and belongs with wheat, barley, and pea to the oldest group of cultivated crops. Its greatest strength is cooking that needs almost no fuel. Dry lentils need no soaking, and red lentils, hulled and split, fall apart like porridge in 10-15 minutes, so cooking and processing C is the highest value among legumes. Water consumption is also S, with total evapotranspiration of 250 mm, less than half that of soybean. Its overall grade is C (41.9 points, 89th of 183 valid species), with short term D (40.5), long term C (43.2), and supplementary food SS (63.4 points). Calories per area are D, at 150 kg per 1,000㎡ and 548 kcal per ㎡, only 0.21 of the self-sufficiency line, and domestic cultivation spread is C, grown only on a trial basis in one or two provinces. Lentils sold in shops for eating are hulled or heat-treated and do not germinate, so securing seed that can be planted and multiplying it over several years by saving your own is the precondition for putting this crop into a plan.

> **In a disaster, fuel runs out as fast as food. The difference between a bean that needs an hour of fire at every meal and one that needs 10 minutes adds up over a year to a heap of firewood.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The lentil seed is flat and only 2-3 mm thick, the thinnest among legumes, so heat has a short distance to travel to the center and the thin seed coat lets water in at once. Micronutrients S is the basis of the supplementary grade SS, with plenty of iron and folate, and it matters that being easy to cook it actually gets eaten often. Calories per area D carries over into land-preparation and digging energy D, so filling a field with lentils alone is a loss in calorie terms. ## 2. Plant Overview
| Item | Content |
|:---|:---|
| Classification | Genus Lens of the legume family, annual. Native to southwest Asia (today's Turkey and Syria) and self-pollinating |
| Leaves, stem | Pinnately compound leaves with 4-7 pairs of leaflets, the tip turning into a short tendril so plants hook onto one another. The stem is slender and upright, small and delicate at 30-45 cm tall |
| Roots, flowers | A straight taproot goes deep and tiny nodules the size of a millet to a grain of rice form on the fine roots. Flowers are white or pale purple, about 5 mm across |
| Pods, seeds | Flat and short with 1-2 seeds, set low within 10 cm of the ground. The seed is a convex disc |

> **Every practical advantage of lentil comes from the thinness of the seed. Heat has a short way to the center so it cooks fast, and water soaks in at once so it needs no soaking.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The growing period is short, 100-110 days, so there is no time to make up for a mistake, and ripening is uneven. Per 100 g of dried seed: 365 kcal, protein 22.64 g (24.8% of calories), fat 1-2 g, dietary fiber 10-30 g, sugars 1.56 g, iron about 7 mg, potassium about 900 mg, magnesium about 100 mg, and it is very rich in folate, and being low in fat it goes rancid slowly. The protein is rich in lysine and slightly short in methionine, so eaten with grain, which is the opposite, each fills the other's gap. Adding something sour (vitamin C) such as vinegar or kimchi greatly raises iron absorption.

## 3. Growing Environment and Varieties
| Condition | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Temperature | Germination 4℃ or above (optimum 18-22℃). Growth 5-28℃ (optimum 15-20℃), and seed set worsens above 25℃. Seed set 15-27℃. If 25℃ or above lasts a few days at flowering, flowers drop and pods do not set. It withstands down to -5℃ |
| Water, soil | Well-drained sandy loam to loam (avoid clay and ground where water collects), pH 5.5-8.0 (optimum 6.0-7.5), liking neutral to slightly alkaline. Beds 15-20 cm or higher (besides drainage, to keep muddy splash from settling mold on the lower pods) |

> **Lentil is hard to place in our climate because two things coincide. Above 25℃ seed set collapses, and if rain continues at ripening disease explodes. Our summer brings both at once.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There are three growing types that ease this constraint. Highland spring cultivation, at 400 m above sea level or higher, is sown from mid March to early April and harvested in June-July, and is the most stable. Late-summer cultivation on flat land is sown from late August to early September and harvested in November-December, with the risk that ripening meets the cold. Southern overwintering cultivation, on the southern coast, is sown from mid to late October and harvested the following May-June, with -5℃ as the limit. Since seed is precious, for the first few years plant a little of each of the three types to see which survives on that ground.

| Type | Characteristics | Cooking time |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Brown lentil | The most common, whole with the hull, and it keeps its shape. All-purpose, and suited to storage and seed | 20-25 minutes |
| Green lentil | Large seeds with a firm texture. Greens-style dishes, dressed dishes, stir-fries | 25-30 minutes |
| Red and yellow lentil | A processed form, hulled and split, that falls apart for porridge and soup and suits the sick, the elderly, and children | 10-15 minutes |

> **Red lentils and yellow lentils are not varieties but processed forms. They are hulled and split in half and will never germinate. If you plant red lentils bought in a shop, nothing comes up.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;When stockpiling, separate what you will eat from what you will plant. Seed to plant must be brown or green lentils with an intact hull, and since even these may have lost germination through heat treatment or fumigation, always run a germination test after obtaining them. Store with the hull on and process only as much as will be eaten.

## 4. Field Preparation and Sowing
| Item | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Making the field | Plow 20-30 cm deep, and because the seed is small and big clods make germination uneven, rub the surface several times with a rake or board to level it finely. Beds 15-20 cm or higher, drainage ditches 30 cm or deeper. On acidic soil (below pH 6.0) apply dolomitic lime 3-4 weeks before sowing (or substitute roasted, ground shells and eggshells or wood ash) |
| Fertilization (1,000㎡) | Phosphorus 20-30 kg (all as basal fertilizer, more than ten times the nitrogen, supplied with bone meal and roasted fish bone powder). Holding back nitrogen is the skill: too much makes only stems and leaves lush so they cover each other, blocks airflow so rust spreads, and reduces pods. Wood ash (100-150 g per ㎡) and urine diluted 10 times (a very little when early growth is poor) |
| Nodule inoculation | Coat seed lightly moistened with water with topsoil 5-15 cm deep from a field that grew lentil, pea, fava bean, or vetch with good nodules, and plant at once. Red nodules on the roots of wild vetch also work. Soil from a soybean or chickpea field is useless because the bacterial species differs |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Lentil nodules are small, so wash off the soil and scan the fine roots in a bright place, splitting them with the tip of a knife. With 10 or more per plant, inoculation went well; almost none means failure, so supplement a little nitrogen when leaves are yellow. Fixation per hectare is 60-100 kg under good conditions and 15-35 kg if poor, and 15-25 kg remains in the stalks and leaves, which are thin and few so easy to return to the field as they are. Do not dig up the roots, and burn only diseased residue.

| Sowing | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Seed preparation | The germination test comes first: wrap 20 seeds in a wet cloth, leave a few days, and count the sprouts. More than half is usable, and with only three or four it is better to eat them. Discard cracked or discolored seeds (split seeds do not germinate), and put the seed in a sieve and rinse with water to wash off dust. Soak 12-24 hours only if the soil is dry (the seed coat is thin and goes soft if soaked long) |
| Method | Depth 2-5 cm, seed spacing 8-10 cm, row spacing 30-40 cm, seeding rate 5-6 kg per 1,000㎡. Because the seed is small, drilling in rows is more efficient than sowing single seeds, and planting a little densely within the row lets plants lean on each other and cover the ground quickly to suppress weeds. Coat seed with fine wood ash to keep off ants, and cover the rows thinly with straw to keep off birds |

## 5. Water Management and Cultivation Management
| Period | Water management |
|:---|:---|
| Germination | The seed is planted shallow, so keep the surface moist for about ten days after sowing |
| Early growth | Leave it to natural rainfall (dryness makes the roots spread deep) |
| Ripening | Stop irrigation (too wet causes shattering and mold) |
| When rain continues | Dig drainage ditches (lower pods that touch the soil rot) |

> **Lentil is only 40 cm tall with slender stems, the legume weakest against weeds. The growing period is short, so once pressed down there is no time to recover. Weeding in the first month almost decides the yield.**

| Management | Method |
|:---|:---|
| Weeding | 1st at 2 weeks after emergence (while weeds are as small as threads), and the 2nd at 4-5 weeks is the last; do not enter the field after flowering (stems break). The fine roots are in the surface layer, so lay the hoe flat and scrape only the top 1-2 cm, and pull by hand close to the plant |
| Mulching, support | Spreading straw or hulls 2-3 cm thick stops muddy splash and saves water (cover after germination has finished since sprouts struggle to push through, and pull some back in the rainy season). Stakes are not needed, but in a place with frequent wind and rain run a line around the field at knee height |

## 6. Pests, Diseases, and Nutrient Problems
| Pest or disease | Symptoms and conditions | Control without chemicals |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Rust | Reddish-brown powdery clumps on leaves and stems, and leaves dry and drop as it spreads. The worst disease, spreading when humid and poorly ventilated, and it moves fast | Space rows widely for airflow, hold back nitrogen, and remove diseased leaves. If it shows on only a few plants early, pull them out whole and burn them. Do not enter the field after rain |
| Fusarium wilt | At flowering, wilting from the lower leaves and whole plants dry and die. Survives for years in the soil as dormant spores | Rotation of 3 years or more is the only measure. Save seed from survivors |
| Gray mold, anthracnose | Gray mold on pods and stems (when rain continues at ripening) / black spots on leaves and stems (humid) | Raise the beds, straw mulch, harvest early / seed selection, rotation, airflow |
| Aphids, cutworms | Cluster on new shoots and flower stalks, suck sap, and carry viruses / cut young stems at the base at night | Knock them off with a strong spray of water and use soapy water (5 g of soap per 1 L of water, dilute because the leaves are thin), and protect ladybugs / dig up the soil around cut plants in the morning to catch larvae |
| Lentil weevil | Lays eggs on pods in the field and eats the seed in storage | Dry completely and seal for storage (see section 9) |

> **Rust is a disease you meet wherever lentil is grown, and resistance is heritable. Choosing and carrying on the plants that held out to the end in a diseased field is the surest measure when you have no chemicals.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Bordeaux mixture is made by dissolving 400 g of copper sulfate and 400 g of quicklime separately in 100 L of water and mixing, used at once on rust and fungal diseases. Fine ash dusted on leaves on a dewy morning suppresses mold and drives off ants.

| Symptom | Cause and remedy |
|:---|:---|
| Whole leaf pale yellow and plant small | Nitrogen deficiency (poor nodules are the most common cause). First dig up the roots and check the nodules, and if there are none and it is early, give a very little urine diluted 10 times. Look over the field closely once at 3-4 weeks after sowing |
| Slow growth and purplish leaves | Phosphorus deficiency. Bone meal and roasted fish bone powder |
| Flowers open but pods do not set | Boron deficiency, or heat at flowering. Compost of mixed materials, and if heat is the cause adjust the sowing time next year |

## 7. Intercropping and Crop Rotation
| Compatibility | Companions |
|:---|:---|
| Good | Barley, wheat, oats (planted between grain rows, the straight stems give lentil something to lean on and they share nitrogen, and since harvest times are similar they can be cut and threshed together and separated with a sieve and wind, but yield halves under 40% shade so space the rows widely), potato (disease does not overlap and lentil is harvested first), Chinese cabbage and radish group (use the nitrogen lentil leaves behind) |
| Fair | Leafy vegetables (they grow together early on, but lentil is weak against weeds and loses the competition) |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The continuous-cropping injury is accumulation of Fusarium wilt and root rot, so rest the ground for 3 years or more. An example rotation is year 1 lentil → year 2 grain (barley, wheat, oats) → year 3 potato or sweet potato → year 4 Chinese cabbage and radish group or green manure → lentil again. Since the area needed is small, simply moving to a narrow spot in a corner of the field each year also counts as rotation.

## 8. Harvest and Seed Saving
| Harvest | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Timing | 100-110 days after sowing, when lower leaves have yellowed and dropped and two thirds of the plant has turned brown. The compromise is when shaking the third pod from the bottom makes the seeds rattle (it ripens from the bottom up and ripe pods burst easily). If rain is forecast, harvest even if not fully ripe and finish drying in shade |
| Method | Cut the stem just above the ground (the pods are set low). Even the vibration of the sickle makes seeds pop out, so move the cut bundles onto a cloth or mat and catch the falling seeds. Spread to dry as soon as cut |
| Threshing, winnowing | The easiest threshing among legumes. Stamp on a tightly woven sack, spread on a mat and rub round with a shod foot, or rub by hand for small amounts. Sieve out soil and bits with a fine sieve, and sort by rolling down a tilted board, since the seed is a convex disc. Winnow in a gentler wind than for other beans, pouring in a thin stream two or three times, since the seeds are small and light |
| Drying | After threshing spread thin on a mat in the sun for 1-2 days (the seeds are thin so dry fast, and crack if left long in strong sun). Moisture 12% or below |

| Seed saving | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Selection | Mark in the field in advance plants with no rust symptoms that survived to the end in a field with Fusarium wilt and carry many pods. Also pick plants that set pods in weather above 25℃ and those that ripened early before the rainy season, and a few years of selection makes a line that holds up in that field. It is self-pollinating, so do not worry about isolation distance |
| Harvest, sorting | Harvest after the pods have turned completely brown but before they burst, and thresh separately and rub by hand so the seeds are not damaged. Discard cracked seeds, spots, discoloration, and small seeds, and dry slowly in shade to moisture of 12% or below. Germination is 70-80% after 2 years |

> **Lentil is a crop for which seed that can be planted is very hard to get again in Korea. Most lentils sold for food in shops do not germinate. Seed saving is not an option but a precondition for growing this crop on.**

## 9. Storage
| Step | Method |
|:---|:---|
| Bean weevil control | Complete sealing matters most (oxygen falls and the insects die): fill the container to leave no empty space and use several small containers rather than one big one. Mix in sifted wood ash at 2-5% of the bean volume (the seeds are small so the ash works in evenly; rinse before eating), and oil coating is 3-5 mL of cooking oil per 1 kg of beans (not for seed) |
| Containers, checking | Thick plastic lasts 1-2 years, and rats chew through it and the small seeds leak out, so use glass jars or metal tins. Put an ear to the container: rustling means insects, a musty smell is mold. Stored with the hull on, the seed coat acts as a shield, and hulled lentils store less well |

## 10. Cooking and Precautions
| Component | What it does | How to remove |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Trypsin inhibitors | Block protein-digesting enzymes, causing abdominal discomfort and lower protein use | 20-30 minutes in boiling water is enough (content is a third or less of soybean) |
| Lectins | Stick to the gut lining and hinder absorption, but small amounts are harmless | Easily destroyed by heat, and unlike kidney bean the toxicity is very weak |
| Oligosaccharides, tannins | Gut fermentation causes gas (low content among legumes) / the astringent component of the seed coat hinders iron absorption | Discard the boiling water / nearly gone once the hull is removed |

> **What actually harms people with lentil is not the bean itself but beans stored wrongly until moldy. If it smells musty or a bluish powder shows on the surface, discard it without regret.**

| Treatment | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Basic | Rinse in water before cooking and skim off what floats. No soaking is needed (at 2-3 mm thick, water soaks in at once); with the hull on, 20-30 minutes in boiling water softens them while keeping their shape, and hulled and split ones fall apart into porridge in 10-15 minutes |
| Saving fuel | Put them with rice into rice or porridge and no separate fire is needed (hulled ones cook faster than rice, so add them later). Retained-heat cooking is boiling 5 minutes, turning off the fire, and wrapping the pot in bedding or a straw box for 1 hour (safe because there is no strong toxic component). Roasted and ground flour is mixed into water to drink |
| Hulling and splitting | Moisten dry lentils briefly, pound very lightly in a mortar to remove only the hulls (without crushing the seeds), blow them off in a winnowing basket, and split in half; cooking time falls from 20 to 10 minutes, but storage worsens, so do only as much as will be eaten in a month or two |
| Sprouting, processing | Soak 8 hours, drain, cover with a wet cloth for 2-3 days, and sprouts come, forming vitamin C and lowering phytic acid so they are easier to digest (a very quick blanching also works). Roasted and ground lentil flour keeps 3-6 months sealed. A batter ground and left overnight to ferment rises and is easier to digest |

> **The value of lentil is that if you put it in with rice when cooking, protein reaches the table without building a separate fire. No other bean can do this.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Lentils are low in oligosaccharides so cause less gas, and hulled red lentils are especially light on the stomach, so when you must feed beans to someone long starved, sick, elderly, or a child, lentil porridge is the safest choice.

## 11. Annual Work Schedule
| Month | Work |
|:---|:---|
| Jan-Feb | Check storage and test seed germination. In February, plan the field, apply lime, turn the compost, and prepare for highland spring sowing |
| Mar-Apr | In March, highland spring sowing (from mid month) and fine leveling of the soil. In April, 1st weeding and thinning (weed control in the first month decides the yield) |
| May-June | In May, 2nd weeding, flowering, and checking nodules. In June, pod filling, watching for rust, and irrigation (secure airflow and do not enter the field after rain) |
| July-Aug | In July, highland harvest, hanging to dry, and threshing. In August, winnowing, storage, and late-summer sowing at the end of the month (flat land) |
| Sep-Oct | In September, weeding the late-summer crop and clearing the field. In October, flowering and pod filling of the late-summer crop, and southern overwintering sowing |
| Nov-Dec | In November, harvest the late-summer crop (harvest even if not fully ripe when frost gets hard), dry, and store. In December, processing (only as much as will be eaten in a month or two), sprouted vegetables, and planning next year |

> **Lentil is a crop that, if you secure seed that can be planted and test its germination, plant it in three growing types, weed during the first month, and harvest when the third pod from the bottom rattles, gives protein with no separate fire just by being put in with rice.**
