## 3. Growing Conditions

### 3-1. Temperature and Light

| Condition | Limit | Optimum | Outside the Range |
|---|---|---|---|
| Germination temperature | 4°C or above | 18-22°C | Germinates slowly even at low temperatures |
| Growth temperature | 5-28°C | 15-20°C | Fruit-set worsens above 25°C |
| Fruiting temperature | 15-27°C | 17-22°C | Range of 12°C |
| Heat during flowering | Several days at 25°C or above | - | Flowers drop and pods fail to set |
| Cold hardiness | Down to -5°C | - | Young plants survive a mild winter |
| Sunlight | 6 hours a day or more | 8 hours a day or more | Yield drops by half at 40% shade |

> **Lentils are hard to fit into South Korea's climate because two problems overlap. Above 25°C, fruit-set collapses, and continuous rain during ripening triggers disease outbreaks. A South Korean summer brings both at once.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There are three ways to work around this constraint. First, high-altitude uplands: in a cool location above 400m elevation, you can sow in spring and harvest in June or July. This is the spot in South Korea that suits lentils best. Second, late-summer sowing: sow in late August to early September to let the plant grow through the cooling autumn, and harvest in November or December, though ripening then runs the risk of hitting cold weather before the seed fills out properly. Third, in the south, sow in fall to let the plant overwinter and harvest in early summer of the following year. A Cold hardiness grade of A opens up this possibility, but since -5°C is the limit, it is only reliable along the southern coast.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There is no way around trying all three and finding what suits your land. Since seed is precious for this crop, it is safer to first split a small amount across all three planting schedules and see which one survives.

### 3-2. Soil and Water

| Condition | Limit | Optimum | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Soil texture | Sandy soil to loam | Well-drained sandy loam to loam | Avoid clay soil and land where water pools |
| pH | 5.5-8.0 | 6.0-7.5 | Prefers neutral to slightly alkaline |
| Effective soil depth | 40cm or more | 60cm or more | The taproot goes in deep |
| Seasonal evapotranspiration | - | About 250mm | Among the lowest of the legumes |
| Flooding | Roots are damaged if submerged for a day or more | - | Very vulnerable to waterlogging |
| Raised bed height | 15cm or more | 20cm or more | Keeps lower pods from touching the soil |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Raising the bed for lentils is not only about drainage. Because the pods hang close to the ground, rain splashes muddy water onto them, and mold starts right there. Raising the bed and laying a thin layer of straw so soil does not splash up greatly reduces this damage. Straw mulch also helps conserve water, but if it is piled on thick and stays constantly damp, disease actually increases, so lay it thin and remove part of it before the monsoon season (jangma, late June to July in South Korea).

## 4. Varieties

### 4-1. Classification by Color and Processing Form

| Type | Features | Cooking Time | Uses |
|---|---|:---:|---|
| Brown lentil | The most common. Unhulled, and holds its shape | 20-25 min | All-purpose. Soups, braises, mixed into rice |
| Green lentil | Large seeds, firm texture | 25-30 min | Namul (seasoned vegetable side dishes), dressed salads, stir-fries |
| Red/yellow lentil | Hulled and split | 10-15 min | Porridge, soup, curry. Breaks down completely |
| Black lentil | Small and firm | 20-25 min | Especially high in protein |

> **Red and yellow lentils are not varieties but a processed form. Because they are hulled and split in half, they never germinate. Planting store-bought red lentils in a field produces nothing at all.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This distinction must be understood without fail. When stockpiling lentils for disaster preparedness, you must think separately about the portion for eating and the portion for planting, and the planting portion must be brown or green lentils with an intact hull. Even those may have lost their ability to germinate through heat treatment or fumigation during distribution, so once obtained, always run a small-scale germination test. Wrap twenty seeds in a damp cloth, leave them for a few days, and count how many sprout.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;From a cooking standpoint, it is ideal to have both on hand. Unhulled brown lentils store well and can also serve as seed, while hulled, split red lentils use the least fuel. Since stored brown lentils can be pounded in a mortar and winnowed to make the red-lentil form whenever needed, the rule is to store them unhulled and process only as much as you will eat.

### 4-2. Selection Criteria by Situation

| Situation | Priority | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| For use as seed | Brown or green lentils with intact hulls | Split ones do not germinate |
| Fuel is scarce | Process into hulled, split form | Cooks in 10-15 minutes |
| Long-term storage | Unhulled brown lentils | The seed coat acts as a protective barrier |
| The sick, elderly, or children | Red lentil porridge | Breaks down completely, easy to digest |
| A field in the highlands | Any type | The spot in South Korea best suited to lentils |
| A field where disease is going around | Save seed from surviving plants | Selects for resistance to rust and wilt |

## 5. Field Preparation and Fertilizer - Root-Nodule Bacteria

### 5-1. How Nitrogen Fixation Happens

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;78% of the air is nitrogen. An endless supply floats over everyone's head, yet plants cannot use it. Atmospheric nitrogen is a molecule in which two nitrogen atoms are locked together by a triple bond, and breaking that bond takes enormous energy. Breaking it industrially to make ammonia is the Haber-Bosch process, and the world's nitrogen fertilizer comes from it. It requires high temperature, high pressure, a catalyst, and vast amounts of fossil fuel.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Root-nodule bacteria pull off this same feat at ordinary temperature and pressure. Using an enzyme called nitrogenase, they break the triple bond and turn nitrogen into ammonia. There is a cost, though. The reaction needs a great deal of energy, and the bacteria cannot photosynthesize on their own. So they live inside the roots of legumes, receive the sugars the plant makes through photosynthesis, and hand over the fixed nitrogen in return. It is a trade relationship.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The bacterium that pairs with lentil is Rhizobium leguminosarum bv. viciae. It is the same species used by pea, fava bean, and wild vetch, and different from the ones that live in symbiosis with soybean or chickpea. This is a major practical advantage. An inoculant can be obtained from the soil of a field that grew peas, or from the roots of wild vetch, common in spring fields.

| Stage | What Happens |
|---|---|
| 1. Signal exchange | Lentil roots release flavonoids, and bacteria in the soil respond to that signal by releasing Nod factors |
| 2. Infection | Root hairs curl inward to wrap around the bacteria, which then travel into the root through an infection thread |
| 3. Nodule formation | Cells inside the root divide, the nodule swells, and bacteria settle inside it |
| 4. Nitrogen fixation | Nitrogenase works inside the nodule, converting atmospheric nitrogen into ammonia |
| 5. Distribution | Ammonia is converted into amino acids and transported to the stems, leaves, and pods |
| 6. Senescence | Upon entering the ripening stage, the nodule loses function and returns to the soil along with the root |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Nitrogenase is extremely sensitive to oxygen and loses function the instant oxygen reaches it. So the inside of the nodule is filled with a red pigment protein called leghemoglobin, which captures oxygen and keeps the oxygen concentration around the enzyme extremely low. It works on the same principle that makes human blood red, and this pigment is what makes the inside of a split-open nodule look red or pink. This fact matters a great deal in practice: red color is visible proof that the nodule is fixing nitrogen right now.

### 5-2. How to Check Whether Nodules Have Formed

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Whether or not you inoculated, checking whether nodules actually formed is something you must do. Without checking, you cannot know the cause of a nitrogen shortage, and you will repeat the same mistake the following year. Because the lentil's growing season is short, there is even less time to notice a mistake and fix it.

| Step | Details |
|---|---|
| 1 | The best time is around when flowering begins, about 6 weeks after sowing |
| 2 | Choose two or three plants that look healthy |
| 3 | Do not pull them out; dig around the plant widely with a spade and lift it out with the soil intact |
| 4 | Put it in a bucket of water and gently wash the soil off |
| 5 | Count the nodules on the taproot and the thicker fine roots |
| 6 | Split a few nodules open with a fingernail and check the color inside |

| Observation | Meaning | Action |
|---|---|---|
| 10 or more nodules per plant | Inoculation succeeded | Leave as is |
| Inside is red or pink when split | It is fixing nitrogen | Good condition |
| Inside is white or pale green when split | Nodules formed but are not fixing nitrogen | Check soil acidity, waterlogging, and phosphorus deficiency |
| Inside is brown or black when split | Already senescent or dead | Normal if ripening, a problem if flowering |
| Almost no nodules | Inoculation failed | If leaves are yellowing, apply a small amount of nitrogen |

> **This one habit - digging up a root, splitting a nodule open, and checking for red color - is the most reliable signal of whether a legume crop will succeed or fail in a world without nitrogen fertilizer.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Lentil nodules are small. Since the plant itself is small, its nodules run from millet-seed to rice-grain size, and if you expect pea-sized nodules like those on soybean, it is easy to wrongly conclude there are none. You have to wash off the soil and examine the fine roots one by one in bright light to see them. When splitting them open, a knife tip works better than a fingernail.

### 5-3. Where to Get an Inoculant

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Commercial inoculants are products that carry live bacteria in peat or clay. They cannot be obtained in a disaster. However, the bacterium lentil uses is a common species shared with pea, fava bean, and wild vetch, so there are several ways to obtain an inoculant.

| Method | Procedure | Effect | Caution |
|---|---|---|---|
| Coat seed with soil from a field with the same bacterial strain | Scoop soil from a depth of 5-15cm of topsoil in a field where lentil, pea, fava bean, or vetch grew and formed good nodules. Moisten the seed slightly with water, coat it with this soil, then sow right away | Most reliable and costs nothing | Soil from a diseased field also carries pathogens along with it |
| Wild vetch roots | In spring, dig up vetch from fields and field edges and check its root nodules. If the nodules are red, use that soil and root together | The most realistic option in areas with no history of growing lentils | Fixation efficiency may be lower than a strain bred for cultivated crops |
| Using the nodules themselves | Collect nodules from dug-up roots, crush them, mix into water, then soak the seed in that water and take it out | Highest bacterial density | Must be used the same day the nodules are dug up |
| Scattering soil over the field | Scatter the collected soil along the row to be sown and till it in together | The bacteria spread through the soil | About one shovelful per 10m² is the standard |
| Leaving root residue | At harvest, cut only the stems and leave the roots in the field | The bacteria remain in the soil until the following year | The least labor-intensive way to maintain it |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There is something you must observe without exception when inoculating. Root-nodule bacteria are very vulnerable to direct sunlight, drying, and heat. A few minutes of UV exposure kills a large share of them, and drying kills them even faster. Do the work of coating seed with soil in the shade, and sow the coated seed right away without spreading it out in the sun. Sowing within 30 minutes is best, and finishing within a few hours at the latest. One more thing: seed disinfection and inoculation cannot be done together. Coating disinfectant-treated seed with soil kills the bacteria. If you disinfected with a hot-water treatment, let it cool completely before inoculating.

| Conditions That Cause Inoculation Failure | Reason |
|---|---|
| Soil pH 5.5 or below | The bacteria cannot survive |
| A field given plenty of nitrogen fertilizer | The plant feels no need to trade and reduces nodule formation on its own |
| A field where water pools | An aerobic bacterium; it dies without oxygen |
| A field short on phosphorus | Nitrogen fixation needs a great deal of energy, and phosphorus is used in that metabolism |
| Deficiency in molybdenum, iron, or cobalt | These are components of nitrogenase |
| Drying inoculated seed in the sun | UV and drying kill the bacteria |
| Using soil from a soybean or chickpea field | The bacterial species differs and will not infect lentil |
