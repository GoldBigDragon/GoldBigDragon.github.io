## 5. Field Preparation and Fertilizer - Nitrogen Balance and Fertilization

### 5-4. The Pitfall of Nitrogen Fixation

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The idea that planting beans improves the soil has hardened into farming common sense. It is half right and half wrong. Without understanding this point precisely, a field where legumes were planted year after year can actually end up more depleted.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Let's work through the numbers. When lentils grow well, 1,000m² yields 150kg of dried seed. Lentil seed is 22.6% protein, and dividing protein by 6.25 gives the nitrogen content. 22.6 ÷ 6.25 is 3.62, meaning about 3.6% of the seed weight is pure nitrogen. The nitrogen contained in 150kg is about 5.4kg, which converts to 54kg per hectare.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Meanwhile, the nitrogen lentil root nodules fix, under good conditions, is 60 to 100kg per hectare. Because the plant is small, the absolute amount is less than fava bean or soybean, but since the amount of seed removed is also smaller, the balance is actually not bad. Placing what leaves and what comes in side by side looks like this.

| Item | Nitrogen per Hectare | Direction |
|---|---|---|
| Nodule fixation (good conditions) | 60-100kg | In |
| Nodule fixation (poor conditions) | 15-35kg | In |
| Removed with the seed | 45-60kg | Out |
| Nitrogen remaining in stems and leaves | 15-25kg | Stays if returned to the field; leaves if burned or hauled away |
| Nitrogen remaining in roots and nodules | 8-15kg | Usually stays in the field |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;What the table reveals is the importance of inoculation. If inoculation succeeds and fixation reaches 80kg, nitrogen is left over even after the seed is removed. If inoculation fails and fixation stops at 20kg, the field takes a clear loss as 50kg leaves with the seed. For lentils, inoculation is not only a matter of yield but also a matter of the field's sustainability.

> **Legumes revive the soil not because they fix nitrogen, but because they leave part of that fixed nitrogen behind in the field, held in the stems and leaves. Haul those stems and leaves away, and the bean becomes a crop that strips the soil instead.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Lentil's stems and leaves are small in quantity and thin, making them easy to return to the field. The stalks left after threshing are thin enough to crumble by hand, and if scattered over the field, they break down within a few weeks. They are not thick and tough like soybean stalks, so there is not even a need to cut them up. There is really no reason not to return them. The one exception is diseased residue, which should be picked out and burned. Lentil's rust and wilt disease survive in residue and carry over to the following year.

| Principle | Details |
|---|---|
| Stems and leaves always go back to the field | Scatter the stalks and leaves left after threshing directly over the field |
| Do not burn them | Burning releases nitrogen as gas, leaving only potassium and phosphorus behind as ash |
| Do not dig up the roots | Cut close to the ground and leave the roots |
| Pick out diseased residue | Rust and wilt disease carry over through residue. Burn only this |
| Take inoculation seriously | Failed inoculation is both a yield loss and a nitrogen drain |
| If fed to livestock, return the manure | If the stems and leaves were fed to livestock, be sure to put that animal's manure back on the same field |

### 5-5. Field Preparation

| Item | Method | Under Disaster Conditions |
|---|---|---|
| Tilling | Till to a depth of 20-30cm and level evenly | Because the seed is small, fine leveling of the soil matters especially |
| Raised bed | 15-20cm high or more | Along with drainage, keeps lower pods from touching the soil |
| Drainage ditch | 30cm deep or more | Vulnerable to waterlogging |
| Acidity correction | pH 6.0-7.5 | Do it 3-4 weeks before sowing |
| Lime | Dolomitic lime for acidic soil (pH below 6.0) | Substitute burnt, crushed shells or eggshells, or wood ash |
| Organic matter | Well-rotted compost, 1,000-1,500kg/1,000m² | Apply as base fertilizer |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Fine leveling of the soil matters more for lentils than for other crops. The seed is small and sown shallow, at 2-5cm, so if the soil clods are large, seed falls into the gaps between clods and either dries out or ends up buried too deep. After tilling, the surface should be raked or dragged with a board repeatedly to break it into fine soil, so that germination comes out even.

### 5-6. Fertilizer Amounts

| Fertilizer | Per 1,000m² | Per m² | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Nitrogen | 0-2kg | 0-2g | Almost unnecessary. Too much suppresses nitrogen fixation |
| Phosphorus | 20-30kg | 20-30g | Applied entirely as base fertilizer. Essential for root development and flowering |
| Potassium | 3-5kg | 3-5g | Applied entirely as base fertilizer |
| Compost | 1,000-1,500kg | 1-1.5kg | Use fully rotted compost |
| Lime | Only for acidic soil | - | 3-4 weeks before sowing |

> **Going easy on nitrogen for lentils is not thrift but technique. Too much makes the stems and leaves grow lush and overshadow each other, blocks airflow so rust spreads, and actually reduces the pods.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Top-dressing is, in principle, unnecessary. However, because the growing season is short, noticeably poor growth early on means losing the whole year outright. If, around the third or fourth true leaf, the leaves overall look pale yellow and growth stalls, dilute urine tenfold with water and pour a very small amount into the soil between plants. Keep it off the leaves, and if this window is missed, applying it later has no effect.

### 5-7. Disaster Substitute Fertilizers

| Substitute Resource | Nutrients Supplied | Usage | Caution |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bone meal | Phosphorus, calcium | Burn, crush, and mix into compost | The key to meeting lentil's high phosphorus demand |
| Fish bones and viscera | Phosphorus, nitrogen | Bury and let decompose before use | Buried as-is, animals will dig them up |
| Wood ash | Potassium, phosphorus, calcium | Scatter 100-150g per m² and mix in | Alkaline. Lentil likes slight alkalinity, so there is room to spare |
| Well-rotted compost | Comprehensive | Apply 1-1.5kg per m² as base fertilizer | Under-rotted compost burns roots and carries disease |
| Diluted urine | Nitrogen, potassium | Dilute tenfold with water and pour into the soil between plants | Only a very small amount, and only for early poor growth |
| Shells and eggshells | Calcium | Burn, crush, and scatter | Substitutes for lime. Slow to act |
| Leaf mold | Comprehensive, organic matter | Mix into the soil | Improves water retention |
| Rice husks and rice straw | Potassium, silica | Mulch thinly | Keeps splashed soil from getting on the pods |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;20-30kg of phosphorus is more than ten times the amount of nitrogen. This is because both lentil's root development and the energy metabolism of nitrogen fixation demand phosphorus. Without chemical fertilizer, bone meal and fish bones are, in practice, the only routes to phosphorus. Collecting animal bones, burning them until they crumble easily, then pounding them in a mortar and mixing them into compost releases phosphorus slowly over several years.

## 6. Sowing

### 6-1. Sowing Time

| Planting Schedule | Sowing Time | Harvest Time | Application |
|---|---|---|---|
| Highland spring cultivation | Mid-March to early April | June-July | Elevation 400m or above. The most reliable |
| Late-summer sowing | Late August to early September | November-December | Lowland. Risk of ripening running into cold weather |
| Overwintering cultivation in southern South Korea | Mid-to-late October | May-June the following year | Southern coast. -5°C is the limit |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;All three planting schedules are conditional in South Korea's climate. Lentil physiology is tuned to a Mediterranean climate, and by that standard, South Korean summer is too hot and too humid. If you have access to a highland site, that is the answer; if not, late-summer sowing and southern overwintering both need to be tried.

> **Because seed is precious for this crop, do not bet it all on one planting schedule. Spend the first few years splitting a small amount across all three schedules to find out what survives on your land.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The trick to late-summer sowing is counting backward from the first frost date. Lentil needs 100-110 days, but since temperatures drop in autumn and slow growth down, finishing ripening before the first frost is difficult. Instead, the approach relies on the fact that lentil withstands -5°C, letting the final ripening proceed through light frost. If a hard frost comes before the seed has fully matured, harvest it and finish drying it indoors.

### 6-2. Seed Preparation

| Item | Method | Under Disaster Conditions |
|---|---|---|
| Germination test | Wrap 20 seeds in a damp cloth, leave for a few days, and count how many sprout | The first thing to do with lentil |
| Sorting | Discard seeds that are cracked or discolored | Split seeds do not germinate |
| Washing | Put in a sieve and rinse with water | Washes off dust and debris |
| Hot-water disinfection | Soak in 50-55°C water for 10-15 minutes | Kills seed-borne pathogens |
| Ash treatment | Coat with fine wood ash | Aids disinfection and drying, and helps repel birds and ants |
| Root-nodule inoculation | Coat with soil from a field where a legume with the same bacterial strain was grown | See Section 5-3. Do not combine with disinfection |
| Soaking | 12-24 hours, only when the soil is dry | The seed coat is thin and turns soft if soaked too long |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;For lentil, the germination test is not a formality. Much of the lentil sold in stores for eating is hulled and split, or has gone through heat treatment, and simply will not sprout at all. Finding this out after the whole field has been sown means losing the entire year. Once you obtain seed, the very first thing to do is wrap twenty seeds in a damp cloth, leave them for a few days, and check. If more than half sprout, it is usable; if only three or four sprout, it is better to eat it rather than use it as seed.

### 6-3. Sowing Method

| Item | Standard | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Sowing depth | 2-5cm | The seed is small and cannot push up if sown too deep |
| Seed spacing | 8-10cm | |
| Row spacing | 30-40cm | Ensures airflow |
| Seeding rate | 5-6kg/1,000m² | On the low side among legumes |
| Sowing method | Row seeding or broadcast seeding | The seed is small, so row seeding is more efficient than hill seeding |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Because lentil seed is small, hill seeding one seed at a time is inefficient. Making a shallow furrow, trickling in seed, and covering it with soil - row seeding - is more practical, and if the field is large, broadcasting the seed and lightly raking it in is also used. However, broadcasting makes weeding harder, so for a crop as vulnerable to weeds as lentil, row seeding is generally better.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;A somewhat dense planting density is preferable. Lentil's tendrils are weak, and plants lean on each other for support, so if planted too sparsely, there is nothing to lean on and they lodge. Dense planting lets them support each other and covers the ground quickly, which also suppresses weeds. However, if it is too dense, airflow is blocked and rust spreads, so a suitable arrangement leaves generous row spacing while keeping plants dense within the row.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The threats right after sowing are birds and ants. The seed is small and buried shallow, so ants carry it off. Coating the seed with fine wood ash reduces ants' approach, and covering the sown row with a thin layer of straw guards against both birds and drying out.

## 7. Water Management

| Stage | Water Management | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Sowing to germination | Keep the soil from drying out completely | Buried shallow, so germination stops if the surface dries out |
| Early growth | Rely on natural rainfall | Dryness makes the roots grow deep |
| Flowering to pod filling | Irrigate if drought is severe | Water shortage in this period cuts yield the most |
| Ripening | Stop irrigation | Excess moisture causes shattering and mold |
| During continuous rain | Dig drainage ditches | Lower pods rot if they touch the soil |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;What is distinctive about water management for lentil is that the germination stage and everything after it are opposites. Since the seed is buried shallow, the surface must not dry out during germination, but once the sprout is up, dry conditions make the roots go deep, letting the plant withstand drought later on. The trick is to keep the surface moist for only about ten days after sowing and leave the rest to nature.

| Symptom | Cause | Action |
|---|---|---|
| Leaves fold at midday and open in the evening | A normal water-conserving response | Leave as is |
| Leaves are still folded in the morning | Severe dryness | Irrigate |
| Flowers drop in large numbers | Dryness during flowering, or heat above 25°C | Irrigate and mulch. If heat is the cause, recovery is difficult |
| Pods have set but seeds are small | Water shortage during pod filling | Irrigation in this period directly determines yield |
| Lower leaves yellow and the base of the stem softens | Root rot from excess moisture | Dig drainage ditches |
| Reddish-brown spots appear on leaves and spread | Rust, when it is humid and airflow is poor | Remove diseased leaves and ensure airflow |
| Black blotches appear on lower pods | Muddy water splashed on them and mold took hold | Lay down a thin layer of straw to keep soil from splashing |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Mulch serves two purposes for lentil: conserving water and keeping soil from splashing. A thin layer of straw or rice husks keeps raindrops from splattering soil onto the lower pods, reducing mold damage. However, piling it on thick so the soil stays constantly damp actually increases disease, so lay it thin, around 2-3cm, and remove part of it during the monsoon season to ensure airflow.
