## 3. Growing Conditions

### 3-1. Temperature and Light

| Condition | Limit | Optimum | If outside the range |
|---|---|---|---|
| Germination temperature | Soil temperature of 15°C or higher | 20-25°C | Below 15°C the seed does not germinate and rots |
| Growth temperature | 18-35°C | 25-30°C | Being of tropical origin, it tolerates heat well |
| Fruit-set temperature | 20-30°C | 24-28°C | The range is 10°C |
| Accumulated temperature | Early-maturing 2,800°C, late-maturing 3,600°C | - | If it falls short, frost arrives before ripening is complete |
| Cold hardiness | None | - | A single frost kills it |
| Sunshine | 6 hours or more per day | 8 hours or more per day | The more sunshine, the higher the yield |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;What serves as the standard for deciding when to sow peanuts is not the air temperature but the soil temperature. The temperature at a depth of 5cm in the soil must exceed 15°C. This is because the seed is large and rich in fat, and if it sits in the soil at a low temperature for long, it rots. If you have no soil thermometer, the standard is that the soil no longer feels cold when you put your hand into it in the morning.

> **Accumulated temperature matters for the peanut because its growing period is long, at 130-150 days. Early-maturing varieties need 2,800°C and late-maturing varieties need 3,600°C, and the annual accumulated temperature in central South Korea leaves no great margin to spare. If sown late, the crop cannot accumulate enough and is hit by frost.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;That is why mulching matters in growing peanuts. Plastic film mulch raises the soil temperature, allowing sowing to be brought forward by 2-3 weeks and securing that much more accumulated temperature. If there is no plastic film, covering the ground with black cloth or with dark, decomposed fallen leaves has some effect, and choosing a south-facing slope or a spot that gets less wind also helps.

### 3-2. Soil and Water

| Condition | Limit | Optimum | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Soil texture | Sandy soil to loam | Sandy loam | Clay soil and stony fields are unsuitable |
| pH | 5.5-7.5 | 6.5-7.0 | Strongly acidic soil is unsuitable |
| Calcium | Must be sufficient in the soil | - | The pods absorb it directly from the soil |
| Effective soil depth | 30cm or more | 40cm or more | |
| Evapotranspiration over the crop season | - | About 450mm | Mid-range among legumes |
| Waterlogging | Especially vulnerable to waterlogging during flowering | - | This is when the pegs enter the soil |
| Raised-bed height | 20cm or more | 25cm | Gives both drainage and soft soil |

> **The peanut's liking for sandy ground is not a matter of taste but of structure. The peg has to pierce the soil and enter it, the pod has to grow larger within the soil, and the crop has to be dug out at harvest. All three become easier in crumbly soil.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Conversely, peanuts fail in clay soil or stony fields. The peg cannot pierce the hardened soil, and even if it does, the pod has no room to grow and becomes deformed, and at harvest pods are left behind in the soil and break off. When choosing a field, squeeze a handful of soil: if it does not clump and crumbles apart, the soil suits peanuts, and if it clumps into a lump, it is better to plant another crop. If clay soil is all you have, you can try mixing in sand or rice hulls to make the beds.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The calcium requirement is a condition unique to the peanut. The pods in the soil absorb calcium directly through their shells, and when this calcium is lacking, pods form but the kernels inside do not fill. This is the phenomenon commonly called empty pods. This is why applying lime as base fertilizer is not optional for peanuts.

## 4. Varieties

### 4-1. Classification by Growth Habit

| Type | Characteristics | Harvest | Suitability for disasters |
|---|---|---|---|
| Erect | Stems stand straight. Pods are clustered at the base of the plant | Most pods come up when the plant is pulled | Highest |
| Semi-erect | An intermediate form | Pods come up fairly well | High |
| Creeping | Stems spread sideways. Pods are scattered widely | Many are missed | Low |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The National Institute of Crop Science (NICS), South Korea, has bred a number of varieties since 1982, and they are divided into those for shelled peanuts and those for green peanuts. The Saedeul and Daegwang peanuts are fully erect, early-maturing, large-seeded varieties grown across South Korea; the Namgwang peanut is a semi-erect, large-seeded variety; Sang-an is known as a roasting type; and Jaseon as a green-peanut type. In disaster conditions an erect, early-maturing variety is the sensible choice. Harvest labor is lower, and the short growing period reduces the risk of frost.

### 4-2. Selection Criteria by Situation

| Situation | Priority | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Labor is short | Erect type | The pods come up when the plant is pulled |
| Central South Korea or farther north, where frost comes early | Early-maturing erect type | An accumulated temperature of 2,800°C is enough |
| You plan to press oil | Large-seeded variety | The larger the kernel, the easier the pressing |
| You need food in summer | Green-peanut type | Eaten boiled before full maturity |
| The field is clay soil | Do not plant peanuts | The pegs cannot enter the soil |
| There is no plastic film | Early-maturing variety | The accumulated temperature has to be reached without mulch |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Let us restate the constraint unique to the peanut in choosing a variety. Because seed life is under 1 year, seed must be saved every year, and the saved seed must be stored in its shell. So whichever variety is chosen, keeping that line going without a break matters more than the choice of variety itself. If the seed is lost even for one year, it has to be sourced again from scratch.

## 5. Field Preparation and Fertilizer - Root-Nodule Bacteria and Lime

### 5-1. How Nitrogen Fixation Happens

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;78% of the air is nitrogen. Limitless nitrogen hangs over people's heads, yet plants cannot use it. Nitrogen in the air is a molecule in which two nitrogen atoms are bound tightly by a triple bond, and breaking this bond takes an enormous amount of energy. Industrially, breaking this bond to make ammonia is the Haber-Bosch process, and the world's nitrogen fertilizer comes from it. It requires high temperature, high pressure, a catalyst, and vast amounts of fossil fuel.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Root-nodule bacteria (rhizobia) do this job at room temperature and ordinary pressure. With an enzyme called nitrogenase they break the triple bond and convert nitrogen into ammonia. There is, however, a price. This reaction requires a great deal of energy, and the bacteria cannot photosynthesize on their own. So they live inside the roots of leguminous plants, receive the sugars the plant makes by photosynthesis, and in return hand over the nitrogen they have fixed. It is a trading relationship.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The bacteria that pair with the peanut are a strain within the Bradyrhizobium group that is specialized for the peanut. Because soil from fields that grew other legumes tends not to work well, using soil from a field that grew peanuts is the surest course.

| Stage | What happens |
|---|---|
| 1. Signal exchange | Peanut roots secrete flavonoids, and bacteria in the soil respond to that signal |
| 2. Infection | Unlike other legumes, in the peanut the bacteria enter through cracks at the root hairs |
| 3. Nodule formation | Cells inside the root divide, a round nodule swells, and the bacteria settle inside it |
| 4. Nitrogen fixation | Inside the nodule, nitrogenase operates and converts atmospheric nitrogen into ammonia |
| 5. Distribution | The ammonia is converted into amino acids and carried to the stems, leaves, and pods |
| 6. Senescence | As ripening begins, the nodules lose their function and return to the soil along with the roots |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Nitrogenase is extremely sensitive to oxygen and loses its function the moment oxygen touches it. So the inside of a root nodule is filled with a red pigment protein called leghemoglobin, which holds on to oxygen and keeps the oxygen concentration around the enzyme extremely low. It is the same principle that makes human blood red, and the red or pink color inside a nodule when you split it is this pigment. This fact is enormously important in practice. The red color is visible evidence that the nodule is fixing nitrogen right now.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Peanut root nodules are rounder and smoother than those of other beans. They look like beads attached to the root, and their number is relatively small. Do not judge a small number as failure; judge by splitting a nodule and looking at the color inside.

### 5-2. How to Check Whether Root Nodules Have Formed

| Step | Details |
|---|---|
| 1 | The best time is when flowering begins, about 6-7 weeks after sowing |
| 2 | Choose two or three plants that look healthy |
| 3 | Do not pull them up; dig widely around each plant with a spade and lift it out with its soil |
| 4 | Put it in a bucket of water and gently wash off the soil |
| 5 | Count the round nodules on the taproot and the fine roots |
| 6 | Split a few nodules with your fingernail and look at the color inside |

| Observation | Meaning | Action |
|---|---|---|
| 10 or more nodules per plant | Inoculation worked well | Leave as is |
| The split nodule is red or pink inside | It is fixing nitrogen | A good condition |
| The split nodule is white or pale green inside | Nodules formed but are not fixing | Check soil acidity, waterlogging, and phosphorus deficiency |
| The split nodule is brown or black inside | Already senescent or dead | After the monsoon season (jangma, late June to July in South Korea), it is highly likely they died from waterlogging |
| Almost no nodules | Inoculation failed | If the leaves are yellow, supplement with a small amount of nitrogen |

> **The single habit of digging up the roots, splitting a nodule, and checking for the red color is the surest signal of success or failure in growing beans in a world without nitrogen fertilizer.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;However, there is something to be careful about with this check in peanuts. The flowering period is also when the pegs are entering the ground. Even if you lift a plant to check and then replant it, its pegs are severed and a harvest from that plant can hardly be expected. Choose plants at the edge of the field to check, and look at only the two or three plants that are truly necessary.

### 5-3. Where to Get the Inoculum

| Method | Procedure | Effect | Caution |
|---|---|---|---|
| Coating the seed with soil from a field that grew peanuts | From a field where peanuts were planted last year and nodules formed well, scoop up soil from the top 5-15cm. Lightly moisten the seed with water, coat it with this soil, and sow immediately | The surest | Never use soil from a field that had baekseo disease |
| Using the nodules themselves | Collect the nodules from dug-up roots, crush them, mix them into water, and soak the seed in that water, then lift it out | The highest bacterial density | Must be used on the same day the nodules are dug |
| Spreading soil over the field | Scatter the soil you brought over the row to be sown and work it in together | The bacteria spread through the soil | About one spadeful per 10m² is the guide |
| Leaving root residue | At harvest, return the stems and roots to the field | The bacteria remain in the soil until the following year | Because peanuts are pulled and dug up, spread the stems back over the field |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;What to be especially careful about in peanut inoculation is soil from a diseased field. Baekseo disease and wilt disease, the soil-borne diseases of peanut, persist in the soil for many years, and in trying to obtain inoculum you can bring the disease along too. Do not use soil from fields where white, thread-like mold has appeared at the stem base or where plants have wilted and died.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There are things you must observe when inoculating. Root-nodule bacteria are extremely vulnerable to direct sunlight, drying, and heat. A good share of them die after a few minutes of ultraviolet light, and they die even faster when dried. Coat the seed with soil in the shade, and plant the coated seed immediately rather than spreading it out in the sun. One more point: once its shell is removed, the embryo of a peanut seed is easily damaged, so do not handle the seed roughly during inoculation.

| Conditions that cause inoculation to fail | Reason |
|---|---|
| Soil pH of 5.5 or below | The bacteria cannot live |
| A field that received plenty of nitrogen fertilizer | The plant feels no need for the exchange and reduces nodule formation on its own |
| A field where water collects | They are aerobic bacteria and die without oxygen |
| A field short of phosphorus | Nitrogen fixation needs a great deal of energy, and phosphorus is used in that metabolism |
| Molybdenum, iron, or cobalt deficiency | These are components of nitrogenase |
| Drying the inoculated seed in the sun | Ultraviolet light and drying kill the bacteria |
| Using soil from a field of another legume | The peanut requires a specialized strain, so efficiency drops |
| Low soil temperature at sowing | Below 15°C, infection hardly occurs |
