## 13. Seed Saving

| Item | Method |
|---|---|
| Selecting seed plants | Choose plants with no disease symptoms, many pods, and a high proportion of filled pods |
| Harvest time | Dig after the pods are fully mature, a little later than for eating |
| Drying | Dry completely and slowly in the shade |
| Storage form | Always store in the shell |
| Storage method | Put in onion nets or ventilated sacks and hang in a cool place |
| Storage temperature | 10-15°C. If it exceeds 20°C, move to a cooler place |
| Storage period | Under 1 year. The limit is until the next spring |
| Shelling | Shell just before planting |

> **Peanut seed cannot last beyond one year. This is because the fat turns rancid and the embryo dies. Seed must be saved every year, and if even one year is skipped, the seed line is broken. This is the peanut's greatest structural weakness.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The storage form is decisive. Shelled peanuts have more surface exposed to air, so rancidity speeds up sharply and germination is lost in a few months. Left in the shell, the shell blocks oxygen and moisture, and germination is kept until the following spring. Storing them shelled looks convenient because of the smaller volume, but doing so turns them from seed into food.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Storage temperature matters too. 10-15°C is ideal, and above 20°C rancidity speeds up. Hang them in ventilated sacks in a place that is cool and constant in temperature, such as a basement, a north-facing room, or an underground storage cellar. Sealing traps moisture and brings mold, so seed peanuts alone are stored ventilated rather than sealed. Remember that this is the exact opposite of food peanuts.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Before planting in spring, always do a germination test. Shell 20 seeds, wrap them in a damp cloth for a few days, and count how many sprout. A germination rate of 70% or more is normal; at half, increase the sowing rate to compensate; below 30%, do not use it as seed.

## 14. Toxicity - Aflatoxin

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Peanuts themselves contain no toxic compounds. They are safe to eat raw, and lectins and inhibitors are not a problem compared with other beans. But peanuts carry a hazard of a different order from other legumes: a toxin made by mold.

### 14-1. What Is Aflatoxin?

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The toxin that a group of molds called Aspergillus produces as it grows on peanuts is called aflatoxin. This substance damages the liver directly, and long-term intake greatly raises the risk of liver cancer. The World Health Organization classifies it as a substance confirmed to be carcinogenic to humans, and countries strictly regulate its content in food.

| Item | Details |
|---|---|
| What produces it | Molds such as Aspergillus flavus |
| When it forms | When peanuts that still hold moisture sit in a warm place. Right after harvest and early in storage are the most dangerous times |
| Acute symptoms | Vomiting, abdominal pain, jaundice, and impaired liver function when a large amount is eaten at once |
| Chronic risk | Even small amounts eaten over a long time raise the risk of liver cancer |
| Removal by heating | Impossible |
| Removal by washing | Impossible |
| Does it remain in oil? | Some carries over into pressed oil as well |

> **Aflatoxin is not completely destroyed even above 280°C. It remains whether you roast, boil, fry, or press the peanuts for oil. There is no way to make moldy peanuts safe. Discarding them is the only way.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This is where it differs from mold on other foods. Mold on cooked rice or tteok (rice cake) is unsightly and tastes bad, but usually you can cut that part away. The aflatoxin in peanuts is spread wider than the visible mold marks, is not destroyed by heat, and accumulates even from small amounts. The feeling that it is a waste is the most dangerous thing.

### 14-2. How to Recognize It

| Sign | Details | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| Bluish-green or yellowish powder on the outer shell | Mold spores | Discard immediately |
| Inner seed-coat color is blotchy or discolored | Mold has penetrated inside | Discard |
| Kernels are shriveled and dark | Mold or insect damage | Discard |
| Bitter taste or sour smell | Rancidity or mold | Spit out and discard |
| Damp, earthy or musty smell | Moisture got in during storage | Inspect the entire batch |
| Holes in the shell | Insects got in and created a pathway for mold | Discard |
| No sound when shaken | Not dry enough | Dry again |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The principle of recognition is simple. If anything seems even slightly off, do not eat it. A bitter taste is an especially important signal. Normal peanuts taste nutty with a slight sweetness; if they taste bitter or sour when chewed, spit them out at once and discard the whole handful. Be especially careful with children. Their intake per body weight is larger, so the same amount carries a greater risk.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;And when one kernel is contaminated, those around it must be suspected too. Mold spores spread inside a storage container. If you find a discolored kernel in a container, spread the entire contents out in a bright place and examine them one by one. You may have to throw away a considerable amount you are not sure about, but that is better than damaging the liver.

### 14-3. How to Prevent It

| Stage | Measure | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Growing | Reduce white-grub damage | Wounds in the shell are a pathway for mold |
| Growing | Stop irrigation from 2 weeks before harvest | Peanuts dug from wet soil are risky |
| Harvest | Do not dig right after rain | Mold grows right away on wet shells |
| Harvest | Pick out spoiled pods on the spot | One kernel contaminates the whole batch |
| Drying | Dry completely to 10% moisture or below | This is the only sure way to prevent it |
| Drying | Dry quickly. Do not pile them up wet | The first few days are the most dangerous |
| Storage | A cool, dry place, sealed or ventilated | Both temperature and humidity must be low |
| Storage | Keep in the shell | The shell acts as a protective barrier |
| Inspection | Check smell and color once a month | Catching it early protects the rest |

> **There is no after-the-fact remedy for aflatoxin. All that matters is how quickly and how completely the peanuts are dried within a few days after harvest.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The most dangerous moment is right after harvest. If peanuts that are soiled and still wet are put in sacks and piled up, heat builds up inside, and the warm, humid interior of the sack becomes perfect conditions for mold. It can begin in a day or two. Never leave harvested peanuts in sacks; spread them out and dry them at once. If rain makes drying impossible, spread them thinly even indoors and turn them from time to time.

### 14-4. Other Cautions

| Item | Details |
|---|---|
| Rancidity | Because of the high fat, they smell of stale oil if kept long. Not a poison, but bad for the body and the taste worsens |
| Allergy | Peanut allergy can be life-threatening when severe. Someone eating them for the first time should test with a small amount |
| Choking | The kernels are round and hard, and a small child can inhale one into the airway. Do not give them whole to children aged four or under |
| Raw peanuts | Safe but somewhat hard to digest. Eating a lot makes the stomach feel bloated |

## 15. Storage Without Refrigeration

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The storability of peanuts is S, one step lower than the SS of other legumes. The standard is one year at room temperature in the pod, and even that assumes complete drying. This is because the high fat makes them go rancid quickly and the mold risk adds to it.

| Approach | Method | Period | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ventilated storage in the shell | Put in onion nets or sacks and hang in a cool, ventilated place | 6 months-1 year | Seed peanuts must always be stored this way |
| Sealed storage in the shell | Dry completely, then put in an earthenware jar or metal container and seal | About 1 year | Suitable for food. Only after complete drying |
| Shelled and sealed | Put in an airtight container in a cool, dark place | 1-2 months | Rancidity is fast, so do not keep long |
| Roasted and sealed | Roast, cool completely, and seal | 1-2 months | Roasting actually speeds rancidity |
| Underground storage cellar | Bury in a container, in the shell | 6 months-1 year | Cool and constant temperature. If water seeps in, the whole store is lost |
| Pressing into oil | Press into oil | Several months | Volume shrinks and handling gets easier |

> **For peanut storage the principles for food and for seed are exactly opposite. Food peanuts are dried completely and sealed; seed peanuts are dried completely and ventilated. If seed is sealed, residual moisture is trapped and brings mold, and respiration is blocked so germination drops.**

| Storage location condition | Target |
|---|---|
| Temperature | 10-15°C. Not above 20°C |
| Humidity | Low. Avoid damp places |
| Light | Block it. Light promotes rancidity |
| Position | Basement or north-facing room, on a stand so they do not touch the ground |
| Inspection | Once a month. Look more often than with other beans |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The most reliable sense for inspection is smell. If you open the container and it smells nutty, all is well; a damp, musty smell or a smell of stale oil means a problem has begun. If it smells musty, spread the whole contents out in a bright place, examine them one by one and pick out the discolored ones, then dry the rest completely again before putting them back. A stale-oil smell is rancidity; it is not toxic but the peanuts should be eaten quickly.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Insects are not as big a problem as with other legumes. This is because the shell is thick and the inside is oily, so bean weevils do not prefer them. However, stored-product pests such as the Indian meal moth come to peanuts that have been shelled, so always seal the shelled ones.

## 16. Cooking and Processing - Pressing Oil

### 16-1. The Legume That Needs the Least Fuel

| Method | Fuel | Procedure |
|---|:---:|---|
| Eating raw | None | Shell and eat as they are. Safe |
| Roasting | Very little | Roast in the shell or shelled in a dry pot for 10-15 minutes |
| Boiling (green peanuts) | Little | Boil in the pod in salted water for 30-40 minutes |
| Pounding into flour | None | Roast and pound, then eat as is or stir into water |
| Grinding into paste | None | Roast and pound in a mortar for a long time, and the oil comes out and forms a paste |
| Pressing oil | Little | Roast, pound, and press |

> **Peanuts are the only legume that can be eaten without fire. When fuel is cut off completely, this one trait is worth more than every other grade.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;If roasted peanuts are pounded in a mortar for a long time, oil comes out of the cells and forms a thick paste. Add a little salt and it can be eaten as it is; stir it into water and it becomes a broth; mix it into grain flour and calories and protein rise together. It is hard to make a better recovery food for a starving person. The high-calorie pastes used in international relief to treat severe malnutrition work on exactly this principle.

### 16-2. Pressing Oil

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The peanut is an oil crop with a fat content of 45-50%. In South Korea, the crops from which oil can be pressed are about sesame, perilla, rapeseed, soybean, and peanut, and among them the peanut has a high content, good oil quality, and a high smoke point, so the oil is used for frying too.

| Step | Procedure | Key point |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Sorting | Pick out every moldy one thoroughly | Aflatoxin carries over into the oil too |
| 2. Shelling | Remove the outer shell and, as far as possible, the inner skin too | With the inner skin, the oil color turns cloudy |
| 3. Roasting | Roast in a dry pot over low heat for 10-15 minutes | The cell walls break and the oil comes out readily |
| 4. Pounding | Pound finely in a mortar or millstone | The finer the pounding, the higher the yield |
| 5. Steaming | Lightly steam the pounded mass | Heat and moisture loosen the oil |
| 6. Pressing | Wrap in cloth and press | Use a lever or screw press |
| 7. Settling | Leave the pressed oil for a few days to let the dregs settle | Pour off only the clear upper part into a container |
| 8. Storage | Keep sealed in a dark, cool place | Light and heat promote rancidity |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Here are methods for when you have no tools. A lever press is not hard to make. Set a long pole between two sturdy wooden posts, wrap pounded peanuts in cloth and wedge them between boards, then hang a weight on the end of the pole to press. This is the way sesame and perilla oil used to be pressed, and the same principle works for peanuts. The pressing yield falls short of a machine and only a little over half of the fat comes out, but the remaining oil cake is also rich in protein, so it is eaten rather than thrown away.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Peanut oil cake is an excellent food with over 40% protein. Break up this lump left after pressing and add it to porridge or mix it into grain flour. However, mold grows readily even though the oil has been removed, so eat it quickly or dry it thoroughly before storing. Aflatoxin remains more in the oil cake than in the oil, so if there was even slight suspicion about the raw material, the oil cake is not eaten by people.

> **Pressing oil is also a way of preserving peanuts. The volume shrinks so storage space is smaller, and the oil keeps longer in sealed storage than shelled peanuts do. However, never press oil from raw material suspected of mold.**

### 16-3. Other Processing

| Product | Procedure summary | Storage | Value |
|---|---|---|---|
| Roasted peanuts | Roast in a dry pot for 10-15 minutes | 1-2 months airtight | Emergency food eaten as is |
| Peanut flour | Roast and pound | 1 month airtight | Mixed into grain flour to raise calories |
| Peanut paste | Roast and pound for a long time until the oil comes out | 1-2 months airtight | Good as a recovery food |
| Boiled green peanuts | Boil immature ones in the pod in salted water | A few days | Eaten right away at harvest time |
| Peanut oil | Roast, pound, and press | Several months | Used for cooking and lamps |
| Oil cake | What is left after pressing | Dried, several months | Over 40% protein |
| Peanut shells | Crush and spread on the field or add to compost | - | Return organic matter and potassium |

## 17. Annual Work Calendar

| Month (South Korea) | Work | Key points |
|---|---|---|
| January | Inspect seed, prepare for germination test | Check the condition of seed stored in the shell with ventilation |
| February | Plan the field, confirm the rotation order | Decide which plot will observe the 4-5 year rotation break |
| March | Apply lime and compost and till, pick out stones | Finish 2 weeks before sowing |
| April | Make raised beds, mulch, sow in southern South Korea (late April) | Check that soil temperature is 15°C before planting |
| May | Sow in central South Korea (early to mid-May), bird protection, check germination | The sowing interval between regions is only 2-3 weeks |
| June | Weeding, flowering begins, first hilling | Soften the soil where the pegs will enter |
| July | Second hilling and calcium supplement, watch for brown leaf spot, check root nodules | Do not enter the field after this |
| August | Pod swelling stage, irrigate at 10-day intervals if dry | Water in this period fills the kernels |
| September | Check maturity by trial digging; harvest begins in central South Korea (end of month) | Judge by the color of the inner pod wall |
| October | Harvest, preliminary drying, separating pods, main drying | Do not dig right after rain |
| November | Confirm complete drying, sorting, storage, returning stems and leaves | Until the rattle sounds when shaken |
| December | Processing (roasting, pressing oil), inspect storage | Check smell and color once a month |

### 17-1. Five Things Not to Forget

| Item | Details |
|---|---|
| Aflatoxin | Peanuts that have changed color or grown mold do not become safe by heating. Always discard them |
| Complete drying | Until they rattle when shaken. This is the only way to prevent aflatoxin |
| Hilling | Peanuts form only when the pegs enter the soil. Soften the soil during flowering |
| Lime | The pods absorb calcium directly from the soil. If it is short, the pods turn out hollow |
| Seed | Seed life is under 1 year. Store in the shell with ventilation and save seed every year |
