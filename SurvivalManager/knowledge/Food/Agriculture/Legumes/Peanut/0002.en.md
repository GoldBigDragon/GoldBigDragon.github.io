### 1-3. How to Read the Strengths

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The only criterion marked SS is Infrastructure needs, but S went to seven: Protein density, Fertilizer demand, Cooking & processing, Storability, Taste, Prevalence in South Korea, and Seed saving. A went to Micronutrients, Water use, Yield retention without synthetic inputs, and Yield stability.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The S for Cooking & processing is the only one among the legumes. All the other beans get either C or D, and only the peanut gets S. The reasoning is simple. It can be eaten raw, and roasting takes 10 minutes. No soaking is needed, and no heating for more than an hour. The peanut is the only legume that can be eaten as it is when fuel has run out completely. In the early days of a disaster, when no fire can be lit, a handful of peanuts is worth more than any other bean.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Judged by grade alone, the C for Calories per area looks like that of the other legumes, but the figures differ. At 1,300kcal per 1m², or 0.50× the self-sufficiency line, it is the highest among legumes. Compared with 0.38× for soybean and 0.16× for mung bean, the difference is plain. The energy return of 8.7× on Tillage & digging energy is also the best among legumes, which means it gives back the most calories for the labor of working the field.

> **The peanut is the only legume that competes on calories. If other beans are side dishes that supply protein, the peanut is a staple food that supplies fat and calories. The fact that it can be eaten without fuel raises its value once more.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The S for Prevalence in South Korea is also important in practice. Peanuts are grown across South Korea, and several varieties bred by the National Institute of Crop Science (NICS), South Korea, are available, so seed is easy to obtain. Unlike chickpea or lentil, obtaining seed is not a constraint.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Read the A for Water use together with the A for Yield retention without synthetic inputs. Total evapotranspiration of 450mm is less than that of soybean, and even without chemical inputs the peanut keeps more than half of the conventional yield. That it grows in sandy soil is also an advantage. A sandy field where other crops cannot grow actually suits the peanut.

### 1-4. How to Read the Weaknesses

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The only criterion marked E is Cold hardiness, and D went to Perenniality, Sunlight - relative yield at 40% shade, Non-food value, and Seed longevity. C went to Calories per area, Speed to harvest, Temperature range, Pest & disease pressure, Pest-control self-reliance, Continuous-cropping tolerance, and Tillage & digging energy.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The D for Seed longevity is the most conspicuous weakness in this table. It is under 1 year at room temperature, overwhelmingly shorter than the 3-4 years that other legumes last. The reason is fat. As the 45% to 50% fat goes rancid, it damages the embryo, and germination ability falls quickly. The practical meaning is clear: peanut seed has to be saved every year, and it cannot be stockpiled. If one year's crop fails, there may be no seed to plant the next year, and this is the biggest reason it is hard to put the peanut at the center of a plan.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The C for Continuous-cropping tolerance and the C for Pest-control self-reliance weigh heavily together as well. Soil-borne diseases such as baekseo disease and wilt disease stay in the soil and require a 4-5 year rotation break. This is the longest period among legumes after pea, and it is a considerable constraint where fields are small. On top of that, early leaf spot (brown spot) and late leaf spot (black spot) make the leaves drop early and can cut the yield by more than half, and they are not easy to manage without pesticides.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The C for Speed to harvest and the E for Cold hardiness together narrow the growing window. The crop needs 130-150 days and is killed by frost. In central South Korea, sowing in early May and harvesting in early October fits exactly, while sowing later means frost arrives before harvest. If the best sowing time is missed, the year is lost.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;And there is one risk that does not appear as an item in the grade table: aflatoxin. This is why the S for Storability carries the explicit caveat that complete drying is essential to prevent aflatoxin. Peanuts that are not dried enough become a matter not of storage duration but of safety. Section 14 covers this in detail.

## 2. What Kind of Plant Is This? - The Peg (Gynophore) and Underground Fruiting

### 2-1. Classification and Origin

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The peanut is an annual herb of the legume family (Fabaceae), genus Arachis. Though a legume, it bears fruit in a way unlike any other legume. Its place of origin is the eastern foothills of the Andes in South America, an area covering what is now Bolivia and northern Argentina. Several wild Arachis species grow in that region, and two of them crossed naturally to become today's cultivated peanut.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The root-nodule bacteria (rhizobia) that pair with the peanut belong to the Bradyrhizobium group but are a strain specialized for the peanut. Soil from fields that grew other legumes tends not to work well as an inoculum source, and soil from fields that grew peanuts is the surest. Fortunately, peanuts are widely grown in South Korea, so obtaining inoculum is not very difficult.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The peanut is self-pollinating. Fertilization is often already complete inside the flower before it opens, so traits do not mix even when several varieties are planted side by side. However, the constraint that seed is short-lived and must be saved every year reduces the real value of the S grade for Seed saving.

### 2-2. The Peg (Gynophore) - How the Peanut Bears Fruit Underground

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Understanding the peanut's most unusual physiology comes first, because it is the key to understanding its cultivation. The sequence goes like this.

| Stage | What happens |
|---|---|
| 1. Flowering | Yellow, butterfly-shaped flowers open at the lower nodes of the stem. A flower opens in the morning and withers the same day |
| 2. Fertilization | Fertilization is completed inside the flower. The petals fall off |
| 3. Gynophore elongation | The base of the fertilized ovary begins to lengthen into a stalk. This is called the gynophore, or ovary stalk (the "peg") |
| 4. Reaching the ground | The tip of the gynophore bends and grows downward until it touches the ground. The tip is pointed and hard, so it pierces the soil |
| 5. Entering the soil | The gynophore digs 3-7cm into the soil |
| 6. Pod formation | Only after it has entered the soil does the tip swell into a pod |
| 7. Enlargement and ripening | In the soil the pod grows larger and a net pattern forms on the shell |

> **The peanut flowers above ground and sets fruit underground. If the peg cannot enter the soil, no matter how many flowers open, not a single peanut forms. Almost every cultivation technique exists to help with this one thing.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Three practical principles follow from this physiology. First, the soil must be soft. If the surface is hard or full of stones, the peg cannot pierce through and enter. This is why sandy soil suits the peanut, and why hilling is done during flowering. Second, the soil must contain calcium. As the pod grows in the soil, the peanut absorbs calcium directly from the soil, through the pod shell rather than through the roots. If calcium is lacking, pods may form but be empty inside, or the kernels shrivel. Third, flowering must occur low on the plant. Flowers that open high up the stem run out of strength before the peg reaches the ground, and come to nothing.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;It is also worth knowing that flowering continues for nearly 100 days. Starting in June in South Korea, new flowers keep opening until September, and pods from late flowers do not ripen by harvest. As a result, the peanuts dug from one plant are a mix of mature and immature ones. This is why judging the harvest time is tricky.

### 2-3. Appearance and Growth Stages

| Part | Characteristics |
|---|---|
| Leaf | A pinnately compound leaf with four leaflets attached in two pairs. The leaflets fold at night |
| Stem | Erect, semi-erect, and creeping types exist. Height 30-50cm |
| Root | A straight taproot that bears root nodules |
| Flower | Yellow and butterfly-shaped. Opens at the lower nodes of the stem |
| Peg (gynophore) | After fertilization the base of the ovary stretches out like a stalk and enters the ground |
| Pod | Grows in the soil. The shell has a net pattern and holds 2-3 kernels |

| Stage | Timing (South Korea) | Key physiology | Important nutrients |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sowing and germination | April-May | Germination takes about 10 days | Phosphorus |
| Vegetative growth | May-June | Stems and leaves increase and root nodules form | Nitrogen is supplied by the nodules |
| Flowering and pod setting | June-September, lasting about 100 days | Flowers open and pegs enter the ground | Phosphorus, calcium |
| Pod enlargement and ripening | August-October | Pods enlarge in the soil | Potassium, calcium |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The difference between erect and creeping types matters in practice. In the erect type the stems stand straight, the flowers open clustered in a relatively narrow area, and the pegs enter the soil concentrated near the base of the plant. When you pull up one plant at harvest, most of the pods come up with it. In the creeping type the stems spread sideways and the pegs enter over a wide area, so many pods are missed at harvest. Where the digging has to be done by hand, the erect type has a clear advantage.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The peanut's nutritional makeup is fundamentally different from other legumes. Where other beans are 60% carbohydrate and 1-2% fat, the peanut is 16-20% carbohydrate and nearly half fat. It is in effect an oil crop. This makeup nearly doubles the calories and at the same time creates the difficulty of storage. Fat meets oxygen and goes rancid, and rancid oil smells bad, loses nutritional value, and is not good for the body.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The amino acid composition is similar to that of other beans. Lysine is plentiful and methionine is slightly short. Eaten together with grains, the two complement each other. Because the peanut is high in fat, the calories rise sharply when it is eaten with grains, which makes it especially good as a recovery food for starving people. In fact, in international relief work, high-calorie pastes based on peanuts are widely used to treat severe malnutrition.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The resveratrol and polyphenols in the inner skin are also worth noting. The thin red skin of roasted peanuts is often peeled off and thrown away, but the antioxidant compounds are concentrated in that skin. It has an astringent taste, but eating it together is better nutritionally.
