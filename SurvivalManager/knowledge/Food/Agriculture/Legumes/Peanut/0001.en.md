## Introduction
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Peanut (Arachis hypogaea) is an annual legume native to South America, from the eastern foothills of the Andes, whose flowers bloom above ground and whose fruit forms underground. After fertilization the base of the ovary stretches like a stalk (the gynophore) and burrows into the soil, and the pod grows at its tip; nearly every cultivation technique comes from this physiology. The value of peanut is calories. Dry peanuts have 520 kcal per 100 g, the highest among legumes, and calories per area is 1,300 kcal per ㎡, 0.50 times the self-sufficiency line, while the calorie recovery ratio against land-preparation and digging energy is 8.7, all the best among legumes. With 45-50% fat it can be pressed into oil, and with a cooking and processing grade of S it can be eaten raw or after just 10 minutes of roasting, making it the only legume that can be eaten without fuel. The overall grade is C (43.6 points, 75th of 183 valid species), with short term C (43.0), long term C (44.2), and supplementary SS (57.6 points). Its weaknesses are a long 130-150 day growing season that dies to frost, a seed life of under a year at room temperature, a 4-5 year rest, and aflatoxin, a mold toxin that does not disappear with heat. The legume with the most calories is also the most demanding legume in storage.

## 1. Disaster Preparedness Evaluation
| Category | Grade | Score |
|:---|:---:|:---:|
| Overall | C | 43.6 |
| Short term | C | 43.0 |
| Long term | C | 44.2 |
| Supplementary | SS | 57.6 |

| Evaluation | Items |
|:---|:---|
| Strengths | SS: facility burden. S: protein density, fertilization, cooking and processing requirement, storability, taste, domestic cultivation spread, seed saving. A: micronutrients, water consumption, yield retention without synthetic inputs, yield stability |
| Weaknesses | E: cold tolerance. D: perenniality, sunlight, non-food value, seed longevity. C: calories per area, harvest speed, temperature range, pest and disease pressure, pest-control self-reliance, continuous-cropping tolerance, land-preparation and digging energy |

> **Peanut is the only legume that competes on calories. If other beans are side dishes that give protein, peanut is a staple that gives fat and calories.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Cooking and processing S belongs to peanut alone among legumes, so in the early days of a disaster, when a fire cannot be lit, a handful of peanuts is worth more than any other bean. Calories per area is the highest of the legumes in value even though the grade is C, and with water consumption A (total evapotranspiration 450 mm) as a plus, sandy ground where other crops cannot grow actually suits it. Seed longevity D is the most conspicuous weakness: the fat turns rancid and damages the embryo, so peanuts must be saved every year, and if one year's farming fails there may be no seed to sow the next year. Continuous-cropping tolerance C and pest-control self-reliance C come from root and pod rot, wilt, and brown spot, and harvest speed C and cold tolerance E combine so that missing the optimal sowing date loses the year. The risk not in the grade table is aflatoxin (Section 10). The grade for each item is in the Crop Evaluation Results document.

## 2. Plant Overview
| Item | Details |
|:---|:---|
| Classification | Annual legume of the genus Arachis in the family Fabaceae. Native to South America, self-pollinating |
| Leaves, stem | Pinnate compound leaves with 4 leaflets that fold at night. Erect, semi-erect, and spreading types; height 30-50 cm |
| Roots, flowers | Straight taproot with round nodules. Yellow butterfly-shaped flowers open on the lower nodes and wither the same day |
| Pods, seeds | Grow underground, with a net pattern on the shell and 2-3 seeds inside |

> **Peanut flowers above ground and sets its fruit underground. If the gynophore cannot enter the soil, not one peanut forms no matter how many flowers bloom.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The base of the fertilized ovary lengthens, bends down to touch the ground, and burrows 3-7 cm into the soil, and only then does its tip swell into a pod. Three principles follow. First, if the surface is hard or stony the gynophore cannot penetrate, so the soil must be soft and hilled at flowering. Second, the pod absorbs calcium directly from the soil through its shell, not through the roots, so with too little calcium the pods are empty. Third, flowers that open higher on the stem run out of strength before the gynophore reaches the ground, so flowering must be low on the plant. Flowering continues for nearly 100 days from June to September, so pods from late flowers do not ripen by harvest, which makes judging harvest time difficult. Dry peanuts per 100 g: 520 kcal, protein 25.74 g (19.8% of calories), fat 45-50 g (mostly unsaturated fatty acids), carbohydrate 16-20 g (the least among legumes), dietary fiber 10.5 g, calcium 67 mg, and iron 2 mg, and they are rich in vitamin E.

## 3. Growing Environment and Varieties
| Condition | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Temperature | Germination needs a soil temperature of 15 °C or more at 5 cm depth (optimum 20-25 °C; below 15 °C the seed rots). Growth 18-35 °C (optimum 25-30 °C). Fruit set 20-30 °C (optimum 24-28 °C). Accumulated temperature 2,800 °C for early varieties and 3,600 °C for late varieties. Sunshine 6 hours or more a day (optimum 8 hours or more) |
| Soil, water | Sandy loam (clay soil and stony fields are unsuitable), pH 5.5-7.5 (optimum 6.5-7.0), effective soil depth 30 cm or more, plenty of calcium in the soil. Especially vulnerable to waterlogging at flowering, so ridges of 20 cm or more |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The standard for judging sowing is soil temperature, not air temperature, and without a soil thermometer the guide is to put a hand in the soil in the morning and feel that it is not cold. Plastic mulching brings sowing forward 2-3 weeks and secures more accumulated temperature. If a handful of soil does not clump but crumbles loosely it suits peanut, and if only clay soil is available, mix in sand or rice hulls to make the ridges.

| Type | Features | Suitability for disasters |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Erect | Pods cluster at the base of the plant and mostly come up with it when pulled | Highest |
| Spreading | Pods scatter widely and many are missed | Low |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Where labor is short and frost comes early, an erect early variety is sensible; for pressing oil choose large-seeded types, which press more easily, and if food is needed in summer, choose types for green peanuts. Saedeul and Daegwang are erect early large-seeded varieties, and Namgwang is relatively resistant to brown spot. Because seed life is under a year, keeping the line going without a break matters more than choosing a variety.

## 4. Field Preparation and Sowing
| Item | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Field preparation | Two weeks before sowing, apply compost and lime and plow deeply to soften the soil, and pick out stones from the surface. Ridge width 70-80 cm, furrow 30-40 cm, height 20 cm or more |
| Lime, calcium | Slaked lime at 150-200 kg per 1,000 ㎡ to bring pH to 6.5-7.0; in peanut it also serves as a calcium fertilizer (above pH 7.5 the absorption of iron, manganese, and zinc is blocked and the leaves turn yellow between the veins, so the upper limit is 200 kg). Roasted and crushed clam and oyster shells and eggshell powder can substitute; apply once more to the soil at the base of the plants at flowering and mix it in with the hilling |
| Fertilization (1,000 ㎡) | Phosphorus 10 kg, potassium 10 kg (all as basal fertilizer), nitrogen at the minimum, and 1-1.5 kg of compost per ㎡. Make up the phosphorus with bone meal and the potassium with wood ash (150-200 g per ㎡) and crushed peanut shells. If boron is short, black hearts form inside the pods, so use compost made from many materials |
| Nodule inoculation | Coat seeds lightly moistened with water with soil from 5-15 cm depth of a field where peanuts grew and formed nodules well, and sow at once. Do not use soil from a field that had root and pod rot or wilt, and soil from fields of other legumes is less effective |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Check nodules around flowering (6-7 weeks after sowing), but a plant lifted for checking has its gynophores cut and can hardly be expected to yield, so look at only two or three plants at the edge of the field.

| Sowing | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Timing | South: late April to early May; central region: early to mid May; mulched cultivation nationwide: early April to early May. Wait for a soil temperature of 15 °C, then hurry (the margin is only 2-3 weeks, and past mid-May in the central region the risk of not ripening before frost grows) |
| Seed preparation | Keep in the shell and shell just before planting (shelled early they go rancid and lose germination). Use only large, plump seeds with a smooth inner skin, and discard shriveled seeds or those that smell moldy. Coat with fine wood ash, and test germination by wrapping 20 seeds in a wet cloth |
| Method | Very shallow, 2-3 cm deep; 2-3 rows on a 70-80 cm ridge, plant spacing 15-20 cm, 2 seeds per hole, set with the pointed end down. Cover with straw or netting to keep out birds and soil insects. With plastic mulch, plant through holes, and around flowering tear or pull back the plastic around the plants so the gynophores can enter |

## 5. Water Management and Cultivation Management
| Stage | Water management |
|:---|:---|
| Flowering and setting, rainy season | Especially vulnerable to waterlogging, so keep water channels clear and dig drainage ditches (southern blight comes after a hot rainy season) |
| Dry spell after early August | Irrigate 2-3 times at 10-day intervals |
| 2 weeks before harvest | Stop irrigation (peanuts dug from wet soil get mold) |

> **The most important time for water in peanut is the pod-filling period in August. If water is short in the dry spell that follows the rainy season, pods already in the soil cannot grow, and giving water just two or three times at 10-day intervals changes the yield greatly.**

| Care | Method |
|:---|:---|
| Hilling | First, around the start of flowering, scrape the soil around the base of the plants soft and pull it up; second, when the gynophores begin to extend, gather more soil and mix in calcium powder. The aim is not to pile soil thick but to soften it, and if an already extended thin stalk is cut, that pod is lost |
| Weeding | Scrape shallowly in early growth, and combine the last weeding before flowering with hilling. After flowering, pull only large weeds within reach and do not enter the field (treading cuts the gynophores and compacts the soil) |

## 6. Pests, Diseases, and Nutrient Problems
| Pest or disease | Symptoms and conditions | Control without chemicals |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Brown spot, black leaf spot | Dark brown spots with a yellow ring form on leaves and the leaves fall early, cutting yield by half to 60%. Mid-July to harvest | Secure airflow, pick off spotted lower leaves from mid-July and burn diseased residue, spray Bordeaux mixture at 10-15 day intervals, rotate crops, select resistant lines |
| Southern blight, root and pod rot | White threadlike mold spreads at the stem base and the plant wilts and dies (after a hot rainy season) / roots and pods rot. Soil-borne | Dig diseased plants up with the surrounding soil and burn them, improve drainage; a 4-5 year rotation is the only fundamental remedy |
| White grubs | The biggest pest, gnawing roots and pods in the soil. Wounds in the shell become a path for mold and the starting point of aflatoxin | Use only fully decomposed compost and catch the grubs you see when plowing |
| Aphids, thrips, mites | Suck sap and carry virus disease (leaves mottle, cannot be treated) (aphids, thrips), or turn leaves yellow and mottled (mites, when dry after the rainy season) | Soapy water (5 g of soap per 1 L of water), spray water hard on the leaves. Pull up and burn plants with virus disease |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Bordeaux mixture is made by dissolving 400 g of copper sulfate and 400 g of quicklime separately in 100 L of water, mixing them, and using it as a preventive.

| Symptom | Cause and action |
|:---|:---|
| Many flowers but no pods | A surface hardened by rain, clay soil, or too little hilling keeps the gynophores from entering the soil. Soften the soil by hilling |
| Pods formed but empty | Calcium deficiency: calcium must be in the layer a few cm below the soil surface where the pods grow, so at flowering scatter roasted and crushed shell powder at the base of the plants and hill it in |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Nodules fix 70-120 kg of nitrogen per hectare and 30-50 kg remains in the stems and leaves, but because peanuts are pulled up, the roots and nodules (10-20 kg) also leave the field. So the stems, leaves, and roots left after removing the pods, and the shells, which are about a third of the harvest, must be returned to the field. If the vines were fed to livestock, put that manure on the same field.

## 7. Intercropping and Crop Rotation
| Compatibility | Companions |
|:---|:---|
| Good | Maize, foxtail millet, and sorghum (do not share diseases and use the nitrogen peanut leaves behind), sesame, chili pepper and watermelon (suitable rotation partners) |
| Poor | Tobacco (grows the same soil pathogens), continuous peanut (southern blight and root and pod rot cut yield greatly) |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Because peanut has a long growing period and the field cannot be entered after flowering, it fits into a rotation rather than intercropping. Southern blight and root and pod rot remain in the soil, so rest the field for 4-5 years; a 5-year rotation example is peanut → maize or foxtail millet → sesame or chili pepper → watermelon or root vegetables → grains or leafy vegetables. If the field is small, move a sandy spot each year, or plant only every second or third year, but because seed life is under a year, plant a few plants for seed separately every year.

## 8. Harvest and Seed Saving
| Harvest | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Timing | 130-150 days after sowing (central region late September to early October; south early October to mid-November). Because they are underground, dig two or three plants as a trial and split the pods; when 60-80% of pods have an inner wall that is brown or black, it is time. If frost is forecast, dig even if not fully ripe |
| Technique | Dig on a clear, dry day. After rain, wait until the soil dries, because wet soil sticks to the shell and mold starts within a day or two. Dig beside the plant and pull by the stem; for spreading types or hard fields, rake the pulled spot once more to find the remaining pods. Turn the pods upward and pre-dry them in the field for a few days, picking out spoiled pods |
| Drying | After 3-7 days of pre-drying, strip the pods and spread them thinly on a tray or mat to dry slowly in well-ventilated shade (strong sun cracks the shell and makes a path for mold). They are dry when the seeds rattle on shaking (target moisture 10% or less). Discard discolored, shriveled, and holed ones, and do not bag and pile harvested peanuts but spread them to dry at once |

| Seed saving | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Selection | Choose plants with no disease symptoms, many pods, and a high share ripe, and dig them a little later than for eating, after they are fully ripe, then dry slowly in shade |
| Storage | Always keep in the shell, in an onion mesh bag or ventilated sack hung in a cool place at 10-15 °C (the limit is the following spring). Just before planting, shell them and wrap 20 seeds in a wet cloth to test germination (70% or more is normal; if half, raise the seed rate; below 30%, do not use as seed) |

> **Peanut seed cannot last beyond one year. The fat turns rancid and the embryo dies, so seed must be saved every year, and skipping even one year breaks the seed line. This is peanut's greatest structural weakness.**

## 9. Storage
| Method | How | Period |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Ventilated, in the shell | Put in an onion bag or sack and hang in a cool, ventilated place (always this way for seed) | 6 months to 1 year |
| Sealed, in the shell | Dry completely, then seal in a clay jar or metal can (for eating) | About 1 year |
| Shelled and sealed, roasted and sealed | Put in a sealed container in a cool, dark place (they go rancid quickly, and roasted ones faster) | 1-2 months |

> **For storage, the rules for eating and for seed are opposite. For eating, dry completely and seal; for seed, dry completely and ventilate. If seed is sealed, leftover moisture is trapped, mold comes, and respiration is blocked so germination falls.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The storage place is cool at 10-15 °C (not above 20 °C), low in humidity and dark, and it is checked by smell once a month. A nutty smell is normal; a smell of oily staleness is rancidity, which is not toxic but should be eaten soon; and if there is a musty smell, spread out everything and examine each seed, pick out discolored ones, and dry the rest completely again before storing. Bean weevils are not a big problem, but shelled peanuts attract storage pests such as the Indian meal moth, so seal shelled ones.

## 10. Cooking and Precautions
| Item | Aflatoxin |
|:---|:---|
| What it is | A toxin made by molds such as Aspergillus, formed when peanuts that still hold moisture sit in a warm place; the period right after harvest is the most dangerous |
| Symptoms | Large amounts at once cause vomiting, abdominal pain, jaundice, and liver damage; even small amounts eaten over a long time raise the risk of liver cancer |

> **Aflatoxin is not completely destroyed even above 280 °C. It remains after roasting, boiling, frying, and pressing oil, and there is no way to make moldy peanuts safe. Throwing them away is the only way.**

| Sign | Verdict |
|:---|:---|
| A bluish or yellowish powder on the outer shell, stains on the inner skin, shriveled and dark seeds, holes in the shell | Throw away at once |
| A bitter taste or sour smell | Spit it out at once and throw away that whole handful |
| A damp earthy or musty smell | Inspect everything |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;If one seed is contaminated, mold spores spread inside the container, so spread out the entire contents of that container in a bright place and examine each seed, and take particular care with children, whose intake per body weight is large. Prevention is reducing white grub damage, stopping irrigation 2 weeks before harvest, not digging right after rain, and picking out spoiled pods, and the only sure method is to dry completely to 10% moisture or less within the first few days. In addition, peanut allergy can be life-threatening, so those eating peanuts for the first time should try a small amount, and whole peanuts should not be given to children under four.

| Cooking | Method |
|:---|:---|
| Raw, roasted | Safe to eat raw; roast in a dry pan for 10-15 minutes, in the shell or shelled only |
| Green peanuts | Boil immature peanuts in the pod in salted water for 30-40 minutes and eat them at once in the harvest season (keeps a few days) |
| Flour, paste | Roasted and ground flour (keeps 1 month sealed) is mixed into grain flour to raise calories, and long pounding in a mortar brings out the oil into a thick paste (keeps 1-2 months sealed) that is good as a recovery food |
| Pressing oil | Pick out moldy ones thoroughly, shell, roast in a dry pan over low heat for 10-15 minutes, pound finely, and steam briefly. Wrap in cloth and press with a lever press, let it stand a few days, and seal the settled upper layer in a dark, cool place (yield is a little over half the fat) |
| Press cake, shells | The press cake is over 40% protein, but if the raw material was even slightly suspect, people do not eat it. Shells are spread on the field or put in compost |

> **Peanut is the only legume that can be eaten without fire. When fuel has run out completely, this one thing is worth more than every other grade.**

## 11. Annual Work Schedule
| Month | Work |
|:---|:---|
| Jan-Feb | Check the condition of the seed hung in the shell, prepare germination tests. In February, plan the field and confirm the plots that will keep the 4-5 year rest |
| Mar-Apr | In March, apply lime and compost and plow, and pick out stones (finish 2 weeks before sowing). In April, make ridges, mulch, and sow in the south (late month; check for 15 °C soil temperature) |
| May-June | In May, sow in the central region (early and mid month), protect against birds, and check germination. In June, weeding, the start of flowering, and first hilling |
| July-Aug | In July, second hilling and calcium top-up, watching for brown spot, and checking nodules (do not enter the field afterward). In August, pod filling, and irrigation at 10-day intervals if dry |
| Sep-Oct | In September, trial digging to check ripeness, and start of harvest in the central region (end of month). In October, harvest, pre-drying, stripping pods, and final drying (do not dig right after rain) |
| Nov-Dec | In November, confirm complete drying, sort, store, and return stems and leaves. In December, processing (roasting, pressing oil) and checking storage |

> **Peanut earns its keep when you throw away anything discolored or moldy, dry until it rattles, hill at flowering, supply calcium, and save seed every year and hang it in the shell in a ventilated place.**
