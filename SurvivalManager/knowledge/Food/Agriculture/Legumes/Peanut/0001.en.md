## Introduction

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The peanut (Arachis hypogaea) came from South America. It originates on the eastern foothills of the Andes between Bolivia and Argentina and has been cultivated since before the Incas. Its Chinese name, luohuasheng (落花生), means "born of the fallen flower," and that name explains everything about the peanut. The peanut flowers above ground and sets its fruit underground. After fertilization, the base of the ovary stretches out like a long stalk, pushes down into the soil, and the pod develops at its tip. This is an unusual habit in the plant kingdom, and almost every cultivation technique follows from this one piece of physiology.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;From a disaster-preparedness standpoint, the peanut's value is clear: calories. At 520kcal per 100g of dry peanuts, it is overwhelmingly the highest of the legumes in this crop table. Soybean has 401, pea 354, and mung bean 352, and only the peanut tops 500. The reason is that fat makes up 45% to 50% of it. Calories per area is also the highest among legumes, at 1,300kcal per 1m², and its calorie return relative to Tillage & digging energy is 8.7×, which is also the best among legumes. It is the bean that gives back the most calories once you have tilled a field and planted it.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There is one more thing: oil. In a disaster, vegetable oil is a resource that covers calorie density, cooking, preservation, and even lamp light, and the crops from which oil can be pressed in South Korea are little more than sesame, perilla, rapeseed, soybean, and peanut. Of these, the peanut has a high pressing yield and good oil quality. On top of that, its Cooking & processing grade is S: just roast it and it is ready to eat in 10 minutes, and it can also be eaten raw. It has the lightest cooking burden of any legume.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;However, the part of this document you absolutely must read is Section 14. Peanuts carry the risk of a mold toxin called aflatoxin.

> **Aflatoxin is a toxin produced by mold. It severely damages the liver and is a confirmed carcinogen. Heat does not destroy it. It remains after roasting, boiling, or frying. Peanuts that have changed color or grown mold must be thrown away without exception, and the only way to prevent this is complete drying after harvest.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;It is not unrelated to this risk that the peanut's Storability grade is S, one step below the SS of the other legumes, and that its Seed longevity grade is D, meaning under 1 year at room temperature. The high fat content makes it go rancid quickly, it grows underground and is exposed to molds in the soil, and its thin shell lets moisture in easily. The legume with the most calories is also the most demanding legume to store.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Finally, there is one misconception shared by legume crops as a whole: the claim that planting beans improves the soil. It is only half true. The peanut, too, lives in symbiosis with root-nodule bacteria (rhizobia) and draws in nitrogen, but if the seeds are harvested and carried out of the field, more nitrogen can leave the field than was fixed. Section 5 works through that dividing line with numbers.
