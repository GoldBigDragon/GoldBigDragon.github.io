## 5. Field Preparation and Fertilizer - Nitrogen Balance and Fertilization

### 5-4. The Trap of Nitrogen Fixation

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The saying that planting beans improves the soil has hardened into common sense in farming. Half of it is right and half is wrong. Without understanding exactly where that line falls, a field planted with legumes year after year can end up growing barren instead.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Let us work it out in numbers. When peanuts grow well, 250kg of dry seed is harvested from 1,000m². The protein in peanut seed is 25.7%, and dividing the protein by 6.25 gives the amount of nitrogen. 25.7 ÷ 6.25 is 4.11, which means about 4.1% of the seed's weight is pure nitrogen. The nitrogen contained in 250kg is about 10.3kg, which converts to 103kg per hectare.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Meanwhile, the nitrogen fixed by peanut root nodules is 70-120kg per hectare under good conditions. Set side by side, what goes out and what comes in look like this.

| Item | Nitrogen per hectare | Direction |
|---|---|---|
| Fixed by root nodules (good conditions) | 70-120kg | In |
| Fixed by root nodules (poor conditions) | 25-50kg | In |
| Removed with the seed | 90-110kg | Out |
| Nitrogen remaining in the haulm (stems and leaves) | 30-50kg | Stays if returned, leaves if hauled away |
| Nitrogen remaining in roots and nodules | 10-20kg | May leave along with the crop, since the plants are pulled up and dug |

> **The peanut has one more problem that other beans do not. Because it is harvested by pulling up and digging, the roots and even the root nodules come out of the field along with it. Other beans can be cut at the stem, leaving the roots behind, but the peanut cannot.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;So for peanuts, returning the haulm becomes even more important. After the pods are stripped from the pulled plants, the stems, leaves, and roots must be spread over that same field as they are. This residue, commonly called peanut vines, is easily carried elsewhere for use as livestock feed and the like, but doing so turns the nitrogen balance firmly negative. If it was fed to livestock, the manure must be returned to that field without fail.

| Principle | Details |
|---|---|
| Haulm and roots go back to the field | Spread the stems, leaves, and roots, with the pods stripped off, over that field |
| Do not burn them | Burning sends the nitrogen off as gas, leaving only potassium and phosphorus behind as ash |
| Separate out only the diseased residue | Burn plants that showed symptoms of baekseo disease or wilt disease |
| If fed to livestock, return the dung | Peanut vines make good feed. If fed, put the manure on that field |
| Return the shells too | Peanut shells are good organic matter. Crush them and spread them on the field or add them to compost |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Let us note down peanut shells separately. About one-third of the harvest by weight is shell, and because it is bulky it is easy to throw away. Yet this shell is good organic matter and contains potassium. Crushed and spread on the field, it acts as mulch, and added to compost it creates an airy structure that helps fermentation. Burned to ash, its potassium can be used right away, but the organic matter is lost, so use each form as the conditions of the field dictate.

### 5-5. Field Preparation and Lime

| Item | Method | In disaster conditions |
|---|---|---|
| Plowing | Apply compost and lime 2 weeks before sowing and plow deeply | The soil must be soft for the pegs to enter |
| Raised bed | Width 70-80cm, furrow 30-40cm, height 20cm or more | Gives both drainage and soft soil |
| Acidity correction | pH 6.5-7.0 | Strongly acidic soil is unsuitable |
| Lime | Slaked lime 150-200kg/1,000m² | For peanuts it both corrects acidity and supplies calcium |
| Removing stones | Pick the stones off the surface | Stones block the pegs and deform the pods |
| Mulching | Plastic film or straw | Raises the soil temperature and brings sowing forward |

> **For peanuts, lime is not a material for correcting acidity but a fertilizer. Because the pods in the soil absorb calcium directly through their shells, without calcium the pods are empty inside and the kernels shrivel.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In disaster conditions, what can stand in for lime is seashells and eggshells. Both are calcium carbonate, so the principle is the same, but left as they are they react very slowly, so roast them in a fire to make them brittle, then pound them in a mortar into a fine powder before use. Oyster shells are especially good, and are easy to obtain near the seashore. Bone meal also supplies calcium and phosphorus together. Wood ash is alkaline, but its main component is potassium, so its effect as a calcium source is weaker than lime.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There is also a knack to the timing of calcium application. Applying it over the whole surface as base fertilizer is the basic method, but for peanuts it is effective to scatter it once more on the soil around the base of the plants at flowering and mix it in with hilling. The reason is that calcium has to be in exactly the soil layer the pegs enter.

### 5-6. Fertilizer Rates

| Fertilizer | Per 1,000m² | Per m² | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Nitrogen | 3kg | 3g | Minimum amount only. For the early stage before the nodules start working |
| Phosphorus | 10kg | 10g | All applied as base fertilizer |
| Potassium | 10kg | 10g | All applied as base fertilizer. Involved in pod enlargement |
| Lime | 150-200kg | 150-200g | 2 weeks before sowing. Calcium supply is the main purpose |
| Compost | 1,000-1,500kg | 1-1.5kg | Use fully rotted compost |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The reason the peanut's Fertilizer demand grade in the evaluation table is S rather than SS is this lime. The total input is 14g per m², more than the under-8g of the other legumes, and the difference comes from the lime. The nitrogen itself is still the minimum amount at 3kg, about one-seventh the level for maize.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Top-dressing is in principle unnecessary. Only when waterlogging damage from the monsoon season (jangma, late June to July in South Korea) has clearly worsened growth, foliar feeding by spraying a 2% urea solution on the leaves is the usual practice. In disaster conditions, dilute urine very weakly, to 30 times or more with water, and spray it on the leaves, or dilute it 10 times and pour a small amount on the soil between plants. After flowering, however, take care, because simply walking into the field can sever the pegs.

### 5-7. Substitute Fertilizers for Disaster Conditions

| Substitute resource | Nutrients supplied | How to use | Caution |
|---|---|---|---|
| Roasted seashells and oyster shells | Calcium | Roast and pound them, then use as base fertilizer and in hilling at flowering | The most important resource for peanuts |
| Eggshells | Calcium | Roast and pound them before use | The quantity is small, so they are a supplementary measure |
| Bone meal | Phosphorus, calcium | Roast and pound it, then mix into compost | Supplies phosphorus and calcium together |
| Wood ash | Potassium, phosphorus, calcium | Scatter 150-200g per m² and mix in | Effective for supplying potassium |
| Peanut shells | Potassium, organic matter | Crush and spread on the field or add to compost | Recycles within the field |
| Well-rotted compost | All-round | Apply 1-1.5kg per m² as base fertilizer | Makes the soil crumbly |
| Fish bones | Phosphorus, calcium | Bury and let them decompose before use | Bury deeply so animals do not dig them up |
| Diluted urine | Nitrogen, potassium | Dilute 10 times with water and apply a small amount | Only during recovery from waterlogging damage |
| Rice hulls and sand | Physical improvement | Mix into clay soil to make it soft | Makes a path for the pegs to enter |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Boron is also worth remembering. The peanut has a boron requirement, and when it is lacking, a black core forms inside the pods. When no chemical borax is available, compost made from vegetable residues, fruit peels, and fallen leaves together is the most realistic alternative. Compost made from only one material is lopsided in trace elements.

## 6. Sowing

### 6-1. Sowing Time

| Cultivation method | Region | Sowing time | Harvest time |
|---|---|---|---|
| Open-field cultivation | Southern South Korea | Late April to early May | Early October to mid-November |
| Open-field cultivation | Central South Korea | Early May to mid-May | Early October to mid-November |
| Mulched cultivation | Across South Korea | Early April to early May | Mid-September to late October |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The sowing time for peanuts is determined between two conditions: the soil temperature must exceed 15°C, and the accumulated temperature must be reached before frost. The first condition prevents early planting, and the second prevents late planting. The interval between them is only 2-3 weeks.

> **The peanut is a crop with a narrow sowing window. Wait for a soil temperature of 15°C, but hurry once it is reached. Past mid-May, the risk grows in central South Korea that the crop will not ripen before frost.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Mulching is the means of solving this problem. Covering with plastic film raises the soil temperature by 2-4°C and allows sowing to be brought forward by 2-3 weeks, securing that much more accumulated temperature. If plastic film is hard to get in disaster conditions, partial substitutes are covering the bed with black cloth or straw and removing it just before planting, choosing a south-facing slope, and running the beds east to west so that the south face gets more sun.

### 6-2. Seed Preparation

| Item | Method | In disaster conditions |
|---|---|---|
| Shelling | Shell just before planting | If shelled in advance, the seed goes rancid and loses germination ability |
| Selection | Use only kernels that are plump, large, and have a smooth inner skin | Discard shriveled ones, discolored ones, and any that smell of mold |
| Checking the embryo | When a kernel is split open, check that the pointed embryo end is intact | A kernel whose embryo has broken off will not sprout |
| Soaking | Not necessary. If done, only for a few hours | Because of the high fat content, soaking too long makes the seed soft |
| Ash treatment | Coat with fine wood ash | Helps disinfect and dry the seed and repels soil insects |
| Root-nodule inoculation | Coat with soil from a field that grew peanuts | See Section 5-3 |
| Germination test | Wrap 20 kernels in a damp cloth, leave for a few days, and count | Because seed life is short, always check |

> **Keep peanut seed in its shell and shell it just before planting. Shelled peanuts go rancid quickly, and their germination ability drops sharply. Shelling in advance looks convenient, but it costs you seeds that no longer sprout.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The practical meaning of the D for Seed longevity shows itself here. The basic practice is to keep peanuts harvested the previous autumn in their shells in a cool place and to shell and plant them the following spring, and seed older than that has a sharply lower germination rate. Always run a germination test before planting to confirm the germination rate; if it is below half, compensate by increasing the sowing rate or obtain other seed.

### 6-3. Sowing Method

| Item | Standard | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Sowing depth | 2-3cm | Plant very shallowly. If too deep, the shoot cannot emerge and the seed rots |
| Bed width | 70-80cm | Plant two to three rows |
| Furrow width | 30-40cm | Serves as both drainage channel and walkway |
| Plant spacing | 15-20cm | |
| Seeds per hole | 2 seeds | Allows for the germination rate |
| Orientation | Embryo (pointed end) facing down | Emergence is faster |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;A hallmark of the peanut is that the sowing depth is very shallow, 2cm to 3cm. The seed is large and rich in fat, so if buried deeply it lacks the strength to push up through the soil, and meanwhile it rots in the soil. It must be planted clearly more shallowly than other beans, but in return, being buried shallowly, it is exposed to birds, soil insects, and drying out, so cover it with straw or netting.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In mulched cultivation the order is different. Either make the beds, cover them with plastic film, then punch holes and plant, or plant first, cover with film, and punch a hole at each spot when the shoots come up. With the latter method, check every day so that shoots are not trapped under the film and scorched to death.

## 7. Water Management

| Period | Water management | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Sowing to germination | Do not let the soil dry out completely | The seed is buried shallowly, so germination stops if the surface layer dries |
| Early growth | Keep moisture adequate but avoid excess wetness | |
| Flowering and pod setting | Especially vulnerable to waterlogging. Put the drainage channels in order | This is when the pegs enter the ground |
| Monsoon season | Dig drainage channels | Southern blight (white silk disease) comes after the hot, humid monsoon |
| Dry spell after early August | Irrigate 2-3 times at 10-day intervals | Directly tied to pod enlargement |
| 2 weeks before harvest | Stop irrigation | Peanuts dug from wet soil develop mold |

> **The most important period in water management for peanuts is the pod-enlargement stage in August. If water is short in the dry spell that follows the end of the monsoon, the pods that have already entered the soil cannot grow large. Just giving water two or three times at 10-day intervals makes a big difference to the yield.**

| Symptom | Cause | Action |
|---|---|---|
| Leaves fold at midday and open again in the evening | A normal water-saving response | Leave as is |
| Leaves are still folded in the morning | Severe drought | Irrigate |
| Many flowers but no pods | The soil is hard, so the pegs cannot enter | Soften the soil by hilling |
| Pods have formed but are empty inside | Calcium shortage | Scatter powder of roasted seashells at the base of the plants and hill them up |
| White, thread-like mold at the stem base | Southern blight. Comes after the hot, humid monsoon | Dig up diseased plants with their soil and burn them. Fix the drainage |
| Lower leaves turn yellow and the roots are black | Root damage from excess moisture | Dig drainage channels |
| Brown spots form on the leaves and they drop early | Early leaf spot (brown spot disease) | Secure ventilation, remove diseased leaves, Bordeaux mixture |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Flowers opening but no pods forming is the most common failure in peanuts. The cause is usually the soil. The surface was hardened by rain, or the soil was clay and the pegs could not pierce it, or hilling was not done and the distance the pegs had to cover to reach the ground was too great. The surest approach is to inspect the field at flowering and check directly whether the pegs are stuck into the soil. If you look closely around the base of a plant, you can see thin stalks pointing down and embedded in the soil.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Stopping irrigation before harvest is also important. Peanuts dug from wet soil have soil and moisture clinging to their shells, and if they are piled up in that state, mold starts within a day or two. This is usually where aflatoxin begins. From 2 weeks before harvest, give no water, and right after rain, wait until the soil dries before digging.
