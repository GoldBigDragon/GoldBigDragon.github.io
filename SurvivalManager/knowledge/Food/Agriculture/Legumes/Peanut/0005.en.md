## 8. Crop Management - Hilling

### 8-1. Hilling Is Everything for Peanuts

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The most efficient task in growing peanuts is hilling. In other crops hilling is a supporting task that prevents lodging and helps drainage, but in peanuts it decides whether there is a harvest at all.

| Task | Timing | Method | Effect |
|---|---|---|---|
| First hilling | Around the start of flowering | Gently scrape up the soil around the base of each plant | Creates a soft layer of soil for the pegs (ovary stalks) to enter |
| Second hilling | When the pegs begin to extend | Gather up more soil and mix in calcium powder | Shortens the distance the pegs must cover and supplies calcium |
| Afterward | Once the pegs have entered the soil | Do not walk into the field | Treading on it breaks the pegs |

> **In the peanut, after a flower opens above ground and is fertilized, the peg grows down into the soil and sets the fruit there. If the soil is hard, no pods form no matter how many flowers bloom. This is why softening the soil during flowering is essential.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There are three keys to hilling. First, the aim is not to pile soil thickly but to make it soft. Scrape the surface with a hand hoe, break up the clods, and gather the soil toward the base of the plant. Second, be careful if the pegs have already started to extend. If a thin stalk is cut, that pod is lost. Third, mixing in powdered roasted shells or eggshells puts calcium exactly where it is needed.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Mulched cultivation raises a different problem, because plastic film blocks the pegs. When mulch is used, therefore, the film around each plant must be slit or pulled away around the start of flowering. If this task is forgotten, the whole gain from warmer soil is lost. Straw mulch can be pierced by the pegs, so the problem is less serious with it.

### 8-2. Weed Management

| Timing | Method | Key point |
|---|---|---|
| Early growth | Weeding | Scrape shallowly |
| Before flowering | Final weeding together with hilling | Do not walk into the field afterward |
| After flowering | Pull only large weeds within easy reach | Take care not to tread on anything |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The rule of not entering the field after flowering is especially strict with peanuts. When a person passes through and treads on the ground, the pegs extending into the soil are broken, and even pegs already in the soil are pressed by the compacted ground so that the pods cannot grow. However unsightly the weeds are, it is better to put up with them after flowering. That is why mulching, or thorough weeding before flowering, becomes more important.

## 9. Pests and Diseases

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The Pest & disease pressure grade for peanuts is C, and their Pest-control self-reliance is also C. This is on the low side among legumes and means that pests and diseases are not easy to manage without chemicals. The estimated Yield loss without pest control is 55.8%, higher than the 48.9% of other legumes, because fungal diseases that attack the leaves and soil-borne diseases arrive together.

### 9-1. Major Diseases

| Disease | Symptoms | When it occurs (South Korea) | Response without chemicals |
|---|---|---|---|
| Brown leaf spot | Dark brown spots appear on the leaves, ringed by a yellow band. Leaves drop early | Mid-July to harvest | Secure ventilation, remove diseased leaves, Bordeaux mixture, crop rotation. If leaves drop early, yield falls by more than half |
| Black leaf spot | Similar to brown leaf spot, but the spots are black | Autumn | Handle the same way as brown leaf spot |
| Net blotch | Yellow-brown net-patterned lesions appear | Late August to September | Secure ventilation, remove diseased leaves |
| Rust | Orange pustules appear on the underside of the leaves | Humid weather in the monsoon season (jangma, late June to July in South Korea) | Secure ventilation |
| Southern blight | White threadlike mold spreads at the base of the stem, and the plant wilts and dies | After a hot, humid monsoon | Dig up the diseased plant together with its soil and burn it. Improve drainage, long crop rotation |
| Baekseo disease | Roots and pods rot. Soil-borne | Mid-to-late growth | A 4-5 year rotation is the only remedy |
| Viral disease | Leaves become mottled in a mosaic pattern | Spread by aphids and thrips | Cannot be treated. Pull up and burn the plants and reduce the vector insects |

> **Brown leaf spot causes the greatest losses in peanuts. When leaves drop early, the peanuts cannot fill, and yield falls by half to 60%. This is because it is the leaves above ground that feed the pods underground.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The order of response to brown leaf spot without chemicals is as follows. First, space the plants generously to secure ventilation. When leaves stay wet for less time, spores cannot gain a foothold. Second, from mid-July, inspect the field, pick off the lower leaves that show spots, and take them out of the field. Third, if you can make Bordeaux mixture, spray it at 10-15 day intervals from mid-July. Fourth, burn diseased residue instead of returning it to the field. Fifth, select and carry on resistant lines. The Namgwang peanut lines are known to be comparatively resistant to brown leaf spot.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Southern blight and baekseo disease are different in character. They persist in the soil for many years, and chemicals do not work well against them either. A 4-5 year rotation is the only fundamental remedy, and a plant that shows the disease must be dug out together with the surrounding soil and burned. The reason the Continuous-cropping tolerance of peanuts is C is these two diseases.

### 9-2. Major Pests

| Pest | Damage | Control without chemicals |
|---|---|---|
| White grubs | Gnaw roots and pods in the soil. The worst pest of peanuts | Catch them as you see them when tilling. Do not apply immature compost. Crop rotation |
| Aphids | Suck sap and transmit viruses | Soapy water (5g of soap per 1L of water), protect ladybugs |
| Thrips | Suck sap and transmit viruses | Shake flowers over white paper to check for infestation |
| Spider mites | Suck sap from the underside of the leaves, leaving them mottled yellow | Increase when it is dry after the monsoon. Spray water forcefully on the leaves |
| Field mice and moles | Eat the pods in the soil | Tidy the field edges and block their passages |
| Birds | Dig up and eat shallowly buried seed right after sowing | Cover with straw or netting until germination |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;White grubs are a special problem in peanuts because the harvested product lies in the soil. In other crops they only nibble at the roots, but in peanuts they eat the product itself. When you dig the plants up, the shells have holes and the insides are empty. Worse, those wounds become pathways for mold and the starting point of aflatoxin. Because under-composted manure draws white grubs, always use fully matured compost, and catch and destroy any grubs you see when tilling.

### 9-3. Pest-Control Materials Without Chemicals

| Material | How to make it | Target | Caution |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bordeaux mixture | In 100L of water, dissolve 400g of copper sulfate and 400g of quicklime separately, then mix | Brown leaf spot, black leaf spot, rust | Can be made only if copper is available. Preventive use |
| Soapy water | 5g of unscented soap in 1L of water | Aphids, mites, thrips | Spraying in the midday heat scorches the leaves |
| Dry ash dusting | Dust fine ash onto the leaves on a dewy morning | Suppresses mold, leaf-eating insects | Also supplies potassium |
| Wood ash water | Soak 1 doe (about 1.8 L) of ash in 10L of water overnight and use the clear liquid on top | Suppresses mold | Alkaline, so use it dilute |
| Wood vinegar | Dilute the liquid collected when making charcoal 300-500 times | Repellent, growth promotion | The undiluted liquid is strongly acidic, so always dilute |
| Diluted milk | Dilute milk 5-10 times with water | Powdery mildew, mites | Leftovers spoil and smell |

### 9-4. Cultural Control

| Method | Principle |
|---|---|
| 4-5 year crop rotation | Waits for the southern blight and baekseo disease pathogens to decline in the soil. The most important |
| Do not place legumes or tobacco before or after | They feed the same soil pathogens |
| Drainage and raised beds | Southern blight comes after a hot, humid monsoon |
| Secure planting distance | With ventilation, brown leaf spot spreads later |
| Use only fully matured compost | Immature compost attracts white grubs |
| Remove diseased leaves and plants | Take them out of the field and burn them as soon as they are found |
| Seed selection | Do not plant seed that smells moldy |
| Selecting resistant lines | In a field where disease is going around, carry on the plants that remain clean |

## 10. Nutrient Deficiency and Excess

| Symptom | Cause | Remedy with natural materials |
|---|---|---|
| Pods form but are empty inside | Calcium deficiency. The most characteristic symptom in peanuts | Sprinkle roasted shell and eggshell powder at the base of the plants and hill up the soil |
| Kernels are small and shriveled | Shortage of potassium or calcium | Use wood ash together with roasted shell powder |
| Whole leaf is pale yellow, plant is small | Nitrogen deficiency. Poor nodulation may be the cause | First dig up a root and check the nodules |
| Growth is slow and leaves take on a purplish tint | Phosphorus deficiency | Bone meal, roasted fish-bone powder |
| Leaf edges dry out brown | Potassium deficiency | Scatter wood ash between the plants |
| A black core forms inside the pod | Boron deficiency | Compost made from a mix of materials |
| Yellowing between the leaf veins | Iron or manganese deficiency. Common in soil leaning alkaline | Stop adding lime and ash. Increase organic matter with leaf mold |
| Only stems and leaves are lush, with few pods | Excess nitrogen | Stop the nitrogen supply immediately |

> **Empty pods are the most common and most regrettable failure in peanuts. The plants grow well in the field and set many pods, but when you dig them up and shell them, the insides are empty. The cause is almost always calcium, and the remedy is to put calcium into the soil during flowering.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;What sets this symptom apart from deficiencies in other crops is the absorption route. Peanut pods do not use calcium sent from the roots; they absorb it directly from the surrounding soil through their own shells. So even if calcium is present deep around the roots, it does no good, and calcium must be in the layer a few cm below the soil surface where the pods grow. This is why mixing calcium powder into the hilling at flowering is the most effective approach.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Overdoing lime causes the opposite problem. Above pH 7.5, the absorption of iron, manganese, and zinc is blocked, and yellowing between the leaf veins appears. Treat 150-200kg per 1,000m² as the upper limit for base fertilizer, and use any more only locally at flowering.

## 11. Intercropping and Crop Rotation

### 11-1. Compatibility

| Compatibility | Crop | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Good | Maize, foxtail millet, sorghum | They share no diseases and use the nitrogen the peanuts leave behind |
| Good | Sesame | Works well as the crop before or after |
| Good | Chili pepper, watermelon | Suitable as rotation partners |
| Poor | Tobacco | Feeds the same soil pathogens |
| Poor | Other legumes | Pathogens and nematodes accumulate |
| Poor | Peanuts after peanuts | Southern blight and baekseo disease cut yield sharply |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Peanuts are a crop that finds its place in rotation rather than in intercropping. The growing period is long and the field cannot be entered after flowering, so it is hard to share a field with other crops. If you do mix, the realistic option is to stand maize along the field edge as a windbreak.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The rule of not placing tobacco before or after peanuts deserves emphasis. Tobacco and peanuts share some soil-borne diseases, so planting peanuts in a field that grew tobacco increases southern blight. Legume crops are avoided in the same way.

### 11-2. Crop Rotation

| Item | Standard |
|---|---|
| Rotation break | 4-5 years |
| Ideal cycle | 5 years |
| Continuous-cropping damage | Southern blight and baekseo disease remain in the soil and yield falls sharply |

| Example of a 5-year rotation |
|---|
| Year 1: Peanuts |
| Year 2: Maize or foxtail millet |
| Year 3: Sesame or chili pepper |
| Year 4: Watermelon or root vegetables |
| Year 5: Grain or leafy vegetables |
| Then peanuts again |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The peanut's Continuous-cropping tolerance of C is the heaviest constraint among legumes after the pea. Keeping to 4-5 years requires dividing the field into five plots, which is not easy where the field is small. Compromises include moving each year among sandy spots in a corner of the field, or not planting peanuts every year but only every second or third year. Because seed longevity is under a year, planting every other year would break the seed line, so in that case a few plants must be sown separately for seed every year, even in small numbers.

> **Peanut seed has a short life, so peanuts must be planted every year, and continuous-cropping damage is severe, so they cannot be planted in the same spot. Peanuts are the only crop under both constraints, which is why the field layout must be planned in advance.**

## 12. Harvest and Drying

### 12-1. Judging When to Harvest

| Criterion | Details |
|---|---|
| Leaves | Lower leaves turn yellow and wither first |
| Pods | In 60-80% of the pods dug as a trial, the net pattern is distinct |
| Inside the shell | When a pod is split, the inner wall has turned brown or black |
| Kernels | The inner seed-coat color has become distinct, the variety's own color |
| Days after sowing | 130-150 days |
| Timing | Central South Korea: late September to early October; southern South Korea: early October to mid-November |

> **Peanuts are in the soil, so how ripe they are cannot be judged from the outside. Before harvesting, always dig up two or three plants as a trial and split the pods to check the color of the inner wall. This one check is the whole of the harvest-timing decision.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The color of the inner wall of the pod is the most reliable indicator. Immature pods are white and smooth inside, while mature pods are mottled brown or black inside. This color change does not reverse, so there is little room for misreading. If, among pods dug from several plants and split, 60-80% have matured, it is the right time to harvest.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Judging harvest timing is tricky because flowering continues for nearly 100 days. Pods from late flowers are still immature, while pods from early flowers already have decayed pegs and are ready to fall into the soil. Dig too early and many are immature; dig too late and well-filled pods drop into the soil and are simply lost. The 60-80% standard is the compromise point.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Frost also enters the decision. Frost kills peanut leaves, and once the leaves are dead the pods no longer fill. If a frost is forecast, it is better to dig even if the pods are not yet mature. Boil immature peanuts to eat them or consume them early, and store only the well-filled ones.

### 12-2. How to Harvest

| Step | Details |
|---|---|
| 1 | Choose a clear, dry day. Right after rain, wait until the soil dries |
| 2 | Dig beside the plant with a hand hoe or shovel to lift the soil, then grasp the stem and pull up |
| 3 | Shake off the soil, but not hard. The pods will fall off |
| 4 | Turn the pulled plants over with the pods facing up and lay them in the field for a few days for preliminary drying |
| 5 | Pods that have changed color or spoiled are picked out at this stage |
| 6 | Hang them upside down or spread them under the eaves or in a barn for main drying |
| 7 | Return the stems, leaves, and roots, with the pods removed, to the field |

> **Never dig peanuts right after rain. Wet soil clings to the shells, and if they are piled up like that, mold begins within a day or two. This is usually where aflatoxin starts.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In upright varieties, most pods come up with the plant when it is pulled. In creeping types or in fields with hard soil, the pegs break and pods stay in the soil, so go over the spot where the plant was pulled once more by hand or with a hand hoe to find the remaining pods. This task makes a noticeable difference in yield.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Preliminary drying, laying the plants out in the field for a few days, is important. If the pulled plants are turned with the pods facing up, the soil and moisture on the outside dry quickly in the sun and wind. If rain is forecast, however, bring them in at once, and in seasons of heavy night dew it is safer to spread them out only by day and bring them in at evening.

### 12-3. Drying - The Only Way to Prevent Aflatoxin

| Step | Method | Check |
|---|---|---|
| Preliminary drying | 3-7 days in the field or in shade, with the plants still intact | Soil and moisture on the outside dry |
| Separating pods | Pull or shake them off by hand | Pick out spoiled ones at this point |
| Main drying | Spread thinly on trays or mats and dry in well-ventilated shade | Strong sun makes the shells crack |
| Judging dryness | When you shake a pod, the kernel rattles inside | The most reliable test |
| Target moisture | 10% or less | Higher than this and mold grows |
| Sorting | Discard those that are discolored, shriveled, or holed | One kernel contaminates the whole batch |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Judging by the rattle when shaken is the surest method without tools. In a pod that is not yet dry, the kernel is stuck to the shell and makes no sound; once fully dry, the kernel shrinks and moves around inside the shell. If you shake several and most make a sound, drying is finished.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;It is also a knack not to dry for long in strong sun. Long exposure to direct sunlight cracks the shells, begins to turn the fat rancid, and fades the color of the inner seed coat. A cracked shell becomes a pathway for mold. It is better to dry slowly in well-ventilated shade or under the eaves, and since this takes longer, start early.
