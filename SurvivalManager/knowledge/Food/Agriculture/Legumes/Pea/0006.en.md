## 13. Seed Saving

| Item | Method |
|---|---|
| Selecting seed plants | Mark in the field in advance the plants that show no disease symptoms, carry many pods, and ripen early |
| Harvest time | Harvest after the pods have turned completely brown, a little later than for eating |
| Separation | Thresh separately. Shelling by hand does not damage the seeds |
| Sorting | Discard every seed that is spotted, wrinkled, discolored, or small. Keep only large, glossy seeds |
| Drying | Moisture 12-13%. A properly dried seed splits crisply when bitten, without the teeth sinking in |
| Storage | Keep in an airtight container in a cool, dark place. Use a container separate from the one for food |
| Storage period | 2-3 years airtight at room temperature; up to 4 years in good conditions |
| Renewal | Every 3 years, obtain seed from another place and mix some in |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Peas are among the easiest crops to save seed from. Fertilization is already finished inside the flower before it opens, and the petals wrap the pistil and stamens so completely that almost no outside pollen can enter. This is why Mendel chose peas as his experimental material, and it means that several varieties planted side by side keep their traits unmixed. There is no need to worry about isolation distance.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;What needs care is seed-borne disease. Brown spot passes to the next generation along with the seed. Picking out seeds with visible spots prevents a good part of it, but latent infections that show no symptoms are not screened out. Obtaining peas from another valley or a neighboring village every 3 years and mixing some in eases this problem.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The habit of marking seed plants in the field beforehand changes the result greatly. If you choose by looking only at the seeds after harvest, you select for nothing but size and shape. Choosing in the field lets you select together the traits that really matter: number of pods, resistance to wilt, heat tolerance, and early ripening. With peas in particular, picking out the plants that ripen before the heat arrives is valuable. Repeating this for just a few years produces a strain matched to the timing of summer in that field.

| Seed storage period and germination rate |
|---|
| Year of harvest: 90% or more |
| After 1 year: 80-90% |
| After 2 years: 60-80% |
| After 3 years: 40-60% |
| After 4 years or more: drops sharply |

## 14. Toxicity and Antinutritional Compounds

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Peas are among the legumes with the lowest toxicity risk. They contain neither the lectin that, as in kidney beans, causes poisoning from a few raw seeds, nor the compound that, as in fava beans, triggers acute hemolysis in people of a particular constitution. A few raw green peas do no harm. Dried peas, however, contain antinutritional compounds just as other beans do, and they need treatment when eaten in quantity.

| Compound | What it does | Symptoms | How to remove it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Trypsin inhibitor | Blocks the action of protein-digesting enzymes | Abdominal discomfort, lower protein utilization | Soak, then heat in boiling water for 20-30 minutes. Content is much lower than in soybeans |
| Lectin | Binds to the intestinal lining and interferes with absorption | Harmless in small amounts | Easily destroyed by heating. Unlike kidney beans, its toxicity is very weak |
| Phytic acid | Binds iron, zinc, and calcium and blocks their absorption | Anemia and zinc deficiency with long-term consumption | Soaking, sprouting, and fermentation reduce it by up to 60-80% |
| Oligosaccharides (raffinose and stachyose) | Humans cannot digest them, so gut bacteria ferment them | Gas and bloating | Discard the soaking water after soaking |
| Saponin | Makes foam | Harmless in small amounts | Skim off the foam while boiling |
| Mycotoxins | Produced by mold during storage | Dangerous | Moldy peas do not become safe by washing or cooking. Discard them |

> **Peas themselves are safe. What actually harms people is not the pea but the pea that was stored badly and grew mold. If there is a musty smell or a bluish-green powder on the surface, discard them without hesitation, however wasteful it feels.**

| Treatment | Time and temperature | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Soaking | 4-8 hours in room-temperature water | Heat reaches the center, and oligosaccharides leach into the water |
| Discarding the soaking water | Always | Throws away the dissolved oligosaccharides and antinutritional compounds along with it |
| Boiling | 20-30 minutes in boiling water | Less than half of the 60 minutes needed for soybeans |
| Split peas | Peas with the skin removed and split in half | Cook in 20 minutes without soaking. Uses the least fuel |
| Checking doneness | Mashes easily when pressed with a finger | If a hard core remains, they are undercooked |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The Cooking & processing grade of C is one step better than soybeans' D, and that difference shows up as fuel. Soybeans must be soaked for 8 hours and boiled for more than an hour, but peas need a four-hour soak and 30 minutes of cooking. Peas that have been skinned and split in half soften in 20 minutes even without soaking. Where fuel is scarce, this difference accumulates every day.

## 15. Storage Without Refrigeration

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The storability of peas is SS. Dried seeds last 3 years at room temperature, and their fat content, only 1-2.5%, is so low that rancidity is slow too. But if conditions go wrong, a single species, the pea weevil, can turn an entire store of peas into empty shells.

> **Beans are lost not to rot but to insects eating them. The art of storage is, in effect, the art of keeping insects out.**

### 15-1. Drying Comes First

| Step | Method | Check |
|---|---|---|
| Preliminary drying | Hang the whole vines upside down under the eaves for two to three days | The pods dry crisp |
| Drying after threshing | Spread thinly on a straw mat or woven mat and sun-dry for 2-3 days | Turn often so they dry evenly |
| Target moisture | 12-13% for eating, 12% for seed | When bitten, the pea splits crisply without the teeth sinking in |
| Salt test | Put dry salt and peas in a jar and shake | If the salt sticks to the jar wall, the peas are still damp |
| Cooling | Bring in from the sun and let cool completely before packing | Sealing them warm causes condensation |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;If drying is insufficient, every measure that follows fails. Above 15% moisture mold grows, and above 13% weevils breed faster. Conversely, below 10% the seed coat cracks and the peas break during storage, and seed loses its germination ability.

### 15-2. Controlling Bean Weevils in Storage

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The pea weevil (Bruchus pisorum) is the greatest threat to stored peas. What makes it frightening is that it starts in the field. Adult weevils glue their eggs onto pods still hanging on the plant, and those eggs hatch inside the store and bore into the peas. The eggs come in with the peas, and since one generation takes less than a month, half a year is enough to fill a sack with shells riddled with holes.

| Method | Principle | How to apply | Effect |
|---|---|---|---|
| Complete sealing | Cuts off oxygen and suffocates the insects | Fill glass jars or metal containers to the brim and seal | The most reliable. In 3-4 weeks even eggs and larvae die |
| Mixing in wood ash | Fine particles scratch the waxy layer of the exoskeleton and dehydrate the insect | Mix in 2-5% of the pea volume | Rinse off with water before eating |
| Diatomaceous earth | Silica shells damage the exoskeleton | 1-3g per 1kg of peas | More effective than ash. Works only when dry |
| Oil coating | The oil film prevents eggs from attaching and hatching | Add 2-5mL of cooking oil per 1kg of peas and rub in | Very effective on legumes. Not used for seed |
| Cold treatment | Eggs and larvae die below freezing | Put outdoors for a few days in midwinter, then bring back in | Beware of condensation when bringing back in |
| Heat treatment | Eggs and larvae die at 60°C | Spread thinly in the sun for half a day | Not used for seed |
| Dried chili pepper, sancho pepper, mugwort | Essential oils suppress egg laying | Layer in among the peas | An auxiliary measure |
| Charcoal, roasted salt | Absorb moisture | Wrap in cloth and put in | Also prevents mold |
| Burying in ash | Gives sealing and physical control at once | Layer ash and peas in an earthenware jar | Widely used traditionally |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The most important measure is complete sealing. Insects breathe too. When the peas and insects use up the oxygen inside a sealed container, oxygen falls and carbon dioxide rises, and the insects die. The peas are dormant and tolerate these conditions. The more fully the container is filled and the less empty space it holds, the faster the effect. Packing several small containers tightly is better than using one large one, and this also has the advantage that if one container spoils, not everything is lost.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Oil coating is a method that small farms in India and Africa have long used. Add two or three mL of cooking oil per 1kg of peas and rub it in by hand so that every seed gets a thin film. The oil film makes it hard for adult weevils to attach eggs and blocks the breathing pores of eggs already attached, which prevents hatching. Use it only on the portion to be eaten within half a year, not on seed, because the oil film hinders respiration and lowers germination. Wood ash is sieved, and only the fine part is mixed in at 2 to 5% of the pea volume. Insects cannot develop resistance to it, and it is harmless to people.

### 15-3. Containers and Locations

| Container | Airtightness | Period | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|:---:|---|---|---|
| Glass jar | Very good | 3 years or more | You can check the condition by eye | Small capacity and does not block light |
| Metal container | Very good | 3 years or more | Completely blocks rats and insects | If condensation forms, that spot spoils |
| Earthenware jar | Good | 2-3 years | Temperature changes slowly, and rats cannot gnaw through | Heavy and breakable |
| Thick plastic bag | Fair | 1-2 years | Light and cheap | Rats gnaw through |
| Underground storage cellar | Good | 2-3 years | Temperature is constant and low | If water seeps in, the whole store is lost |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The most reliable senses for inspection are hearing and smell. If you put your ear to the container and hear a rustling sound, insects are active, and if there is a musty smell, mold has begun. Small round holes on the pea surface mean the weevils have gone through at least one generation. Even then, you can protect the remainder by taking everything out, sun-drying it, floating it in water to skim off the hollow peas, mixing it with ash or oil, and sealing it again. Inspect once every two months as a rule.

### 15-4. Short-Term Storage of Green Peas

| Approach | Method | Period |
|---|---|---|
| In the pod in a cool place | Do not wash; put in a ventilated basket | 3-5 days |
| Blanch and dry | Blanch lightly, drain, and dry in the sun until crisp | Several months. Revive by soaking in water |
| Salt pickling | Blanch, then layer with salt in an earthenware jar | Several months. Soak in water to remove the salt before eating |
| Underground storage | Keep in a cool underground cellar | About 1 week |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;From the moment green peas are picked, their sugar begins turning into starch, and after a day the sweetness drops noticeably. Without refrigeration there is practically no way to keep green peas for long. It comes down to one of three things: eat them the same day, blanch and dry them, or preserve them in salt. Blanched, dried peas soaked in water in winter and added to soup or porridge recover their color and flavor to a considerable degree.

## 16. Cooking and Processing

### 16-1. Pea Sprouts - A Winter Vegetable Grown Without Soil

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Dried peas contain virtually no vitamin C. Yet once they sprout, vitamin C that was not there appears, because the physiological shift of germination makes the plant synthesize it anew. In winter, when there are no fresh vegetables at all, a handful of dried peas, water, and a container are enough to produce fresh vegetables in five to seven days. No field or soil is needed.

| Step | Procedure | Key point |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Selecting | Discard cracked or insect-eaten peas | Cracked peas rot and spoil the whole batch |
| 2. Washing | Rinse several times and skim off the ones that float | Floating peas are usually dead |
| 3. Soaking | Soak in lukewarm water for 6-8 hours | The peas should swell to twice their size |
| 4. Placing | Put in a container that drains and cover with a damp cloth | Standing water causes rot |
| 5. Watering | Pour water over them generously from above 2-3 times a day and let it run through | Also removes waste and regulates temperature |
| 6. Harvest | 5-7 days, stems 5-10cm | Unlike kongnamul (soybean sprouts), light is allowed |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Pea sprouts differ from kongnamul. Kongnamul is grown with light completely blocked to produce white stems, but pea sprouts are more nutritious when exposed to light and allowed to produce green leaves. Keep them dark for the first three or four days so the roots and stems extend, then move them to a bright place for two days; the leaves turn green and chlorophyll and vitamins increase. These green leaves are called sunchae (shoot greens) and are eaten as namul (seasoned vegetable side dish).

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;If you sow peas thickly in a shallow box of soil, you get longer, sturdier shoots. When they reach 15-20cm, cut them at the base with scissors to harvest; if left as they are, they grow once more. Rotating two or three boxes indoors in winter yields green vegetables without interruption. Vitamin C deficiency develops quietly over months and then shows up as bleeding gums and wounds that do not heal; pea sprouts are a cheap and reliable answer to this problem.

### 16-2. Fuel-Saving Cooking

| Method | Fuel | Procedure |
|---|:---:|---|
| Split-pea porridge | Very little | Skinned, split peas turn soft in 20 minutes without soaking |
| Pressure cooking | Least | Pressure-cook soaked peas for 10 minutes |
| Retained-heat cooking | Very little | Boil for 10 minutes, turn off the heat, wrap the pot in a box of straw or rice husks or in quilts, and leave for 1-2 hours |
| Roasted peas | Little | Roast dried peas over low heat. They can be eaten as they are |
| Pea flour | Almost none | Roast and grind, then mix into water to eat. Low in fat, so it keeps long |
| Sprouts | None | Germination needs no heat |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Split peas are an especially valuable form under disaster conditions. Pound dried peas lightly in a mortar and blow off the skins, and the peas split in half on their own. In this state, water reaches the center at once, so no soaking is needed, and they mash down like porridge in 20 minutes. That India's dal and Europe's pea soup both use this form reflects long-standing wisdom about saving fuel. The skins should not be thrown away either; collect them and use them as livestock feed or compost.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Retained-heat cooking is also very effective with peas. Boil for only 10 minutes and put the pot in an insulating box, and the inside temperature stays high for more than an hour while cooking continues. Unlike soybeans, peas have no strong toxic compounds that must be destroyed completely, so retained-heat cooking cooks them safely. A wooden box filled with straw or rice husks, or a thick quilt wrapped around the whole pot, serves as the insulating box.

### 16-3. Processing for Storage

| Product | Procedure summary | Storage | Value |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pea flour | Roast and grind | 3-6 months airtight | No cooking fuel needed |
| Blanched, dried green peas | Blanch, drain, and dry until crisp | Several months | Added to soup and porridge in winter |
| Pea doenjang (Korean soybean paste) | Boil and mash, ferment like meju (fermented soybean blocks), then immerse in brine | Several years | An alternative when soybeans are short |
| Pickled pea shoots | Salt young shoots | Several months | Carries spring greens through to winter |
| Roasted peas | Roast over low heat | 1-2 months airtight | A snack and emergency food |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Making doenjang-style paste (jang) from peas is not a widely known method, but the principle is the same as with soybeans. Boil and mash the peas, shape them into blocks, wrap them in straw to ferment, and then immerse them in brine. Peas have less protein than soybeans and so less savory depth, but fermentation breaks down antinutritional compounds and raises digestibility, and with salt the paste keeps for several years. It is a route worth trying in a year when soybeans could not be secured and peas are plentiful.

## 17. Annual Work Calendar

| Month (South Korea) | Work | Key points |
|---|---|---|
| January | Inspect stored peas, test seed germination, repair stake materials | Put an ear to the container to listen for insects. Grow sprouts indoors |
| February | Plan the field, apply lime, turn the compost, early sowing in late February | Sow as soon as the soil thaws. Hurrying is almost always right |
| March | Main sowing, bird protection, setting up stakes | Count back 90 days from the first hot weather in the local area to find the last sowing date |
| April | Thinning, first weeding, guiding the vines, picking young shoots | Eat the picked shoots as namul. Aphids are removed along with them |
| May | Flowering, intensive watering, aphid control, checking root nodules | Dig up a root, split a nodule, and check for red color |
| June | Successive harvest of green peas; harvest of autumn-sown peas (southern South Korea) | Pick diligently every three or four days. Picking encourages more pods |
| July | Harvest of dry seed, hanging to dry, threshing, returning stems and leaves to the field | If rain is forecast, do not wait for full maturity |
| August | Cleaning, drying, storage, tidying the field | Do not burn the vines; chop them up and spread them over the soil |
| September | Preparing the field for autumn sowing, applying lime | Autumn cropping works only in southern South Korea |
| October | Autumn sowing (southern South Korea), preparing for overwintering | Set the sowing date 40-50 days ahead of the first frost |
| November | Covering with straw and fallen leaves, checking drainage ditches | Prevents frozen ground from heaving the plants |
| December | Inspect stored peas, grow sprouts, plan for next year | Winter vitamin C comes from sprouts |

### 17-1. Five Things Not to Forget

| Item | Details |
|---|---|
| Sowing time | Planting late is the biggest cause of failure. Plant early even if the plants take a little frost |
| Continuous cropping | A rotation break of 5-7 years is needed, the longest of any legume. Ignore it and you lose the field |
| Stakes | Tendrils grab only thin supports. Driving in only thick posts is of no use |
| Stems and leaves | If vines, leaves, and roots are not returned to the field, peas become a crop that robs the soil |
| Harvest | Picking encourages more pods, and leaving the pods stops production. Harvest in succession every three or four days |
