## 5. Field Preparation and Fertilizer - Nitrogen Balance and Fertilization

### 5-4. The Trap of Nitrogen Fixation

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The saying that planting beans improves the land has hardened into common sense in farming. Half of it is right and half of it is wrong. Without understanding this point precisely, a field planted with legumes year after year can end up becoming barren instead.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Let us work it out in numbers. When peas grow well, 1,000m² yields 250kg of dried seed. The protein in pea seed is 25.4%, and dividing protein by 6.25 gives the amount of nitrogen. 25.4 ÷ 6.25 is 4.06, so about 4% of the seed's weight is pure nitrogen. The nitrogen contained in 250kg is about 10kg, which converts to 100kg per hectare.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Meanwhile, the nitrogen fixed by pea nodules is 80-130kg per hectare when conditions are good. That is the value when inoculation has gone well, the acidity is right, and the water is adequate; when conditions are poor it stops at 30kg. Putting what goes out next to what comes in gives this.

| Item | Nitrogen per hectare | Direction |
|---|---|---|
| Fixed by nodules (good conditions) | 80-130kg | In |
| Fixed by nodules (poor conditions) | 25-50kg | In |
| Removed in the seed | 90-110kg | Out |
| Nitrogen left in foliage (vines and leaves) | 25-40kg | Stays if returned; leaves if burned or carried off |
| Nitrogen left in roots and nodules | 10-20kg | Mostly stays in the field |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The table makes it clear. If only the seed is harvested and the vines and leaves are returned to the field, the fixed amount and the amount removed are roughly equal, or there is a small surplus. But if the pea vines are also cleared away and carried off as livestock feed or burned, the nitrogen balance turns decisively negative. That is, more nitrogen leaves the pea field than enters it.

> **Legumes restore the land not because they fix nitrogen, but because they leave part of the fixed nitrogen in the field, stored in stems and leaves. If those stems and leaves are carried away, beans become a crop that plunders the land.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The pea has one favorable condition for solving this problem: the harvest comes as early as June. Soybeans are harvested in October, and even if the foliage is returned, almost no decomposition proceeds over the winter, but pea foliage rots quickly in June's warm, moist soil. On top of that, the pea stem is hollow and watery, and its tissue is soft. Cut and spread over the soil, it loses its shape within a few weeks and its nitrogen is used immediately by the summer crop. The long-standing custom of using the pea as green manure rests on this.

| Principle | Details |
|---|---|
| Foliage always goes back to the field | Return all the vines, leaves, and pod husks left over after threshing to the field |
| Do not burn it | When burned, nitrogen escapes as gas and only potassium and phosphorus remain in the ash |
| Do not dig up the roots | The nitrogen in roots and nodules is the share that is easiest to leave behind |
| Cut into 20-30cm pieces and spread over the soil | Serves as mulch and fertilizer in one and lowers soil temperature for the summer crop |
| The option of plowing under as green manure | Plowing the whole plant under at flowering leaves the most nitrogen behind. It gives up the seed in exchange for restoring the field |
| If fed to livestock, return the dung | If pea vines were fed to livestock, the manure of those animals must be put back on that same field |

### 5-5. Field Preparation

| Item | Method | Under disaster conditions |
|---|---|---|
| Tillage | Till to a depth of 20-30cm and level evenly | Turn the soil with a spade or dig it up with a hoe. If tilling the whole surface is difficult, dig and turn only the rows to be planted |
| Raised bed | Height of 20cm or more | The key to preventing waterlogging injury |
| Drainage ditch | Depth of 30-50cm | The crop's season overlaps with the spring rains and the start of the monsoon season (jangma, late June to July in South Korea) |
| Acidity correction | pH 6.5-7.5 | Done in advance, 15 days to 3 weeks before sowing |
| Lime | 150kg/1,000m² on acidic soil | Substitute roasted and ground shells or eggshells, or wood ash |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Lime has to be applied in advance because the reaction takes time. If it is applied just before sowing, the alkalinity becomes locally strong and damages the seed and young roots, and at the time it is actually needed the reaction has not progressed far enough. Also, if lime and nitrogen fertilizer are put in the same spot together, ammonia escapes as gas and nitrogen is lost. The order is kept: lime first, fertilizer two weeks later. If there is no lime, shells and eggshells are used. Both are calcium carbonate, so the principle is the same, but as they are the reaction is very slow, so they are roasted over a fire to make them brittle, then ground into powder in a mortar before use.

### 5-6. Fertilizer Rates

| Fertilizer | Per 1,000m² | Per m² | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Nitrogen | 8.7kg | 8.7g | All as base fertilizer. For the early stage before the nodules start working |
| Phosphorus | 50kg (as fused phosphate) | 50g | All as base fertilizer. Peas have a high phosphorus requirement among legumes |
| Potassium | 16.8kg | 16.8g | All as base fertilizer |
| Compost | 1,500kg | 1.5kg | Use fully rotted compost |
| Lime | 150kg (acidic soil) | 150g | 15 days before sowing |

> **Holding back nitrogen on peas is not thrift but technique. Too much makes only the vines lush and the pods fewer, and the heavier vines fall over and disease spreads.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;As a rule, top-dressing is not needed. However, if around flowering the leaves turn a pale yellow overall and growth is clearly poor, it is a sign that the nodules are not working properly. Only then is a small amount supplemented. In a disaster, diluting urine 10-fold with water and pouring it on the soil between plants is the easiest alternative. Keep it off the leaves, and apply it dilute and several times rather than in one large dose.

### 5-7. Substitute Fertilizers for Disasters

| Substitute resource | Nutrients supplied | How to use | Caution |
|---|---|---|---|
| Wood ash | Potassium, phosphorus, calcium | Spread 100-200g per m² and mix in | Alkaline. Peas are relatively tolerant of alkalinity, so they have more leeway than other legumes |
| Well-rotted compost | All-round | Apply 1-1.5kg per m² as base fertilizer | Underrotted compost burns roots and carries disease |
| Diluted urine | Nitrogen, potassium | Dilute 10-fold with water and pour on the soil between plants | Keep it off the leaves. Small amounts only |
| Bone meal | Phosphorus, calcium | Roast, grind, and mix into compost | The key material for meeting the pea's high phosphorus requirement |
| Fish bones and fish entrails | Phosphorus, nitrogen | Bury and let them decompose, then use | If simply buried, animals dig them up |
| Shells and eggshells | Calcium | Roast, grind, and scatter | A substitute for lime. The reaction is slow |
| Leaf mold | All-round, organic matter | Mix into the soil | Improves water retention |
| Rice hulls and rice straw | Potassium, silica | Spread as mulch and work in the following year | Working in material that is not yet decomposed robs the soil of nitrogen |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The most striking figure in pea fertilization is phosphorus. 50kg per 1,000m² is about six times the amount of nitrogen, unusually high among legumes. Phosphorus is a key element in the energy metabolism of nitrogen fixation, and if phosphorus is short, pea nodules, even when they form, fail to do their share. Where there are no chemical fertilizers, bone meal and fish bones are practically the only routes to phosphorus. If animal bones are collected, roasted until they crumble easily, ground in a mortar, and mixed into compost, they release their phosphorus slowly over many years. Bones and entrails left over after eating fish are likewise buried in a corner of the field to decompose and used later.

## 6. Sowing

### 6-1. Sowing Time

| Cropping system | Sowing time | Harvest time | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Spring cultivation (across South Korea) | Late February to early April | Mid-June to late July | The most common. The earlier the better |
| Autumn cultivation (southern South Korea) | Early October to the end of October | Mid-May to mid-June of the following year | Overwintering cultivation. The harvest comes earlier |
| Sprout and shoot harvest | Year-round | 5-20 days after sowing | Possible indoors as well |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The sowing time of peas is calculated backward. Find the day in the area when the daytime high begins to exceed 25°C, and counting back 85-95 days from there gives the latest sowing date. In central South Korea, early April is usually the limit, mid-March is the best time, and sowing in late February, when the soil thaws, is also fine. Peas recover by themselves from leaf tips being damaged by a late frost.

> **Most failures in pea sowing come from planting late. While sowing is put off for fear of frost, the heat approaches, and if the flowering period is pushed into the heat, the flowers simply drop.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Autumn sowing is a strategy for southern South Korea. Peas are sown between early and late October and brought through the winter at two or three true leaves and a height of about 10cm. If sown too early and they enter winter large, they will instead freeze to death, so the trick is to set the sowing date 40-50 days before the first frost. In winter, straw or fallen leaves are spread over them to keep frozen ground from heaving. In inland central South Korea this cropping system mostly fails, so spring sowing is used instead.

### 6-2. Seed Preparation

| Item | Method | Under disaster conditions |
|---|---|---|
| Sorting | Discard seed with spots, discoloration, wrinkles, or insect holes | Sort by hand, one seed at a time. The cheapest and most effective task |
| Size grading | Select and use large, heavy seeds | Float them in water and skim off those that float |
| Hot-water disinfection | Soak in 50-55°C water for 10-15 minutes to kill surface pathogens | If the temperature is exceeded, germination ability drops |
| Ash treatment | Coat with fine wood ash | Aids disinfection and drying and repels birds and insects |
| Nodule inoculation | Coat with soil from a field that grew the same legume | See Section 5-3. Not done together with disinfection |
| Soaking | Soak in water for a few hours before planting | Germination speeds up, but soaking too long causes rot. Do not exceed 6 hours |
| Germination test | Wrap 20 seeds in a wet cloth, leave for a few days, and count how many sprout | Always check old seed first |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Unlike soybeans, peas must not be soaked for long. The seed coat is thin and the tissue soft, so if soaked for more than 8 hours the inside turns mushy and rots right away in the soil. If the soil is sufficiently moist, it is safer to plant without soaking at all; if the soil is dry, soak for only three or four hours, dry off the surface moisture, and then plant.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There is a knack to controlling the temperature in hot-water treatment. Without a thermometer, the range of 50-55°C is the temperature at which it is hard to keep a hand in the water for 5 seconds. The amount of water should be at least ten times that of the seed so that the temperature does not drop suddenly when the seed is added. When finished, cool the seed right away in cold water, dry off the moisture, and plant. Above 60°C germination ability is damaged.

### 6-3. Sowing Method

| Item | Standard | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Sowing depth | 3-5cm | If shallow, it dries out and birds peck at it. Beyond 7cm the seedlings cannot emerge |
| Seeds per hole | 2-3 seeds | Allows for the germination rate. Later thin to leave 2 plants |
| Row spacing (single row) | 60cm | Secures airflow and an access path for management |
| Plant spacing | 20-30cm | Close for dwarf types, wide for tall types |
| Double-row cultivation | Bed width 60cm, furrow width 50cm | Plant two rows facing each other and set a support in the middle |
| Seeding rate | 60-80kg/1,000m² | A high seeding rate among legumes |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Double-row cultivation is a layout that saves on supports. If two rows are planted on one bed and only one support is set in the middle, the vines on both sides share the same support. Where materials are short, being able to cover two rows with one support makes a big difference.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Right after sowing, the biggest threats are birds and rodents. Pea seed is large and sweet, so pigeons, magpies, and pheasants dig it up and eat it, and rodents underground find it too. A thin layer of branches or straw is laid over the sowing rows, or netting or an old insect screen is placed over them until germination, or shiny string is criss-crossed over them. Coating the seed with fine wood ash repels pests through smell and feel. The surest measure is to walk the field every morning and evening for the first ten days of germination.

## 7. Water Management

| Stage | Water management | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Sowing to germination | Do not let the soil dry out completely | The seed must absorb water to germinate |
| Early growth | Leave it to natural rainfall | Dryness at this stage actually makes the roots reach deeper |
| Flowering to pod filling | Water generously so the soil does not dry out | This period directly determines yield |
| Maturation | Stop irrigation | Otherwise the pods do not dry and mold grows |
| Start of the monsoon (jangma) | Clear the drainage ditches in advance | Peas are very vulnerable to waterlogging injury |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Water management for peas is different in character from that of other legumes. Summer crops worry about drought, but peas worry more about excess moisture. This is because the growing period falls in the season of spring rains, the stem is hollow with soft tissue, and the root-nodule bacteria are aerobic. In a pea field, drainage comes before irrigation.

| Symptom | Cause | Action |
|---|---|---|
| Leaves droop at midday and recover in the evening | Temporary water shortage. Within the normal range | Leave as is |
| Leaves are still drooping in the morning | The roots cannot lift water | Water generously |
| Leaves turn grayish white and dry out | Severe dryness or high temperature | Irrigate and mulch together |
| Flowers drop in large numbers | Dryness during flowering or high temperature above 25°C | Lower the soil temperature with irrigation and mulch. If high temperature is the cause, recovery is difficult |
| Pods have set but the peas are small | Water shortage during pod filling | Irrigation at this stage directly governs yield |
| Lower leaves turn yellow and the stem base goes soft | Root rot from excess moisture | Clear the drainage ditch and hill up the soil. If severe, pull the plants out |
| The whole leaf is pale yellow but not wilting | Nitrogen shortage because the nodules are not working | Dig up the roots and check the nodules |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Where water is scarce, selection and concentration are the answer. Hold back in the early stage, and concentrate the water over the three weeks from the moment the first flower appears until the pods plump up. Rather than giving a little often, it is more advantageous to water once so that the soil is wet to a depth of 20cm and then lengthen the interval before the next watering, because this makes the roots go deep. Mulching is the cheapest means of saving water. Even without plastic film, covering the soil 5-10cm deep with straw, rice hulls, fallen leaves, or cut weeds cuts surface evaporation to half or less and eases swings in soil temperature. Since peas are weak in high temperatures, the effect of lowering soil temperature is especially valuable.
