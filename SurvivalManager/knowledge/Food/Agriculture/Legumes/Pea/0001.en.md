## Introduction
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Pea (Pisum sativum) is an annual climbing legume native to Southwest Asia and the eastern Mediterranean, one of the oldest cultivated crops. A plant of the Mediterranean climate, with rainy winters and dry summers, it grows when it is cool and dies when it gets hot, and while other beans are summer crops, pea alone is a spring and autumn crop. Sown in mid-March and harvested in June, or in the south sown in October and harvested the following May, it leaves the field empty by June, when beans are sown, so one field can give two harvests a year. The overall grade is A (48.4 points, 31st of 183 valid species), with short term B (48.2), long term A (48.6), and supplementary SS (64.7 points). Its protein-to-calorie ratio is 28.7%, almost three times the 10% minimum, it takes 85-95 days from sowing to harvest, uses about 400 mm of total evapotranspiration, and withstands -5 °C. It has three weaknesses. Calories per area is C: 885 kcal per ㎡, only 0.34 times the self-sufficiency line, so peas alone cannot fill the stomach; continuous-cropping tolerance is D, requiring a 5-7 year rest; and being a climber, it needs stake support.

| Evaluation | Items |
|:---|:---|
| Strengths | SS: protein density, fertilization, facility burden, storability. S: micronutrients, taste, domestic cultivation spread, seed saving. A: harvest speed, water consumption, cold tolerance, yield retention without synthetic inputs, yield stability. B: pest-control self-reliance, seed longevity |
| Weaknesses | D: perenniality, sunlight, continuous-cropping tolerance. C: calories per area, cooking and processing requirement, temperature range, pest and disease pressure, land-preparation and digging energy, non-food value |

> **The value of pea lies not in yield but in timing. Filling the fields of March to June, which no other crop uses, with a protein crop is why pea earned an A.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Few legumes carry both harvest speed A and cold tolerance A. Sown in early March and harvested in June, it fills the hardest stretch of late spring to early summer, when old grain has run out and the autumn harvest is far off, and green peas can be picked and eaten as soon as the pods fill out. Temperature range C comes from a fruit-set temperature of only 13-23 °C and flowers dropping above 25 °C. Pest and disease pressure C reflects an untreated loss rate of 48.9%, double soybean's 23%, but since pests and diseases rise with temperature, sowing and harvesting early avoids much of them, which is the basis of pest-control self-reliance B. The most overlooked weakness is continuous-cropping tolerance D. The grade for each item is in the Crop Evaluation Results document.

## 2. Plant Overview
| Item | Details |
|:---|:---|
| Classification | Annual climbing legume of the genus Pisum in the family Fabaceae. Self-pollinating (fertilization is complete before the flower opens) |
| Leaves, stem | Pinnate compound leaves whose tips turn into tendrils. Hollow climbing stem; dwarf types 30-60 cm, tall types 1-2 m |
| Roots, flowers | Straight taproot with fine roots and nodules. White or reddish-purple butterfly-shaped flowers |
| Pods, seeds | Cylindrical pods 5-10 cm long holding 4-10 seeds. Seeds are round or wrinkled, green, yellow, or grayish brown |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Pea flowers and ripens in turn from the lower nodes upward, so green peas must be picked several times at intervals of three or four days. If ripe pods are left on, the plant judges that it has finished reproducing and stops flowering, so picking is itself the management that raises yield. Nodules take 3-4 weeks to start handing over nitrogen, so putting a small amount of nitrogen in the base fertilizer is meaningful. Dry peas per 100 g: 354 kcal, protein 25.37 g, fat 1-2.5 g, about 60 g carbohydrate, 20-25 g dietary fiber, and 4-5 mg iron, with almost no vitamin C. Green peas have 80-97 kcal and 35 mg of vitamin C and serve as a fresh vegetable, so eat the early ones as green peas and dry the late ones to store as protein. Pea is rich in the lysine that grains lack and lacks the methionine that grains have, so eaten with rice the two complement each other, and its low fat keeps even the flour for a long time.

## 3. Growing Environment and Varieties
| Condition | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Temperature | Germination possible even at 1-2 °C (optimum 18-25 °C). Growth 4-25 °C (optimum 10-20 °C); above 25 °C growth stops and disease increases. Fruit set 13-23 °C (optimum 15-20 °C). Withstands -5 °C but is vulnerable to frost after flowering |
| Soil, water | Well-drained loam (waterlogged ground is the worst), pH 6.0-8.0 (optimum 6.5-7.5), water table 50 cm or deeper (optimum 80 cm or deeper) |

> **Every decision in growing pea is made in the direction of escaping the heat. When to sow, which variety to choose, and when to harvest are all counted back from the date of the first heat in that region.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Pea has the highest acidity requirement among legumes: below pH 6.0 growth worsens and nodule bacteria cannot live, so nitrogen fixation stops. Because the stem is hollow and the nodule bacteria are aerobic, even one day of flooding makes the roots and nodules lose function together, so raising ridges to 20 cm or more and digging deep drainage ditches are not optional.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Dwarf types (30-60 cm) need no stakes or only simple ones, so they take less work but yield less, while tall types (1-2 m) require stakes but yield more and are harvested over a long period in several passes. If stake materials and labor are plentiful, tall types are realistic; if both are short, dwarf types. Edible-pod types (snow pea, sugar snap) have no waste and are the most valuable, and must be picked young before a tough membrane forms. The first criterion for choosing a variety is whether seed can be saved: F1 hybrids segregate in the next year, but most cultivated varieties are fixed lines whose saved seed can be planted as it is, and local landraces are adapted to the local climate and diseases. In the south choose cold-hardy varieties for autumn sowing, and in a field where peas were grown, wilt-resistant lines.

## 4. Field Preparation and Sowing
| Item | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Field preparation | Plow 20-30 cm deep and dig drainage ditches 30-50 cm deep. On acidic soil, apply 150 kg of lime per 1,000 ㎡ 15 days to 3 weeks before sowing (roasted and crushed shells, eggshells, or wood ash can substitute). Leave a 2-week gap between lime and manure to prevent ammonia loss |
| Fertilization (1,000 ㎡) | Nitrogen 8.7 kg, phosphorus 50 kg (about 6 times the nitrogen), potassium 16.8 kg, and 1,500 kg of compost (all as basal fertilizer). Saving nitrogen is the skill: too much makes only the vines lush, pods fewer, and the plants fall over and spread disease. Make up the phosphorus with bone meal and roasted fish-bone powder, and apply wood ash at 100-200 g per ㎡. Top dressing is unnecessary, but if the leaves are pale yellow around flowering, give a small amount of urine diluted 10 times |
| Nodule inoculation | Coat seeds lightly moistened with water with soil from 5-15 cm depth of a field where pea, fava bean, lentil, or vetch grew and formed nodules well, and sow within 30 minutes in the shade (do not combine this with seed disinfection). Soil from a soybean field has different bacteria and is useless, and soil from a field with wilt should be avoided |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Check nodules around flowering (5-7 weeks after sowing) by lifting a plant with a spade together with its soil, washing it, and splitting the nodules. With 20 or more per plant and a red or pink interior, they are fixing nitrogen; white or pale green means they are not fixing; brown or black means dead (normal at ripening). With 5 or fewer, the inoculation failed.

| Sowing | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Timing | Spring sowing from late February to early April (optimum mid-March in the central region; the earlier the better). The last sowing date is counted back 85-95 days from the day the daily maximum temperature starts to exceed 25 °C. Autumn sowing in the south is from early to late October, counted 40-50 days before the first frost so that plants overwinter at 2-3 true leaves and about 10 cm tall, covered with straw or fallen leaves (if sown early and grown large they freeze to death, and in the inland central region it mostly fails) |
| Seed preparation | Discard seeds with spots, discoloration, wrinkles, or insect holes, disinfect by soaking in 50-55 °C water for 10-15 minutes and cooling immediately (above 60 °C germination is damaged). Soak for no more than 6 hours, and do not soak if the soil is moist |
| Method | Depth 3-5 cm, 2-3 seeds per hole (thin to the 2 strongest plants at 10 cm tall), row spacing 60 cm, plant spacing 20-30 cm, seed rate 60-80 kg per 1,000 ㎡. In double-row planting, two rows face each other on a 60 cm ridge with 50 cm furrows and share one stake in the middle, saving materials. To keep out birds and rats, cover with straw or netting until germination |

## 5. Water Management and Cultivation Management
> **Pea is a crop that worries about excess moisture more than drought, so drainage comes before irrigation, and drainage ditches are dug in advance before the rainy season starts. Since water directly decides yield from flowering to pod filling, if water is scarce, concentrate it in these 3 weeks, and rather than giving a little often, soak the soil to 20 cm deep at once.**

| Care | Method |
|:---|:---|
| Mulching | Cover with 5-10 cm of straw, rice hulls, or fallen leaves to cut surface evaporation by more than half and lower soil temperature (especially valuable for heat-sensitive pea) |
| Stake support | Vines lying on the ground spread disease and rot the pods. Tendrils grip only thin things about 5 mm in diameter, so do not drive only thick stakes; hang thin string or branches. String training is the most realistic: drive stakes at 2 m intervals, tie strings horizontally at 20, 50, and 80 cm above the ground, and raise them one level at a time as the vines grow. Twigs with leaves still on, stuck in at a slant along the row, need neither string nor netting, and netting gives the highest yield |
| Thinning, weeding | First scrape shallowly 2-3 weeks after sowing, and second 5-6 weeks after sowing combined with hilling. The first month of weeding is everything; after flowering the vines tangle and tear, so do not enter the field |
| Tip pinching | Pinching when vines reach the top of the stakes increases side shoots and also removes the growing tips where aphids gather. Blanch the pinched tips and eat them as greens |

## 6. Pests, Diseases, and Nutrient Problems
| Pest or disease | Symptoms and conditions | Control without chemicals |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Powdery mildew | White powder on leaves and stems as if flour were sprinkled. When days are hot, nights cool, and the weather dry; the biggest disease in the late growth stage | Secure airflow, spray milk diluted 5-10 times with water on a clear morning, early harvest |
| Brown spot | Brown spots on leaves, stems, and pods. When rainy and humid; seed-borne | Select seeds, secure airflow, Bordeaux mixture |
| Root rot, wilt | Wilting from the lower leaves with yellowing, and brown rot of the stem base and roots. Excess moisture, continuous cropping | A 5-7 year rotation is the only fundamental remedy; drainage and raised ridges |
| Aphids, virus disease | Cluster on growing tips and flower stalks to suck sap and carry virus disease (leaves curl and mottle and growth stops); the biggest pest | Tip pinching, soapy water (5 g of soap per 1 L of water). Virus disease cannot be treated, so pull up and burn at once |
| Pea weevil | Lays eggs on pods in the field and eats the seeds in storage | Store sealed after complete drying (see Section 9) |

> **Continuous-cropping damage in pea is the worst among legumes. The resting spores of wilt and root rot survive in the soil for many years, and a field once contaminated cannot be restored with chemicals; moving to another field is the only way.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Bordeaux mixture is made by dissolving 400 g of copper sulfate and 400 g of quicklime separately in 100 L of water, mixing them, and using it immediately.

| Symptom | Cause and action |
|:---|:---|
| Poor growth and short roots | Suspect acidity before fertilizer. Below pH 6.0, giving nitrogen will not restore it, so apply lime or roasted and crushed shell powder |
| Pale yellow from the lower leaves, small plant | Nitrogen deficiency (poor nodules). Dig up the roots and check the nodules; if there are none, give a small amount of urine diluted 10 times that year and make sure to inoculate next year |
| Flowers bloom but no pods set | Boron deficiency or high temperature at flowering. Compost made from many materials; if heat is the cause, sow earlier next year |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Nodules fix 80-130 kg of nitrogen per hectare (25-50 kg under poor conditions), 90-110 kg leaves with the seed, and 25-40 kg remains in the vines and leaves. If the vines are returned, the balance is about even, but carrying them off or burning them is a definite loss. In the warm, moist soil of June, pea stems and leaves decay quickly, so vines cut to 20-30 cm and spread as mulch give that nitrogen to the summer crop at once, and plowing the whole plant in at flowering leaves the most nitrogen at the cost of giving up the seed. If the vines were fed to livestock, put that manure back on the same field.

## 7. Intercropping and Crop Rotation
| Compatibility | Companions |
|:---|:---|
| Good | Lettuce and spinach (grow in the shade of pea and share space, with the same growing period), radish and turnip (different rooting depths, so their nutrient layers do not overlap), potato (diseases do not overlap and pea is harvested first), carrot (uses the nitrogen pea leaves behind) |
| Fair | Maize (in theory it serves as a stake, but the sowing times are two months apart, so set up separate stakes) |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Continuous cropping builds up wilt and root rot until the field can no longer grow peas within a few years, so rest it for at least 5-7 years. A 6-year rotation example is pea (spring) with Chinese cabbage or radish (autumn) → maize or sorghum → potato or sweet potato → soybean or cowpea → barley or wheat followed by buckwheat → root vegetables or green manure. If the field is small, divide it into plots and move to a new plot each year, or plant in sacks of soil replaced every year, and for sprouts and young shoots water alone is enough.

## 8. Harvest and Seed Saving
| Harvest | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Edible pods, green peas | Edible-pod varieties at 50-60 days, when the pods are flat and the seeds still small. Green peas at 60-80 days (20-30 days after flowering): sweetest when the pods are plump and the surface smooth, and the last moment is when a net pattern appears. Pick at intervals of three or four days |
| Dry seed | 85-95 days, when 80% or more of the pods have turned yellow or brown, the seeds rattle when shaken, and a seed bitten splits cleanly (13-15% moisture). Seeds shatter easily, and dry pods that get rained on burst at once, so if rain is forecast, cut the whole vine without waiting for full maturity |
| Drying, threshing | On a clear day after the dew has dried, cut the base of the vines with a sickle and leave the roots in the field. Tie in bundles, hang upside down under the eaves, and dry with a cloth or mat spread below. When pressed, the pods should pop open; thresh by treading dry vines in a sack, flailing them thinly spread on a mat, or beating them against the inside wall of a tub (if not dry enough they will not open, and if too dry the seeds crack) |
| Cleaning | Winnow in the early morning or at dusk when the wind is steady, pouring in a thin stream two or three times. Floating them in water separates seeds eaten by pea weevils and empty seeds, but dry them again that day, and do not use them for seed |

| Seed saving | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Selection | Mark in the field beforehand plants with no disease symptoms, many pods, and early ripening before the heat arrives. Fertilization is complete before the flower opens, so isolation is unnecessary |
| Drying, storage | Dry to 12-13% moisture; the right state is when a bitten seed splits cleanly. Seal at room temperature for 2-3 years, up to 4 years in good conditions. Brown spot is seed-borne, so discard spotted seed, and every 3 years obtain seed from elsewhere and mix some in |

## 9. Storage
| Step | Method |
|:---|:---|
| Drying | Hang vines upside down under the eaves for 2-3 days, then adjust to 12-13% moisture for eating and 12% for seed |
| Weevil control | Complete sealing matters most: fill containers completely to reduce empty space, and use several small containers. Mix in sifted wood ash at 2-5% of the volume (rinse before eating). Rub in 2-5 mL of cooking oil per 1 kg of peas (only for what will be eaten within half a year; not for seed) |
| Containers | Glass jars and metal cans: 3 years or more; clay jars and underground storage pits: 2-3 years; thick plastic: 1-2 years. Watch for condensation in metal cans, flooding in underground pits, and rat damage in plastic |
| Inspection | Once every two months. Put an ear to the container and check for rustling (insects), a musty smell (mold), and small round holes (weevils). If found, take everything out, dry it in the sun, float it in water to skim off empty seeds, mix in ash or oil, and seal again |
| Green peas | The sweetness drops right after picking, so eat them that day, or blanch and dry them (keeps for months; revive by soaking), or blanch and salt them (keeps for months). Left in the pod in a cool place, they keep 3-5 days |

## 10. Cooking and Precautions
> **Pea itself is safe. What actually harms people is beans that were stored badly and molded. If there is a musty smell or a bluish powder on the surface, washing or cooking will not make them safe, so throw them out even if it is a pity.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;It has neither the strong lectins of kidney bean nor the hemolytic compounds of fava bean, so green peas cause no trouble even if a few are eaten raw, and the trypsin inhibitors in dry peas (which block protein-digesting enzymes) are far fewer than in soybean, so 20-30 minutes of heating is enough. But because of the high fiber, suddenly feeding a lot to someone who has been starved for a long time, or to a child, causes bloating and diarrhea, so start with a small amount and increase over several days.

| Cooking | Method |
|:---|:---|
| Boiling | Soak in room-temperature water for 4-8 hours (discard the soaking water to reduce oligosaccharides) and boil 20-30 minutes (less than half of soybean's 60 minutes) |
| Split peas | Pound dry peas lightly in a mortar and blow off the hulls and they split in half; they cook to a porridge in 20 minutes without soaking. The hulls are used as animal feed and compost |
| Saving fuel | Boil for 10 minutes, turn off the fire, and wrap the pot whole in a straw- or rice-hull-filled box or a quilt for 1-2 hours |
| Roasted peas, flour | Roast over low heat and eat as they are, or grind to flour and mix into water (keeps 3-6 months sealed and needs no cooking fuel). Dried blanched green peas go into soups and porridge in winter |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Dry peas contain no vitamin C, but sprouting creates it anew, so a handful of dry peas, water, and a container give fresh vegetables in 5-7 days. Soak in lukewarm water for 6-8 hours until doubled, put in a container with drainage, cover with a damp cloth, and pour water over generously 2-3 times a day to drain. Unlike bean sprouts, keep them dark for the first three or four days and in the light for two days to turn the leaves green, which increases vitamins, and harvest at 5-10 cm stems to eat as greens. Sown thickly in a shallow box of soil, they give sturdy shoots of 15-20 cm that can be cut with scissors, and one more crop can be had.

## 11. Annual Work Schedule
| Month | Work |
|:---|:---|
| Jan-Feb | Check storage, test seed germination, mend stake materials, and grow sprouts. In February, spread lime, turn the compost, and sow early in late February when the soil thaws |
| Mar-Apr | In March, main sowing, bird protection, and setting up stakes (count the last sowing date back 85-95 days from the first heat). In April, thinning, 1st weeding, training vines, and harvesting young shoots |
| May-June | In May, flowering, concentrated irrigation, aphid control, and checking nodules. In June, successive harvests of green peas (every three or four days) and harvesting the autumn-sown crop in the south |
| July-Aug | In July, harvest dry seed, hang to dry, thresh, and return stems and leaves. In August, winnow, dry, store, and clear the field (cut the vines and spread them rather than burning them) |
| Sep-Oct | In September, prepare the field for autumn sowing and spread lime (south). In October, autumn sowing (40-50 days before the first frost) and preparing for winter |
| Nov-Dec | In November, cover with straw and fallen leaves and check drainage ditches. In December, check storage, grow sprouts, and plan next year |

> **Pea earns its keep when you sow early, keep the 5-7 year rest, support the vines with thin string and branches, return the vines to the field after harvest, and pick every three or four days.**
