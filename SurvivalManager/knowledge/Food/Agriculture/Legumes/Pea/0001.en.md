## Introduction

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The pea (Pisum sativum) came from Southwest Asia and the eastern Mediterranean. It is one of the oldest crops people have ever grown, first cultivated alongside wheat and barley in the early farming of the Fertile Crescent. That origin explains almost everything about the pea's character. Because it is a plant that grew up in a Mediterranean climate, with rain in winter and a bone-dry summer, the pea grows when it is cool and dies when it turns hot. This one fact is why the pea alone is a crop of spring and autumn, while other beans are summer crops.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;From the standpoint of disaster preparedness, the pea's place is clear. It is a crop that uses the field in a season when other beans have not even sprouted yet. In South Korea it is sown in mid-March and harvested in June; in southern South Korea it is sown in October, overwinters, and is harvested the following May. In June, when beans are being planted, the pea field is already empty. Here lies the key to taking two harvests a year from the same field, and the ability to fill this gap in time is the pea's strength.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Here are the numbers. 100g of dried peas contains 354kcal of energy and 25.37g of protein. Protein makes up 28.7% of the calories, nearly three times the 10% requirement line, the minimum people need. This criterion is graded SS, and the Supplementary scenario grade of SS (64.7 points) also comes from it. From sowing to harvest takes 85-95 days, which is fast for a legume, and with total evapotranspiration of around 400mm, the pea also uses less water than soybean. The ability to withstand 5°C below zero and to be sown in autumn and overwintered is an uncommon advantage among legumes.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;But there are three weaknesses. First, Calories per area is graded C. One square meter yields only 885kcal from 1m², just 0.34 times the self-sufficiency line, so peas alone cannot fill a person's stomach.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Second, Continuous-cropping tolerance is graded D. The Rural Development Administration (RDA) of South Korea calls for a rotation break of 5-7 years, which is unusually long for a legume crop. Compared with 1 year for soybean and 3 years for kidney bean, the pea is the legume that is hardest to plant again in the same spot.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Third, because it is a climbing plant, it needs supports. Infrastructure needs is graded SS, but that only means no structures such as greenhouses or plastic greenhouses are required; supports made of bamboo and string still demand hands-on work.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;And there is one misconception that applies to legume crops as a whole: the saying that planting beans improves the soil. It is only half true. The pea, too, lives in symbiosis with root-nodule bacteria and draws nitrogen from the air, but if the seeds are harvested and carried out of the field, more nitrogen can leave the field than was fixed. Whether a pea field restores the soil or robs it depends on how the stems, leaves, and vines are handled. This document works through that fork in the road in numbers in Section 5.
