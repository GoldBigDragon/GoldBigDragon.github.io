## 3. Growing Conditions

### 3-1. Temperature and Light

| Condition | Limit | Optimum | When outside the range |
|---|---|---|---|
| Germination temperature | Germinates even at 1-2°C | 18-25°C | Germinates even in the cold, but very slowly |
| Growth temperature | 4-25°C | 10-20°C | Above 25°C growth stops and disease surges |
| Fruiting temperature | 13-23°C | 15-20°C | A narrow range of only 10°C |
| Heat during flowering | Several days at 25°C or above | - | Flowers and young pods drop |
| Cold hardiness | Down to 5°C below zero | - | Young seedlings withstand 4-7°C below zero |
| Frost | After flowering, vulnerable to frost | - | Flowers and pods are damaged |
| Sunshine | 6 or more hours a day | 8 or more hours a day | Under 40% shade, yield drops by half |

> **Every decision in pea growing is made in the direction of escaping the heat. When to plant, which variety to choose, and when to harvest are all calculated backward from the date of the first heat in the area.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The pea's tolerance of cold and its tolerance of heat are asymmetric. Young peas withstand 5°C below zero, and in southern South Korea they overwinter in the field. Above 25°C, on the other hand, the flowers start to drop, and by 30°C the leaves dry up and powdery mildew covers them. So rushing the sowing is almost always right. In South Korea, the loss from sowing in early March and being hit by a late frost once or twice is far smaller than the loss from sowing in April and pushing the flowering period into the heat of June.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Autumn sowing is possible only in southern South Korea. Peas are sown between early and late October and overwinter at the stage of two or three true leaves; they resume growing in March of the following year and are harvested from mid-May to mid-June. The value of this cropping system is great. The roots settle in deeper than with spring sowing, so the crop resists drought, and the harvest comes 2-3 weeks earlier, avoiding the heat entirely. However, there is a risk of failing to overwinter, so it is safer to secure a spring-sown batch as well and share the risk. In inland central South Korea, where winter cold is severe, autumn sowing mostly fails.

### 3-2. Soil and Water

| Condition | Limit | Optimum | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Soil texture | Sandy soil to clay soil | Well-drained clay loam to loam | Waterlogged ground is the worst |
| pH | 6.0-8.0 | 6.5-7.5 | In acidic soil growth is markedly poor |
| Effective soil depth | 30cm or more | 50cm or more | The taproot goes deep |
| Evapotranspiration over the season | - | About 400mm | Low among legumes |
| Flooding | Roots are damaged if submerged for a day or more | - | Very vulnerable to waterlogging injury |
| Water table | 50cm or deeper | 80cm or deeper | Coincides with the period of frequent spring rain |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Of the legumes, the pea tends to have the highest soil pH requirement. A pH of 6.5-7.5 is optimal, and below 6.0 growth clearly worsens. The fact that its region of origin, the eastern Mediterranean, has many calcareous soils explains this. Fields in South Korea receive a lot of rain and generally lean acidic, so in a pea field liming comes before fertilizer. And this requirement is also that of the root-nodule bacteria rather than of the plant itself. In acidic soil the bean plant manages to grow somehow, but the bacteria cannot live and nitrogen fixation stops.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Its vulnerability to waterlogging also needs emphasis. The pea's growing period overlaps with the spring rains and the beginning of the monsoon season (jangma, late June to July in South Korea). Because the stem is hollow and the tissue soft, and because root-nodule bacteria are aerobic, even one day of flooding makes the roots and nodules lose function together. Raising the beds at least 20cm and digging deep drainage ditches is not optional for peas.

## 4. Varieties

### 4-1. Classification by Height

| Type | Height | Support | Harvest | Characteristics |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Dwarf type (dwarf pea) | 30-60cm | Not needed, or a simple one is enough | Short and concentrated | Less work. Yield is low |
| Tall type (climbing pea) | 1-2m | Absolutely necessary | Long and in several rounds | High yield. Supports and training take a lot of work |
| Intermediate type | 60-100cm | A simple support | Medium | Well balanced |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In a disaster, this choice comes down to labor and materials. Tall types yield far more from the same area, but they keep requiring 2m-high supports, netting to be tied, and vines to be trained upward. If bamboo and string are plentiful and there are hands to work, tall types are the right choice; if both materials and people are scarce, dwarf types are the realistic one. Dwarf types too can be largely kept from lodging by planting them densely so that the plants lean on one another and running only a low line of string around the edge of the field.

### 4-2. Classification by Use

| Type | Part eaten | Characteristics | Suitable situation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Edible-pod type (snow pea, sugar snap) | Whole pod | The pod is not tough and is sweet | When vegetables are scarce. The pod wall is eaten too, so nothing is wasted |
| Green-pea type | Immature seeds | Large, sweet seeds containing vitamin C | Fresh vegetable from spring to early summer |
| Dry-seed type | Dried seeds | Stores well and concentrates protein | Reserve food to carry through the winter |
| Sprout and shoot type | Young shoots and tendrils | Harvested 5-20 days after sowing | Fresh vegetable in winter and early spring |
| Green-manure type | Whole foliage | Grown to leave nitrogen behind | When restoring a field |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;From the disaster-preparedness viewpoint, the most valuable type is the edible-pod type. Because the pod and the peas are eaten together, the edible weight from the same field goes up and there is no part to trim away. The pods contain dietary fiber and vitamins, so the range of nutrition widens as well. However, to eat them whole they must be picked young; if left, a tough membrane forms on the inside of the pod wall and makes them hard to eat.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The first criterion in choosing a variety is whether seed can be saved. The traits of a first-generation (F1) hybrid scatter when it is planted the next year. Fortunately for peas, most cultivated varieties are fixed lines and self-pollinating, so saved seed can be planted as it is, but if the seed packet says hybrid, nothing should be expected of the following year. Local landraces yield less, but they are already adapted to the local climate and soil diseases, and their seed can be carried on from year to year.

### 4-3. Selection Criteria by Situation

| Situation | Priority | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| No materials for supports | Dwarf type | It stands up to a degree even without supports |
| Little land but plenty of people | Tall type | It grows upward, so yield per area is high |
| No winter vegetables | Dual-use for sprouts and shoots | Sprouts can be grown with water alone, without soil |
| Stored food is needed | Dry-seed type, round-seeded lines | Stores better than wrinkled seeds |
| Regions where the heat comes early | Early-maturing dwarf type | The crop must finish in a short time |
| Southern South Korea | Cold-hardy variety for autumn sowing | Overwintering brings the harvest forward |
| Peas have been grown in the field before | Wilt-resistant lines | Soil pathogens remain |

## 5. Field Preparation and Fertilizer - Root-Nodule Bacteria

### 5-1. How Nitrogen Fixation Happens

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;78% of the air is nitrogen. Even though an unlimited supply of nitrogen floats over people's heads, plants cannot use it. Atmospheric nitrogen is a molecule in which two nitrogen atoms are bound tightly by a triple bond, and breaking this bond takes an enormous amount of energy. The industrial way of breaking this bond to make ammonia is the Haber-Bosch process, and the world's nitrogen fertilizer comes from it. It requires high temperature, high pressure, a catalyst, and enormous amounts of fossil fuel.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Root-nodule bacteria do this work at ordinary temperature and pressure. With an enzyme called nitrogenase they break the triple bond and turn nitrogen into ammonia. There is, however, a price. This reaction needs a great deal of energy, and the bacteria cannot photosynthesize on their own. So they live inside the roots of legumes, receive the sugars the plant makes by photosynthesis, and in return hand over the nitrogen they have fixed. It is a trading relationship.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The bacterium that partners with the pea is Rhizobium leguminosarum bv. viciae. It is a different species from the soybean's Bradyrhizobium japonicum, and the two are not interchangeable. This point is very important in practice. Coating pea seed with soil from a field that grew soybeans has no effect. On the other hand, this bacterium lives in symbiosis not only with the pea but also with fava bean, lentil, and the vetch group. That is, the inoculum for peas can also be obtained from the soil of a field that grew fava beans, lentils, or wild vetch.

| Step | What happens |
|---|---|
| 1. Signal exchange | Pea roots secrete flavonoids, and bacteria in the soil respond to that signal by releasing Nod factors |
| 2. Infection | The root hair curls inward and encloses the bacteria, and the bacteria move into the root along the infection thread |
| 3. Nodule formation | Cells inside the root divide and the nodule swells, and the bacteria settle inside it |
| 4. Nitrogen fixation | Inside the nodule, nitrogenase operates and converts atmospheric nitrogen to ammonia |
| 5. Distribution | Ammonia is converted into amino acids and moves to the vines, leaves, and pods |
| 6. Senescence | In the ripening stage the nodule loses function and returns to the soil together with the root |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Nitrogenase is extremely sensitive to oxygen and loses function the moment oxygen touches it. So the inside of the nodule is packed with a red pigment protein called leghemoglobin, which holds on to oxygen and keeps the oxygen concentration around the enzyme extremely low. It is the same principle that makes human blood red, and the red or pink color inside a nodule when it is split is this pigment. This fact is enormously important in practice. The red color is visible proof that the nodule is fixing nitrogen right now.

### 5-2. How to Check Whether Nodules Have Formed

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Whether or not the seed was inoculated, checking that nodules have actually formed is something that must be done. Without checking, the cause of a nitrogen shortage cannot be known, and the same mistake is repeated the following year.

| Step | Details |
|---|---|
| 1 | Around the start of flowering, about 5-7 weeks after sowing, is the best time |
| 2 | Choose two or three plants that look healthy |
| 3 | Do not pull the plant up; dig around it widely with a spade and lift it out together with the soil. If pulled, the nodules on the fine roots stay behind in the soil |
| 4 | Put it in a bucket of water and gently wash off the soil |
| 5 | Count the nodules on the taproot and the thick fine roots |
| 6 | Split a few nodules with a fingernail and look at the color inside |

| Observation | Meaning | Action |
|---|---|---|
| 20 or more nodules per plant, especially clustered on the upper taproot | Inoculation went well | Leave as is |
| The split surface is red or pink | It is fixing nitrogen | In good condition |
| The split surface is white or pale green | Nodules formed but are not fixing | Check soil acidity, waterlogging injury, and phosphorus deficiency |
| The split surface is brown or black | Already senescent or dead | Normal at the ripening stage, a problem at flowering |
| 5 or fewer nodules | Inoculation failed | If the leaves are yellow, supplement nitrogen |
| Many small nodules only at the tips of the fine roots | Infected by native bacteria, but efficiency is low | Change the inoculum the following year |

> **This one habit of digging up the roots, splitting the nodules, and checking for the red color is the surest signal of whether bean farming will succeed or fail in a world without nitrogen fertilizer.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The position of the nodules also gives information. Thick nodules on the upper part of the taproot, within 10cm of the soil surface, are the most efficient. This is where they form when inoculation has gone well. Conversely, if there are none on the taproot and only small nodules scattered at the tips of the fine roots, it is often a case of native bacteria already present in the soil infecting late, and fixation efficiency is lower.

### 5-3. Where to Get the Inoculum

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Commercial inoculants are products that sell live bacteria carried in peat or clay. In a disaster they cannot be obtained. But root-nodule bacteria are common microbes that live in soil, and the soil of a field that grew the same legume is itself an inoculum.

| Method | Procedure | Effect | Caution |
|---|---|---|---|
| Coating seed with soil from a pea field | Dig up soil to a depth of 5-15cm from the topsoil of a field where peas, fava beans, lentils, or vetch were planted last year and nodules formed well. Moisten the seed slightly with water, coat it evenly with this soil, and plant right away | The surest method, and it costs nothing | Soil from a diseased field carries pathogens along with it. Never use a field that had pea wilt |
| Spreading soil over the field | Scatter the soil brought in along the sowing row and work it in together | Needs more soil than coating seed, but the bacteria spread through the soil | The guideline is about one spadeful per 10m² |
| Using the nodules themselves | Collect nodules from dug-up pea roots, crush them and stir them into water, then dip the seed in that water and lift it out | The highest bacterial density | Must be used on the same day the nodules were dug. If they dry out, the bacteria die |
| Leaving root residue | At harvest, cut only the stems and leave the roots in the field | The bacteria remain in the soil until the following year | The method that takes the least work to maintain |
| Wild vetch roots | Collect nodules from the roots of vetch in fields and on field banks | The same bacteria as the pea's live in symbiosis | Fixation efficiency may be lower than with strains for cultivated species |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There are things that must be observed when inoculating. Root-nodule bacteria are extremely sensitive to direct sunlight, drying, and heat. A good share of them die after a few minutes of ultraviolet light, and they die even faster when dry. The work of coating seed with soil is done in the shade, and the coated seed is planted right away rather than spread out in the sun. Planting within 30 minutes is best, and it should be finished within a few hours at the latest. If the seed is left for more than a day before planting, the effect almost disappears. One more thing: seed disinfection and inoculation cannot be done together. If seed treated with a fungicide is coated with soil, the bacteria die. If the seed was disinfected by hot-water treatment, it is inoculated only after it has cooled completely.

| Conditions that cause inoculation failure | Reason |
|---|---|
| Soil pH 6.0 or below | The bacteria cannot live. Peas are especially sensitive to acidity |
| A field given plenty of nitrogen fertilizer | The plant feels no need for the trade and cuts back nodule formation on its own |
| A waterlogged field | They are aerobic bacteria and die without oxygen |
| A field short of phosphorus | Nitrogen fixation requires a lot of energy, and phosphorus is used in that metabolism |
| Molybdenum, iron, or cobalt deficiency | They are components of nitrogenase |
| Drying inoculated seed in the sun | The bacteria die from ultraviolet light and drying |
| Using soil from a soybean field | The bacterial species is different and does not infect peas |
