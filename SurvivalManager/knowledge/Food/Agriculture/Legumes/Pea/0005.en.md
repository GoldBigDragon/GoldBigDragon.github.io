## 8. Crop Management - Supports and Training

### 8-1. Why Supports Are Needed

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The pea's stem has almost no woody tissue and is hollow, so it cannot stand on its own. Evolutionarily, the pea chose the path of leaning rather than standing, and in exchange it turned its leaf tips into tendrils. If this fact is ignored in the field, the vines end up lying on the ground. Four things happen at once to a pea lying on the ground. The leaves overlap and the lower part gets no light, airflow is blocked and powdery mildew and brown spot spread, the pods touch the soil and rot, and at harvest the vines have to be pushed apart, which takes several times as much labor.

| Method | Material | Effect | Suitability for disasters |
|---|---|---|---|
| No support | None | Yield drops markedly and disease increases | Second-best, for dwarf types only |
| String training | Stakes and string | Moderate labor, good yield | The most realistic |
| Net training | Stakes and netting | The highest yield | Best if netting is available |
| Brushwood (pea sticks) | Twigs | Costs nothing | A traditional method. Suited to small areas |
| Living supports | Maize, sunflower | No separate support needed | The sowing times do not line up, so it generally does not suit peas |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Under disaster conditions the most realistic option is string training. Stakes are driven in at 2m intervals, and string is tied horizontally at heights of 20cm, 50cm, and 80cm above the ground so that the peas between the two lines lean on the string. As the vines grow, the string is simply raised one level at a time. If string is short, kudzu vines or straw are twisted into rope.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Sticking in twigs is a method that has long been used in both Europe and South Korea. If twigs with their leaves left on are stuck in at a slant along the row to be planted, the pea tendrils coil around those twigs and climb on their own. Twigs that branch a lot, like birch or bush clover (Lespedeza), are best, and neither string nor netting is needed. Branches can simply be cut in winter, dried, and used in spring.

> **Pea tendrils grip thin objects of about 5mm in diameter best. They cannot coil around thick posts. If only thick stakes are driven in as supports, the vines fall over without having gripped anything.**

### 8-2. Thinning and Weeding

| Task | Timing | Method |
|---|---|---|
| First thinning | 2-3 true leaves | Clear only the places that are overly dense |
| Second thinning | Height of 10cm | Leave the 2 most vigorous plants per hole |
| First weeding | 2-3 weeks after sowing | Scrape shallowly. Take care not to damage the roots |
| Second weeding | 5-6 weeks after sowing | Combined with hilling |
| After that | Do not enter the field after flowering | The vines are tangled and break when a person passes through |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Peas grow slowly early on and are short, so they are easily overwhelmed by weeds. On the other hand, once the vines stretch out and climb the supports, they no longer compete with weeds for space. So it is no exaggeration to say that weeding during the first month is everything. As a rule, no one enters the field after flowering. The tendrils are tangled together, so when a person passes, several plants are torn up at once, and if people go back and forth in the morning before the dew has dried, disease is carried across the whole field.

### 8-3. Pinching and Shoot Picking

| Task | Timing | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Shoot picking | When the vines reach the top of the supports | Side branches increase and the lower pods grow larger |
| Harvesting young shoots | At any time during growth | Eaten as a vegetable while also giving the effect of pinching |
| Removing diseased leaves | As soon as found | Slows the spread of powdery mildew and brown spot |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Picking the shoots of peas has one more side benefit. Aphids are usually concentrated on the youngest growing points. When the shoots are picked off, a considerable part of the aphid population is removed with them. Do not throw away the picked shoots; blanch them and eat them as namul (seasoned vegetable side dish). The same method is used for fava beans, and it is a rare case in which, without chemicals, one task brings pest control, harvest, and higher yield together.

## 9. Pests and Diseases

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The pea's Pest & disease pressure is C. At an estimated Yield loss without pest control of 48.9%, it is more than double that of soybean. However, most pea pests and diseases surge as temperatures rise, so the avoidance strategy of planting early and harvesting early works quite well.

### 9-1. Major Diseases

| Disease | Symptoms | Conditions for occurrence | Response without chemicals |
|---|---|---|---|
| Powdery mildew | A white powder, as if flour had been sprinkled, covers the surface of leaves and stems | When days are hot, nights cool, and the weather dry. The biggest disease of the late growth stage | Secure airflow, spray milk diluted 5-10-fold with water, avoid by harvesting early |
| Virus disease (stunt) | Leaves curl, shrivel, and become mottled. Growth stops | Spread by aphids | Cannot be treated. Pull out and burn at once when found, and reduce aphids |
| Brown spot | Brown spots form on leaves, stems, and pods | When rain is frequent and humidity high. Also seed-borne | Seed selection, secure airflow, Bordeaux mixture |
| Root rot and wilt | Leaves yellow and wilt from the bottom up, and the stem base and roots rot brown | Excess moisture, continuous cropping | A 5-7-year rotation is the only fundamental remedy. Drainage and raising the beds |
| Rust | Orange powdery masses form on the leaves | High humidity | Remove diseased leaves, secure airflow |
| Downy mildew | Yellow spots on the upper leaf surface, grayish-white mold on the underside | When cool and humid | Adequate plant spacing, drainage, Bordeaux mixture |

> **The pea's continuous-cropping injury is the most severe of the legumes. The dormant spores of wilt and root rot hold on in the soil for years without a host. A field that has once been contaminated cannot be restored with chemicals, and there is no remedy other than moving to another field.**

### 9-2. Major Pests

| Pest | Damage | Control without chemicals |
|---|---|---|
| Aphids | Cluster on growing points and flower stalks, suck sap, and spread virus diseases. The pea's worst pest | Physically remove the population by picking the shoots. Spray soapy water (5g of soap per 1L of water) on the undersides of the leaves. Protect ladybugs and lacewings |
| Pea weevil | Lays eggs on the pods in the field and eats into the seeds during storage | The key is airtight storage after complete drying. See Section 15 |
| Black bean aphid | Black aphids cling densely to the stems | Shoot picking, soapy water |
| Leafminers | Tunnel inside the leaves and leave white streaks | Pick off and destroy the damaged leaves |
| Birds (pigeons, magpies, pheasants) | Eat the seed and young shoots right after sowing | Cover with straw or netting until germination |
| Rodents | Dig up and eat the planted seed underground | Coat the seed with wood ash and keep to the sowing depth |

### 9-3. Pest-Control Materials That Need No Chemicals

| Material | How to make it | Target | Caution |
|---|---|---|---|
| Diluted milk | Dilute milk 5-10-fold with water | Powdery mildew | The natural material with the best confirmed effect on peas. Spray on a clear morning |
| Soapy water | 5g of unscented soap per 1L of water | Aphids, spider mites | Spraying in the heat of midday burns the leaves |
| Bordeaux mixture | Dissolve 400g of copper sulfate and 400g of quicklime separately in 100L of water and mix | Bacterial and fungal diseases | Can be made only if copper is available. Use immediately after mixing |
| Wood-ash water | Soak 1 doe (about 1.8 L) of ash in 10L of water overnight and use the clear liquid on top | Suppresses mold | It is alkaline, so use it dilute |
| Dusting with dry ash | Sprinkle fine ash on the leaves on a morning with dew | Leaf-eating insects, mold | Washed off when it rains |
| Wood vinegar | Dilute the liquid produced when making charcoal 300-500-fold | Repellent, growth promotion | The undiluted liquid is strongly acidic, so it must be diluted |
| Hot pepper and garlic water | Grind, steep in water, strain, and use | Repellent | The effect does not last long, so spray often |

### 9-4. Cultural Control

| Method | Principle |
|---|---|
| Early sowing | Most pea pests and diseases increase with temperature. Finishing the harvest before the heat arrives avoids a good part of them |
| Long rotation | For wilt and root rot, a 5-7-year rotation break is the only remedy |
| Seed selection | Brown spot is transmitted by seed. Remove seed with spots and discoloration |
| Plant spacing and supports | With good airflow the leaves dry quickly and mold cannot take hold |
| Drainage | More than half of pea diseases are related to excess moisture |
| Shoot picking | Physically reduces the aphid population |
| No entering the field after rain | When people brush against wet leaves, pathogens are passed on |
| Residue handling | Burn or deeply bury diseased residue. Return only healthy residue to the field |

## 10. Nutrient Deficiency and Excess

| Symptom | Cause | Remedy with natural materials |
|---|---|---|
| The whole leaf turns pale yellow starting from the lower leaves, and plants are small | Nitrogen deficiency. In peas, poor nodule formation is the most common cause | First dig up the roots and check the nodules. If there are none, make sure to inoculate the next year, and in the current year apply a small amount of urine diluted 10-fold |
| Leaf margins dry brown | Potassium deficiency | Scatter wood ash between plants and lightly rake it in |
| Growth is slow and leaves take on a purplish tint | Phosphorus deficiency. Common because peas have a high phosphorus requirement | Bone meal, roasted fish-bone powder, wood ash |
| Flowers open but no pods set | Boron deficiency, or high temperature at flowering | Compost made from mixed materials. If high temperature is the cause, sow earlier the next year |
| Veins stay green but the tissue between veins is yellow | Iron or manganese deficiency. Common in soil leaning alkaline | Stop using ash. Increase organic matter with leaf mold and fully matured compost |
| New leaves are small and deformed and the growing point dies | Calcium or boron deficiency | Roasted eggshell powder, shell powder |
| Only the vines are lush and there are few pods | Nitrogen excess | Stop the nitrogen supply immediately. Pick shoots to induce side branches |
| Growth is poor overall and roots are short | Acidic soil | Lime or roasted shell powder. Peas clearly do worse below pH 6.0 |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;When pea growth is poor, the first thing to suspect is not fertilizer but acidity. The pea is the most sensitive of the legumes to acidic soil, and below pH 6.0 both root development and nodule formation get worse. On the surface it looks like nitrogen deficiency, but it does not recover even when nitrogen is given. Next come the nodules. If, on digging up the roots, the nodules are well attached and red inside when split, yet the leaves are yellow, the problem is something other than nitrogen.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Trace-element deficiencies are hard to address one by one. Without chemical fertilizers there is practically no way to obtain molybdenum or cobalt. Instead, compost made by rotting together vegetable scraps, fruit peels, eggshells, fish bones, fallen leaves, weeds, and ash contains trace elements in an even spread. The principle of putting everything into the compost heap is the practical strategy for managing trace elements.

## 11. Intercropping and Crop Rotation

### 11-1. Compatibility

| Compatibility | Crop | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Good | Lettuce, spinach | They grow in the shade of the peas and share the space. Their growing periods are the same |
| Good | Radish, turnip | Root depths differ, so the nutrient layers do not overlap |
| Good | Potato | Their diseases do not overlap, and the peas are harvested first |
| Good | Carrot | Uses the nitrogen the peas leave behind afterward |
| Fair | Maize (corn) | In theory it can serve as a support, but the sowing times are two months apart, so in practice it does not suit |
| Poor | Garlic and onion group | There are reports that their root exudates suppress root-nodule bacteria |
| Poor | Other legumes | Pathogens and nematodes accumulate |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The combination of pea and maize often appears in books but does not really work in practice. In March, when peas are sown, maize cannot be planted, and in June, when maize has grown enough to act as a support, the peas are already aging. This combination works for kidney bean and maize, and for peas it is better to set up a separate support.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The combination of peas and leafy vegetables really does work. If lettuce or spinach is planted between the pea rows, the two crops grow without interfering with each other under the weak spring sun. When the weather turns hot, the shade made by the pea vines delays the leafy vegetables from sending up flower stalks. By the time the peas are harvested, the leafy vegetables are also finished, so the field empties all at once, and summer crops can be planted there right away.

### 11-2. Crop Rotation

| Item | Standard |
|---|---|
| Rotation break | 5-7 years. The longest among legumes |
| Ideal cycle | 6 years or more |
| Continuous-cropping injury | Persistence of wilt and root rot in the soil. There is no chemical remedy, and resistant varieties are of limited availability too |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The D in the pea's Continuous-cropping tolerance is the item in this document that must be taken most seriously. Compared with 1 year for soybean, 3 years for kidney bean, and 2 years for fava bean, the pea's 5 to 7 years is overwhelmingly long. Meeting this requirement is not easy where land is limited, but if it is not met, within a few years the field becomes one where peas cannot be planted.

| Example of a 6-year rotation |
|---|
| Year 1: pea (spring), followed by napa cabbage or radish (autumn) |
| Year 2: maize or sorghum |
| Year 3: potato or sweet potato |
| Year 4: soybean or cowpea |
| Year 5: grain (barley, wheat), followed by buckwheat |
| Year 6: root vegetables or green manure |
| Then peas again |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Here is a compromise for when the field is small and there is no room to run a 6-year rotation. The simplest way is to divide the field into several plots and plant peas in one plot at a time, moving along one plot each year. In a small field that cannot be divided into plots, at the very least shift the position of the pea row substantially every year. Another option is to grow them outside the field. Although the pea's roots go deep, it also grows in large tubs or sacks. If it is planted in sacks of soil, the soil can be replaced every year and continuous-cropping injury avoided completely, and if the purpose is sprouts and young shoots, water alone without soil is enough.

## 12. Harvest and Threshing

### 12-1. Harvest Timing by Use

| Use | Harvest time (after sowing) | Criteria |
|---|:---:|---|
| Sprouts | 5-7 days | Stems 5-10cm. Possible with water alone, without soil |
| Young shoots | 15-20 days | Stems 15-20cm. They grow back after cutting |
| Whole pods (edible-pod varieties) | 50-60 days | When the pod is flat and the peas inside are still small |
| Green peas | 60-80 days | 20-30 days after flowering. When the pod is plump and still green |
| Dried seed | 85-95 days | When the pod dries yellow and the peas harden |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;All five of these come from a single pea plant. In a disaster, this variety is valuable in itself. Sprouts are obtained indoors before the field is even made, young shoots are eaten as namul once spring is in full swing, fresh vitamins come from green peas in early summer, and finally dried peas are harvested to carry through the winter.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There is a knack to catching the best time to harvest green peas. The sweetest time is when the pod has plumped up but the surface is still smooth and glossy. A little later a net-like pattern appears on the surface and it becomes bumpy; this is the last point at which the peas are largest yet still worth eating. After that, the sugar turns to starch, the sweetness disappears, and the skin becomes tough. Feeling and comparing pods from several nodes by hand quickly gives a sense of it.

> **Pick peas and they produce more; leave them and they stop. If ripe pods are left on the plant, it judges that reproduction is finished and halts flowering. Picking diligently at intervals of 3-4 days is the surest management for raising yield.**

### 12-2. Harvesting Dried Seed

| Criterion | Details |
|---|---|
| Leaves | Dry yellow from the bottom up |
| Pods | At least 80% (8 out of 10) of all pods have turned yellow or brown |
| Sound | When shaken, the peas rattle inside the pods |
| Peas | When bitten, the teeth do not sink in and the pea splits with a snap |
| Moisture | Seed moisture 13-15% |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Peas shatter more readily than soybeans. As the pods dry they twist and split open on their own, and when dry pods get rained on and dry again repeatedly, they burst in an instant. Once the lower pods begin to turn brown, check daily, and if rain is forecast, do not wait for full maturity but cut the vines and bring them in. Slightly immature peas can be dried and used, but peas that have fallen to the ground cannot be gathered.

| Step | Details |
|---|---|
| 1 | Do it on a clear, dry day, after 10 a.m., once the dew has dried |
| 2 | Cut the base of the vines with a sickle. Leave the roots in the field |
| 3 | Tie into bundles and hang upside down under the eaves or in a shed to dry |
| 4 | Spread a cloth or a meongseok (woven straw mat) underneath to catch peas that fall by themselves |
| 5 | Thresh when the pods are brittle-dry and rattle when shaken |

### 12-3. Threshing - Methods Without Tools

| Method | Tool | Procedure | Efficiency |
|---|---|---|---|
| Trampling in a sack | A thick burlap bag or sack | Put dry vines in the sack, tie the opening, then trample with the feet or rub by hand | The easiest for small quantities. The peas do not fly out |
| Flail | A tool with a switch attached to a long handle | Spread a meongseok or cloth, lay the vines out thinly, and strike them from above | Suited to medium quantities. Strike while turning them over two or three times |
| Beating with a club | A wooden stick | Put in a sack or spread on a meongseok and beat | Used in place of a flail when none is available |
| Rubbing with the feet | None | Spread on a meongseok and rub round with a shod foot | Suited to small quantities |
| Striking against a board | A plank or a tub | Hold a bundle of vines and strike it against the inside wall of the tub | The peas collect in the tub, so losses are small |
| Shelling by hand | None | Twist open the pods one by one | Used for small quantities of seed. The peas are not damaged |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Success or failure in threshing depends on drying. Vines that are not dry enough will not have their pods open no matter how hard they are beaten, and over-dried peas have their seeds broken when beaten. The right time is when a pod pops open when pressed by hand, and doing it on the afternoon of a sunny day makes it much easier. Pea pods are softer than soybean pods and open more readily, so threshing is much easier. Trampling in a sack alone knocks nearly all of them loose.

### 12-4. Cleaning - Separating by Wind

| Method | Principle | Procedure |
|---|---|---|
| Winnowing by wind | Peas are heavy, while husks and debris are light | On a windy day, put them in a winnowing tray or wide vessel, hold it high, and pour slowly. The peas drop straight down and the chaff blows away |
| Tray winnowing | Shake up and down while tossing forward | The heavy peas stay at the back and the light material is pushed out to the front |
| Fanning | One person pours while another makes wind with a fan | Used on windless days |
| Sieving | Separate by hole size | Remove vine fragments with a coarse sieve, and let soil and small peas fall through a fine sieve |
| Floating in water | Empty husks and insect-eaten peas are light | Pour into a bucket and skim off what floats. What is fished out must always be dried again |
| Rolling down a slanted board | Round peas roll well while debris catches | Set a plank at a slant and roll the peas down it. Peas are round, so this works especially well |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Winnowing by wind is a method used since people first began handling grain. There are three tips. Choose early morning or dusk when the wind blows steadily, do not pour everything at once but let it fall in a thin stream, and repeat two or three times.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The method of floating in water does cleaning and sorting at the same time. Peas eaten out by the pea weevil are hollow and float, and empty husks float too, so sorting this way greatly reduces storage losses. However, peas that have been in water must be completely dried again the same day, and this is not used for seed.
