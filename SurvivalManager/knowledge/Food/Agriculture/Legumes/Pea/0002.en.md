## 1. Disaster-Preparedness Evaluation

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The pea's Overall grade is A, with a score of 48.4 points. Broken down by scenario, the Short-term ability to get through the early phase of a disaster is B (48.2), the Long-term ability to stay self-reliant over many years is A (48.6), and the Supplementary value, seen as a side-dish resource apart from the staple, is SS (64.7). The fact that the Short-term and Long-term values are almost identical sums up the pea's character. It is a well-balanced crop that puts food on the table quickly and yet does not falter as the years go by.

### 1-3. How to Read the Strengths

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The criteria graded SS are four: Protein density, Fertilizer demand, Infrastructure needs, and Storability. On top of that, A went to Speed to harvest, Water use, Cold hardiness, Yield retention without synthetic inputs, and Yield stability, and S went to Micronutrients, Taste, Prevalence in South Korea, and Seed saving.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The combination of A in Speed to harvest and A in Cold hardiness is the pea's identity. Legumes that earn both together are uncommon. Because it is cold hardy, it can be sown in March in South Korea, earlier than other crops, and because it grows fast, it is harvested by June. As a result, the pea yields one more harvest without encroaching on the sowing season of summer crops. In a disaster, the most dangerous period is between late spring and early summer, when last year's grain has run out and the autumn harvest is still far off, and the pea ripens in exactly that window. On top of that, green peas can be picked and eaten from the time the pods begin to plump up, so there is no need to wait for them to dry completely.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The SS in Protein density is a value second only to soybean. A P:E of 28.7% is three to four times that of grains, and a handful of dried peas adds real heft to a side dish. Peas are rich in lysine, which grains lack, and grains contain methionine, which peas lack, so when they are eaten together with rice, their amino acid profiles fill in for each other. This complementarity is a shared advantage of combining beans with grains.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The A in Water use is also worth a closer look. The total evapotranspiration of 400mm is clearly less than the 450-700mm of soybean. The reason is not an outstanding ability to save water, but that the growing period falls in a cool season. Growing under low spring temperatures and low evaporation, the plant needs less water to build the same bulk. It also means that the pea sidesteps the summer, when droughts are frequent.

> **The pea's value lies not in yield but in timing. Filling the window from March to June, which no other crop can use, with a protein crop is the reason the pea earned an A.**

### 1-4. How to Read the Weaknesses

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The criteria graded D are three: Perenniality, Sunlight - relative yield at 40% shade, and Continuous-cropping tolerance. C went to Calories per area, Cooking & processing, Temperature range, Pest & disease pressure, Tillage & digging energy, and Non-food value.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The D in Continuous-cropping tolerance is the most practical constraint, and also the one most often overlooked. By the standard of the Rural Development Administration (RDA) of South Korea, the pea is in the 5-7-year rotation-break group. This is the exact opposite of the common belief that legume crops generally tolerate continuous cropping, and the reason is that the soil pathogens that cause pea wilt and root rot survive in the soil for a very long time. The dormant spores of the Fusarium and Aphanomyces groups hold on for years without a host. In a field that has once been contaminated, the same thing repeats every time peas are planted again: the plants look fine early on, then around flowering they turn yellow from the lower leaves up, wilt, and die. There is no chemical remedy for this disease, and resistant varieties are only of limited availability in South Korea, so the only practical response is to move to another field.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The C in Calories per area is the same as for the other legumes: 885kcal per 1m², 0.34 times the self-sufficiency line. The pea cannot be made a staple. The arrangement has to be one in which potatoes, sweet potatoes, and grains supply the calories, with peas added on top for protein and vitamins. The pea does, however, have a complement that other legumes lack: its sprouts and young shoots can be harvested separately. Sprouts can be eaten five to seven days after sowing and young shoots in 15-20 days, so greens reach the table without waiting for the seeds.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The C in Temperature range is the pea's inborn limit. The temperature at which it can set pods is only 13-23°C, a range of just 10°C. When days above 25°C continue, the flowers drop and no pods set, and near 30°C the plant itself collapses. That spring-sown peas rapidly look ragged after mid-June in South Korea is not a disease but normal physiology. When deciding the sowing date, peas have to be calculated backward. Find the day on which the daytime high in the area begins to exceed 25°C, and counting back 85-95 days from that day gives the latest possible sowing date.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The C in Pest & disease pressure reflects a Yield loss without pest control of 48.9%, more than double the 23% of soybean. Virus diseases carried by aphids, in particular, have no means of treatment. However, the pests and diseases of the pea mostly surge as temperatures rise, so the avoidance strategy of planting early and harvesting early works quite well. The B in Pest-control self-reliance is due to this possibility of avoidance.

## 2. What Kind of Plant Is This?

### 2-1. Classification and Origin

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The pea is an annual climbing plant of the genus Pisum in the legume family (Fabaceae). It belongs to the same family as soybean, adzuki bean, mung bean, kidney bean, cowpea, fava bean, and lentil. This fact means two things. One is that it shares the nitrogen-fixing ability that comes from symbiosis with root-nodule bacteria; the other is that, because it shares soil pathogens and nematodes common to legumes, planting members of the bean group one after another causes disease to build up. In the pea, this second trait is unusually strong.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Its region of origin stretches from Southwest Asia to the eastern Mediterranean. Wild peas around Turkey and Iran are thought to be its ancestors, and peas are unearthed together with wheat and barley at archaeological sites. This origin explains almost all of the pea's traits. It is adapted to a Mediterranean climate with rainy winters and dry summers, so it grows when it is cool and moist and, when it gets hot, sets seed and dies. This is why, in South Korea, spring and autumn are the pea's seasons and midsummer is a season it cannot endure.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;One more thing: the pea is a plant whose name is recorded in the history of genetics. The reason Mendel chose peas for his experiments also matters in practical terms. The pea completes self-pollination inside the flower before it opens, and the petals wrap the pistil and stamens completely, making it hard for outside pollen to get in. So even when several varieties are planted side by side, their traits do not mix, and seeds saved and planted as they are produce plants like their parents. The S in Seed saving comes from this.

### 2-2. Appearance

| Organ | Characteristics |
|---|---|
| Leaf | Pinnately compound. It bears 1-3 pairs of leaflets, and the leaf tip turns into a tendril that coils around anything it touches |
| Stem | Hollow and climbing. Dwarf types grow to 30-60cm, tall types to 1-2m |
| Root | A straight taproot goes deep, and nodules form on the fine roots |
| Flower | Butterfly-shaped, white or reddish purple. Fertilization is already complete before it opens |
| Pod | Cylindrical, 5-10cm long. Holds 4-10 seeds |
| Seed | Round or wrinkled. Green, yellow, and grayish-brown types exist |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The tendril is a characteristic organ of the pea. It is a modified leaf tip that coils around whatever it touches to hold the plant upright. The pea's stem lacks strength and cannot stand on its own, so it must lean on something, and because of this structure, supports become essential. Tendrils grip thin objects of about 5mm in diameter best. They cannot coil around thick posts, so when putting up supports, thin string or netting must be strung between thick stakes.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The hollow stem also has practical significance. The tissue is soft and watery, so it is easily damaged by frost and cannot recover once trampled or broken. On the other hand, thanks to this trait the foliage rots down quickly after harvest, so as green manure it decomposes fast.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The difference between wrinkled and round seeds is also worth pointing out. Wrinkled seeds are ones in which the sugar has not fully converted to starch and shrivels as it dries, and they are correspondingly sweeter. In exchange for being good as green peas, the storability and germination ability of the dried seeds are slightly lower than those of round seeds. For seed that will be saved, round-seeded lines are the safe choice.

### 2-3. Growth Stages

| Stage | Timing | Key physiology | Important nutrients |
|---|---|---|---|
| Germination and seedling stage | 3 weeks from sowing | Seeds germinate and root nodules begin to form | Phosphorus, a small amount of nitrogen |
| Vegetative growth stage | 3-5 weeks | Vines extend and tendrils grip the supports | Nitrogen is supplied by the nodules |
| Flowering stage | 5-7 weeks | Flowers open in turn starting from the lowest nodes | Phosphorus, boron |
| Pod-filling stage | 7-9 weeks | Pods plump up and the seeds swell | Potassium, phosphorus |
| Maturation stage | 9-12 weeks | Pods dry and change color starting from the bottom | Stop irrigation |

> **Peas flower and ripen in order from the lower nodes upward. So a single plant carries ripe pods, filled-out pods, and nodes still in flower all at the same time. Try to harvest everything at once and you miss half.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This sequential pattern determines the character of the pea harvest. A soybean field turns yellow all at once and is cut in one pass, but peas ripen from the bottom up. When eaten as green peas, they have to be picked several times at intervals of three or four days, and picking them this way keeps the plant producing new flowers, so the harvest period lengthens. Conversely, if ripe pods are left on the plant, it judges that reproduction is finished and stops flowering. The act of picking itself is a form of management that raises yield.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There is also something to point out about early nitrogen. The root nodules do not work right after sowing. It takes 3-4 weeks from the seed germinating and the roots spreading, to bacteria colonizing them, nodules forming, and nitrogen being handed over. Until then, the young pea gets by on the nutrients stored in the seed and the nitrogen in the soil. That is why putting a small amount of nitrogen into the base fertilizer makes sense. It must, however, be a small amount. If the soil has plenty of nitrogen, the plant feels no need to do business with the bacteria and cuts back on nodule formation by itself.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Dried peas and green peas should be considered separately. They are, in effect, different foods. Green peas are high in water and provide only 80-97kcal per 100g, but they contain as much as 35mg of vitamin C and serve as a fresh vegetable. Dried peas concentrate the energy and protein to four times, at the cost of losing the vitamin C. If peas are grown in a disaster, it is right to make use of both. The early ones are eaten as green peas to get vitamins, and the late ones are dried and stored to be used as protein.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The very low fat content of 1-2.5% works in its favor for storage. Soybean has 15-20% fat, so soy flour develops a rancid smell within a few months, but pea flour keeps much longer. Compared with peanuts, the difference is even larger. Its energy density is low, but it is on the easy side to keep.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;A fiber content of 20-25g is high even among legumes. If a person who has gone hungry for a long time, or a child, is suddenly given a large amount, abdominal bloating and diarrhea follow. Starting with a small amount and increasing it over several days, and always discarding the soaking water to reduce oligosaccharides, considerably eases this problem.
