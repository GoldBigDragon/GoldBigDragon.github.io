## 8. Crop Management

### 8-1. The First 5 Weeks Are Everything

| Timing | Task | Key Point |
|---|---|---|
| Sowing to 2 weeks after emergence | First weeding | Pull weeds while they are small |
| 4-5 weeks after emergence | Second weeding | Combine with hilling |
| Before flowering | Finish all weeding | Weeding after flowering reduces yield |
| 2-3 true leaves | Thinning | Leave only the sturdiest 1 plant per hole |

> **The cowpea is a crop that yields something even if you plant it and leave it alone, but that does not mean you can do nothing at all. Weed control in the first 5 weeks is the one thing you must do.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The cowpea's early growth is slow. This is because it sends roots down deep before building up its above-ground parts, and if weeds get established first during that period, the cowpea gets suppressed and cannot recover. Conversely, once past these 5 weeks and the leaves start covering the ground, it no longer has to compete with weeds. The semi-upright and vining types spread their leaves wide and cover the ground on their own.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Weed shallowly. The cowpea's fine roots spread through the topsoil, and a hoe pushed in deep cuts them. Lay the hoe flat and scrape only the top 2cm of soil to lift weeds out, and pull weeds near the plant by hand. Covering with a thin layer of straw or rice hulls after sowing can reduce how often you need to weed.

### 8-2. Picking Leaves and Shoots

| Task | Timing | Key Point |
|---|---|---|
| Picking young leaves | From when several true leaves have appeared until before flowering | Choose and pick mature leaves lower on the plant |
| Pinching shoots | When a vining plant reaches the top of its stake | Increases side branches, and the picked shoot becomes namul |
| Picking limit | Within one-third of a plant's total leaves | Picking too much reduces seed yield |
| When to stop | Once flowering begins | After that, the leaves are needed to fill the pods |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Picking leaves to eat is an advantage the cowpea alone offers. It needs balance, though. Leaves are the plant's photosynthesis factory, so picking too many lowers seed yield. The rule of thumb is not to exceed one-third of a plant's total leaves, and to stop once flowering begins. Leaves after flowering are needed to make the nutrients that fill the pods.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There is a trick to how you pick, too. Choose fully grown leaves lower down, not the topmost new shoot, and pick them together with the petiole. The plant keeps growing only if you leave the growing point untouched. Eat the picked leaves blanched the same day, or, if there is a large amount, blanch and dry them for use in winter. Section 16 covers this in detail.

### 8-3. Managing Lodging

| Cause | Response |
|---|---|
| Excess nitrogen | Stick to the recommended rate. The cowpea needs almost no nitrogen |
| Overly dense planting | Stick to 15,000 plants per 1,000m² |
| Vining variety | Put up stakes, or switch to an upright type |
| Wind and rain | Tie a string around the field at knee height |
| Skipping hilling | Mound soil around the base during the second weeding |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Upright and semi-upright cowpeas need no stakes. Their stems are straight and firm enough to stand on their own. Only the vining type, growing up to 2-3m, requires stakes, and under disaster conditions there is not much reason to deliberately choose it. Saving on staking material and labor is generally the more sensible choice.

## 9. Pests and Diseases

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The cowpea's pest-control self-reliance is S, a value among the top in this crop table. It means it can be managed with cultivation methods alone even with no pesticide whatsoever. There are two pests to watch for, though: the pod borer moth in the field, and the adzuki bean weevil in storage.

### 9-1. Major Diseases

| Disease | Symptoms | Conditions for Outbreak | Response Without Chemicals |
|---|---|---|---|
| Wilt and root rot | The plant wilts and dies, and the base of the stem turns brown and soft | Excess moisture, continuous cropping | Drainage and a higher bed are nearly the whole answer |
| Powdery mildew | The leaf surface is covered in white powder | Dry conditions with large day-night temperature swings | Ensure airflow; milk diluted 5-10× |
| Rust | Orange powdery lumps form on the leaves | High humidity late in growth | Remove diseased leaves; ensure airflow |
| Mosaic virus | Leaves become mottled and crinkled, and growth stops | Spread by aphids | Cannot be cured. Pull and burn the plant, and reduce aphids |
| Bacterial blight | Angular brown spots form on the leaves | Spreads by wind-driven rain | Stay out of the field after rain |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Most cowpea diseases are related to water. Wilt and root rot are the most common and cause the greatest losses, and both come from excess moisture. This single measure — raising the bed at least 20cm and cutting drainage channels properly — accounts for half of managing cowpea disease. This is a case where moving soil with a spade does more good than making or spraying any remedy.

### 9-2. Major Pests

| Pest | Damage | Control Without Chemicals |
|---|---|---|
| Pod borer moth | Larvae enter flowers and young pods and eat the seed. The single biggest pest in the field | From flowering on, check flowers and young pods every morning and pick off larvae. Remove and destroy damaged pods |
| Adzuki bean weevil | Lays eggs on pods in the field and hollows out the seed during storage. The entire stock can be damaged within half a year | Harvest on time and seal it up after complete drying. See Section 15 |
| Stink bugs | Pierce pods and suck out sap, leaving empty husks | Hand-pick early in the morning while dew is on the plants |
| Aphids | Suck sap and spread viruses | Soapy water (5g soap per 1L water); a strong spray of water |
| Birds | Eat the shallowly buried seed and young shoots | Cover with straw or netting until germination |

> **The pod borer moth starts inside the flower. By the time a pod is visible, it is already inside. The habit of looking into the flowers every morning during flowering is the surest response to this pest.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The pod borer moth is a specialist pest that came from Africa along with the cowpea; it lays eggs on the inside of the petals, and the hatched larvae bore into the flower and young pod. Split open a pod that looks fine on the outside, and you find a larva inside with no egg to be seen. There are three ways to deal with it without chemicals. First, check the flowers early in the morning during flowering, and if a larva is inside, pick that flower off. Second, as soon as you find a damaged pod, pick it and destroy it away from the field. Third, there are reports that intercropping with basil or soybean reduces the damage.

### 9-3. Materials for Control Without Chemicals

| Material | How to Make | Target | Caution |
|---|---|---|---|
| Soapy water | 5g unscented soap per 1L water | Aphids, mites | Spraying in the hot midday sun scorches the leaves |
| Dusting with dry ash | Dust fine ash on the leaves on a dewy morning | Leaf-eating insects, fungi | Washes off if it rains |
| Wood ash water | Soak 1 doe (about 1.8 L) of ash in 10L of water overnight and use the water on top | Suppresses fungi | Alkaline, so use it diluted |
| Bordeaux mixture | Dissolve 400g copper sulfate and 400g quicklime separately in 100L water and mix | Bacterial and fungal diseases | Can only be made if copper sulfate is available |
| Wood vinegar | Dilute the liquid produced when making charcoal 300-500× | Repellent, growth promotion | The concentrate is strongly acidic, so it must be diluted |
| Hot chili and garlic water | Grind, steep in water, strain, and use | Repels moths | The effect does not last long, so spray often |
| Diluted milk | Dilute milk with water 5-10× | Powdery mildew | Leftover diluted milk spoils and smells |

### 9-4. Cultural Control

| Method | Principle |
|---|---|
| Drainage and raised beds | Most cowpea diseases are related to excess moisture. The single most effective measure |
| Morning rounds during flowering | The pod borer moth starts inside the flower. Catching it then is the only chance |
| Harvesting on time | Leaving ripe pods in the field too long lets weevils lay eggs on them |
| Intercropping with basil or soybean | There are reports that pest occurrence decreases |
| Keeping to planting density | Good airflow dries the leaves quickly and keeps fungi from taking hold |
| Seed sorting | Sowing insect-damaged seed means bringing in the weevil from the very start |
| No entering the field after rain | Brushing against wet leaves spreads bacterial disease |
| Handling residue | Burn diseased residue and return only healthy residue to the field |

## 10. Nutrient Deficiency and Excess

| Symptom | Cause | Natural-Material Remedy |
|---|---|---|
| The whole plant's leaves are pale yellow, and it is small | Nitrogen deficiency. In the cowpea, this is most often caused by root nodules dying from waterlogging | First dig up the roots and check the nodules. Fix the drainage and give a small amount of urine diluted 10× |
| Growth is slow and leaves turn purplish | Phosphorus deficiency. The most common deficiency on poor soil | Bone meal, roasted ground fish bone. The element to secure first for the cowpea |
| Leaf edges turn brown and dry | Potassium deficiency | Scatter wood ash between plants and lightly rake it in |
| Flowers open but pods do not set | Boron deficiency, or the pod borer moth | Check inside the flowers first. If it is not the pest, compost mixed from several materials |
| Yellowing between the leaf veins | Iron or manganese deficiency. Common in soil that has turned alkaline | Stop using ash. Increase organic matter with leaf mold and fully rotted compost |
| Only stems and leaves are lush, with few pods | Excess nitrogen | Stop the nitrogen supply immediately |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The most common deficiency in cowpeas is phosphorus. Since it is a crop planted on poor farmland, that farmland often lacks phosphorus, and while the plant can make its own nitrogen, it cannot do the same for phosphorus. Suspect phosphorus deficiency when growth is slow and leaves turn purplish. Without chemical fertilizer, bone meal and fish bones are the only routes to phosphorus. Gather animal bones, roast them over fire until they crumble, grind them in a mortar, and mix them into compost, and they release phosphorus slowly over several years.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Micronutrient deficiencies are hard to address one by one. Without chemical fertilizer, there is essentially no way to obtain molybdenum or cobalt. Instead, compost rotted down from a mix of vegetable scraps, fruit peels, eggshells, fish bones, fallen leaves, weeds, and ash contains micronutrients across the board. The principle of putting everything into the compost heap is the practical strategy for managing micronutrients.

## 11. Intercropping and Crop Rotation

### 11-1. Companion Planting

| Compatibility | Crop | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Good | Maize, sorghum, foxtail millet | The cowpea shares nitrogen, and the amino acid profiles complement each other |
| Good | Basil | There are reports of a clear drop in pest occurrence |
| Good | Barley, wheat, garlic, potato | As a preceding crop, it leaves the field empty in June to plant cowpea as a follow-on crop |
| Good | Napa cabbage, radish | Planted after cowpea, it makes use of the leftover nitrogen |
| Fair | Soybean | A pest-reducing effect has been reported, but being the same legume family, diseases overlap |
| Bad | Adzuki bean, mung bean | Being the same genus, they share pathogens and the adzuki bean weevil directly |
| Bad | Crops that need heavy watering | The cowpea is the least tolerant of excess moisture |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Pairing the cowpea with sorghum or millet is an arrangement that has continued in Africa for thousands of years. The coarse grain uses the light above while the cowpea covers the ground below, and part of the nitrogen the cowpea fixes flows to the grain. They complement each other nutritionally too: the grain is short on lysine while the cowpea has plenty, and the cowpea is short on methionine while the grain is not. Put the two crops from one field into a single bowl, and the protein profile is complete. If you grow sorghum, millet, or foxtail millet in South Korea, this same arrangement of planting cowpea between them applies directly.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;It is best not to place adzuki bean and mung bean next to it. Being the same genus Vigna, they share pathogens, and above all, the adzuki bean weevil moves freely among the three crops. Growing them together in one field doubles the weevil damage in storage.

### 11-2. Crop Rotation

| Item | Standard |
|---|---|
| Break period | 2-3 years. Among the best continuous-cropping tolerance of any legume |
| Ideal cycle | 3 years |
| Continuous-cropping damage | Wilt disease and nematodes build up. They accumulate more slowly than in other legumes |

| 3-Year Rotation Example |
|---|
| Year 1: Barley or garlic (winter), then cowpea (summer) |
| Year 2: Sorghum or maize |
| Year 3: The napa cabbage/radish group, or potato |
| Back to cowpea |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The cowpea's continuous-cropping tolerance is S, the most comfortable of any legume. This is a great advantage where fields are small and rotation is hard to arrange. You can assign one poor plot to the cowpea and plant it there for several years running, harvesting the seed one year and plowing it under at flowering the next to use as green manure. After two or three years of this, the soil in that plot changes noticeably, and that is when another crop can be brought in.

> **The cowpea is a crop that reclaims farmland. Rotate a plot where no other crop will grow through cowpea for two or three years, and nitrogen and organic matter build up, bringing that field back to life.**
