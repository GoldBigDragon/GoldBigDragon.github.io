## 12. Harvest and Threshing

### 12-1. Judging Harvest Time

| Use | Harvest timing (after sowing) | Criterion |
|---|:---:|---|
| Young leaves (as greens) | From true-leaf emergence until flowering | Pick fully grown lower leaves |
| Green pods (for vegetable use) | 45-60 days | While the pod is still slender and tender |
| Dry seed (early variety) | 75-90 days | When pods turn brown and the seeds harden |
| Dry seed (late variety) | 90-125 days | |
| Harvest frequency | Split over 2-3 rounds | Pods mature 8-15 days after flowering |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Cowpea keeps flowering, so it cannot be harvested all at once. New flowers open every morning, and those pods mature in 8-15 days. So ripening moves upward from the bottom of the plant, and picking happens in two or three rounds. If a ripe pod is left too long it splits and drops its seeds, and more importantly, cowpea weevils lay eggs on it. Picking at the right time protects both the yield and its storability at once.

> **A pod left hanging ripe in the field for too long is what causes weevil damage in storage later. For cowpea, harvesting on time is not a matter of laziness — it is a matter of whether there will be any food left six months from now.**

### 12-2. Harvest Technique

| Step | Details |
|---|---|
| 1 | Do it on a clear, dry day, after the dew has dried |
| 2 | Twist off by hand only the pods that have turned brown |
| 3 | For the final harvest, cut or pull the whole plant and dry it together with the pods |
| 4 | Spread the pods on a net or mat and dry in the sun for 3-5 days |
| 5 | Once bone dry, thresh by beating |
| 6 | Return the stems and leaves to the field |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Drying in a net is useful for cowpea too. Some pods split open on their own as they dry, and if they are inside a net, the seeds that pop out collect at the bottom of the net. An onion net or napa-cabbage net works, or a tightly woven sack if neither is available. Spread it out on a sunny day and shake it now and then, and half the threshing is done on its own.

### 12-3. Threshing — Doing It Without Tools

| Method | Tool | Procedure | Efficiency |
|---|---|---|---|
| Stomping in a sack | A thick burlap sack | Put dry pods in the sack, tie the mouth shut, and stomp on it | Most convenient for small amounts |
| Beating inside a net | Onion net, napa-cabbage net | Put dry pods in the net and beat it with a stick | No seeds get lost |
| Flail | A tool with a whip-stick hung from a long handle | Spread pods thinly on a floor mat and strike down on them | Suited to a medium scale |
| Beating with a club | A wooden stick | Put in a sack or spread on a floor mat and beat | Substitutes when there is no flail |
| Shelling by hand | None | Twist the pod open | Not bad efficiency, since one pod holds 10-20 seeds |
| Rubbing with the feet | None | Spread on a floor mat and rub around with shod feet | Suited to small amounts |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Since each cowpea pod holds ten to twenty seeds, shelling by hand goes fast even that way. At a small scale, hand-shelling is the surest way to avoid damaging the seeds, and it especially suits the portion set aside for seed. When using a flail or a club, striking lightly many times, rather than hard, breaks fewer seeds.

### 12-4. Cleaning — Sorting by Wind

| Method | Principle | Procedure |
|---|---|---|
| Winnowing in the wind | Seeds are heavy; hulls and debris are light | On a windy day, hold a winnowing basket or wide bowl up high and pour slowly |
| Basket tossing | Shake up and down while tossing forward | Heavy seeds stay at the back while light material is pushed forward |
| Fanning | One person pours while another fans a breeze | Used on a day with no wind |
| Sieving | Sort by hole size | Filters out broken seeds and debris |
| Floating in water | Empty husks and insect-eaten seeds are light | Pour into a tub of water and skim off what floats. Dry again the same day |
| Sorting by hand | By eye | Weevil holes are clearly visible |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;For cowpea, it is worth always including the water-floating step. A seed that a cowpea weevil has started eating into is hollow inside and floats. Filtering these out at this stage greatly reduces damage during storage. However, cowpea that has been soaked in water must be dried fully again the same day, and this method is not used for seed stock. For seed, sort by eye and by hand instead. Because cowpea seeds are large and have a dark ring at the "navel," weevil holes are easy to spot.

## 13. Seed Saving

| Item | Method |
|---|---|
| Selecting seed plants | Mark plants in the field ahead of time that show no disease symptoms, carry many pods, and have not lodged |
| Harvest timing | Pick right after the pods have turned fully brown |
| Separation | Shell by hand so the seeds are not cracked |
| Sorting | Discard anything with insect holes, spots, cracked seeds, or discoloration |
| Drying | 12% moisture or below |
| Storage | Keep in an airtight container in a cool, dark place, in a container separate from the food supply |
| Storage period | 3-4 years sealed at room temperature |
| Renewal | Every 3-4 years, mix in some seed from another source if available |

> **Cowpea's prevalence in South Korea is graded B, so outside its main growing areas it is not easy to obtain seed again. Saving your own seed is a precondition for continuing to grow this crop at all.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;What matters most in saving cowpea seed is choosing seed that weevils have not gotten into. Weevil eggs are not visible from outside; they sit on the seed coat as tiny white specks. Sort out seed stock right at harvest, dry it completely, seal it, and then give it a cold treatment — setting it outside for a few days in the depths of winter to freeze and then bringing it back in — which kills the eggs and larvae. This treatment does not harm germination, making it one of the few methods usable for seed stock.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Cowpea self-fertilizes, so there is no need to worry about isolation distance. Even so, if adzuki bean or mung bean are growing nearby, keep harvest and storage clearly separate so the seeds do not get mixed. Marking seed plants in the field ahead of time also lets you select for traits that actually matter, such as wilt resistance, pod count, and resistance to lodging.

| Seed storage period and germination rate |
|---|
| Harvest year: 90% or more |
| After 1 year: 85-90% |
| After 2 years: 70-85% |
| After 3 years: 50-70% |
| After 4 years: drops sharply |

## 14. Toxicity and Antinutrients

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Among legumes, cowpea carries relatively low toxicity risk. It has neither the strong lectin that can poison someone from just a few raw kidney beans, nor a compound like the one in fava beans that triggers acute hemolysis in people with a certain condition. Even so, the dry seed contains antinutrients like other legumes, and must be cooked thoroughly.

| Compound | What it does | Symptoms | How to remove it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Trypsin inhibitor | Blocks the action of protein-digesting enzymes | Stomach pain, diarrhea, reduced protein utilization | Soak 6 hours, then boil for 1 hour |
| Lectin | Attaches to the intestinal lining and hinders absorption | Digestive discomfort | Destroyed by heating. Much weaker than in kidney beans |
| Phytic acid | Binds iron, zinc, and calcium and blocks their absorption | Anemia and zinc deficiency with long-term intake | Reduced by 60-80% through soaking, sprouting, or fermentation |
| Oligosaccharides | Not digested by humans; fermented by gut bacteria instead | Bloating | Discard the soaking water after soaking |
| Oxalic acid in the leaves | Binds calcium | Harmless in small amounts | Blanching and discarding the water removes a substantial amount |
| Mold toxins | Produced by mold during storage | Dangerous | Moldy seed is not made safe by washing or cooking. Discard it |

> **Cowpea leaves are not eaten raw either. Because they contain oxalic acid and a small amount of other antinutrients, always blanch them before eating, and discard the blanching water.**

| Treatment | Time/temperature | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Soaking | 6 hours in room-temperature water | Softens the seed coat and shortens cooking time |
| Discarding the soak water | Always | Removes the oligosaccharides and antinutrients that leached into it |
| Boiling | 1 hour at a boil | 20-30 minutes for seeds that have been dehulled and split |
| Blanching the leaves | 1-2 minutes in boiling water | Discard the blanching water |
| Checking doneness | Mashes easily when pressed with a finger | If a hard core remains, it is undercooked |

## 15. Storage Without Refrigeration

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Cowpea's storability grade is SS. The dry seed lasts three years at room temperature, and because it is low in fat, it also goes rancid slowly. Cowpea weevils, however, are the biggest threat to stored cowpea, and left with no countermeasures, the whole stock can be damaged within half a year.

> **Beans are not lost to rot — they are lost to insects eating them. For cowpea, storage is a task as important as growing it.**

### 15-1. Drying Comes First

| Step | Method | Check |
|---|---|---|
| Preliminary drying | Put picked pods in a net and dry in the sun for 3-5 days | Some pods begin splitting open on their own |
| Drying after threshing | Spread thinly on a floor mat and dry in the sun for 2-3 days | Turn often for even drying |
| Target moisture | 12% or below, for both food and seed use | When bitten, teeth do not sink in — the seed cracks with a snap |
| Salt test | Put dry salt in a jar with the beans and shake | If salt sticks to the jar walls, it is still damp |
| Cooling | Let cool completely before packing away | Sealing while still warm causes condensation |

### 15-2. Controlling Cowpea Weevils in Storage

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The cowpea weevil is the biggest threat to stored cowpea. What makes it frightening is that it starts already in the field. Adult weevils lay eggs on pods still on the plant, and those eggs hatch inside storage and bore into the beans. A generation takes less than a month, and because cowpea seeds are large, several larvae can grow inside a single seed. This is why cowpea storage losses have traditionally been such a serious problem in Africa.

| Method | Principle | Application | Effect |
|---|---|---|---|
| Full sealing | Cuts off oxygen and suffocates them | Fill a glass jar or metal tin completely and seal it | Most reliable. In 3-4 weeks even eggs and larvae die |
| Triple bagging | Three layers of plastic bag block oxygen | Nest thick plastic bags inside one another and tie each one tightly | Widely used in West Africa; there are cases of weevil emergence being completely suppressed for four months |
| Mixing in wood ash | Fine particles scratch the exoskeleton's waxy layer and dehydrate them | Mix in 2-5% of the beans' volume | Rinse with water before eating |
| Diatomaceous earth | Its silica shells damage the exoskeleton | 1-3g per kg of beans | More effective than ash. Only works when dry |
| Oil coating | An oil film stops eggs from attaching and hatching | Mix 2-5mL of cooking oil per kg of beans and rub it in | Not used for seed stock |
| Cold treatment | Eggs and larvae die below freezing | Set outside for a few days in the depths of winter, then bring in | One of the few methods usable for seed stock |
| Heat treatment | Eggs and larvae die at 60 degrees | Spread thinly in the sun for half a day | Not used for seed stock |
| Dried chili, Sichuan pepper, or mugwort | Essential-oil compounds suppress egg-laying | Layer them in with the beans | A supplementary measure |
| Burying in ash | Combines sealing with physical control | Layer ash and beans in an earthenware jar | Traditionally used widely |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The triple-bagging method deserves a note of its own. It works by putting a bag inside a thick plastic bag, and another bag inside that one, then twisting and tying each mouth tightly shut. As the beans and insects breathe, the oxygen inside is used up and carbon dioxide builds up, and the layers of plastic block any exchange with outside air, suffocating the insects. It is known as a method that dramatically cut cowpea storage losses in West Africa, and it is a practical alternative where glass jars or metal tins are in short supply. Rats can bite through plastic, though, so the bags must always be kept inside a container or up on a shelf where rats cannot reach them.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Cold treatment is especially valuable for seed stock. Setting a container of seed outside for a few days in the depths of winter lets sub-freezing temperatures kill the eggs and larvae, without harming germination. When bringing it back in, do not open the container right away — leave it for a few hours until it matches room temperature. Opening cold beans immediately in a warm room causes condensation on the surface, which leads to mold.

### 15-3. Storage Containers and Inspection

| Container | Sealing | Duration | Notes |
|---|:---:|---|---|
| Glass jar | Very good | 3+ years | Condition can be checked by eye |
| Metal tin | Very good | 3+ years | Fully blocks rats and insects |
| Earthenware jar | Good | 2-3 years | Temperature changes slowly, and rats cannot get through |
| Triple plastic bag | Good | 1-2 years | Keep out of rats' reach |
| Underground storage pit | Good | 2-3 years | Water seeping in loses the entire stock |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;When inspecting, the most reliable senses are hearing and smell. A rustling sound when you put your ear to the container means insects are active, and a damp smell means mold has started. Small round holes visible on the surface mean weevils have already gone through a generation. Even at this point, taking everything out, drying it in the sun, floating it to skim off the hollow seeds, mixing in ash, and sealing it again can still save what remains. Because weevil damage progresses quickly in cowpea, checking once a month is appropriate.

## 16. Cooking and Processing — Leaf Greens and Seed

### 16-1. Cowpea Leaves — A Summer Vegetable

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This is the biggest reason cowpea stands apart from other legumes: you eat the leaves. The young leaves contain vitamin A and vitamin C, which the dry seed does not have, and plenty of iron besides. That means the same field yields a vegetable all through the summer while you wait for the beans to ripen.

| Step | Details | Key point |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Picking | Pick fully grown lower leaves along with their petiole | Do not disturb the growing point |
| 2. Limit | Within one-third of a plant's total leaves | Taking more reduces seed yield |
| 3. Timing | Stop once flowering begins | After that, the leaves are needed to fill out the pods |
| 4. Washing | Rinse under running water | |
| 5. Blanching | 1-2 minutes in boiling water | Not eaten raw |
| 6. Rinsing | Rinse in cold water and squeeze out the water | Discard the blanching water |
| 7. Seasoning | Toss with salt, oil, garlic, and the like | Also good tossed with doenjang |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Cowpea leaves are thick and have a slightly grassy smell, and blanching softens their texture. The blanching water is discarded to remove the oxalic acid and small amount of antinutrients along with it. Squeezed dry after blanching and tossed as a seasoned side dish, it takes much the same place as spinach or Swiss chard.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;When there is a large quantity, blanch and dry it for storage. Blanched, squeezed dry, spread thin, and dried hard in the sun, it keeps until winter. Soaked in water and tossed as a side dish, or added to soup, this brings a summer vegetable to the winter table. In winter, when fresh vegetables disappear, this dried green becomes a source of vitamins and minerals. This is exactly the method behind muknamul (dried, rehydrated greens) in Korean food culture, and cowpea leaves are handled the same way.

### 16-2. Cooking the Seed and Saving Fuel

| Method | Fuel | Procedure |
|---|:---:|---|
| Dehulling and splitting | Little | Soaked, dehulled, and split beans cook in 20-30 minutes |
| Pressure cooking | Least | Pressure-cook soaked beans for 15 minutes |
| Residual-heat cooking | Very little | Boil for 15 minutes, turn off the heat, then wrap the pot in a straw or chaff box or a blanket and leave it for 2-3 hours |
| Roasted cowpea flour | Almost none | Roast, grind, and store; mix with water to eat |
| Green pods | Very little | Young pods only need a few minutes of blanching |
| Leaves | Very little | Just 1-2 minutes of blanching is enough |
| Sprouting | None | Sprouting produces vitamin C |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Dehulling and splitting is a useful processing step for cowpea too. In West Africa, there is a long tradition of grinding cowpeas, winnowing away the hulls, and then frying or steaming the resulting paste. Lightly pounding soaked cowpeas in a mortar to remove the hulls, splitting them in half, and drying them cuts cooking time to less than half. Dehulled beans store less well, though, so process only as much as will be eaten within a month or two.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Residual-heat cooking also suits cowpea well. If you boil it for 15 minutes and then put it into an insulated box, the internal temperature stays high for more than two hours and cooking continues on its own. Cowpea has no strong toxic compound like kidney beans that requires reaching a full 100 degrees, so it can be cooked safely this way. A wooden box packed with straw or rice hull, or simply a thick blanket wrapped entirely around the pot, serves as that insulated box.

### 16-3. Storage Processing

| Product | Procedure summary | Storage | Value |
|---|---|---|---|
| Blanched, dried cowpea leaves | Blanch, squeeze dry, and dry hard | Until winter | Used as muknamul |
| Cowpea flour | Roast and grind | Sealed, 3-6 months | Needs no cooking fuel |
| Dehulled, split cowpea | Soak, dehull, and split | Sealed, 1-2 months | Cuts cooking time in half |
| Blanched, dried green pods | Blanch and dry | A few months | Added to winter soups and braises |
| Cowpea doenjang (made the same way as doenjang, Korean soybean paste) | Boil, mash, ferment, then soak in brine | Several years | An alternative when soybean is scarce |

## 17. Annual Work Calendar

| Month | Task | Key point |
|---|---|---|
| January | Check stored stock, cold treatment, seed germination test | Freeze seed in the depths of winter to kill weevils |
| February | Plan the field, assign poor plots to cowpea | Cowpea is the crop that takes on poor land |
| March | Turn the compost, prepare bone meal | Phosphorus is the most important element for cowpea |
| April | Manage the preceding crop, plan beds and drainage ditches | A 20cm bed height is the precondition |
| May | Prepare the field, build beds, cut drainage ditches | Check that water drains before the monsoon |
| June | Sow (central South Korea from late month, southern South Korea from mid month), inoculate, cover with straw | Plant in the early morning or at dusk |
| July | First and second weeding, thinning, the cutoff for late sowing | Weed control in the first five weeks is everything |
| August | Flowering, watch for pod borer, pick leaves, check root nodules | Look inside the flowers every morning |
| September | Staggered harvest begins, dry in nets | Do not leave ripe pods in the field for long |
| October | Finish harvest, thresh, clean, return stalks and leaves to the field | Finish before frost |
| November | Dry, store, dry leaf greens | Pack into triple bags or airtight containers |
| December | Check storage, process, plan for next year | Open the container once a month |

### 17-1. Five Things Not to Forget

| Item | Details |
|---|---|
| Drainage | Cowpea is the most drought-tolerant and the least flood-tolerant. A 20cm bed height is the precondition |
| Phosphorus | It fixes its own nitrogen but cannot make its own phosphorus. Keep bone meal and fish bones on hand |
| The first 5 weeks | Early growth is slow and gets choked out by weeds. Weeding during this period decides the yield |
| Leaves | The same field yields a vegetable all summer. Up to one-third of the leaves, only before flowering |
| Storage | Cowpea weevils can ruin the whole stock within half a year. Full drying and sealing matter as much as growing it |
