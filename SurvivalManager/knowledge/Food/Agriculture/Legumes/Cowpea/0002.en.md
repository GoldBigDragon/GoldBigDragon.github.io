## 1. Disaster-Preparedness Evaluation

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The cowpea's overall grade is C, a score of 43.0. Broken down by scenario, its short-term ability to get through the early stage of a disaster is C (41.3), its long-term self-reliance ability across multiple years is B (44.8), and its value as a supplementary food beyond the staple is SS (64.2). The fact that its long-term score is 3.5 points higher than its short-term score sums up the cowpea's character: it is a crop whose relative standing rises the more years pass and the worse conditions get.

### 1-3. How to Read the Strengths

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The criteria graded SS are three: fertilizer demand, infrastructure needs, and storability. Those graded S are seven: protein density, micronutrients, water use, pest-control self-reliance, continuous-cropping tolerance, taste, and seed saving. A legume with this many S grades is uncommon.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Pest-control self-reliance's S stands out most in this table. Few crops in this table earn an S on this criterion. It means the cowpea can be managed with cultivation methods alone even with no pesticide whatsoever, and in practice, smallholder farms in Africa grow it with almost no inputs at all. Having lived alongside people for a long time in its homeland, it has reached a balance with the pests and diseases of that region, while in South Korea, where its history of cultivation is short, the density of crop-specific pests and diseases is itself low.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Continuous-cropping tolerance's S is also a rare value among legumes. Pea requires 5-7 years and kidney bean and chickpea require 3, yet the cowpea is among the best in the legume family for continuous-cropping tolerance. Where fields are small and rotation is hard to arrange, this difference greatly increases the freedom to plan a layout. And because it also doubles as green manure, it can even be managed by planting it in the same spot again and again — harvesting the seed one year and plowing it under at flowering the next.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Water use's S should be read together with its tolerance for poor soil. The cowpea is a plant adapted to the savanna climate's 400-700mm of annual rainfall, and it tolerates a pH from 4.5 to 8.0. It grows in sandy ground and in soil worn dry by years of continuous cropping alike. This ability does not show under good conditions. The worse conditions get, the wider the gap with other crops becomes.

> **The cowpea's value lies not in how much it yields on good farmland, but in whether it yields anything at all on bad farmland. In a drought year, a year when fertilizer has run out, in the sandy corner of a field, the cowpea still delivers something.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Speed to harvest's A is also of great value. It can be harvested in 80-90 days and planted as late as mid-to-late July, so it can fill in for a spot where another crop has failed. Along with mung bean, it is a crop worth keeping seed of on hand for recovering from failure.

### 1-4. How to Read the Weaknesses

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The one criterion graded E is cold hardiness. D went to calories per area, perenniality, sunlight response, cooking & processing, tillage energy, and non-food value; C went to pest & disease pressure; and B went to temperature range and prevalence in South Korea.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Prevalence in South Korea at B is the first practical wall you run into. The cowpea is grown mainly in 3-4 provinces, with clearly defined main growing areas, and seed is not easy to obtain elsewhere. It is not as difficult as chickpea or lentil, but not as easy as kidney bean or mung bean either. Once you have secured seed, you need to carry it forward by seed saving; fortunately, being self-pollinating, the line stays pure and it keeps for 3-4 years at room temperature.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Calories per area at D come to 686kcal per 1m², only 0.26× the self-sufficiency line. The cowpea cannot fill the stomach on its own. That said, this figure assumes good conditions; on poor, dry farmland other crops fall far more sharply, so the relative standing shifts.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Cold hardiness at E and vulnerability to waterlogging together narrow the growing conditions. One frost kills it, and conversely, once water pools, it collapses faster than any other legume. It is no coincidence that a crop this tolerant of drought is this weak against flooding: a root structure built for dry savanna cannot survive in oxygen-starved soil. Raising the bed at least 20cm on a cowpea field is not optional.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Pest & disease pressure's C sitting alongside pest-control self-reliance's S may look puzzling. It means pests and diseases do occur, but they can be managed without chemicals. In practice, though, there are two pests to watch for: the pod borer moth, which bores into pods, and the adzuki bean weevil, which hollows out the beans in storage. The adzuki bean weevil especially can ruin an entire stored batch of cowpeas within half a year, so Section 15 covers it separately.

## 2. What Kind of Plant Is This?

### 2-1. Classification and Origin

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The cowpea is an annual herb of the legume family (Fabaceae), genus Vigna. It shares its genus with adzuki bean and mung bean, and the three are close relatives. This fact matters in practice. The root-nodule bacteria that pair with the cowpea belong to the Bradyrhizobium group, the same group used by adzuki bean and mung bean. Soil from a field that grew adzuki beans works as-is as an inoculant for the cowpea, and since adzuki bean is widely grown in South Korea, obtaining an inoculant is not difficult. On the other hand, it also shares pests, diseases, and weevils with adzuki bean, so it is better not to plant it next to, or in succession with, adzuki beans.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Its center of origin is the savanna of West Africa. That environment, with its sharply divided wet and dry seasons and poor soil, explains the cowpea's traits entirely. Its growing period shortened so it could finish growth within the brief rainy season; because water is scarce, its roots grow deep and its leaves conserve water; and because the soil lacks nutrients, its capacity for nitrogen fixation grew strong. It tolerates heat up to 35°C, while, conversely, it knows no frost at all.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The cowpea self-pollinates. Its flowers open in the morning and fertilization is complete within the day, with the petals enclosing the reproductive parts. Even when several varieties are planted side by side, their traits barely mix, and seed saved and replanted grows into the same thing as its parent.

### 2-2. Appearance

| Organ | Characteristics |
|---|---|
| Leaves | Trifoliate, three leaflets to a set. Heart-shaped, thick, and sometimes tinged purple |
| Stem | The upright type grows 50-80cm; the vining type reaches 2-3m. A semi-upright, intermediate type also exists |
| Roots | A straight taproot goes down deep, with thick nodules on the fine lateral roots |
| Flowers | Butterfly-shaped, white, pale purple, or purple. Open in the morning and close the same day |
| Pods | Slender and cylindrical, reaching 10-25cm. Hold 10-20 seeds |
| Seeds | Kidney-shaped; many have a black ring around the hilum. White, red, or black |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;That the cowpea's leaves are thick and glossy on the surface is the physical basis for its drought tolerance. A thick cuticle layer blocks moisture evaporation, and in hot weather the leaves stand upright to reduce the area exposed to sunlight. This same thick leaf also gives it value as a vegetable: blanched, it turns tender with a pleasant bite, making it good eaten as namul (a seasoned vegetable side dish).

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;It is also distinctive that the pods are long and hold as many as 10 to 20 seeds. Compared with chickpea or lentil, whose pods hold only one or two seeds, the value of a single pod is large. Its low yield per area comes from bearing few plants per area, not from the pods themselves being poor. Young pods can be eaten whole, and in parts of Asia, dedicated lines exceeding 30cm in length are grown as a vegetable.

### 2-3. Growth Stages

| Stage | Timing | Key Physiology | Key Nutrients |
|---|---|---|---|
| Germination & seedling stage | Sowing to 2 weeks | Germination is fast. Roots go down deep first | Phosphorus, a small amount of nitrogen |
| Vegetative stage | Weeks 2-6 | Stems and leaves increase and root nodules become active | Nitrogen supplied by root nodules |
| Flowering stage | Weeks 6-8 | New flowers open every morning | Phosphorus, boron |
| Pod-filling stage | Weeks 8-10 | Pods lengthen and seeds thicken | Potassium, phosphorus |
| Ripening & maturity stage | Weeks 10-13 | Lower pods turn brown and dry first | Potassium; stop irrigation |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;It is worth knowing that early growth is slow. The cowpea sends its roots down deep before building up its above-ground parts. For the first few weeks after planting it shows no visible growth and looks like a failure, but by then the roots have already spread considerably. This strategy carries forward into its later drought tolerance. During that period, though, if weeds get established first, the cowpea gets suppressed. This is why weed control in the first 5 weeks after sowing is nearly the whole of growing cowpeas.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;From flowering to maturity is very fast. Pods ripen 8-15 days after the flower falls. So while the lower pods are ripening, new flowers keep opening above, and harvest ends up split into two or three rounds.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There is something worth noting about nitrogen in the early stage. Root nodules do not work right from sowing. It takes 3 weeks for bacteria to infect the roots, nodules to form, and nitrogen to start being handed over. In the meantime, the young cowpea survives on the seed's own nutrients and nitrogen already in the soil. However, the cowpea's strong tolerance for poor soil lets it get through this stretch better than other beans. Nitrogen in the base fertilizer is set at just 0-2kg, the lowest among legumes, and it is often fine to leave it out entirely.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Looking at the cowpea's nutrition without the leaves is seeing only half the picture. The dried seed has no vitamin C, but young leaves have it in abundance, and carotene, a precursor of vitamin A, is also present in the leaves. This is why Africa treats cowpea leaves as an important vegetable, and it is a value that nutrition counts based on the seed alone never reveal. If you grow cowpeas in a disaster situation, it is right to pick and eat the leaves all summer long while waiting for the seed.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The amino acid profile is also worth a look. Cowpea protein is rich in lysine and a little short on methionine. Coarse grains like sorghum, millet, and maize are exactly the opposite. The African custom of eating sorghum together with cowpea, and the practice of intercropping the two in the same field, both rest on this complementarity. In our own diet, too, mixing cowpea into a bowl of mixed-grain rice achieves the same effect.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Fat is low, around 1.5%, which favors storage. It almost never turns rancid, so even ground into flour it keeps for a long time. In exchange for a lower calorie density, it is on the easy-to-store side.
