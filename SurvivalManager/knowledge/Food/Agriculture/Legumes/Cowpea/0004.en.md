## 5. Field Preparation and Fertilizer - Nitrogen Balance and Fertilization

### 5-4. The Trap in Nitrogen Fixation

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The saying that planting beans improves the soil has hardened into farming common sense. It is half right and half wrong. Without understanding this point precisely, a field planted with legumes for several years running can end up more depleted instead.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Let's check it with numbers. When the cowpea grows well, it yields 200kg of dried seed from 1,000m². Cowpea seed is 19.6% protein, and dividing protein by 6.25 gives the nitrogen amount. 19.6 ÷ 6.25 is 3.14, meaning about 3.1% of seed weight is pure nitrogen. The nitrogen contained in 200kg is about 6.3kg, or 63kg converted to a hectare.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Meanwhile, the nitrogen the cowpea's root nodules fix, under good conditions, is 80-120kg per hectare. Fitting for a crop adapted to poor soil, this is among the higher figures for legumes. Setting what goes out against what comes in looks like this.

| Item | Nitrogen per Hectare | Direction |
|---|---|---|
| Nodule fixation (good conditions) | 80-120kg | In |
| Nodule fixation (nodules killed by waterlogging) | 15-30kg | In |
| Removed in the harvested seed | 55-70kg | Out |
| Nitrogen remaining in stems and leaves | 25-40kg | Stays if returned to the field; leaves if burned or carried away |
| Nitrogen remaining in roots and nodules | 10-20kg | Usually stays in the field |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The cowpea has one of the most comfortable nitrogen balances of any legume. Fixation clearly exceeds removal, so even returning just the stems and leaves leaves nitrogen behind in the field. This is the basis for the evaluation table's fertilizer-demand SS along with its rating as doubling for green manure, and in practice the cowpea is also widely used purely as green manure in Africa and the southern United States.

> **A legume improves the soil not because it fixes nitrogen, but because part of the nitrogen it fixes stays in the field, held in its stems and leaves. Carry those stems and leaves away, and the bean becomes a crop that strips the soil instead.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There is one point to watch for with the cowpea, though: picking the leaves to eat as a vegetable. The leaves contain a fair amount of nitrogen, so nitrogen leaves the field in proportion to how much leaf you pick and carry away. A small amount is not a problem, but if you keep picking leaves as a main vegetable, that much less nitrogen is left to remain in the stems and leaves. Keep in mind the cycle of returning the food scraps and human waste that result from eating the leaves, as compost, back into that same field.

| Principle | Details |
|---|---|
| Stems and leaves always go back to the field | Return all the stems and leaves left after threshing |
| Do not burn them | Burning releases nitrogen as gas, leaving only potassium and phosphorus in the ash |
| Do not dig up the roots | The nitrogen in the roots and nodules is the share easiest to leave behind |
| If you pick a lot of leaves, think about the cycle | Return as much nitrogen through compost as left with the leaves |
| Choosing green manure | Plowing it under at flowering leaves 80-120kg of nitrogen per hectare in the field |
| If fed to livestock, return the manure | If the stems and leaves were fed to livestock, be sure to put that animal's manure back into the same field |

### 5-5. Field Preparation

| Item | Method | Under Disaster Conditions |
|---|---|---|
| Tilling | Till 20-30cm deep and level the surface evenly | If it is a spot where the previous crop was just harvested, digging and turning just the row is enough |
| Raised bed | 20cm or higher | The key to preventing waterlogging damage |
| Drainage channel | 30cm deep or more, so water drains all the way out | Test by pouring water in before the monsoon |
| pH correction | Tolerates pH 5.0-8.0 | Not urgent, given the wide tolerance range |
| Lime | 200kg/1,000m² when below pH 5.5 | Substitute roasted, ground shellfish shells or eggshells, or wood ash |
| Organic matter | Fully rotted compost 1,000-1,500kg/1,000m² | It grows even on poor soil, but yield rises if this is added |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The cowpea's pH tolerance range of 4.5-8.0 is the widest of any legume, so liming has a low priority. As long as the field is not severely acidic, it grows fine without lime. It makes more sense overall to save the lime and use it on another crop's field instead, such as pea or soybean.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Instead, effort should go into drainage. Till deep to open a path for water to drain downward, raise the bed, and dig the drainage channels deep. Choosing a spot where water does not collect from the moment you pick the field is the surest approach, and on a sloped field, the upper side is where the cowpea belongs.

### 5-6. Fertilizer Rates

| Fertilizer | Per 1,000m² | Per m² | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Nitrogen | 0-2kg | 0-2g | The lowest among legumes. It is fine to leave it out entirely |
| Phosphorus | 4-7kg | 4-7g | All as base fertilizer. Essential for nodule development on poor soil |
| Potassium | 3-5kg | 3-5g | All as base fertilizer |
| Compost | 1,000-1,500kg | 1-1.5kg | Use fully rotted compost |
| Lime | 200kg if below pH 5.5 | 200g | 3-4 weeks before sowing |

> **The cowpea is a crop that needs almost no nitrogen fertilizer. Giving it nitrogen instead only makes the stems and leaves lush while pods decrease, and blocked airflow increases disease. Planting it on poor farmland is this crop's true purpose.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Phosphorus is different. What actually governs the cowpea's yield on poor farmland is phosphorus. Because nitrogen fixation needs a great deal of energy and phosphorus is used in that metabolism, if phosphorus is lacking, nodules may form but fail to do their job. Without chemical fertilizer, bone meal and fish bones are essentially the only routes to phosphorus. When planting cowpeas in poor farmland, this phosphorus is the very first thing to secure, before anything else.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Top-dressing is, in principle, unnecessary. Only when growth is clearly poor, dilute urine 10× with water and pour a very small amount onto the soil between plants. Keep it off the leaves, and skip it if the leaf color is already dark and lush.

### 5-7. Disaster Substitute Fertilizers

| Substitute Resource | Nutrients Supplied | How to Use | Caution |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bone meal | Phosphorus, calcium | Roast, grind, and mix into compost | The single most important resource for cowpeas on poor soil |
| Fish bones and fish guts | Phosphorus, nitrogen | Bury and let them rot before using | Burying them as-is invites animals to dig them up |
| Wood ash | Potassium, phosphorus, calcium | Spread 100-150g per m² and mix in | The cowpea tolerates alkaline conditions too, so there is margin for it |
| Well-rotted compost | All-around | Apply 1-1.5kg per m² as base fertilizer | Under-rotted compost burns roots and carries disease |
| Diluted urine | Nitrogen, potassium | Dilute 10× with water and pour onto the soil between plants | Very small amounts only. Usually not needed |
| Shellfish shells, eggshells | Calcium | Roast, grind, and spread | Only when pH is below 5.5 |
| Previous crop's residue | Organic matter | Cover with barley straw or garlic stalks as mulch and fertilizer both | The cowpea is often planted as a follow-on crop |
| Rice hulls, rice straw | Potassium, silica | Cover as mulch | Prevents soil splash and weeds |

## 6. Sowing

### 6-1. Sowing Time

| Region/Cropping Type | Optimal Sowing Time | Sowing Limit | Harvest Time |
|---|---|---|---|
| Central South Korea | Late June-early July | Mid-July | Mid-to-late September |
| Southern South Korea | Mid-June-mid-July | Late July | Late September-early October |
| Follow-on crop (after a previous crop) | Mid-June-mid-July | Mid-July | Late September |
| Grown as green manure | June-July | - | Plowed under at flowering |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The cowpea is a crop that can be sown late. Its growing period is a short 80-90 days and it likes heat, so sowing it in the height of summer causes no problem. A trial in South Jeolla Province even recorded the highest yield from sowing on July 25. Since frost sets the sowing limit, just count back 90 days from the expected date of first frost.

> **The cowpea's ability to be sown late creates real practical value. It can go into a June field left empty after barley or garlic is harvested, or a July field plowed after a crop was wrecked by the monsoon. Along with mung bean, the cowpea is a crop that keeps you from losing an entire year.**

### 6-2. Seed Preparation

| Item | Method | Under Disaster Conditions |
|---|---|---|
| Sorting | Discard seeds with insect holes, discoloration, or wrinkling | Check first for adzuki bean weevil damage |
| Water float test | Soak in water for 3-4 hours and use only what sinks | What floats is hollow or insect-damaged |
| Hot-water treatment | Soak in 50-55°C water for 10-15 minutes | Kills seed-borne pathogens |
| Ash treatment | Coat with fine wood ash | Aids disinfection and drying, and helps deter birds and ants |
| Nodule inoculation | Coat with soil from a field that grew adzuki bean or cowpea | See Section 5-3. Do not combine with disinfection |
| Germination test | Wrap 20 seeds in a wet cloth, leave for a few days, and count | Germination is fast, so results come in three or four days |

### 6-3. Sowing Method

| Item | Standard | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Sowing depth | 1-3cm | Sow shallow. Germination suffers if too deep |
| Seeds per hole | 2-3 | Thin to leave 1 plant later |
| Target planting density | 15,000 plants per 1,000m² | The optimal density confirmed in South Korean trials |
| Plant spacing | 20-25cm | |
| Row spacing | 60-70cm (single row); narrower if 2 rows per bed | Ensures airflow and a path for harvesting |
| Seeding rate (single cropping) | 1.5-2kg/1,000m² | On the low side among legumes |
| Seeding rate (follow-on/late sowing) | 2-3kg/1,000m² | The later it is sown, the denser the planting |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;A sowing depth of just 1-3cm is different from other beans. The cowpea prefers its seed buried shallow, and emergence suffers if it is buried deep. But because it is shallow, it dries out easily, so care must be taken right after sowing to keep the surface from drying, and a thin layer of straw laid over it helps.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Since this is a summer sowing, also account for the soil surface being hot under the blazing sun. Sowing early in the morning or toward evening keeps the root-nodule bacteria coated on the seed alive, and keeps the young shoot from being scorched as it emerges. Watering thoroughly once right after sowing and covering with straw makes emergence more even.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Right after sowing, the threats are birds and ants. Because the seed sits shallow, ants carry it off and pigeons dig it up to eat. Coating the seed with fine wood ash reduces their interest, and a thin layer of straw over the sowing row keeps off both birds and drying alike.

## 7. Water Management

| Stage | Water Management | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Sowing to germination | Keep the soil from drying out completely | Since the seed sits shallow, germination stops if the surface dries |
| Early growth to before flowering | Leave it to natural rainfall | Dryness drives the roots to go deep |
| Flowering to pod-filling | Irrigate if drought is severe | Water shortage during this period cuts yield the most |
| Monsoon season | Clear the drainage channels | The most dangerous stretch in growing cowpeas |
| Ripening stage | Stop irrigation | Mold grows if ripe pods get wet |

> **Water management for the cowpea is not the art of giving water but the art of taking it away. It endures drought on its own, but dies within days if water pools.**

| Symptom | Cause | Action |
|---|---|---|
| Leaves fold at midday and open in the evening | The cowpea's normal water-conserving response | Leave it as is |
| Leaves are still folded in the morning | Severe dryness | Irrigate. This is rare for the cowpea, though |
| Lower leaves turn yellow and roots are black | Root rot from excess moisture | Clear the drainage channels and mound up the soil |
| Plant suddenly wilts and dies | Flooding or wilt disease | Dig up the roots to check. If water is the cause, improve drainage; if disease, pull and burn it |
| The whole plant's leaves are pale yellow but not wilting | Root nodules died from waterlogging, cutting off nitrogen | Dig up the roots to check, then give a small amount of diluted urine |
| Flowers open but pods do not set | Severe dryness during flowering, or the pod borer moth | Irrigate, and split pods open to check for larvae |
| Only the stem grows long, with few pods | Excess nitrogen or excess moisture | Stop nitrogen and fix the drainage |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;A cowpea's leaves folding at midday is not disease but normal. It stands or folds its leaves to reduce the area exposed to sunlight and suppress transpiration. It opens again in the evening. This movement is one reason the cowpea can finish a growing season on just 250mm of water, and if the leaves are still folded in the morning, that is when water is genuinely short.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Mulching serves two purposes: conserving water and keeping soil from splashing up. A thin layer of straw or rice hulls reduces surface evaporation and stops raindrops from splashing soil onto the lower pods. However, if it is piled thick and the soil stays constantly damp, root rot increases instead, so lay it thin at 3-5cm, and during the monsoon, pull it back a little from the shoulders of the bed so the soil can dry.
