## Introduction
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Cowpea (Vigna unguiculata) is an annual legume native to West Africa, domesticated in the dry savanna south of the Sahara. Its clearest strength is tolerance of poor soil and drought: it sets seed on 400 mm of annual rainfall, grows in strongly acidic soil down to pH 4.5, and completes a season on total evapotranspiration of 250 mm, so water consumption is S, and pest-control self-reliance is S, the highest among legumes. What other legumes lack is that you eat its leaves, and it yields young leaves, green pods, and dry seed in turn. Its overall grade is C (43.0 points, 82nd of 183 valid species), with short term C (41.3), long term B (44.8), and supplementary food SS (64.2 points). Calories per area are D, at 686 kcal per ㎡, only 0.26 of the self-sufficiency line; cold tolerance is E, so a single frost kills it; and for all its tolerance of drought it is the weakest of the legumes against standing water. Domestic cultivation spread is B, so outside 3-4 provinces seed is not easy to get, and if you have secured it you must carry it on by saving your own seed. The place for cowpea is not as the main crop of a good field but where other crops cannot grow: sandy ground, dry slopes, and exhausted plots.

> **The value of cowpea lies not in how much it yields in a good field but in whether it can yield in a bad one. In a drought year, a year when fertilizer has run out, on the sandy corner of a field, cowpea still gives something.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Pest-control self-reliance S means it can be managed by cultural methods alone with no chemicals, and continuous-cropping tolerance S is the most relaxed among legumes. Harvest speed A means harvesting in 80-90 days and being able to plant until mid-to-late July, which fills a place where another crop has failed. Pest and disease pressure C can be handled without chemicals, but two pests, the legume pod borer and the adzuki bean weevil, need care. The grade for each item is in the Crop Evaluation Results document.

## 2. Plant Overview
| Item | Content |
|:---|:---|
| Classification | Annual of the genus Vigna in the legume family. Native to the West African savanna and self-pollinating |
| Leaves, stem | Leaves are trifoliate, with three leaflets to a set, thick and glossy |
| Roots, flowers | A straight taproot goes deep and thick nodules form on the fine roots. Flowers are butterfly-shaped, white, light violet, or purple, opening in the morning and fading that same day |
| Pods, seeds | Slender cylindrical pods 10-25 cm long, with 10-20 seeds. The seed is kidney-shaped, often with a black ring around the hilum, in white, red, or black |

| Stage | Period | Key points |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Germination, seedling stage | 2 weeks after sowing | Germination is fast but top growth is slow and the root goes deep first |
| Vegetative growth | Weeks 2-6 | Stems and leaves increase and the nodules become active |
| Flowering | Weeks 6-8 | New flowers open every morning |
| Pod filling | Weeks 8-10 | Pods lengthen and seeds swell |
| Ripening, maturity | Weeks 10-13 | Pods dry to brown, starting from the bottom |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The thick leaves of cowpea block evaporation and let it withstand drought, and when blanched they turn tender and become a vegetable. From flowering to ripeness takes only 8-15 days, so while the lower pods ripen new flowers keep opening above, and harvest is divided into two or three rounds. Per 100 g of dried seed: 343 kcal, protein 19.61 g (22.9% of calories), fat about 1.5 g, carbohydrate 60-65 g, dietary fiber 10-20 g, potassium about 1,100 mg, iron 5-8 mg, and it is rich in calcium and magnesium, and being low in fat it goes rancid slowly. Vitamin C and carotene, which dried seed lacks, are in the young leaves.

## 3. Growing Environment and Varieties
| Condition | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Temperature | Germination 12-15℃ or above (optimum 25-30℃). Growth 15-40℃ (optimum 20-35℃), the most heat-tolerant of the legumes. Seed set 20-32℃. It has no cold tolerance, and a single frost kills it |
| Soil | Well-drained sandy soil or sandy loam (adapts from sandy ground to clay), pH 4.5-8.0 (optimum 5.5-7.0, the widest among legumes), effective soil depth 30 cm or more (optimum 50 cm). It dies if submerged for a day or more |

> **Cowpea, the most drought-tolerant, is the weakest against flooding. The root evolved for the dry savanna cannot endure ground where water collects and no oxygen reaches. In a cowpea field, beds and drainage ditches are not an option but a precondition.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Its heat tolerance makes midsummer sowing possible, and it can be planted even in acidic fields that have had no lime. In Korea, the biggest difficulty is that the growing period coincides with the rainy season, so assign it ground where water does not collect, and the upper part if the field slopes.

| Habit | Height, support | Characteristics |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Upright type | 50-80 cm, not needed | Resists lodging and is easy to harvest. Early lines finish in 80 days |
| Semi-upright type | 80-120 cm, usually not needed | High yield, little work, and many leaves, suited to picking leaves |
| Climbing type | 2-3 m, needed | Long harvest period but needs stake materials and labor |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Domestically bred varieties known are Jeonnam 1 and Jeonnam 2 (semi-upright) and Okdang and Seonhyeon (upright). In a disaster, upright or semi-upright types that need no stakes are sensible, and in regions with early frost choose an early upright line.

## 4. Field Preparation and Sowing
| Item | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Making the field | Plow 20-30 cm deep and level evenly. Beds 20 cm or higher (the key to preventing waterlogging), drainage ditches 30 cm or deeper so water drains all the way, and check by pouring water before the rainy season. Fully matured compost 1,000-1,500 kg per 1,000㎡ |
| Fertilization (1,000㎡) | Nitrogen 0-2 kg (the least among legumes, and more makes only stems and leaves lush, reduces pods, and increases disease), phosphorus 4-7 kg (all as basal fertilizer, supplied with bone meal or roasted fish bone powder), lime 200 kg only when pH is below 5.5 (3-4 weeks before sowing) |
| Nodule inoculation | Coat seed lightly moistened with water with topsoil 5-15 cm deep from a field that grew adzuki bean, mung bean, or cowpea, in shade, and plant within 30 minutes (the bacteria are weak to direct sun, drying, and heat). Nodules from the roots of wild adzuki bean can also be used. Soil from soybean or pea fields is less effective, and soil from a diseased field also carries pathogens |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Inoculation fails in waterlogged fields, phosphorus-poor fields, and under the midsummer sun when the soil surface exceeds 60℃, so plant in the early morning or at dusk. Around flowering (about 6 weeks after sowing), lift with a spade with the soil, wash, and split the nodules. With 20 or more per plant and red inside, it went well; white or pale green means checking for waterlogging and phosphorus deficiency; brown or black means dead (waterlogging if after the rains); and 5 or fewer is failed inoculation, so supplement a little nitrogen when leaves are yellow. Check twice, before and after the rainy season.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Fixation per hectare is 80-120 kg under good conditions (15-30 kg if waterlogging kills the nodules), and 55-70 kg leaves with the seed, so the nitrogen balance is the most comfortable of the legumes and it also serves as green manure. Return all the stalks and leaves left after threshing to the field, do not dig up the roots, and return the amount taken away with picked leaves as compost.

| Sowing | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Timing | Central region late June-early July (limit mid July), southern region mid June-mid July (limit late July). Harvest from mid September to early October. The limit date is 90 days counted back from the expected first frost |
| Seed preparation | Discard seeds with insect holes, discoloration, or wrinkles. Use only those that sink after soaking in water for 3-4 hours, and disinfect in 50-55℃ water for 10-15 minutes. Do not do inoculation together with disinfection. For a germination test, wrap 20 seeds in a wet cloth, leave 3-4 days, and count |
| Method | Depth 1-3 cm (deeper makes germination worse), 2-3 seeds per hill, row spacing 60-70 cm, target density 15,000 plants per 1,000㎡. Seeding rate per 1,000㎡ is 1.5-2 kg for a sole crop and 2-3 kg for a following crop or late sowing |

## 5. Water Management and Cultivation Management
| Period | Water management |
|:---|:---|
| Sowing to germination | Keep the soil from drying out completely (it is planted shallow, and germination stops if the surface dries) |
| Early growth to before flowering | Leave it to natural rainfall (dryness makes the root go deep) |
| Rainy season | Dig drainage ditches (the most dangerous stretch) |
| Ripening | Stop irrigation (ripe pods that get wet grow mold) |

> **Water management for cowpea is not the art of giving but the art of draining. It endures drought on its own, but if water stands it dies within days.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Leaves folding at midday and opening in the evening is a normal reaction that holds back transpiration, and only when they are still folded in the morning is it truly short of water. If lower leaves are yellow and roots are black, it is root rot from excess moisture, so dig drainage ditches and mound up the soil.

| Management | Method |
|:---|:---|
| Weeding | 1st at 2 weeks after emergence (while weeds are small), 2nd at 4-5 weeks (together with hilling), and finish all weeding before flowering, leaving only the strongest seedling per hill at 2-3 true leaves. The fine roots are in the surface layer, so lay the hoe flat and scrape only the top 2 cm, and pull by hand close to the plant. Once past 5 weeks and the leaves cover the ground, it no longer competes with weeds |
| Picking leaves | From when several true leaves have come out until before flowering, pick fully grown leaves from the lower part, not the new shoots, with the leaf stalk (do not touch the growing point). No more than a third of all leaves on a plant, and stop when flowering starts |
| Lodging | The causes are excess nitrogen and dense planting, so keep to the fertilizer rate and density. If wind and rain are a worry, run a line around the field at knee height and mound soil at the base at the 2nd weeding |

> **Cowpea gives something even if left alone after planting, but that does not mean you need do nothing. Weed control in the first 5 weeks is the one thing you must do.**

## 6. Pests, Diseases, and Nutrient Problems
| Pest or disease | Symptoms and conditions | Control without chemicals |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Fusarium wilt, root rot | Plants wilt and die and the stem base turns brown and soft. Excess moisture and continuous cropping | Drainage and raising the beds are nearly everything |
| Rust, bacterial leaf blight, mosaic virus | Orange powdery clumps on leaves (humid late growth) / angular brown spots on leaves (spread by wind and rain) / leaves mottled and crinkled and growth stops (spread by aphids) | Remove diseased leaves and secure airflow / do not enter the field after rain / no cure, so pull and burn and reduce aphids with soapy water (5 g of soap per 1 L of water) |
| Legume pod borer | Larvae hatched from eggs laid inside the petals bore into flowers and young pods and eat the seed, the worst pest in the field | From flowering, check flowers and young pods every morning and pick any with larvae and destroy them away from the field |
| Adzuki bean weevil | Lays eggs on pods in the field and eats the seed in storage, so everything can be lost in half a year | Harvest on time, dry completely, and seal (see section 9) |
| Stink bugs, birds | Pierce pods and make them empty / eat shallowly planted seed and sprouts | Catch by hand in the early morning while dew is on / cover with straw or netting until germination |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Bordeaux mixture is made by dissolving 400 g of copper sulfate and 400 g of quicklime separately in 100 L of water and mixing, and is used on bacterial and fungal diseases, while dry ash dusted on leaves on a dewy morning reduces leaf-eating insects and mold. Burn diseased residue and return only healthy residue to the field.

| Symptom | Cause and remedy |
|:---|:---|
| Whole leaf pale yellow and plant small | Nitrogen deficiency (most often because waterlogging killed the nodules). First dig up the roots and check the nodules, fix drainage, then give a little urine diluted 10 times |
| Slow growth and purplish leaves | Phosphorus deficiency, the most common on poor ground. Bone meal and roasted fish bone powder come first |
| Flowers open but pods do not set | Boron deficiency, severe drought at flowering, or the legume pod borer. Look inside the flowers first, otherwise compost of mixed materials |

## 7. Intercropping and Crop Rotation
| Compatibility | Companions |
|:---|:---|
| Good | Maize, sorghum, millet (they share nitrogen, and cowpea is rich in lysine while the grains are rich in methionine, so each fills the other's lack), basil (reported to reduce pests), barley, wheat, garlic, potato (as preceding crops they leave the field empty in June for cowpea as a following crop), Chinese cabbage, radish (they use the nitrogen left after cowpea) |
| Poor | Adzuki bean, mung bean (same genus, so they share pathogens and the adzuki bean weevil), crops that are watered heavily (cowpea is weakest against excess moisture) |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The rest period is 2-3 years and the ideal cycle is 3 years; Fusarium wilt and nematodes accumulate, but more slowly than with other legumes. An example 3-year rotation is year 1 barley or garlic (winter) followed by cowpea (summer) → year 2 sorghum or maize → year 3 Chinese cabbage and radish group or potato → cowpea again. On poor plots it is also possible to harvest seed one year and plow it under at flowering as green manure the next.

> **Cowpea is a crop that repairs a field. If you cycle a plot where no other crop would grow through cowpea for two or three years, nitrogen and organic matter build up and the field comes back to life.**

## 8. Harvest and Seed Saving
| Harvest | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Timing | Young leaves from after the true leaves open until before flowering; green pods 45-60 days after sowing while pods are still slender and soft; dry seed at 75-90 days for early and 90-125 days for late lines, when pods turn brown and the seeds harden. Seeds ripen 8-15 days after flowering, so pick in 2-3 rounds |
| Method | On a clear, dry day after the dew has dried, twist off only pods that have turned brown by hand. Spread the pods on a net or mat and dry in the sun for 3-5 days, shaking now and then |
| Threshing, winnowing | Stamping on a sack (small amounts), beating inside a net with a club (seeds do not spill), flail (strike many times with little force, medium scale), and shelling by hand (10-20 seeds per pod makes it quick and suits seed for sowing). Sift out broken seeds with a sieve, skim floating empty and insect-eaten seeds off in water, and dry them again that same day |

> **Pods left hanging ripe in the field for long cause weevil damage in the store. For cowpea, timely harvest is a question of whether there will be food half a year later.**

| Seed saving | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Selection, harvest | Mark in the field in advance plants with no disease symptoms, many pods, and no lodging. When pods turn completely brown, pick at once and shell by hand so the seeds do not break |
| Sorting, drying | Discard anything with insect holes, spots, broken seeds, or discoloration. Weevil eggs stick to the seed coat as tiny white dots and are hard to see from outside, so sort by eye and hand (do not float seed for sowing in water), and dry completely to moisture of 12% or below |
| Storage | 3-4 years sealed at room temperature. Cold treatment (see section 9) kills weevil eggs and larvae without harming germination. Keep harvest and storage separate from adzuki bean and mung bean so the seeds do not mix |

## 9. Storage
| Step | Method |
|:---|:---|
| Drying | Moisture of 12% or below for both food and seed, so that a bite leaves no dent and the bean cracks crisply. Pack only after cooling completely |
| Adzuki bean weevil control | A generation takes less than a month, and in cowpea, which has large seeds, several can grow in one seed. A triple bag (three thick plastic bags, each with its mouth tied tightly) cuts off oxygen and suffocates the insects, and since rats chew through, keep it inside a tin or on a shelf where rats cannot reach. Oil coating is 2-5 mL of cooking oil per 1 kg of beans (not for seed). Cold treatment is putting it outside for a few days in midwinter and bringing it back, and when bringing it in, do not open the container and leave it for a few hours until it matches room temperature |
| Containers, checking | Glass jars and metal tins last 3 years or more, and triple plastic bags 1-2 years. Put an ear to the container: rustling means insects are active, a musty smell is the start of mold, and small round holes on the surface mean weevils have passed a generation. Check once a month, and if there is damage, take everything out, dry it in the sun, skim off empty seeds floating in water, mix in ash, and seal again |

> **Beans are lost not by rotting but by being eaten by insects. For cowpea, storage is as important a job as cultivation.**

## 10. Cooking and Precautions
| Component | What it does | How to remove |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Trypsin inhibitors | Block protein-digesting enzymes, causing abdominal pain, diarrhea, and lower protein use | Soak 6 hours, then 1 hour in boiling water |
| Oligosaccharides | Gut bacteria ferment them and cause belly gas | Discard the soaking water after soaking |

> **Do not eat cowpea leaves raw either. They contain oxalic acid and a small amount of antinutrients, so always blanch them and discard the blanching water.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Cowpea has a low toxicity risk among legumes: it lacks the strong lectin of kidney bean and the component of fava bean that causes acute hemolysis, but it has antinutrients, so cook it thoroughly.

| Treatment | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Basic seed preparation | Soaking 6 hours in room-temperature water softens the seed coat and shortens cooking. Boil 1 hour in boiling water |
| Hulled and split | Soak, pound lightly in a mortar to remove the husks, split in half, and dry; it cooks in 20-30 minutes. Process only as much as will be eaten in a month or two |
| Saving fuel | Pressure cooking is 15 minutes under pressure for soaked beans. Retained-heat cooking is boiling 15 minutes, turning off the fire, and leaving the pot wrapped in a straw or rice-hull box or bedding for 2-3 hours. Roasted and ground flour is mixed into water to drink. Sprouting forms vitamin C |
| Leaves, green pods | Rinse leaves in running water, blanch 1-2 minutes in boiling water, rinse in cold water and squeeze well, then dress with salt, oil, garlic, or doenjang. Young pods can be eaten whole after blanching a few minutes |
| Preserved products | Blanched and dried leaves (until winter, as dried greens), roasted and ground cowpea flour (3-6 months sealed), hulled and split cowpea (1-2 months sealed), blanched and dried green pods (a few months), cowpea doenjang made by boiling, mashing, and fermenting (an alternative when soybeans are short) |

## 11. Annual Work Schedule
| Month | Work |
|:---|:---|
| Jan-Feb | Check storage, cold treatment, and seed germination test. In February, plan the field and assign a poor plot to cowpea |
| Mar-Apr | In March, turn the compost and prepare bone meal. In April, manage the preceding crop and plan 20 cm beds and drainage ditches |
| May-June | In May, prepare the field, make beds, and dig drainage ditches. In June, sowing (late month in the central region, from mid month in the south), inoculation, and straw cover |
| July-Aug | In July, 1st and 2nd weeding, thinning, and the late-sowing limit. In August, flowering, watching for the legume pod borer (checking inside flowers every morning), picking leaves, and checking nodules |
| Sep-Oct | In September, start divided harvesting and dry in nets. In October, finish harvest, thresh, winnow, and return stalks and leaves (finish before frost) |
| Nov-Dec | In November, dry, store, and dry leaf greens. In December, check storage, process, and plan next year |

> **Cowpea gives leaves, green pods, and seed in turn even in fields where other crops cannot grow, if you provide drainage, look after phosphorus, weed in the first 5 weeks, check inside the flowers at flowering, and dry completely and seal.**
