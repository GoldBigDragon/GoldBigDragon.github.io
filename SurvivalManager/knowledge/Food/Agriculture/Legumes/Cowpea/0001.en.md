## Introduction

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The cowpea (Vigna unguiculata) came from West Africa. It was domesticated in the dry savanna south of the Sahara and remains a primary protein source for people in Nigeria, Niger, and Burkina Faso today. It is grown in more than 60 countries worldwide, and its English name means a bean fed to cattle. In South Korea it has long been grown in a niche similar to the adzuki bean, and it is known as the white bean with a black eye (the black-eyed pea) or the red cowpea.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The cowpea's identity can be summed up in one line: of all legumes, it is the most tolerant of poor soil and drought. It sets seed even at 400mm of annual rainfall, roots even in strongly acidic soil at pH 4.5, and grows even in sandy ground where other beans starve. A total evapotranspiration of 250mm earns it a water-use grade of S, and its pest-control self-reliance is also S, the highest of any legume. It is, in effect, a crop that yields something even if you plant it and leave it alone.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;One more thing sets it apart from other legumes: its leaves are edible. Young cowpea leaves and shoots are an important vegetable across much of Africa, and in South Korea, too, cowpea leaves were blanched and eaten as namul (a seasoned vegetable side dish). Getting a vegetable from the leaves while waiting for the beans to ripen is worth a great deal in a disaster situation. Where most legumes offer only their seed, the cowpea offers three things in succession: leaves, green pods, and dry seed.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Yet the cowpea's overall grade is C, a score of 43.0. Calories per area are D: 686kcal per 1m², only 0.26× the self-sufficiency line. Cold hardiness is E - one frost kills it - and for all its drought tolerance, it is the least tolerant of waterlogging of any legume. And because its prevalence in South Korea is B, seed is not easy to obtain outside the main growing areas.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;So the cowpea's place is clear. It is not the main crop for good farmland, but the crop that takes on the spot where nothing else will grow: the sandy corner of a field, the dry soil of a slope, a plot worn out from years of continuous cropping, a year when drought has forced other crops to be given up. Under those conditions, the cowpea still delivers something.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Finally, there is one misconception common to legumes as a whole: that planting beans improves the soil. That is only half true. The cowpea, too, draws in nitrogen through symbiosis with root-nodule bacteria (rhizobia), but once the seed is harvested and carried off the field, more nitrogen can leave the field than was fixed. Section 5 works through that trade-off in numbers.
