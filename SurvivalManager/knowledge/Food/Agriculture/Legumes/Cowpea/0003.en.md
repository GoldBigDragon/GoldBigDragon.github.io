## 3. Growing Conditions

### 3-1. Temperature and Light

| Condition | Limit | Optimum | Outside This Range |
|---|---|---|---|
| Germination temperature | 12-15°C or above | 25-30°C | Does not germinate below 10°C |
| Growth temperature | 15-40°C | 20-35°C | The most heat-tolerant of any legume |
| Fruiting temperature | 20-32°C | 24-30°C | A range of 12°C, wide for a legume |
| Cold hardiness | None | - | Killed by a single frost |
| Sunlight | 6+ hours a day | 8+ hours a day | Yield drops by half at 40% shade |
| Rainfall | Grows even at 400mm/yr | 400-700mm | Waterlogging is more dangerous than drought |

> **The cowpea is one of the few crops that heat does not kill. Through a summer of unbroken 35°C that makes other beans' flowers drop in showers, the cowpea keeps opening new flowers every morning. The more heat waves there are, the higher the cowpea's relative standing rises.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This heat tolerance makes it possible to sow the cowpea in the height of summer. The right time is late June to early July in central South Korea and mid-June to mid-July in the south, and a trial in South Jeolla Province even recorded its highest yield from sowing on July 25. Frost sets the sowing limit: counting back 90 days from the expected date of first frost gives that area's cutoff date.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Being able to sow late carries great practical value. The cowpea can go into a June field left empty after barley, garlic, or potatoes are harvested, or a July field plowed after a crop was wrecked by the monsoon. Along with mung bean, the cowpea is a crop for recovering from failure.

### 3-2. Soil and Water

| Condition | Limit | Optimum | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Soil texture | From sandy ground to clay soil | Well-drained sandy soil to sandy loam | Adapts to the widest range of any legume |
| pH | 4.5-8.0 | 5.5-7.0 | Tolerates strongly acidic soil. The widest range of any legume |
| Effective soil depth | 30cm or more | 50cm or more | The taproot goes down deep |
| Season evapotranspiration | - | About 250mm | Among the lowest of any legume |
| Flooding | Dies if submerged a day or more | - | Among the most vulnerable to waterlogging of any legume |
| Salt tolerance | Low | - | Not suited to saline ground |
| Bed height | 20cm or more | 25cm | The key to preventing waterlogging damage |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Tolerating soil down to pH 4.5 is a clear advantage for the cowpea. Farmland in South Korea tends to be acidic because of heavy rainfall, and where lime is hard to obtain, this makes a real difference. Pea collapses below pH 6.0 and soybean loses its nodules at 5.5, while the cowpea grows even in soil more acidic than that. It is one of the few beans that can be planted in a field turned acidic by years of continuous cropping, or one where lime could not be applied.

> **The cowpea, the most drought-tolerant of all, is also the most vulnerable to flooding. This is no coincidence. A root structure built for the dry savanna cannot withstand soil that has pooled with water and lost its oxygen. On a cowpea field, the raised bed and drainage channel are not optional — they are a precondition.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In South Korea, the cowpea's growing period overlaps exactly with the monsoon (jangma, late June to July in South Korea). This alone is the biggest difficulty in growing cowpeas, and the response is physical. Raise the bed at least 20cm, dig the drainage channels deep, and check before the monsoon that water drains all the way out. From the moment you choose the field, assign the cowpea to a spot where water does not collect — the upper side, if there is a slope.

## 4. Varieties

### 4-1. Classification by Growth Habit

| Type | Height | Stakes | Characteristics |
|---|---|---|---|
| Upright type | 50-80cm | Not needed | Resists lodging; easy to harvest |
| Semi-upright type | 80-120cm | Usually not needed | Higher yielding and less labor |
| Vining type | 2-3m | Needed | Long harvest period, but needs stakes |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Among cowpea varieties bred in South Korea, 'Jeonnam 1' and 'Jeonnam 2' are known as semi-upright, and 'Okdang' and 'Seonhyeon' as upright. The upright type resists lodging and is easy to manage, while the semi-upright type yields a bit more. In trials, 'Jeonnam 2' has recorded yields of 221 to 224kg per 1,000m², and 'Seonhyeon' around 226kg.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In a disaster situation, the upright or semi-upright type is the sensible choice. It needs no stakes, resists lodging so harvest labor is lower, and is also easy to pick leaves from. Consider the vining type only when you have both the material and the labor to put up stakes.

### 4-2. Selection Criteria by Situation

| Situation | Priority | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| No staking material available | Upright or semi-upright | Stands without stakes |
| Field is poor and dry | Any cowpea line | No other bean can replace the cowpea under these conditions |
| Want to use the leaves as a vegetable | Leafy semi-upright type | Keeps producing leaves even after picking |
| Region with early frost | Early-maturing upright type | Finishes in 80 days |
| Need to improve the field | Any line, used as green manure | Plowing it under at flowering leaves nitrogen behind |
| Seed is scarce | Upright type | Fixed planting density makes calculation easy |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The first criterion for choosing a variety is whether seed can be saved. Most cultivated lines of cowpea are fixed lines that self-pollinate, so seed you have can be replanted as is. Since prevalence in South Korea is B, obtaining seed again is not easy, so the rule is to keep carrying forward whatever line you have secured. If a local landrace exists, it is likely already adapted to the conditions of that land.

## 5. Field Preparation and Fertilizer - Rhizobia

### 5-1. How Nitrogen Fixation Happens

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;78% of the air is nitrogen. Even though an inexhaustible supply floats right over everyone's heads, plants cannot use it. Atmospheric nitrogen is a molecule in which two nitrogen atoms are locked together by a triple bond, and breaking that bond takes enormous energy. The industrial process that breaks this bond to make ammonia is the Haber-Bosch process, and the world's nitrogen fertilizer comes from it. It requires high temperature, high pressure, a catalyst, and huge amounts of fossil fuel.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Root-nodule bacteria (rhizobia) do this same job at ordinary temperature and pressure. An enzyme called nitrogenase breaks the triple bond and turns nitrogen into ammonia. There is a price, though: this reaction needs a great deal of energy, and the bacteria cannot photosynthesize on their own. So they live inside the roots of legumes, take the sugar the plant makes through photosynthesis, and hand over the nitrogen they fix in return. It is a trade.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The bacteria that pair with the cowpea belong to the Bradyrhizobium group. They are compatible with the strains used by adzuki bean and mung bean, and this group tolerates acidic soil relatively well. One reason the cowpea tolerates soil down to pH 4.5 is the acid tolerance of its symbiotic bacteria. In practice, soil from a field that grew adzuki bean, mung bean, or cowpea serves as an inoculant.

| Stage | What Happens |
|---|---|
| 1. Signal exchange | The cowpea's roots release flavonoids, and bacteria in the soil respond to the signal by releasing Nod factors |
| 2. Infection | Root hairs curl inward to enclose the bacteria, and the bacteria travel into the root along an infection thread |
| 3. Nodule formation | Cells inside the root divide, the nodule swells, and the bacteria settle inside |
| 4. Nitrogen fixation | Inside the nodule, nitrogenase converts atmospheric nitrogen into ammonia |
| 5. Distribution | Ammonia is converted into amino acids and moved to the stems, leaves, and pods |
| 6. Senescence | Entering the ripening stage, the nodule loses function and returns to the soil along with the root |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Nitrogenase is extremely sensitive to oxygen and loses its function the instant oxygen touches it. To manage this, the inside of the root nodule is filled with a red pigment protein called leghemoglobin, which holds oxygen in check and keeps the oxygen concentration around the enzyme extremely low. It works on the same principle that makes human blood red, and this pigment is what makes the inside of a split nodule look red or pink. This fact matters a great deal in practice, because that red color is visible proof that the nodule is fixing nitrogen right now.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The cowpea's nitrogen-fixing ability is among the higher end for legumes. It is a trait that developed to survive in poor soil, and under good conditions it fixes around 100kg per hectare. This is where the fertilizer-demand grade of SS and its rating as doubling for green manure come from.

### 5-2. How to Check Whether Nodules Have Formed

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Whether or not you inoculated, you must check whether nodules actually formed. Without checking, you cannot know the cause of a nitrogen shortage, and you end up repeating the same mistake the following year.

| Step | Details |
|---|---|
| 1 | The best time is around when flowering begins, about 6 weeks after sowing |
| 2 | Choose two or three plants that look healthy |
| 3 | Do not pull them up — dig a wide area around the plant with a spade and lift it out with the soil intact |
| 4 | Put it in a bucket of water and gently wash the soil off |
| 5 | Count the nodules on the taproot and the thick lateral roots |
| 6 | Split a few nodules open with a fingernail and check the color inside |

| Observation | Meaning | Action |
|---|---|---|
| 20 or more nodules per plant | Inoculation succeeded | Leave as is |
| Red or pink inside when split | Fixing nitrogen | A good sign |
| White or pale green inside when split | Nodule formed but not fixing | Check for waterlogging and phosphorus deficiency |
| Brown or black inside when split | Already senescent or dead | If this is after the monsoon, waterlogging likely killed it |
| 5 or fewer nodules | Inoculation failed | If leaves are yellow, add a small amount of nitrogen |

> **This one habit — digging up roots, splitting a nodule, and checking for red — is the surest signal of whether a bean crop will succeed in a world without nitrogen fertilizer.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;For the cowpea, it is best to check twice, before and after the monsoon. If nodules that were red before the monsoon are black and dead afterward, waterlogging is the cause. Root-nodule bacteria are aerobic, so once submerged they die before the roots themselves do. In that case, clear the drainage channels, and if the leaves are yellow, give a very small amount of diluted urine to help the plant get through the rest of the season.

### 5-3. Where to Get an Inoculant

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Commercial inoculants are products that sell live bacteria carried in peat or clay. They cannot be obtained in a disaster situation. However, the bacteria strain the cowpea uses is a common one shared with adzuki bean and mung bean, so getting an inoculant is relatively easy.

| Method | Procedure | Effect | Caution |
|---|---|---|---|
| Coat seed with soil from a field that grew adzuki bean, mung bean, or cowpea | Scoop topsoil from 5-15cm deep in a field where these were planted last year and nodulation was good. Lightly moisten the seed with water, coat it with this soil, and sow right away | The most reliable method and costs nothing | Soil from a diseased field also carries the pathogens |
| Use the nodules themselves | Gather nodules from dug-up roots, crush them, mix into water, then soak the seed in that water and drain | The highest bacterial density | Must be used the same day the nodules are dug up |
| Scatter soil on the field | Scatter the soil you brought along the row to be sown and till it in together | Spreads the bacteria through the soil | About one shovelful per 10m² is the standard |
| Leave root residue | At harvest, cut only the stem and leave the roots in the field | The bacteria remain in the soil until the following year | The lowest-effort way to maintain it |
| Wild saepat roots | Collect nodules from wild saepat roots in fields and on field banks | Being the same genus, the bacteria are compatible | Fixation efficiency may be lower than a strain suited to the cultivated species |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There is something you must observe when inoculating. Root-nodule bacteria are very vulnerable to direct sunlight, drying, and heat. A few minutes of UV exposure kills a large share of them, and drying kills them even faster. Do the work of coating seed with soil in the shade, and sow the coated seed right away rather than spreading it out in the sun. Sowing within 30 minutes is best, and finishing within a few hours at the latest. This matters especially for the cowpea, since it is a crop sown in the height of summer. Soil baked under the blazing sun can exceed 60°C at the surface, which kills the bacteria coated on the seed instantly. Sow it early in the morning or toward evening.

| Condition That Causes Inoculation to Fail | Reason |
|---|---|
| Field where water collects | Being aerobic, the bacteria die without oxygen. The most common cause of failure with cowpeas |
| Field given plenty of nitrogen fertilizer | The plant feels no need for the trade and scales back nodule formation on its own |
| Field low in phosphorus | Nitrogen fixation needs a great deal of energy, and phosphorus is used in that metabolism. Especially important on poor soil |
| Molybdenum, iron, or cobalt deficiency | These are components of nitrogenase |
| Sowing under the blazing summer sun | The hot soil surface kills the bacteria coated on the seed |
| Drying inoculated seed in the sun | UV light and drying kill the bacteria |
| Using soil from a soybean or pea field | A different bacterial strain, so efficiency is lower |
