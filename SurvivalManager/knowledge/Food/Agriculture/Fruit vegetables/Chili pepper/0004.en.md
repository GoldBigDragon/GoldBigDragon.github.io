## 4. Varieties

### 4-1. Comparison of Major Cultivars

| Cultivar | Form | Maturity | Yield | Anthracnose Response | Characteristics |
|---|---|:---:|:---:|:---:|---|
| Maeuntan | Large-fruited | Early | High | Strong | Relatively resistant to anthracnose and viruses, favorable for chemical-free growing |
| Speed Kaltan | Large-fruited | Early | High | Moderate | Short internodes, good fruit set, combined disease resistance |
| Bugang | Standard type | Mid-season | Moderate-high | Moderate | Even quality and dries well |
| Muhanjilju | Standard type | Mid-season | Moderate-high | Moderate | High capsaicinoid content gives strong pungency |
| Urigeon | Standard type | Mid-season | Moderate | Moderate | Stable quality |
| Traditional Chilseongcho and Subicho lines | Medium-fruited | Mid-season | Low | Weak | Open-pollinated, so seed can be saved and carried forward; strongly aromatic |
| Cheongyang type | Small-fruited | Mid-season | Moderate | Moderate | Very strongly pungent, so a small amount serves for seasoning and insect repelling |
| Bell pepper / paprika pepper | Large-fruited sweet pepper | Mid-season | Moderate | Weak | Not pungent, so children and the elderly can eat it |

### 4-2. Selection Criteria by Situation

| Situation | Choice | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| No chemical treatment available at all | Anthracnose-resistant lines such as Maeuntan | When there is no means of pest control, the cultivar is the only defense |
| Planning to save seed and carry it forward | Traditional Chilseongcho and Subicho lines | Being open-pollinated, they produce the same chili pepper again the next year |
| Wanting to increase harvest before the monsoon | Early varieties (Maeuntan, Speed Kaltan) | A substantial amount is harvested before anthracnose peaks |
| Producing seasoning on minimal area | Cheongyang type | Strong pungency means a small amount gives the same effect |
| When children and the elderly eat together with others | Bell pepper / paprika lines | Contains almost no capsaicin |
| Chili powder for storage | Large-fruited lines with thick skin | Yields more powder when dried |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Choosing a cultivar matters more for chili peppers than for eggplant. There is one reason: without chemical treatment, a variety's own resistance is nearly the only means of standing up to anthracnose. The Pest-control self-reliance grade of D means there is little room for human intervention, and most of that little room depends on which seed was planted.

> **There is, however, a contradiction peculiar to disaster preparedness here. Varieties resistant to anthracnose are usually F1 hybrids, and F1 hybrid seed cannot be saved and reused. Traditional open-pollinated varieties, whose seed can be saved and carried forward, are weak against anthracnose. There is no option that satisfies both at once.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The realistic solution is to plant both types together. While seed of a commercial variety remains available, use it to secure yield, and at the same time plant a few traditional open-pollinated plants, saving seed every year to keep the line going. If the seed supply is cut off, switch over to the traditional variety, accepting that yield will drop and disease losses will grow at that point. One more thing worth adding: if a traditional variety is grown in the same field year after year, the individuals adapted to that soil's pathogens survive and leave seed, so with each generation the line grows a little stronger in that particular field. It is a slow but reliable direction.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The degree of pungency also factors into the choice. As a seasoning, a chili pepper that delivers a big effect from a small amount makes more efficient use of area. Pungent varieties like the Cheongyang type cover a much wider range of seasoning per unit weight. Conversely, when several people are eating together, there will always be someone who cannot handle spicy food, so sweet-pepper lines are needed as well. Planting both in the same field, however, causes the seed to mix, a problem addressed in Section 13.

## 5. Field Preparation and Fertilizer

### 5-1. Field Preparation

| Item | Standard | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Plowing depth | 25-30cm | Deepens the topsoil to create a layer for shallow roots to spread into |
| Bed height | 20-30cm | A structural measure against late blight |
| Drainage channels | Lower the field edges and the ends of the ridges to create a path for water | Standing water is the route for late blight zoospores |
| Lime application | 2-3 weeks before transplanting | Applying it on the same day as compost causes nitrogen loss |
| Compost application | 1-2 weeks before transplanting | Use only fully matured compost |
| Rotation check | Whether nightshade crops were grown within the last 3-5 years | The first thing to check |

### 5-2. Fertilizer Amounts

| Fertilizer | Per 1,000m² | Per m² | Distribution |
|---|:---:|:---:|---|
| Nitrogen (N) | 19.0kg | About 19g | 50% base fertilizer + 50% top-dressing |
| Phosphorus (P) | 11.2kg | About 11g | All as base fertilizer |
| Potassium (K) | 14.9kg | About 15g | 60% base fertilizer + 40% top-dressing |
| Compost | 1,500-2,000kg | 1.5-2kg | Fully matured compost |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium together come to 45g per m², or 42g by the evaluation table's figure. This requirement is the basis for the Fertilizer demand grade of D. It also matters that half the nitrogen and 40% of the potassium go in as top-dressing. Because chili pepper is a crop that forms fruit over four months, from late June to October, if the nutrient supply is delivered all at once, the fruit starts getting smaller and paler from August onward.

### 5-3. Timing of Base Fertilizer and Top-Dressing

| Category | Timing | Details |
|---|---|---|
| Lime | 2-3 weeks before transplanting | pH correction |
| Base fertilizer | 1-2 weeks before transplanting | All the compost + all the phosphorus + 50% of the nitrogen + 60% of the potassium |
| Top-dressing 1 | About 30 days after transplanting | Around when the first fruit sets |
| Top-dressing 2 | 20-25 days after the 1st | When harvest begins |
| Top-dressing 3 | 20-25 days after the 2nd | Mainly potassium |
| Top-dressing 4 | 20-25 days after the 3rd | Final feeding for autumn coloring |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Top-dressing should not be applied right next to the plant. Because the roots spread out shallowly, contact with concentrated fertilizer damages them. The rule is to place it 15-20cm away from the plant, or in the furrow between ridges, and cover it with soil. Liquid feed is likewise poured into the furrow.

### 5-4. How to Match the Same Amount Without Chemical Fertilizer

| Substitute | Nutrients Supplied | How to Use | Caution |
|---|---|---|---|
| Well-rotted compost | Nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, and micronutrients generally | 2-3kg per m² as base fertilizer | Under-rotted compost burns the roots |
| Diluted urine | Mainly nitrogen, with small amounts of potassium and phosphorus | Dilute 10-15× with water and pour into the furrow | Burns leaves on contact. Stop 2 weeks before harvest |
| Wood ash | Potassium, calcium, phosphorus | Mix a handful (50-100g) per m² into the soil | Strongly alkaline; overuse raises pH too much |
| Bone meal | Phosphorus, calcium | Mix into compost as base fertilizer | Breaks down slowly |
| Sesame/perilla oil cake | Nitrogen, phosphorus | Soak in water and ferment for 2-3 weeks, then dilute 10× | Using it unfermented damages roots with fermentation heat |
| Grass mulch fertilizer | Nitrogen, organic matter | Cover the ridge with cut, dried grass | Grass with mature seed spreads weeds |
| Burned eggshell/seashell powder | Calcium | Mix into the soil as base fertilizer | A substitute for lime |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Calcium deserves separate mention for chili peppers. A shortage of calcium causes blossom end rot, in which the tip of the fruit blackens and rots, and this is not a disease caused by a pathogen but a physiological disorder. The cause is usually not an absence of calcium in the soil but uneven watering that prevents calcium from reaching the tip of the fruit. Calcium is an element that moves along with water, so alternating between dry and wet soil interrupts its movement. This is why the fundamental countermeasure for blossom end rot is not calcium fertilization but even irrigation and mulching.

> **One common misconception with chili peppers is that giving more nitrogen means more fruit. It is the opposite. Excess nitrogen only makes the stems and leaves grow lush while flowers drop, and a plant that has grown too lush darkens inside, giving anthracnose and soft rot a place to settle in. Without chemical treatment, overusing nitrogen is a shortcut to disease.**

## 6. Sowing and Transplanting

### 6-1. Timing

| Task | Region | Timing | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sowing (start of seedling raising) | Central South Korea | Mid- to late February | 70-80 days before transplanting |
| | Southern South Korea | Early to mid-February | |
| Transplanting | Central South Korea | Early to mid-May | After the last frost has fully passed |
| | Southern South Korea | Late April to early May | |
| Green chili harvest | Across South Korea | From mid-June | 40-50 days after transplanting |
| Red chili harvest | Across South Korea | Late July to October | Every 7-10 days |

### 6-2. Raising Seedlings - Without Heating

| Item | Standard |
|---|---|
| Pre-sowing treatment | Soak in 30°C water for 10 hours, then wrap in a damp cloth and germinate at 25-30°C |
| Sowing depth | 0.5-1cm |
| Germination temperature | 25-30°C, 4-7 days |
| Seedling temperature | Day 22-30°C, night 15°C or above |
| Pricking out | Once, at 3-4 true leaves (35-40 days after sowing) |
| Seedling period | 70-80 days |
| Seedlings ready for transplanting | 5-7 true leaves, around when the first flower bud appears |
| Hardening off | Set outside during the day starting 1 week before transplanting |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The reason raising chili pepper seedlings is harder than eggplant lies in nighttime temperature. If the night temperature during the seedling stage drops below 15°C frequently, flower buds fail to form properly, and the result shows up later as poor fruit set after transplanting. Daytime temperatures can be raised with sunlight, but keeping the night above 15°C is not easy in February and March.

| Method | Principle | Achievable Temperature | Limitation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hotbed | Mixing horse manure, rice straw, and rice hulls with water and treading it in lets microbes generate heat as they decompose it | Maintains 20-30°C at the surface for 3-6 weeks | Requires substantial material gathering and labor |
| Indoor south-facing windowsill | Daytime sunlight warms the soil and water | Day 25-30°C, night 15-18°C | Securing night temperature is the key challenge |
| Water-barrel heat storage | Placing black water containers next to the seedling tray stores daytime heat | Raises night temperature by 3-5°C | A supplementary measure |
| Double covering | Cover the tray with a straw mat or cloth, then cover it again on top | Raises night temperature by 2-4°C | Failing to remove it during the day causes legginess |
| The cooler end of an ondol (floor heating) room | Uses residual heat from a living space | Night 18-22°C | Light is often insufficient, causing legginess |

> **If raising seedlings is not possible, there is the option of delaying the sowing date. Sowing in April and transplanting in mid-June pushes the green chili harvest back to August and leaves almost no red chili peppers, but it lets you raise seedlings without any heating. It effectively becomes a year in which you give up on chili powder and get only green chili peppers.**

### 6-3. Transplanting

| Item | Standard | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Planting depth | Slightly deeper than the seedling's soil ball | Because shallow roots make it prone to toppling, plant deeper than eggplant |
| Single-row planting | Ridge 80-90cm, 35-40cm between plants | About 3 plants per m² |
| Double-row planting | Ridge 150cm, 35-40cm between plants | The wider row spacing secures airflow |
| Planting method | Pour plenty of water into the hole, and set the seedling in after it drains | Speeds up establishment |
| Stakes | Set 1.5m stakes at the same time as transplanting | Setting them later cuts the roots |
| Time of transplanting | Afternoon on a cloudy day | Transplanting at midday on a clear day causes severe wilting |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The reason chili peppers are planted deeper than eggplant lies in root structure. New roots emerge from the part of the stem buried in soil, so planting a little deeper widens the root zone and makes the plant more resistant to toppling. However, planting too deep keeps the base of the stem wet for too long, letting late blight in. A suitable depth is one where the top of the seedling's soil ball sits about 1-2cm below the surface of the field.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;A plant spacing of 35-40cm is narrower than for eggplant. This is because a single chili pepper plant spreads less than eggplant, but narrowing it further blocks airflow and worsens anthracnose. Under chemical-free conditions, it is actually better to set it wider than the standard, at 40-45cm. The total yield drops slightly from having fewer plants, but the loss from disease would be larger than that.

## 7. Water Management

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Water management for chili peppers means navigating between two risks. Too dry, and flowers drop, fruit stays small, and blossom end rot appears; too wet, and late blight moves in. Because the plant is shallow-rooted, both risks show up quickly.

| Stage | Water Management | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Right after transplanting - establishment | Pour plenty into the planting hole, then keep it from drying out for 3-4 days | Water uptake is weak until the severed roots recover |
| After establishment - before flowering | When the topsoil dries, every 5-7 days | Keeping it slightly dry makes roots spread wider |
| Flowering & fruit set | Every 3-4 days, evenly | Uneven watering causes blossom end rot |
| Fruit enlargement & coloring | Every 3-4 days | The fruit grows larger and pigments form |
| Monsoon season | Stop irrigation; check drainage channels | Late blight moves the moment water pools |
| Late harvest period | Reduce gradually | Watering too late delays coloring |

### 7-1. Signs of Shortage and Excess

| Condition | Symptom | Explanation |
|---|---|---|
| Early water shortage | Flowers and young fruit drop | Appears before the leaves wilt |
| Advancing water shortage | Fruit stops growing while thin and short, with a wrinkled surface | The result of cells failing to swell with water |
| Uneven watering | The fruit tip turns black, sinks in, and rots (blossom end rot) | The result of interrupted calcium movement, not a pathogen |
| Early overwatering | Lower leaves yellow and drop | The roots are unable to breathe |
| Advancing overwatering | The base of the stem turns dark brown, narrows, and the plant wilts suddenly | This is late blight. It does not recover |

> **When a chili pepper plant suddenly wilts and dies in the field, the instinct is to water it more. That is usually the worst choice. In summer, sudden wilting in chili peppers is caused by late blight far more often than by water shortage, and adding more water lets the zoospores swim over to neighboring plants.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The way to tell them apart is to look at the base of the stem. A plant wilted from water shortage recovers by morning and has a healthy stem base. A plant with late blight does not recover even by morning, and its stem base is dark and pinched in. If it is the latter, pull it up and burn it immediately, then mound soil over the spot to cut off the water path.

### 7-2. When Water Is Scarce

| Method | Principle | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Straw/grass mulch | Blocks surface evaporation and keeps soil moisture even | Lengthens the interval between waterings and reduces blossom end rot |
| Evening watering | Less evaporation loss | However, water only the soil so the leaves do not stay wet overnight |
| Furrow irrigation | Water does not touch the leaves or fruit | Prevents anthracnose spores from splashing |
| Per-plant drip | Insert a bottle with a hole in the bottom into the soil to let water seep slowly | Wets only the root zone using little water |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;For chili peppers, mulching is both a way to save water and a way to prevent disease. Anthracnose spores linger in diseased plant debris in the soil, then splash up onto the lower fruit when raindrops hit. When straw covers the soil, this splashing is blocked. This is why straw mulch alone noticeably reduces anthracnose incidence, and this method is especially valuable where plastic sheeting is unavailable.
