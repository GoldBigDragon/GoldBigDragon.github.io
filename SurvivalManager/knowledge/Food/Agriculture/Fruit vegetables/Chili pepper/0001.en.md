## Introduction

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The chili pepper (Capsicum annuum) is an annual fruit vegetable in the nightshade family, genus Capsicum. It originates in Central and South America and arrived on South Korea's soil a little over 400 years ago. In that short span, it became one of the hardest ingredients to remove from the Korean table. The backbone of kimchi, fermented pastes, and soup broths rests on chili powder, and people find a diet without chili peppers hard to bear. This is not a matter of nutrition but of dietary habit, and in a disaster that distinction blurs.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Yet the evaluation table's verdict on the chili pepper is unambiguous. Calories per area is graded E: it does not qualify as a food crop. Even a yield of 300kg from 1,000m² converts to only 255kcal per m², just 0.10× the self-sufficiency line. Since this falls short of the 15% exclusion threshold, the chili pepper is excluded from the food-crop ranking from the outset. The hypothetical score without applying the gate would be 30.5, ranking 183rd out of 183 valid species. To anyone who counts a field in calories, the chili pepper is the first crop to cross off the list.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Even so, this document covers the chili pepper because it is a tool, not a food. Its value lies not in the calories gained by eating it but in its power to make other food edible. The two grades of Storability S and Cooking & processing SS are the basis for that power. Fully dried chili peppers keep for one to two years at room temperature and can be ground and used immediately without heating. When salted vegetables are too salty to eat and grain is too bland to get down, a single spoonful of chili powder turns that food into something edible. Added to this are capsaicin's antimicrobial action and its power to repel insects. The evaluation table's Non-food value grade of D comes from meeting just one of eight criteria—pest control—and that one criterion is capsaicin.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The Micronutrients grade of S is also worth noting. Assuming an intake equivalent to 2,100kcal a day, 17 nutrients exceed 10% of the RDA. Raw chili peppers contain 44-78mg of vitamin C per 100g—several times that of tangerines—and a substantial amount of beta-carotene as well. Still, these numbers should not be taken at face value. No one eats 2.5kg of chili peppers a day. The amount actually used runs from a few grams to a few dozen grams a day, and the nutrition to expect from that amount is not large. The chili pepper's nutritional contribution is minor; its real value lies in seasoning and preservation.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The weaknesses come in two clusters. The first is disease. Pest-control self-reliance is graded D, among the lowest grades on this table, because there is no cure for anthracnose. When anthracnose takes hold in the hot, humid conditions of the monsoon season (jangma, late June to July in South Korea), ripening red chili peppers rot and drop one after another, and in severe cases the entire harvest is lost. Late blight and viral diseases compound the problem. The estimated yield loss without pest control is 50.1%, but in a year when anthracnose breaks out, the losses run far higher. Growing chili peppers without chemical treatment means that in some years you may harvest nothing at all. This is not glossed over here—it is addressed directly in Section 9.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The second is time and labor. Speed to harvest is graded D: 160-180 days on a direct-sowing basis, 70 of which are spent raising seedlings. Infrastructure needs is graded D because stakes and rain shelters must be put up and taken down every year, and Continuous-cropping tolerance is graded D, requiring a rotation break of 5-7 years. The chili pepper is a labor-intensive crop.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;On the other side of the ledger is Seed saving, graded S. Chili peppers are predominantly self-pollinating, so seed can be saved and carried forward, and a single fruit yields tens to hundreds of seeds. Even if the seed supply is cut off, the chili pepper carries on. There is one catch: planting hot and sweet peppers in the same field causes the pungency to mix into next year's seed, a problem addressed in Section 13. In short, the chili pepper does not replace rice, but it is what makes rice go down. A realistic goal is to plant twenty to thirty plants to secure a year's supply of chili powder, and any land beyond that is better given to a crop that yields calories.
