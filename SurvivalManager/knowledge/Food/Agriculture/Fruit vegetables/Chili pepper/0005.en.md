## 8. Crop Management - Side Shoots and Training

### 8-1. Removing Side Shoots Below the First Fork

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Like eggplant, chili pepper produces its first flower at a certain node and forks at that point. This first branching point is the first fork, and removing the side shoots that emerge below it is the first management task in growing chili peppers.

| Item | Details |
|---|---|
| Timing | 30-40 days after transplanting, when the side shoots are about the length of a thumb |
| Target | All side shoots emerging below the first fork |
| Method | Snap them off sideways by hand |
| Reason | Lower side shoots take up nutrients while bearing almost no flowers, and they block airflow near the ground |
| Caution | Removing them too early leaves too little leaf area for photosynthesis, delaying early growth |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There is a reason for the caution against removing them too early. In a young plant, the leaves on the lower side shoots also contribute to photosynthesis. The time to remove them is when the side shoot has grown to about finger length and starts consuming nutrients on its own; before that, it is better to leave it alone.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Above the first fork, chili pepper differs from eggplant. Eggplant is trained down to three stems, but chili pepper is usually left as is, because flowers form at every node, so reducing stems would reduce flowers along with them. However, stems that grow inward and overlap each other, and stems that sag low enough to touch the ground, are thinned out. The purpose is not to control yield but to maintain airflow.

> **Without chemical treatment, pruning chili peppers is not about making the fruit bigger — it is about letting air move through the plant. Wherever moisture lingers is where anthracnose begins.**

### 8-2. Removing the First Fruit

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The technique of picking off the first fruit that sets at the first fork while it is still young is the same as for eggplant. If a still-small plant concentrates its nutrients on that single first fruit, the stem and roots fail to grow, and heading into summer in that state reduces the overall yield. Picking the first fruit off while it is finger-sized lets those nutrients go toward growing the plant's frame instead, and a larger plant produces far more fruit.

### 8-3. Training

| Method | Details | Characteristics |
|---|---|---|
| Single-stake training | A stake is set for each plant and the stem is tied to it | Simple, but branches that spread sideways sag |
| Two-rail training | Stakes are set on both sides of the ridge and string is strung in two or three tiers to enclose the plants | Supports many plants at once |
| X-frame training | 1.8m stakes are crossed and set, with a crossbar laid at the intersection | Good for both airflow and support |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Training is not optional for chili peppers. If a fruit-laden chili plant topples in a typhoon or downpour, the fruit touches the soil, and fruit that touches the soil almost invariably develops soft rot or anthracnose. What is more, righting a plant that has already toppled severs its shallow roots, and it does not recover. Stakes are set at the same time as transplanting, and the stem is tied in two or three stages through July, keeping pace with its growth.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Twine can be substituted with straw rope, kudzu vine, scraps of hemp cloth, or strips of cloth torn from old clothes. When tying, wrap it in a figure eight so it does not constrict the stem, and leave slack to allow for the stem thickening. Stakes can be prepared every year by cutting straight branches from around the field in winter and letting them dry. This annual task is what the evaluation table's Infrastructure needs grade of D refers to.

### 8-4. Rain Shelters Without Plastic Sheeting

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Among anthracnose countermeasures, the rain shelter is the most effective. The principle is simple: if raindrops do not touch the fruit directly, the chance for spores to attach is reduced. The problem is the condition of having no plastic sheeting.

| Alternative Method | Principle | Limitation |
|---|---|---|
| Growing under eaves or against a wall | Plant a few plants where they are less exposed to rain | Small area, and sunlight may be insufficient |
| Thatch or straw-mat roof | Woven straw is laid over the ridge | Blocks a lot of light and becomes heavy when wet |
| Wood planks or tile shards | Placed only over the spots where fruit clusters | Only partial coverage |
| Straw mulch | Blocks raindrops from splashing up from the soil | Does not block rain falling from above |
| Adjusting ridge orientation | Orient the ridges along the direction of prevailing wind so they dry faster | Limited effect |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There is no complete substitute. Stacking straw mulch, wide plant spacing, and resistant varieties together is the realistic combination, and even so, losses are hard to avoid in a long, wet monsoon year. This is why planting a few plants separately in a spot that gets less rain, such as under the eaves or by a wall around the house, serves as real insurance. Even if the whole field is lost, seed can still be saved from those few plants the following year.

## 9. Pests and Diseases - The Anthracnose Wall

### 9-1. Major Diseases

| Disease | Symptoms | Conditions for Onset | Response Without Chemicals |
|---|---|---|---|
| Anthracnose | Round, water-soaked-looking spots appear on ripening fruit, sink in, and form black spore masses | Hot, humid conditions during the monsoon season (jangma, late June to July in South Korea), after rain | No cure. Pick diseased fruit immediately and burn it. Resistant varieties, rain shelter, straw mulch |
| Late blight | The base of the stem turns dark brown and narrows, and the plant wilts and dies suddenly | Overwatering, poor drainage, spots where water pools | High beds and drainage channels are the only structural countermeasure. Remove infected plants immediately |
| Bacterial spot | Small, water-soaked-looking spots appear on the leaves and turn brown | High humidity, after wind and rain | Preventive Bordeaux mixture spray, ensure airflow |
| Viral disease | Leaves become mottled and curl, and fruit becomes deformed | Carried by aphids and thrips | No cure. Control the vector insects and remove infected plants |
| Gray mold | Gray mold grows on flowers and fruit, which then soften and rot | Cool, humid conditions, poor airflow | Shake off spent flowers and ensure airflow |
| Bacterial wilt | Wilts at midday and recovers by morning, repeatedly, until the plant dies | High temperatures, soilborne | No cure. Remove infected plants and use long-term crop rotation |
| Powdery mildew | Leaves become covered in white powder and drop | When dry and humid conditions alternate | Diluted milk spray, diluted baking soda spray |

### 9-2. Understanding Anthracnose Separately

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Anthracnose is caused by a fungus of the genus Colletotrichum. This fungus overwinters in diseased plant debris left in the field and on seed, then produces spores in spring and waits for its chance. Infection occurs while water droplets sit on the fruit's surface; when a temperature of 25-30°C and a wet surface persist for several hours, the spores germinate and penetrate the skin. The monsoon season is exactly that condition.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This disease is especially tricky because of latent infection. The fungus enters while the fruit is still a young green pepper and waits quietly without producing symptoms. Then, as the fruit ripens and reddens, once the tissue softens and sugar increases, the fungus becomes active and produces spots. This is why a field that looked perfectly fine can collapse within days of the red chili peppers starting to ripen. Once infection has already occurred, any countermeasure comes too late.

> **There is no cure for anthracnose. This fact is what produces the evaluation table's Pest-control self-reliance grade of D. All that can be done is to reduce infection itself, and there are five means of doing so: resistant varieties, rain shelters, straw mulch, wide spacing, and immediate removal of diseased fruit.**

| Measure | Effect | Material Burden |
|---|:---:|---|
| Resistant varieties | High | Securing seed is the key challenge |
| Rain shelter | High | Requires covering material |
| Straw mulch | Medium | Covered by rice straw or grass |
| Wide plant spacing | Medium | Requires no materials |
| Immediate removal of diseased fruit | Medium | Labor-intensive |
| Burning field debris | Medium | Reduces next year's source of infection |
| Hot-water seed treatment | Low-medium | Reduces seed-borne infection |
| Early harvest (mainly as green chili) | High | At the cost of giving up chili powder |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The last item is, in practice, the most reliable way to avoid the disease. Since anthracnose strikes ripening fruit, picking it before it ripens leaves the disease nothing to act on. In a year when the monsoon looks likely to be long, the decision to keep picking green chili peppers instead of insisting on red ones reduces overall losses. In exchange, that year's chili powder is given up, so it is also necessary to prepare by using the S grade for Storability to keep dried chili peppers set aside from the previous year.

### 9-3. Major Pests

| Pest | Damage | Response Without Chemicals |
|---|---|---|
| Aphids | Suck sap from new shoots and the underside of leaves, and transmit viruses | Soapy-water spray, crushing by hand, protecting ladybugs and lacewings |
| Thrips | Scar flowers and young fruit, and transmit viruses | Blue sticky traps, straw mulch, removing surrounding weeds |
| Tobacco budworm | The larva enters the fruit and eats the seeds and placenta | Immediately pick off and destroy any fruit with a hole, and hand-pick the larvae |
| Two-spotted spider mite | Sucks sap from the underside of leaves, turning them whitish | Spray the underside of leaves forcefully with water. Worsens in dry conditions |
| Stink bugs | Suck sap from the fruit, hardening that spot | Hand-pick them in the early morning |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The tobacco budworm deserves separate mention. The larva bores a hole near the stem of the fruit and goes inside to eat the placenta and seeds. Once it is inside, even spraying chemicals does not reach it. Without chemical treatment, all that can be done is to find fruit with a hole and pick it off immediately, and since the larva inside would grow up to produce the next generation, the picked fruit must be crushed underfoot or burned.

### 9-4. Pest-Control Preparations Made Without Chemicals

| Preparation | How to Make | Use | Caution |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bordeaux mixture | Dissolve 1kg of copper sulfate and 1kg of quicklime separately, let them cool, combine them, and make up to 100L with water | Prevents bacterial spot and late blight | A preventive agent. Using it concentrated in hot weather burns the leaves |
| Soapy water | Dissolve 20g of unscented soap in 1L of water | Aphids, mites | Effective only when it contacts the underside of the leaves |
| Diluted milk | Mix milk and water at a ratio of 1:1 to 1:2 | Powdery mildew | As it dries, it presses down and pins the fungal threads |
| Wood ash | Lightly dust sifted ash over the leaves and soil | Repels pests, supplies potassium | Washes off in rain |
| Wood vinegar | Dilute 300-500× | Repellent, activates microbes | The undiluted liquid is strongly acidic |
| Egg yolk oil | Emulsify 1 egg yolk with 60mL of cooking oil, then mix into 20L of water | Powdery mildew, mites | Hard to use when oil is scarce |

### 9-5. Cultural Control

| Method | Principle |
|---|---|
| Crop rotation | Rotating nightshade crops on a 5-7 year cycle lowers the density of soilborne disease |
| High beds and drainage channels | Cut off the travel route of late blight zoospores |
| Wide plant spacing | Airflow lets leaves and fruit dry quickly |
| Straw mulch | Blocks raindrops from splashing up from the soil |
| Immediate removal of diseased fruit and plants | Reduces the source of infection in the field |
| Burning debris after harvest | Eliminates next year's primary source of infection |
| Hot-water seed treatment | Kills fungus attached to the seed |
| Orienting ridges with the wind | Leaves and fruit dry quickly |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Post-harvest debris disposal should not be taken lightly. Anthracnose fungus overwinters in diseased fruit and stems left in the field and becomes next year's first source of infection. A field where diseased fruit is left lying around after the chili plants are cleared will see the disease start much earlier the following year. Burning the cleared stems and fruit on the spot, or taking them out of the field to dispose of them, accounts for half of next year's disease control.

## 10. Nutrient Deficiency and Excess

| Symptom | Cause | Remedy with Natural Materials |
|---|---|---|
| Yellowing starts from the lower leaves and spreads overall; the plant stays small | Nitrogen deficiency | Dilute urine 10-15× and pour into the furrow, or add well-rotted compost |
| Leaf edges scorch brown and the fruit is small | Potassium deficiency | Mix a handful of wood ash into the soil per plant |
| Growth is slow, and the underside of leaves and the stem take on a purplish tinge | Phosphorus deficiency | Bone meal, wood ash. In cold periods, cold soil can sometimes prevent uptake even when phosphorus is present |
| The fruit tip turns black, sinks in, and rots | Impaired calcium movement (blossom end rot) | Even irrigation and mulching are the fundamental countermeasures. Burned seashell powder is used as a supplement |
| Flowers open and then simply drop | Boron deficiency, high temperature, excess nitrogen, water shortage | Since there are several possible causes, check them in order |
| Only the tissue between leaf veins yellows, while the veins stay green | Magnesium deficiency | Dolomite powder, diversifying compost materials |
| Leaves grow lush and deep green, but flowers are few | Excess nitrogen | Cut off nitrogen and supplement potassium with wood ash |
| Fruit color is pale and blotchy | Potassium shortage or insufficient sunlight | Add wood ash and thin out overlapping stems |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The most frequently encountered problem in chili peppers is blossom end rot. Although the name includes "rot," it is not a disease caused by a pathogen but a physiological disorder that arises when calcium fails to reach the tip of the fruit. Calcium is an element that moves along with the flow of water, so alternating between dry and wet soil interrupts that flow. In particular, if there is one major water shortage during the period when fruit is growing rapidly, all the fruit enlarging at that time shows the symptom at once.

> **Adding more calcium in response to blossom end rot is usually wasted effort. It is rare for the soil to actually lack calcium — the problem is the flow of water. Keeping soil moisture even with mulching is the real solution.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Flower drop has several possible causes, so they must be checked in order. First, look at temperature. If days above 35 degrees have continued, heat is the cause, and there is not much a person can do. Second, look at the soil. If it is dry, it is water shortage. Third, look at the plant. If the leaves are deep green and lush, it is excess nitrogen. Fourth, look at the sunlight. If cloudy days have continued because of the monsoon, it is insufficient photosynthesis. Only after checking these four does it become the turn to suspect boron deficiency.

## 11. Intercropping and Crop Rotation

### 11-1. Companion Compatibility

| Compatibility | Partner | Reason |
|:---:|---|---|
| Good | Legumes (kidney bean, cowpea) | Fix nitrogen and leave it in the soil |
| Good | Marigold | Suppresses root-knot nematodes |
| Good | Welsh onion, garlic, garlic chive | Sulfur compounds lower the density of soil pathogens |
| Good | Perilla, shiso | Strong aroma confuses pests |
| Bad | Eggplant, tomato, potato | Share late blight and bacterial wilt entirely |
| Bad | Maize | Casts shade and competes heavily for nutrients |
| Bad | Cucurbits (squash, cucumber) | Vines spread over and block airflow |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Planting perilla between chili pepper ridges has long been common. Perilla's strong aroma disrupts pests' search for a host, and perilla itself is used for its leaves and seeds. However, once perilla grows tall it shades the chili peppers, so it is better placed at the ends of the ridges or the edge of the field. Planting green onion and garlic among the chili peppers follows the same logic, and since they stay low, there is no shading problem.

### 11-2. Crop Rotation

| Year | Crop | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| Year 1 | Chili pepper | |
| Year 2 | Legumes (soybean, kidney bean, cowpea) | Restore nitrogen, cut off the food source for nightshade pathogens |
| Year 3 | Allium crops (Welsh onion, garlic, onion) | Suppress soil pathogens with sulfur compounds |
| Year 4 | Grains (maize, sorghum, foxtail millet) | Return organic matter, change rooting depth |
| Year 5 | Leafy vegetables or root vegetables | |
| Year 6 onward | Resume chili pepper | |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Keeping a rotation break of 5 to 7 years is difficult on a small plot of land. This is why moving the planting site becomes a realistic alternative for chili peppers. Since twenty to thirty plants cover one household's yearly consumption, it is possible to plant not in one large field but around the house and along field edges, shifting the spot every year. This keeps pathogens from building up in the same soil, and it also allows choosing a spot that gets less rain, such as under the eaves.

> **If chili peppers are planted in the same spot for three years or more in a row, things look fine at first, and then one year half the plants suddenly die from late blight. Soil pathogens build up quietly and then appear all at once. Moving the planting site is something done before the disease strikes, not after.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;What to plant after chili pepper is also worth considering. Chili pepper draws heavily on nutrients over four months and leaves pathogens behind in its debris. Legumes are the first candidate because they restore nitrogen while not serving as a host for nightshade pathogens. Allium crops are the second candidate because the sulfur compounds released from their roots change the composition of soil microbes and lower pathogen density. Planting garlic where chili peppers were just pulled and letting it overwinter is a way to get a disinfecting effect without leaving the land idle.

## 12. Harvest - Green and Red Peppers

| Purpose | Harvest Timing | Criteria |
|---|---|---|
| Green chili pepper | 20-30 days after flowering | When it has grown to full size and the skin is glossy |
| Red chili pepper (for drying) | 45-50 days after flowering | When it turns deep red and the surface starts to wrinkle |
| Harvest interval | 3-5 days for green chili, 7-10 days for red chili | |
| Harvest time | Early morning, after the dew has dried | Picking while wet leads to mold during storage |
| Picking method | Snap off by hand or cut with scissors, leaving the stem cap on | Avoid tearing the stem |

### 12-1. Which One to Harvest

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Green chili peppers and red chili peppers are not different crops but different points in time of the same fruit, so which one to harvest is a matter of choice. That choice greatly changes the year's balance of gains and losses.

| Item | Green Chili Pepper | Red Chili Pepper |
|---|---|---|
| Harvest timing | Early (from mid-June) | Late (from late July) |
| Anthracnose exposure | Short | Long. This disease strikes ripening fruit |
| Storage | Almost impossible. 1-2 weeks at room temperature | 1-2 years if dried |
| Vitamin C | Intact | Mostly lost during drying |
| Plant lifespan | Continued picking keeps new flowers coming | Ripening it wears the plant out early |
| Use | A side dish eaten right away | A seasoning used all year round |

> **The best-balanced approach is to pick the lower fruit as green chili peppers and let the upper fruit ripen into red chili peppers. The lower fruit, being close to the soil, faces a high risk of anthracnose, while the upper fruit gets good sun and wind and ripens safely.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In a year when the monsoon looks likely to be long, this decision should be made earlier. If ripening fruit gets diseased and drops, nothing at all is left, but if it is picked as green chili pepper, at least that weight is saved. Conversely, in a dry year, the proportion of red chili peppers is increased to secure a year's supply of chili powder. This decision is made in early July, and it is the single most important decision in growing chili peppers.

### 12-2. Harvesting Technique

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There is a reason for picking red chili peppers with the stem cap left on. Mold gets in through the spot where the cap was removed, and during drying, blackening starts from that spot. Drying with the cap still attached keeps that opening sealed, making drying more stable. The cap can be removed after drying, before grinding, and by then it is bone-dry and comes off easily by hand.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Picking only after the dew has dried is also a rule. Piling up wet chili peppers as they are lets mold start within a few hours. After rain, wait at least half a day, and preferably a full day. If fruit wet from rain must be picked, spread it out to dry off the surface moisture before starting the actual drying.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Diseased fruit is picked separately and taken out of the field. Mixing it in with healthy fruit lets spores transfer during transport, and they spread to neighboring chili peppers on the drying rack. Even after drying, a chili pepper with anthracnose spots keeps spreading at that spot, and it ends up having to be thrown out entirely.
