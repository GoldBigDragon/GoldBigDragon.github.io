## 2. What Kind of Plant Is This?

### 2-1. Classification and Origin

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The chili pepper is a plant of the nightshade family (Solanaceae), genus Capsicum. Its scientific name is Capsicum annuum; annuum means "annual," but in the tropics it actually grows as a perennial. It becomes an annual in South Korea's climate only because it cannot survive the winter. There are five cultivated species in the genus Capsicum, and almost everything grown in South Korea is Capsicum annuum. The rest are Capsicum frutescens (tabasco), Capsicum chinense (habanero), Capsicum baccatum, and Capsicum pubescens.

| Species | Examples | Grown in South Korea | Cross-pollination |
|---|---|---|---|
| Capsicum annuum | Traditional chili peppers, bell peppers, paprika peppers, jalapeños | Most | Cross-pollinates fully within the same species |
| Capsicum frutescens | Tabasco | Rare | Partially cross-pollinates with annuum |
| Capsicum chinense | Habanero, Bhut Jolokia | Rare | Partially cross-pollinates with annuum |

> **There is a fact worth remembering here. The hot chili peppers, green chili peppers, bell peppers, and paprika peppers we grow are all the same species. Planted in the same field, they exchange pollen with one another, and seed collected from that cross produces plants with mixed pungent and sweet traits the following year.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Belonging to the nightshade family also has direct practical consequences. Chili pepper shares late blight, bacterial wilt, fusarium wilt, and viral diseases with eggplant, tomato, and potato. If chili peppers are planted where potatoes were just dug up, late blight fungus carried over from the potatoes will attack the chili peppers, and if tomatoes are planted the following year where chili peppers were just pulled, the same thing happens again. This is where the evaluation table's Continuous-cropping tolerance grade of D comes from.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Its native range is the tropical highlands of Central and South America, stretching from Mexico to Bolivia. This origin determines its growing requirements. First, being a warmth-loving plant, its growth stops below 10 degrees and it dies at frost. Second, as a plant from a region with distinct wet and dry seasons, its roots are shallow and spread widely. Third, in the wild, birds ate the fruit and dispersed the seeds. This third fact explains capsaicin's reason for existing. Mammals feel pain from capsaicin and crush the seeds with their teeth, but birds have different capsaicin receptors, so they do not taste the heat and excrete the seeds intact. The chili pepper's pungency is a device for repelling mammals and attracting birds.

### 2-2. Appearance

| Organ | Characteristics |
|---|---|
| Leaves | Broadly ovate to lance-shaped with smooth margins. The leaf surface is glossy |
| Stem | Grows upright, becoming woody at the base. It forks into two or three branches at a certain node, and this first branching point is called the first fork. Height 60-100cm |
| Roots | Has a straight taproot, but it is severed during transplanting, so fine roots concentrate within 20cm of the topsoil. A typical shallow-rooted plant |
| Flowers | Small white flowers bloom facing downward, one or two per node. Self-pollination predominates |
| Fruit | A berry. Green at first, turning red, yellow, or orange as it ripens. Hollow inside, with seeds attached to the placenta |
| Seeds | Flat, disc-shaped, 3-4mm in diameter, pale yellow. Attached to the placenta |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;It is worth knowing the structure of the chili pepper fruit. The inside is hollow, with a white, wick-like tissue in the center to which the seeds are attached. This white tissue is the placenta, and most of the capsaicin is made there. The seeds themselves are not pungent; they become pungent only from contact with the placenta. This means that to reduce the heat, it is the placenta, not the seeds, that must be removed.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The chili pepper's branching structure is similar to eggplant's. The stem grows to a certain node, produces its first flower, forks into two or three branches at that point, and each branch repeats the same pattern. As a result, a single chili pepper plant takes on an umbrella shape that grows denser toward the top. This structure determines the pruning approach in Section 8.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Shallow roots are the biggest physical constraint on growing chili peppers. Because the roots are concentrated within 20cm of the topsoil, that layer runs short of water as soon as it dries out, and the plant suffocates as soon as water pools in that layer. Furthermore, being shallow-rooted, it topples easily in wind. There are two reasons stakes are essential: the plant's weight, and its shallow roots.

### 2-3. Growth Stages

| Stage | Timing | Key Physiology | Key Nutrients |
|---|---|---|---|
| Seedling stage | Sowing to transplanting (70-80 days) | Grows to 5-7 true leaves. The right time to transplant is around when the first flower bud appears | Nitrogen, phosphorus |
| Transplanting & establishment | 1-2 weeks after transplanting | New roots emerge to replace the severed ones. It is normal for there to be no visible growth above ground | Phosphorus |
| Vegetative growth | 2-5 weeks after transplanting | The stem thickens and the first fork forms | Nitrogen |
| Flowering & fruit set | 40-50 days after transplanting | The first flower blooms at the first fork. Flowers continue at every node afterward | Phosphorus, boron, calcium |
| Fruit enlargement & green-mature stage | 20-30 days after flowering | The fruit grows to full size. This stage is the green chili pepper | Potassium |
| Coloring & full ripeness | 45-50 days after flowering | Changes from green to red as capsaicin and pigments accumulate | Potassium |

> **Green chili peppers and red chili peppers are not different fruits — they are the same fruit at different points in time. At 20-30 days after flowering it can be eaten as a green chili pepper, and if left on the plant, it turns red at 45-50 days. Which one to harvest is a judgment call based on that year's disease situation and your needs.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This distinction has real practical meaning in a disaster. Picking green chili peppers means an earlier harvest, gathered before the risk window for anthracnose passes, but they cannot be dried and stored. Green chili peppers hold a lot of water, so drying turns their color black and lowers their quality. Leaving them to become red chili peppers allows a year of dried storage, but they must stay in the field that much longer, extending their exposure to anthracnose. What is more, when a plant is allowed to ripen red chili peppers, it pours nutrients into making seed, and new flowers decrease. Continually picking green chili peppers keeps a plant productive longer, while ripening red chili peppers wears a plant out sooner.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;It is also worth knowing when capsaicin accumulates. Capsaicin keeps being produced as the fruit grows and peaks during the coloring stage. So even within the same variety, red chili peppers are hotter than green ones, and fruit that has fully ripened until its surface wrinkles is the hottest of all. It is also a known fact that chili peppers grown under water-scarce conditions turn out hotter, because stress increases the production of defensive compounds.

### 2-4. Nutrition per 100g

| Nutrient | Raw chili pepper (red) | Dried chili pepper | Notes |
|---|:---:|:---:|---|
| Calories | 85kcal | 280-330kcal | Concentrated as moisture is lost during drying |
| Moisture | About 70g | 10-13g | This figure is the target of drying |
| Protein | 3.12g | 10-13g | 14.7% of calories |
| Carbohydrates | About 18g | 55-60g | Measured sugars 5.48g |
| Dietary fiber | 4-5g | 25-30g | Dried chili peppers are essentially a lump of fiber |
| Vitamin C | 44-78mg | Mostly lost | Nearly disappears with drying and heating |
| Vitamin A (beta-carotene) | High | Very high | Much of the red color comes from carotenoids |
| Vitamin E | Present | Present | Fat-soluble, so relatively resistant to drying |
| Potassium | Present | High | Becomes concentrated |
| Capsaicin | 0-0.5% depending on variety | Concentrated | Concentrated in the placenta |
| Capsanthin | In the red skin | Concentrated | The red pigment in chili powder |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;What stands out in this table is the fate of vitamin C. Raw chili peppers contain 44-78mg of vitamin C per 100g, an extremely high level, and this is the main basis for the Micronutrients grade of S. However, this vitamin is almost entirely lost in the process of drying and grinding. Vitamin C is a substance vulnerable to heat, oxygen, and light, and drying it in the sun for several days is a process that exposes it to all three.

> **To use chili peppers as a source of vitamin C, they must be eaten raw. Dipping green chili peppers in doenjang (Korean soybean paste) in summer is the only real way to get vitamin C from chili peppers — there is nothing to expect from winter chili powder.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Carotenoids such as beta-carotene and capsanthin, by contrast, are fat-soluble and much more resistant to drying. The fact that dried chili peppers keep their red color means these pigments remain intact, and they are absorbed when cooked with oil. The nutritional contribution of winter chili powder lies not in vitamin C but on the vitamin A side.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The fact that dried chili peppers exceed 280kcal per 100g is easy to misread. Looking at that number alone, it seems like a calorie crop, but no one eats dried chili peppers 100g at a time. A realistic amount is 10-30g a day, which comes to 30-90kcal — 2-4% of daily needs. Drying concentrates not only calories but capsaicin as well, so a large amount simply cannot be eaten.

## 3. Growing Conditions

### 3-1. Temperature

| Category | Limit | Optimum | Notes |
|---|:---:|:---:|---|
| Germination temperature | 15-35°C | 25-30°C | Germination is very slow below 20°C |
| Seedling temperature (day) | 20-32°C | 25-30°C | |
| Seedling temperature (night) | 15°C or above | 18-20°C | Sustained temperatures below 15°C impair flower bud differentiation |
| Growing temperature | 15-35°C | 25-32°C | |
| Fruit-set temperature | 20-30°C | 20-25°C | This 10°C range is the basis for the evaluation table's Temperature range grade of C |
| Heat limit | 35°C or above | - | Pollen viability drops and flowers fall |
| Cold limit | 10°C or below | - | Growth stops |
| Lethal | 0°C or below | - | Killed by frost |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;A distinctive feature of the chili pepper is that its optimal growing temperature and optimal fruit-set temperature differ. The best temperature for stem and leaf growth is 25-32°C, while the best temperature for flower fertilization and fruit set is 20-25°C. As a result, during the hottest stretch of midsummer, a state arises where the plant grows lush while new fruit fails to set well. From mid-August onward, as temperatures fall, fruit set increases again, and the fruit that sets at this time becomes the autumn red chili peppers.

### 3-2. Light

| Category | Standard | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Required sunlight | 6 hours or more a day | More sunlight means more flowers set |
| Minimum sunlight | 4 hours a day | The plant grows, but fruit set drops sharply |
| At 40% shade | Relative yield 102% | A value common to fruit vegetables; the basis for the evaluation table's grade of S |
| Intense-light stress | Direct midsummer sun | Sunscald forms on the fruit skin, leaving white patches that then rot |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Sunscald causes real losses in chili peppers. When a fruit not shaded by leaves takes the full force of direct midsummer sun, the tissue in that spot dies, turning from white to brown, and fungus then moves in. For this reason, the rule with chili peppers is not to remove too many leaves. This is the opposite of eggplant, where lower leaves are actively cleared away. A chili pepper's leaves double as a sunshade for its fruit.

### 3-3. Water and Soil

| Item | Standard | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Total evapotranspiration | About 750mm per season | Basis for the evaluation table's Water use grade of C |
| Soil moisture | 60-70% of maximum water-holding capacity | Kept slightly drier than for eggplant |
| Soil texture | Loam or sandy loam with both good drainage and water retention | |
| Soil depth | 30cm or more | Fine roots concentrate in the topsoil |
| pH | 6.0-6.5 | Slightly acidic |
| Bed height | 20-30cm | The key measure against late blight |
| Salt tolerance | Weak | Growth stops if salts accumulate |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;One thing to remember about water management for chili peppers is that overwatering is more dangerous than drought. Whereas eggplant responds to water shortage by dropping fruit, chili pepper's shallow roots mean that late blight moves in as soon as water pools. Late blight is caused by the water mold Phytophthora capsici, which produces swimming zoospores that spread along standing water. This means that the moment water pools in the field, a route opens for this pathogen to travel.

> **Raising the bed in a chili pepper field is not just a drainage trick — it is the only structural defense against late blight. Without chemical treatment, bed height is what decides that year's harvest.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The target pH of 6.0-6.5, slightly lower than for eggplant, reflects the fact that chili pepper prefers slightly acidic soil. However, dropping below 5.5 causes phosphorus fixation and aluminum toxicity, so this lower limit must be respected. Where lime is unavailable, wood ash can substitute, but wood ash is strongly alkaline, and adding a lot at once pushes pH well past 7. It is safer to add it a handful per m² at a time, spread out, and adjust the following year based on how the soil responds.
