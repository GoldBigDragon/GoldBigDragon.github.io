## 1. Disaster-Preparedness Evaluation

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The chili pepper is a crop that received a verdict of Excluded, with a Calories-per-area grade of E. Excluded means its calories fall short of 15% of the self-sufficiency line, so it does not qualify as a food crop, and for that reason it is not given the four scores of Overall, Short-term, Long-term, and Supplementary. The score assuming the exclusion threshold were not applied is 30.5, corresponding to 183rd place out of 183 valid species—the lowest position on this table.

### 1-3. How to Read the Exclusion Verdict

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The Excluded verdict does not mean the chili pepper is useless; it means the arithmetic of counting chili peppers as a food crop does not work out. The numbers make this clear. A field of 1,000m²—that is, 300 pyeong (about 990 m²)—yields 300kg of chili peppers. The same area yields 2,500kg of potatoes or 2,000kg of sweet potatoes. The harvested weight itself is smaller by nearly an order of magnitude, and this is then multiplied by the low calorie content of 85kcal per 100g. The result is 255kcal per m², 10% of the self-sufficiency line of 2,616.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The meaning of this figure becomes clearer when converted into field area. To meet one adult's annual calorie needs with chili peppers alone would take nearly 3,000m² of field. Feeding the same person with potatoes would take just 300m²—a tenfold difference. So the smaller the field, the smaller the area given to chili peppers should be, and in an extreme situation, giving up on chili peppers altogether is the right call.

> **Every pyeong of field planted with chili peppers is a pyeong given up from potatoes. Whether this trade is worth it depends on how urgent the food situation is. It is not worth it when people are starving, but it is when there is food but it is hard to get down.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;So where does the case for planting chili peppers lie? In three places. The first is seasoning. A monotonous diet of grains, tuber crops (tubers/storage roots), and dried vegetables becomes hard for people to get down after a few days. A state actually arises where a person is hungry but cannot bring the spoon to their mouth, and if that continues, the amount eaten drops, leading to malnutrition. Heat and aroma get people over that wall. The second is preservation. Capsaicin has antimicrobial action, so food containing chili powder spoils more slowly than food without it. This is chili's contribution to helping kimchi last through the winter. The third is insect repellence. Capsaicin causes pain in mammals and repels insects. This property is used to protect stored grain.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;A caveat is needed for the Micronutrients grade of S. This grade comes from the assumption of eating 2,471g a day, and no one actually eats that much chili pepper. Real-world intake is only around 10-30g of chili powder a day, and the vitamins gained from that amount are negligible. What is more, vitamin C is mostly lost during drying and heating. Eating raw chili peppers whole makes them a source of vitamin C, but in the dried, powdered form, almost none of that value remains.

### 1-4. How to Read the Strengths

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;SS goes to Cooking & processing alone, and S goes to six: Micronutrients, Sunlight response, Storability, Yield stability, Prevalence in South Korea, and Seed saving.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The most substantial point is the combination of Storability S and Cooking & processing SS. Fruit vegetables that receive a Storability grade of S are rare on this table. Eggplant gets D and tomato gets D, and the reason chili pepper alone gets S is that people dry it before use. Fresh chili peppers spoil within a week, no differently from other fruit vegetables. But fully dried chili peppers drop below 12% moisture, reaching a state where microbes cannot grow, and they hold that state for one to two years. Added to this is the fact that no heating is needed. Dried chili peppers can be used immediately just by pounding them in a mortar. That means zero fuel, and in a situation where fuel is scarce, this carries great value.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Seed saving S is the basis for seed self-sufficiency. Chili pepper flowers often complete self-pollination before the flower even opens, and although the rate of cross-pollination has been reported to range from 2% to 50% depending on conditions, self-pollination generally dominates. A single fruit holds tens to hundreds of seeds, so a few well-ripened red chili peppers are enough to fill next year's field. There is one limit, though: Seed longevity is graded C, only 1 to 2 years at room temperature. This limit is addressed in Section 13.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Yield stability S and Prevalence in South Korea S are also not to be dismissed. Because the chili pepper has been grown in South Korea's climate for a long time, locally adapted lines exist, and year-to-year yield variation is not large. This stability, however, assumes pest control is in place, and in a year when anthracnose breaks out, this figure loses its meaning.

### 1-5. How to Read the Weaknesses

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;E goes to two: Calories per area and Cold hardiness. D goes to Speed to harvest, Fertilizer demand, Infrastructure needs, Pest-control self-reliance, Continuous-cropping tolerance, Non-food value, and Taste. C goes to Water use, Temperature range, Pest & disease pressure, Yield retention without synthetic inputs, and Tillage & digging energy.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Pest-control self-reliance D is the item this document treats most heavily. The basis for this grade can be summarized in one line: there is no cure for anthracnose, so the approach is prevention-focused. Anthracnose is caused by a fungus of the genus Colletotrichum; it produces round, water-soaked-looking spots on ripening fruit, and as the spots spread, the fruit rots and drops. Because its spores are splashed and carried by raindrops, it spreads explosively during the monsoon season (jangma, late June to July in South Korea), and once it starts spreading, the red chili peppers in that field collapse one after another. Even where chemical treatment is available, this disease can only be held back with preventive spraying, and without it, the only remaining measures are rain shelters, resistant varieties, and removing diseased fruit.

> **Growing chili peppers without chemical treatment, some years you will harvest plenty, some years you will lose half, and in a long, wet monsoon year you will salvage almost no red chili peppers at all. Green chili peppers fare better, because they are picked before anthracnose takes full hold.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;So the realistic strategy under chemical-free conditions is not to rely solely on red chili peppers. Securing an early harvest as green chili peppers through early July, and treating red chili peppers from late July onward as a bonus if they come and no great loss if they do not, reduces losses. And it is especially important for this crop not to use up all the dried chili peppers harvested in a given year, but to set some aside against next year's poor harvest. The fact that Storability is graded S is what makes this kind of preparation possible.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Infrastructure needs D is also a real problem. Chili peppers cannot be grown without stakes. Once fruit sets, the stems grow heavy and topple in summer downpours and typhoons. When a plant topples, its fruit touches the soil and is immediately exposed to anthracnose and soft rot. On top of this comes the rain shelter. A rain shelter is a structure that blocks raindrops from reaching the fruit directly, cutting off the spread of anthracnose spores; it is at once the most effective means of reducing anthracnose without chemicals and the most material-intensive one. How to substitute for it when plastic sheeting is unavailable is the task of Section 9.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Continuous-cropping tolerance D is a problem chili pepper shares with eggplant, tomato, and potato. Late blight and wilt disease remain in the soil for 5 to 7 years. Late blight in particular erupts where water pools during the monsoon season, and once one plant is infected, it spreads to neighboring plants along the flow of water. Because chili pepper is shallow-rooted, raising the bed cannot fully avoid this, and in the end, the only option is to lower planting density through crop rotation.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Taste D is an item that is easy to misread. This grade does not mean chili peppers taste bad; it means they are classified as a food that can trigger off-putting reactions in smell and aroma. Pungency is not a taste but a pain sensation: capsaicin stimulates pain receptors on the tongue and in the mouth, sending a signal of heat. For young children and people unaccustomed to spicy food, this stimulus registers directly as an off-putting reaction. When many people share meals from the same pot in a disaster, it should be kept in mind that not everyone can eat spicy food. Planting sweet-pepper lines alongside solves this problem.
