## 13. Seed Saving — The Problem of Mixed Heat

### 13-1. Why Saving Chili Pepper Seed Is Both Easy and Hard

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Chili pepper's seed-saving grade is S. Each pepper flower has both pistil and stamens, and in most cases fertilization is complete by the time the flower opens, with its own pollen reaching its own stigma. A single red pepper holds anywhere from dozens to hundreds of seeds, so a handful of well-ripened fruits are enough to fill next year's field. That is the easy side.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There are two hard sides: cross-pollination, and seed longevity.

| Problem | Details | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Cross-pollination | Self-fertilization dominates, but bees carry pollen between plants at a rate that reaches 2-50% depending on conditions | If hot and sweet peppers grow near each other, next year's seeds produce an intermediate heat level |
| Seed longevity | 1-2 years at room temperature; germination drops off quickly | A strategy of saving several years' worth of seed at once does not work |

### 13-2. The Problem of Mixed Heat

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The hot peppers, green (unripe) peppers, bell peppers, and paprika we grow are all a single species, Capsicum annuum. Because they are the same species, there is no barrier to cross-pollination. If a bell pepper flowers next to a hot pepper and bees travel between them, some of that year's bell pepper seeds end up fertilized by the hot pepper's pollen.

> **The taste of the fruit you harvest that same year does not change. The result of cross-pollination is recorded in that fruit's seeds and shows up only when you plant them the following year. The fact that this year's bell peppers were not hot does not mean their seeds are safe.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The ability to produce capsaicin is inherited dominantly. So if a sweet pepper's pistil receives pollen from a hot pepper, the plants grown from that seed the next year are generally hot. In the reverse case — a hot pepper receiving pollen from a sweet pepper — the heat is preserved. As a result, when cross-pollination happens, only the sweet side is lost. This is exactly why a plan to keep a separate supply of sweet peppers for children and the elderly falls apart.

| Method | Details | Practicality |
|---|---|---|
| Isolation distance | Keep 100-500m away from other lines | Close to impossible on a small plot |
| Staggered planting | Let only one line flower in a given year | Not easy, since seed longevity is only 1-2 years |
| Bagging individual flowers | Cover flowers with a net or cloth bag before they open, and remove it once fruit has set | Most reliable, and suited to small scale |
| Covering with netting | Cover a few seed plants with netting | Reliable. Since the flowers self-fertilize, no hand-pollination is needed inside |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This is why netting is easier with chili peppers than with kale. Kale is self-incompatible, so someone has to move pollen by hand with a brush inside the netting, but chili peppers self-fertilize, so you can simply cover them and leave them alone. Covering two or three seed plants with mosquito netting or loosely woven cloth is enough to keep the line pure. Once fruit has set, the covering can come off, because fertilization is already complete.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The simplest solution is to grow just one line at all. If you plant only hot peppers or only sweet peppers, the cross-pollination problem disappears. If someone in the household cannot handle heat, adjusting by using less hot pepper in cooking can be simpler than managing two separate lines.

### 13-3. Seed-Saving Procedure

| Stage | Timing | Details |
|---|---|---|
| Selecting seed plants | July | Choose plants with no disease, strong vigor, and clear varietal traits |
| Isolation | Before flowering | Cover with netting or keep apart from other lines |
| Choosing seed fruit | Late July-August | Choose and mark fruit set low on the plant while it was still vigorous |
| Full ripening | September-October | Leave until fully red and the surface begins to wrinkle |
| After-ripening | After harvest | Leave in the shade for 1-2 weeks so the seeds mature further |
| Seed extraction | After after-ripening | Split the fruit open and scrape the seeds off the placenta |
| Sorting | Right after extraction | Put in water and discard any that float |
| Drying | After extraction | 1-2 weeks in the shade, spread out in a single layer |
| Storage | Once fully dry | Store in an airtight container with a desiccant |

> **If you touch the placenta with bare hands while extracting seeds, capsaicin soaks into your skin and stings for hours. Rubbing your eyes afterward hurts badly, and soap does not remove it easily. Wrap your hands in cloth or plastic, and after the work wipe them with oil or milk before washing.**

### 13-4. Seed Longevity and Storage

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The seed-longevity grade of C on the evaluation table means 1-2 years at room temperature, with germination declining quickly. This is because chili pepper seed contains a fair amount of lipid, which goes rancid over time. This longevity, however, depends heavily on storage conditions.

| Condition | Germination retained |
|---|:---:|
| Room temperature, not sealed | 1 year |
| Room temperature, sealed + desiccant | 2-3 years |
| Cool (10°C or below) and dry, sealed | 4-5 years |
| Low temperature above freezing, fully dry, fully sealed | Longer |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;So what to do in a disaster situation is clear: dry the seed completely, seal it with a desiccant, and keep it in the coolest spot in the house. Candidates include a root-cellar pit, the base of a north-facing wall, or under the floor. And do not skip saving fresh seed every year. Unlike kale or squash, where one batch of seed can last several years, chili pepper is a crop whose seed has to be renewed year after year.

| Item | Standard |
|---|---|
| Target moisture | 8% or below |
| Desiccant | Roasted and cooled rice, charcoal, dry wood ash |
| Storage container | Glass jar, metal tin |
| Germination check | Each year, place 20 seeds on a wet cloth and count the germination rate after ten days |
| Germination threshold | If it drops below 70%, sow more seed or save fresh seed |

### 13-5. Seed Disinfection

| Method | Procedure | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Hot-water soak | Soak in 50-55°C water for 10-15 minutes, then cool in cold water | Kills anthracnose fungus and bacteria on the seed |
| Sorting | Soak in water and discard what floats | Diseased seed and empty husks float |
| Wood-ash dusting | Coat dry seed with fine ash | Suppresses mold and blocks moisture |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Seed disinfection matters especially for chili pepper. Anthracnose fungus overwinters on the seed surface and under the seed coat, and seedlings grown from that seed become the first source of infection the following year. The hot-water soak is one of the few genuinely effective measures you can do without tools, and it only requires keeping to the right temperature: above 60°C the seed dies, and below 45°C the fungus survives.

## 14. Toxicity and Compounds of Concern

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Chili pepper has no acute toxicity. Capsaicin is not a poison but an irritant, and it causes no problem at the amounts people eat in daily life. Even so, understanding the nature of that irritation is useful in several situations.

| Compound | What it is | What problem it causes | Response |
|---|---|---|---|
| Capsaicin | An alkaloid produced in the placenta; stimulates pain receptors | Excessive intake causes stomach irritation and diarrhea; severe pain on contact with eyes or wounds | Wipe off with milk or oil. Water does not remove it well |
| Solanine-type compounds | Trace alkaloids common to nightshades | At a level with no real effect | No action needed |
| Mold toxins | Produced by mold growing on improperly dried peppers | Can produce substances harmful to the liver | Discard any moldy peppers |

### 14-1. Why Capsaicin Does Not Wash Off With Water

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Capsaicin dissolves in fat but not in water. That is why drinking water after eating something hot does little good, while milk or fatty food works much better — the casein protein in milk wraps around capsaicin and pulls it away from the receptors. The same applies when it gets on your hands: wipe first with cooking oil or milk, then wash with soap.

> **Rubbing your eyes with hands that have handled chili peppers is extremely painful. Washing does not help much, and you will suffer for thirty minutes to an hour. Always wash your hands after grinding chili powder or extracting seeds, and do not touch your face until you have.**

### 14-2. Moldy Dried Chili Peppers

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;What is actually dangerous in dried chili peppers is not capsaicin but mold. If they are sealed while incompletely dried, mold grows inside, and some molds produce toxins harmful to people. These toxins are not destroyed by heating, and once ground into powder they become invisible.

| Condition | Judgment |
|---|---|
| Looks like it has a faint white powder on it | May be mold, so check the smell |
| Damp, musty smell | Discard |
| Black or blue-green spots | Discard |
| Mold on only part of it | Cutting out that part is not safe; spores have already spread |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;That is why the drying covered in Section 15 is not just a storage technique but a matter of safety. If you keep incompletely dried peppers because you cannot bear to waste them, you end up throwing away the whole batch in the end.

## 15. Storage Without Refrigeration — Drying Is Everything

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The storability grade of S for chili pepper refers to dried pepper. Fresh pepper, like any other fruit vegetable, spoils in one to two weeks at room temperature. Fully dried pepper drops below 12% moisture, a state where microbes cannot grow, and keeps for one to two years as is.

### 15-1. Drying Methods

| Method | Conditions | Duration | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sun drying | Clear, windy days; spread on a mat or reed screen, or strung on thread | 7-15 days | The oldest method and needs no fuel |
| Shade drying | Hung in a well-ventilated shady spot | 15-30 days | Keeps a good color, but takes longer and fails during the monsoon (jangma, late June to July in South Korea) |
| Fire drying | Residual heat above a cooking fire, on the warm side of an ondol floor, or beside a hearth | 2-5 days | Works even on humid days and uses no separate fuel |
| Combined | A few days in the sun, finished off beside a fire | 5-10 days | Practical during the monsoon |

| Step | Method | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Sorting | Use only fruit that is disease-free and fully red | Spotted fruit spreads disease while drying |
| 2. Removing moisture | If wet, spread out and dry for a day | Stacking it wet lets mold start within hours |
| 3. Spreading | Spread in a single, non-overlapping layer, or string on thread | Overlapping spots do not dry |
| 4. Turning | Turn once or twice a day | Moisture collects on the underside |
| 5. Night care | Bring in or cover so dew does not fall on it | If night dew wets it again, drying starts over from the beginning |
| 6. Judging doneness | It snaps cleanly when bent by hand, and rattles with the sound of seeds when shaken | If it bends instead, it is still not dry enough |
| 7. Storage | Keep in an airtight container or sack in a dark, cool place | Color fades on exposure to light |

> **There is only one test for whether drying is finished: does it snap when bent? If it bends instead, it is not dry enough, and storing it in that state will grow mold within a month. Even if it feels wasteful, it is better to dry it half a day longer.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Stringing peppers up has several advantages. It uses vertical space, so it takes up less room; air passes through from every side, so drying is even; and if it rains, the whole string is easy to bring inside. Thread a needle through near the stem and string the peppers along a thick thread one after another. Hung under the eaves or on a wall, they can sometimes stay stored that way right through winter.

### 15-2. Storing Dried Chili Peppers

| Item | Standard |
|---|---|
| Target moisture | 12% or below |
| Storage temperature | Lower is better; 1-2 years at room temperature |
| Storage location | Dark, cool, dry place. A root-cellar pit, or the base of a north-facing wall |
| Container | Earthenware jar, double-layered sack, airtight container |
| Desiccant | Charcoal, roasted and cooled rice, dry wood ash |
| Inspection | Open once a month to check for dampness and mold |
| If it turns damp | Spread out in the sun again for half a day, then put it back |
| Pests | Indian meal moth larvae get in; block them with sealing and dryness |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Which is better, keeping whole dried peppers or grinding them into powder, depends on the situation. Whole peppers keep far longer: their smaller surface area slows oxidation, and less aroma escapes. Powder is more convenient to use, but its color fades faster and its aroma weakens. So the rule is to store them whole and grind only as much as you need at a time, using a mortar or millstone as needed.

### 15-3. Other Storage Methods

| Method | Details | Duration |
|---|---|---|
| Salt brining | Soak green peppers in brine (10-15%) | A few months; soak in water before eating |
| Soy-sauce jangajji (peppers pickled in soy sauce) | Soak green peppers in soy sauce that has been boiled and cooled | A few months to a year |
| Lacto-fermented pepper | Soak in brine and let it lacto-ferment | A few months; turns sour and soft |
| Gochujang (Korean chili paste) | Ferment chili powder with grain starch, meju (fermented soybean block) powder, and salt | Several years |
| Chili oil | Heat chili powder in oil, then strain and keep | A few months; only possible if oil is available |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Salting green peppers is especially useful in a year when it is hard to let peppers ripen to red. If anthracnose is going around and they cannot be left to ripen, pick them green and salt them. As a seasoning they are weaker than chili powder, but they are perfectly good as a salty side dish, and above all they serve as insurance in a year when drying fails.

## 16. Cooking and Processing — Seasoning and Preservative in One

| Use | Method | Fuel |
|---|---|---|
| Chili powder | Remove the stem from dried peppers and grind in a mortar or with a millstone | None |
| Whole dried pepper | Add whole to soup, then remove | Shared with other cooking |
| Raw green pepper | Rinse and eat as is, dipped in doenjang (Korean soybean paste) or other pastes | None |
| Chili oil | Steep chili powder in oil over low heat | Small |
| Gochujang | Mix grain, meju powder, and salt and ferment | Only for cooking the grain |
| Kimchi seasoning | Mix chili powder, salt, and vegetables | None |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The cooking-and-processing grade of SS on the evaluation table means no heating is needed. Dried pepper can be ground and used right away, so fuel use is zero. In a situation where soybeans need eight hours of soaking and an hour of boiling, and grain has to be cooked too, an ingredient that can change the flavor of food with no fuel at all is precious.

### 16-1. Preservative Effect

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Capsaicin has antimicrobial action. It disrupts the cell membranes of bacteria and mold, slowing their growth, and is effective against several organisms that cause food poisoning in particular. This is part of why hot-climate cuisines use so many spices, and it is also part of what chili pepper contributes to kimchi surviving the winter.

> **Chili pepper does not reverse food that has already spoiled, however. It only slows spoilage, and covering already-spoiled food with a spicy seasoning can be more dangerous, since it masks the smell that would have warned you. The preservative effect only applies when it is added from the start.**

### 16-2. Use as an Insect Repellent

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The non-food-value grade of D on the evaluation table reflects meeting one of eight criteria — pest control — and the basis for that is capsaicin. Both mammals and insects avoid it.

| Use | Method | Target |
|---|---|---|
| Protecting stored grain | Put a few dried peppers into the grain sack or jar | Weevils, Indian meal moths |
| Seed storage | Put a piece of dried pepper into the seed container | Grain pests |
| Field repellent | Soak dried pepper in water for a day, strain, dilute, and spray | Soft-bodied insects, stink bugs |
| Repelling rodents and animals | Sprinkle chili powder along their runways | Rats, moles |
| Seed coating | Coat seed to be sown with chili powder | Birds and ants |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Putting dried pepper into a jar of stored grain is an old method, and it is especially valuable in a disaster situation. Insects are the biggest cause of loss when storing rice and beans for a long time, and there are few means available without insecticide. The traditional combination has been to seal dried pepper together with charcoal and garlic. Pepper does not kill insects, but it slows their entry and suppresses the multiplication of any that have already gotten in.

### 16-3. How to Control the Level of Heat

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Controlling heat level is a practical issue when cooking for a group. This is where the fact that capsaicin is concentrated in the placenta comes in useful. When using a whole dried pepper, shaking out the white core and seeds in the middle cuts the heat a great deal. Conversely, if you want it hotter, grind the placenta in along with the rest. Whether or not the placenta goes in when making chili powder lets you get two grades of powder from the same peppers. Chili oil steeped in fat is hot even in small amounts, since capsaicin dissolves so readily in oil.

## 17. Annual Work Calendar

| Month | Task |
|---|---|
| January | Check seed and test germination rate. Gather hotbed materials and trim stakes |
| February | Sow in central South Korea mid to late month, in southern South Korea early to mid month. Start hotbed heating |
| March | Manage seedlings. Transplant once at 3-4 true leaves. Apply lime to the field |
| April | Apply compost and base fertilizer and raise the beds. In southern South Korea, transplant in late month. Begin hardening off |
| May | In central South Korea, transplant in early to mid month and set stakes at the same time. Cover with straw mulch |
| June | Remove side shoots below the first branching point and pick off the first fruit. First top-dressing. Green-pepper harvest begins mid month |
| July | Check drainage during the monsoon. Watch for anthracnose and remove diseased fruit immediately. Second top-dressing. Red-pepper harvest begins late month |
| August | Harvest red peppers every 7-10 days and dry them. Third top-dressing. Mark seed fruit |
| September | Red-pepper harvest and drying continue. Fourth top-dressing. Wait for seed fruit to fully ripen |
| October | Finish harvesting and salt any remaining green peppers. Gather seed fruit and let it after-ripen |
| November | Pull up plants and burn diseased residue. Clean and store seed. Put away dried peppers |
| December | Check the condition of dried peppers. Decide next year's planting spot |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The tightest month on this calendar is July. The monsoon, anthracnose, and the first red-pepper harvest all overlap in a single month. Whether you tend the drainage ditches and diligently remove diseased fruit this month, or let it go, decides how much you harvest in August and September. And it is in this month that you decide whether to let peppers ripen red or pick them green instead.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Residue disposal in November is not a small matter either. If diseased stems and fruit are left in the field, they become next year's first source of infection, and without chemical treatments, that difference shows up in a big way. Chili pepper is a crop where next year is decided more by how it is cleared away than by how it is planted.
