## 1. Disaster-Preparedness Evaluation

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Winter squash has an Overall grade of A, with a score of 48.4. Broken down by scenario, its Short-term ability to withstand the early stage of a disaster is B (47.5), its Long-term self-reliance over several years is A (49.4), and its Supplementary value, which treats it as a resource for foods other than the staple, is S (55.0). The fact that Long-term is higher than Short-term sums up the character of this crop in one line. It is not a crop that you harvest as soon as you plant it, but one that uses a full year and carries you through the winter.

### 1-3. The Structure Built on Storability A

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The key to reading this table is Storability A. Among fruit vegetables, this grade is exceptional. Eggplant is D, chili pepper is the same on a raw basis, and tomato and cucumber are also near D. The general rule for fruit vegetables is that watery, thin-skinned fruit does not last long at room temperature, and winter squash breaks that rule.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The reason it breaks the rule lies in the purpose of this fruit. From the plant's point of view, a fully ripe squash is a vessel that holds the seeds and carries them through the winter. So the rind turns woody and hard, a wax layer forms on the surface, the flesh becomes relatively less watery, and starch accumulates. This structure becomes the storage container just as it is. People are, in effect, borrowing the winter storage device that the plant has built.

> **The storability of winter squash comes not from storage technique but from the structure of the fruit itself. Therefore an unripe squash will not last long no matter how well it is kept. Whether storage succeeds or fails is decided not in the storehouse but in the field, by when the harvest time is set.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The value of this trait for disaster preparedness becomes clear when you look at the winter diet. From November to March of the following year, the five months when there is nothing in the fields in South Korea, only three kinds of vegetables can be eaten: dried ones, pickled ones, and stored roots and fruits. The first two involve large processing losses and a monotonous taste. A stored squash gets through the winter without any processing, just as it was when harvested in autumn. Beta-carotene, potassium and dietary fiber remain without loss, and when steamed and mashed it becomes a soft food that children and the elderly can eat too.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Calories per area, graded B, adds to this. 38kcal per 100g is on the high side for a vegetable, and above all, 4kg comes from 1m². The energy return of 16.2× is also worth noting. It means the calories harvested are 16 times the human energy spent plowing the field and making the raised beds, and compared with 5.4× for eggplant and 5.5× for chili pepper, it is three times as much. In a situation where labor is itself calories, this multiple has practical meaning.

### 1-4. How to Read the Strengths

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;SS went to one criterion, Continuous-cropping tolerance, and S went to five: Micronutrients, sunlight response, Taste, Prevalence in South Korea, and Seed longevity. A went to Infrastructure needs, Storability, Yield stability, and Seed saving, and B to Calories per area, Fertilizer demand, Water use, Cooking & processing, and Tillage & digging energy. The character of this crop is that C and D are few and the grades are spread evenly across the upper-middle range.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Continuous-cropping tolerance SS holds an exceptional position among cucurbit crops. Cucumber, watermelon and chamoe (Korean melon) need a rotation break of 3 to 5 years because of Fusarium wilt and nematodes, but winter squash is classified in the no-harm group for continuous cropping. For a situation where you have to hold out for many years on a small piece of land, this trait is the exact opposite of the 5-to-7-year rotation break of eggplant or chili pepper. It does not suffer much when planted in the same spot year after year, and so it can be left permanently in a fixed spot such as beneath a fence or beside a compost heap.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Seed longevity S is special too. 6 to 10 years sealed at room temperature is among the longest in the whole table, and compared with 1 to 2 years for chili pepper, it differs by an order of magnitude. This is because squash seeds are large, with thick coats and a low moisture content. In practice, this means there is no need to save seed every year. A single well-ripened squash yields 200-500 seeds, and that is several years' worth of seed. Because this is a crop whose cross-pollination is tricky to manage, this trait is especially useful. A strategy becomes possible in which you collect plenty of seed in a year when isolation was properly kept and use it for several years.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Infrastructure needs A means that the crop can be grown untrained. There is no need for stakes, training twine or plastic film; you simply let the vines run along the ground. Compared with D for chili pepper and C for eggplant, almost no materials or labor have to be put in each year. Under conditions where supplies are cut off, this difference is larger than you might think.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Taste S and Micronutrients S should be looked at together. A ripe squash tastes sweet and has a soft texture, so few people find it off-putting. The fact that it is a food that people who cannot eat spicy things, elderly people who find chewing difficult, and young children can all eat has value where many people eat from one pot. Beta-carotene becomes almost the only channel for vitamin A in a winter diet, and vitamin A deficiency is a real danger that starts with night blindness and leads to weakened immunity.

### 1-5. How to Read the Weaknesses

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;E went to one criterion, Cold hardiness; D went to Perenniality and Non-food value; and C went to Protein density, Speed to harvest, Temperature range, Pest & disease pressure, Pest-control self-reliance, and Yield retention without synthetic inputs.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Perenniality D is the constraint that defines the character of this crop. It is harvested once a year and that is the end of it. Unlike eggplant and chili pepper, which give a little at a time over four months, squash all comes at once in September and October (in South Korea). This trait makes sense only when paired with Storability A. The structure is one of harvesting everything at once and keeping it for a long time, and if the keeping fails, a whole year's farming vanishes. Put the other way, if the keeping succeeds, food is secured all winter long. This is where the weight of Section 15 comes from.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The 120-150 days of Speed to harvest C is no small matter either. Planted in mid-April, with the first harvest in early September (in South Korea), the crop occupies the field for nearly five months. Nothing else can be planted in that spot during that time, and when the area the vines run over is counted too, a single plant uses 3-5m². If the sowing window is missed, the fruit cannot ripen before frost, and that year there is no storage squash. This is the most irreversible mistake with this crop.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Protein density C is an item to keep in mind when designing the diet. A P:E of 9.3% falls short of the requirement line of 10%. Eating a lot of squash fills the calories but actually dilutes the protein. Getting through the winter on squash alone leaves you with a full stomach while your muscle wastes away. Protein has to be added alongside, from beans, dried fish, or the squash seeds themselves, for the diet to balance. Squash seeds contain more than 25g of protein per 100g, so it is interesting that this deficiency can be partly made up within the same crop.

> **Squash gives calories and vitamin A but not protein. Planting beans next to squash and not throwing away the seeds inside the squash are the two ways to make up for this weakness.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Pest & disease pressure C and Pest-control self-reliance C are at a middling level. There is no deadly disease as there is with chili pepper, but powdery mildew and downy mildew wear down the leaves all summer and aphids spread viruses. The estimated Yield loss without pest control of 50.6% means that you lose half if you do no pest control at all, though in this crop the loss shows up as a reduced number of fruits rather than as rotting fruit. It is some consolation that home-made control agents such as egg-yolk oil and wood vinegar work to a certain extent.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There is one more weakness that does not show up directly in the evaluation table: dependence on pollinating insects. Squash bears female and male flowers separately, and the pollen is heavy and sticky, so the wind cannot carry it. Bees or bumblebees have to come and go for fruit to set. The basis for Non-food value D is that it is a honey plant, that is, a flower that draws bees, and this also means that it is in trouble without bees. In a year when the monsoon season (jangma, late June to July in South Korea) runs long and bees cannot fly, female flowers open but simply wither and drop. Hand pollination, in which a person carries the pollen over by hand, is the answer to this problem, and Section 8 covers it.
