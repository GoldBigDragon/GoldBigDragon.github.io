## 4. Varieties

### 4-1. The Squash Groups and the Place of Winter Squash

| Group | Scientific name | Representative types | Storability | Disaster suitability |
|---|---|---|:---:|---|
| East Asian group | Cucurbitamoschata | maetdol-hobak (winter squash), neulgeun-hobak (old pumpkin), aehobak (young squash), ttangkong-hobak (butternut squash) | Very good | Tolerates heat, stores well, and suits South Korea's climate |
| Western group | Cucurbitamaxima | bamhobak (chestnut squash; also called danhobak, sweet squash), yakhobak (medicinal squash) | Good | High in sugar but weak in heat, and suited to cool highlands |
| Pepo group | Cucurbita pepo | zucchini, guksu-hobak (spaghetti squash) | Poor | Early maturing and quick to eat, but it does not store |

### 4-2. Comparing Winter Squash Types

| Variety | Characteristics | Yield | Storability | Disaster suitability |
|---|---|:---:|:---:|---|
| Traditional winter squash | Deeply grooved and flat, with deep orange flesh | Average | Very good | A fixed variety, so seed can be saved and carried on |
| Cheongdung-hobak | Belongs to the same group as winter squash, with a rounder shape | Average | Very good | Same character |
| Improved winter squash | Higher yield and reinforced disease resistance | High | Good | If it is an F1 hybrid, seed cannot be saved |
| Ttangkong-hobak (butternut) | Bell-shaped with a long neck; the seed cavity is small, so the proportion of flesh is high | Average | Very good | Same species, so it crosses with winter squash |

### 4-3. Selection Criteria by Situation

| Situation | Choice | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| The goal is securing winter food | Traditional winter squash | Best storability, and seed can be saved and carried on |
| Plan to save seed and carry it on | Traditional lines, fixed varieties | Seed saved from F1 hybrids segregates in traits the following year |
| Small plot | Ttangkong-hobak, Pepo group | Short vines or small fruit give better space efficiency |
| Need something to eat right away in summer | Pepo group (zucchini), varieties for aehobak | Early maturing, and fruit sets quickly |
| Cool highlands | Western-group danhobak | Prefers coolness to heat |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;For disaster preparedness, there are two reasons to choose winter squash: storability and the possibility of seed saving. Traditional winter squash is a fixed variety whose seed has been saved and carried on in South Korea for a long time, which means that seed saved this year produces the same squash next year too. Improved varieties lead in yield and uniformity, but many of them are F1 hybrids, so if the seed supply is cut off, they are useful for the first year only.

> **Squash seed keeps for 6-10 years sealed at room temperature. It is the longest-lived seed of all the crops compared. If you save plenty of seed in a year when cross-pollination was properly managed, you can go several years afterward without saving seed, and this is the biggest strategic advantage of this crop.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;One more point: planting several types in the same field invites a cross-pollination problem. Winter squash, aehobak and ttangkong-hobak are all C.moschata, so they mix completely with one another. If you plan to save seed, the simplest approach is to plant only one type of that species. Conversely, danhobak (C.maxima) and zucchini (C. pepo) are different species, so the seeds do not mix even when they are planted together. If you lay out the planting with this in mind, you can grow several kinds and still save seed.

## 5. Field Preparation and Fertilizer

### 5-1. Field Preparation

| Item | Standard | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Tillage depth | 25-30cm | The taproot goes deep |
| Raised bed height | 20-30cm | Prevents excess moisture in the monsoon season (jangma, late June to July in South Korea) |
| Drainage ditch | Field edges and ends of the ridges | Standing water lets late blight in |
| Lime application | 2-3 weeks before transplanting | Applied on the same day as compost, it makes the nitrogen escape |
| Compost application | 2 weeks before planting | Use only fully rotted compost |
| Site selection | Space for the vines to run 3-5m | Field edges and the foot of a fence are suitable |
| Sunlight | A spot that gets 6 or more hours a day | |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There is one method peculiar to preparing a squash field. Instead of spreading fertilizer evenly over the whole surface, you dig a pit at each planting spot and concentrate the compost there only. Dig about 50cm across and 30cm deep, fill it with a half-and-half mixture of compost and soil, cover it with soil into a gentle mound, and then put in the seed. This gets a big effect from a small amount of compost, and there is no need to put fertilizer in the outer ground the vines run over. It is an especially sensible method where fertilizer is scarce.

### 5-2. Fertilizer Rates

| Fertilizer | Per 1,000m² | Per m² | Distribution |
|---|:---:|:---:|---|
| Nitrogen (N) | 10-15kg | 10-15g | Split between base fertilizer and top-dressing |
| Phosphorus (P) | 8-12kg | 8-12g | All as base fertilizer |
| Potassium (K) | 10-15kg | 10-15g | Split between base fertilizer and top-dressing |
| Compost | 1,500-2,000kg | 1.5-2kg | 2 weeks before planting |
| Lime | 100-150kg | 100-150g | Corrects acidic soil |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium together come to 30-40g per m², or 24g on the evaluation table's basis. Fertilizer demand B is a much lighter burden than D for eggplant and D for chili pepper. There is a common belief that squash is a heavy feeder, but per unit area that is not so. The total only looks large because a single plant occupies so much area.

> **With squash, nitrogen calls for particular care. If there is too much nitrogen, only the vines grow vigorously and no female flowers appear. If the leaves are dark green, the vines are thick, the internodes are long and there are no female flowers, it is almost certainly nitrogen excess.**

### 5-3. Timing of Base Fertilizer and Top-Dressing

| Item | Timing | Details |
|---|---|---|
| Lime | 3-4 weeks before planting | Corrects acidity |
| Base fertilizer | 2 weeks before planting | All the compost + all the phosphorus + 50% of the nitrogen and potassium |
| First top-dressing | Vine elongation stage (one month after transplanting) | Mainly nitrogen, in a small amount |
| Second top-dressing | Right after fruit set | Mainly potassium |
| Stop | 20-30 days before harvest | Applying nitrogen late leaves the rind less hard and lowers storability |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The last item is directly tied to storability. If nitrogen keeps going in during the maturation stage, the plant keeps trying to grow and the rind of the fruit does not harden enough. A squash with a soft rind rots in storage. The rule is to cut off nitrogen after mid-August (in South Korea) and supplement only potassium, and once September arrives, nothing is applied.

### 5-4. Supplying the Same Amount Without Chemical Fertilizer

| Alternative resource | Nutrients supplied | How to use | Caution |
|---|---|---|---|
| Well-rotted compost | Nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium and trace elements in general | Mix half and half with soil in the planting pit | Under-rotted compost burns the roots |
| Diluted urine | Nitrogen | Dilute 10-15 times with water and use only during the vine elongation stage | Do not apply after the fruit-set stage |
| Wood ash | Potassium, calcium and phosphorus | A handful around each plant after fruit set | Strongly alkaline, so do not overuse |
| Bone meal | Phosphorus and calcium | Mix into the pit as base fertilizer | Decomposes slowly |
| Fallen leaves and grass | Organic matter and potassium | Lay on the bottom of the pit and cover with soil | If not yet rotted, they give off fermentation heat |
| Kitchen scraps | A mixture of nutrients | Bury in the pit and cover thickly with soil | Animals dig them up |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There is an old method of planting squash beside or on top of the compost heap. It uses the nutrients and heat released as the compost rots just as they are, and at the same time the vines and leaves cover the heap and keep it from drying out. You do not have to haul fertilizer separately, and it takes up no extra space, so it kills two birds with one stone. However, planting directly on under-rotted compost damages the roots through fermentation heat and ammonia, so the edge of a heap that was piled two or three months ago is suitable.

## 6. Sowing and Transplanting

### 6-1. Timing

| Cropping type (South Korea) | Sowing | Transplanting | Harvest |
|---|---|---|---|
| Direct sowing in the open field | Mid- to late April | - | Early September to mid-October |
| Transplanting after raising seedlings | Late March to mid-April | Early to late May | Early September to mid-October |

> **Winter squash takes 120-150 days from sowing to full ripeness. It has to be planted in April to ripen in September (in South Korea). If it is planted after May, the rind does not harden before frost, and what is left is a half-baked squash that cannot be stored. The most irreversible mistake with this crop is missing the sowing window.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Whether to choose direct sowing or raised seedlings depends on the region and conditions. Direct sowing is simple and the roots are not cut, so early growth is good, but it can be done only after the risk of late frost has passed, so the growing season is shortened. Raising seedlings can bring the season forward by 2 to 3 weeks, but it needs heating. Fortunately, the optimum germination temperature of squash is 25-30°C, similar to chili pepper and eggplant, but the seedling period is much shorter at 3-4 weeks. Compared with eggplant's 70-80 days, the burden is very different.

### 6-2. Raising Seedlings

| Item | Standard |
|---|---|
| Seed treatment | Soak overnight in water, wrap in a damp cloth and sprout at 25-30°C |
| Sowing depth | 2-3cm |
| Germination temperature | 25-30°C, 3-5 days |
| Seedling temperature | Day 22-28°C, night 15°C or above |
| Seedling period | 3-4 weeks, until 3-4 true leaves |
| Seedling at the right time to transplant | 3-4 true leaves, with the cotyledons still alive |
| Hardening off | From 1 week before transplanting, put the seedlings outdoors during the day |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Do not grow squash seedlings large. Once they pass 5 true leaves, the roots tangle inside the container and transplant shock is severe. The best seedling has 3-4 true leaves, with cotyledons that have not yet yellowed and withered, and a seedling past that stage is actually worse than a small one.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Squash seeds are large and thick, so shedding the seed coat at germination takes effort. It is common for seedlings to come up with the coat still capping the cotyledons, and if you pull it off by force the cotyledons tear. Sprinkle a little water to soften it and then peel it off carefully, or leave it alone and it will come off by itself within a few days. Soaking the seeds overnight before sowing reduces this problem.

### 6-3. Planting

| Item | Standard | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Ridge width | 3.5-4m | Space for the vines to run |
| Plant spacing | 60-100cm (30-40cm on a small plot) | |
| Planting density | 300-400 plants per 1,000m² | |
| Sowing depth | 2-3cm | |
| Seeds per hole | 2-3 seeds | Insurance for germination. Leave only one plant later |
| Thinning | Leave only the sturdiest one at 2-3 true leaves | |
| How to plant | Pour plenty of water into the pit and plant after the water has drained away | |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;A ridge width of 3.5-4m looks wide, but that is for untrained cultivation. On a small plot, you can train the vines in one direction to reduce the width, or lift them up altogether.

| Method | Description | Features |
|---|---|---|
| Untrained | Leave the vines on the ground as they are | Needs no work, and adventitious roots emerge from the nodes, making the plant sturdy |
| Training in one direction | Turn the vine tips to one side | Reduces the width and is easy to manage |
| Training on a fence or wall | Lift the vines onto a wall or fence | Uses no field area, and the fruit does not touch the soil, so it rots less |
| Training over a roof or trellis | Build a trellis, raise the vines onto it and hang the fruit from it | The fruit is heavy, so it must be supported with cord |
| Climbing a tree | Let the vines climb a dead tree or a hedge | No materials needed |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Training on a wall or fence is especially advantageous on a small plot. The vines get sunlight without using the field's horizontal area, and because the fruit hangs in the air and does not touch the soil, rotting of the underside is reduced. However, winter squash fruit can weigh 5-10kg, so if left alone the stalk snaps and the fruit falls. Make a sling under the fruit from cloth or netting, and tie its cord to the trellis.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;When the fruit lies on the ground, you must lay a support under it. A handful of rice straw, a piece of board, a piece of roof tile or a heap of dry grass is enough. The side in direct contact with the soil does not color because of the moisture and rots easily, and that spot is the first to turn to mush during storage.

## 7. Water Management

| Stage | Watering | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Sowing to germination | Keep from drying out | The seeds are large and thick and need plenty of moisture |
| Seedling stage | Every 3-4 days | The roots are still shallow |
| Vine elongation stage | Every 5-7 days | The roots have deepened and withstand drought |
| Flowering and fruit-set stage | Every 4-5 days, generously | If water runs short, female flowers drop |
| Fruit enlargement stage | Every 4-5 days, generously | Most of the fruit's weight is the water taken in at this time |
| Maturation stage (20-30 days before harvest) | Reduce gradually and stop at the end | Storability rises |
| Monsoon season (jangma) | Stop irrigation, check drainage ditches | Excess moisture invites late blight and Fusarium wilt |

### 7-1. Why Water Is Cut Off During Maturation

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Cutting back water from 3-4 weeks before harvest has a large effect on storability. If water keeps going in, the fruit keeps absorbing it, so the flesh is soft and the rind does not harden enough. If water is reduced, on the other hand, the plant stops growing and shifts toward building up storage substances. Starch accumulates, the rind turns woody and the stalk dries. This is the process by which a squash becomes storable.

> **If rain is frequent in autumn, the storability of squash drops. Even if people give no water, the water that falls from the sky cannot be stopped. In that case, secure as much drainage of the beds as possible, and it is better to delay the harvest a little and harvest after several clear days in a row.**

### 7-2. Signs of Too Little and Too Much Water

| Condition | Symptoms | Explanation |
|---|---|---|
| Early water shortage | The leaves droop limply at midday and recover in the evening | Squash leaves are large and thin and often do this. If they recover in the evening, it is within the normal range |
| Progressing water shortage | The leaves do not recover even in the evening, and female flowers turn yellow, wither and drop | Fruit set stops at this stage |
| Severe water shortage | Young fruit stops growing and becomes wrinkled | |
| Early excess moisture | The lower leaves turn yellow and the area near the roots turns soft | |
| Progressing excess moisture | The base of the vine splits and sap oozes out, and the plant wilts | This is Fusarium wilt. The plant does not recover |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Squash leaves drooping at midday is common and usually normal. The leaves are large and thin and transpire a lot, so for a while they outpace the rate at which the roots supply water. They recover as the sun goes down. Real water shortage is when they do not recover even in the evening, and by then female flowers have already begun to drop.

### 7-3. When Water Is Scarce

| Method | Principle | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Straw or grass mulching | Prevents surface evaporation | Irrigation intervals lengthen and the fruit does not touch the soil |
| Covering vine nodes with soil | Adventitious roots emerge from the nodes and the absorbing area grows | The plant no longer depends on a single root |
| Planting in a pit | Make the planting spot concave so that water collects | A small amount of water is concentrated at the roots |
| Evening watering | Less loss to evaporation | |
| Using leaf shade | When vines and leaves cover the ground, the soil beneath does not dry out | A mulch that squash makes for itself |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The last item is a hidden strength of squash. When the vines run out and cover the ground, the area beneath becomes shaded, so the soil does not dry out and weeds cannot grow. Because of this trait, planting squash at the edge of a field to suppress weeds has long been used. Planting squash together under maize works on the same principle, and Section 11 covers it.
