## 2. What Kind of Plant Is This?

### 2-1. Classification and Origin

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Winter squash is an annual vine of the gourd family (Cucurbitaceae), genus Cucurbita. Its scientific name is Cucurbitamoschata, and the plants that Koreans commonly call hobak (pumpkin or squash) in fact span three different species.

| Species | Group | Representative types | Characteristics |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cucurbitamoschata | East Asian group | maetdol-hobak (winter squash), neulgeun-hobak (old pumpkin), aehobak (young squash), puthobak (green squash), ttangkong-hobak (butternut squash) | Tolerates heat well and stores well. The fruit stalk is pentagonal and flares widely |
| Cucurbitamaxima | Western group | bamhobak (chestnut squash; also called danhobak, sweet squash), yakhobak (medicinal squash) | Prefers a cool climate and has a high sugar content. The fruit stalk is round and corky |
| Cucurbita pepo | Pepo group | zucchini, guksu-hobak (spaghetti squash), ornamental pumpkins | Short vines and early maturing. The fruit stalk has five distinct ridges |

> **These three are different species, so as a rule they do not cross. This fact determines everything about seed saving in Section 13. Plants of the same species mix completely with one another, and plants of different species almost never do.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;That said, it is hard to assert that they never mix at all. There are reports that hybrids occasionally form between C.moschata and C.maxima, and those hybrids usually fail to set mature seed. In practice, you only need to guard against crossing within the same species, and the way to tell the species apart is to look at the shape of the fruit stalk. The stalk of winter squash is a widely flared pentagon and becomes firmly woody. The stalk of danhobak is round and corky, soft like a sponge. The stalk of zucchini is star-shaped with five distinct ridges.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Its place of origin is the region stretching from Central America to northern South America. C.moschata is a lineage that grew in the hot lowlands there, so it tolerates high temperatures and therefore copes well with the heat and humidity of South Korean summers. This contrasts with the Western-group C.maxima, which does well in cool highlands. The reason winter squash is more reliable than danhobak in most regions of South Korea lies in this origin.

### 2-2. Appearance

| Organ | Characteristics |
|---|---|
| Leaf | Broadly heart-shaped, with shallow lobes and teeth along the margin. The surface has coarse hairs, and some lines show white blotches. Diameter 20-30cm |
| Stem (vine) | Creeps along the ground and runs 3-5m. Pentagonal in cross-section and hollow. Adventitious roots emerge from the nodes and take root in the soil |
| Tendril | Emerges from the leaf axil and coils around anything it meets. Used when training the vines |
| Root | A straight taproot goes deep and fine roots spread widely. The roots are on the deep side among fruit vegetables |
| Flower | A large yellow flower with fused petals. Female and male flowers open separately and are borne on the same plant. They open in the early morning and close around midday |
| Fruit | Round, flattened and deeply grooved. Green when young, it turns yellowish brown when ripe, and a white bloom appears on the surface |
| Seed | Flat ellipses 1.5-2cm long, white to pale brown. 200-500 per fruit |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The fact that adventitious roots emerge from the nodes is useful in practice. As the vine creeps over the ground, wherever a node touches the soil, roots emerge at that spot and absorb water and nutrients separately. So if you cover the middle of the vine lightly with soil, the roots multiply and the whole plant becomes sturdier. This is called root induction, and it is especially effective where fertilizer is scarce. Conversely, if you often move and lift the vines, these roots are torn off.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The structure of the flower is the heart of this crop. Squash flowers open as separate males and females. The male flower sits at the end of a long flower stalk and has a stamen in the center heavily coated with pollen. The female flower sits on a short flower stalk and already has a small squash-shaped ovary beneath the flower. This ovary grows into the fruit. This is exactly how to tell female flowers from male ones: if a tiny squash is attached beneath the flower, it is female, and if not, it is male.

| Feature | Male flower | Female flower |
|---|---|---|
| Flower stalk | Long and thin | Short and thick |
| Beneath the flower | Nothing | A small squash-shaped ovary is attached |
| Order of opening | Opens first. Only male flowers appear early on | Opens after the vine has grown to some extent |
| Number | Far more | Fewer |
| Time of opening | Early morning, around sunrise | Same. Closes during the morning |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;It is common to worry on seeing many male flowers and no female flowers early on. This is normal. Squash makes female flowers only after the vine has grown past a certain length, and until then it produces only male flowers. If there is too much nitrogen, this state is prolonged, so that the vines just keep growing and no female flowers appear.

### 2-3. Growth Stages

| Stage | Timing (South Korea) | Key physiology | Important nutrient |
|---|---|---|---|
| Germination and seedling stage | From sowing to 4-5 true leaves (3-4 weeks) | The cotyledons spread wide and the taproot goes down | Phosphorus |
| Vine elongation stage | May-July | The vines grow more than 10cm a day. Only male flowers open | Nitrogen |
| Flowering and fruit-set stage | July-August | Female flowers open and pollination takes place | Phosphorus, potassium, boron |
| Fruit enlargement stage | August-September | The fruit swells rapidly. It reaches nearly full size 20-30 days after flowering | Potassium |
| Maturation stage | September-October | Growth in size stops, the rind hardens, starch accumulates and the color changes | Reduce watering |

> **The month after the fruit reaches full size decides its storability. Size is settled by 30 days after flowering, but it takes another 20-30 days after that for the rind to turn woody, the fruit stalk to dry out and the white bloom to appear on the surface. If you harvest without waiting out this period, Storability A is lost.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;That is why aehobak and the old pumpkin are the same fruit at different points in time. Pick it 7-10 days after flowering and it is aehobak; wait 50-60 days and it is an old pumpkin. Which one to choose depends on the purpose. Aehobak is a summer side dish, and the old pumpkin is winter food. If you keep picking aehobak from a plant, you will not get old pumpkins, and conversely, if you aim for old pumpkins, you will have nothing to pick and eat in summer. In a disaster, the latter usually takes priority.

### 2-4. Nutrition per 100g

| Nutrient | Old pumpkin (raw) | Pumpkin seeds (dried) | Notes |
|---|:---:|:---:|---|
| Energy | 38kcal | 550-570kcal | The seeds are packed with oil |
| Water | about 88g | 5-7g | |
| Protein | 0.88g | 25-30g | The fruit's P:E of 9.3% falls short of the requirement line |
| Carbohydrate | 6-12g | 10-15g | The riper the fruit, the more starch it has, and during storage the starch turns into sugar |
| Fat | 0.1g | 45-50g | Oil can be pressed from the seeds |
| Dietary fiber | 2-3g | 6-7g | |
| Beta-carotene | Very high | None | The orange color of the flesh is this pigment |
| Vitamin C | 10-15mg | Trace | A considerable amount is lost during cooking |
| Vitamin E | Present | High | Abundant in the seed oil |
| Potassium | 200-400mg | High | Involved in sodium balance |
| Magnesium | Moderate | Very high | Especially abundant in the seeds |
| Iron and zinc | Moderate | High | The seeds are a source of minerals |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;What this table should tell you is that the flesh and the seeds are completely different foods. The flesh is a low-calorie vegetable that is 88% water, while the seeds are a high-calorie seed food that is 45% fat and more than 25% protein. In effect, one fruit holds two things of opposite character, and in a disaster it makes sense to use both.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The seeds from one squash come to roughly 50-100g. Dried and roasted, they make a snack as they are, and they can also be pounded in a mortar and pressed for oil. 100g of seeds yields 30-40g of oil. It is a small amount, but in a situation where oil is completely cut off, even this is a resource. Above all, it makes up, within the same crop, for the fruit's weakness of Protein density C. If you mix ground squash seeds into hobakjuk (pumpkin porridge), protein and fat go in together.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Beta-carotene is the nutritional identity of this crop. The deeper the orange of the flesh, the higher the content, and it hardly declines even during storage. It is a precursor that turns into vitamin A in the body, and because it is fat-soluble, absorption improves when it is eaten with oil. Adding perilla seeds or sesame to hobakjuk, or eating the squash stir-fried, raises the absorption rate.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;It is also worth noting that the flavor improves during storage. A squash kept for one to two months is sweeter than a freshly harvested one, because the starch accumulated in the flesh slowly turns into sugar. So it is better to let it sit for a while than to eat it right after harvest, and for a storage crop this is an unusual advantage. Unlike potatoes and sweet potatoes, whose quality declines in storage, there is a stretch during which it actually gets better.

## 3. Growing Conditions

### 3-1. Temperature

| Parameter | Limit | Optimum | Notes |
|---|:---:|:---:|---|
| Germination temperature | 15-35°C | 25-30°C | No germination at 15°C or below |
| Growth temperature | 15-35°C | 18-30°C | Relatively tolerant of heat |
| Fruiting temperature | 20-30°C | 22-28°C | The Temperature range C in the evaluation table is this 10°C span |
| High-temperature limit | 35°C and above | - | Pollen loses vigor and fertilization fails |
| Low-temperature limit | 10°C and below | - | Growth stops |
| Lethal | 0°C and below | - | A single frost kills the leaves, turning them black |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Winter squash belongs to the East Asian group and tolerates heat well. Unlike the Western-group danhobak, which struggles above 30°C, it holds out up to around 35°C. Above 35°C, however, pollen loses its vigor and fertilization fails. This is why, in the hottest stretch from late July to early August (in South Korea), female flowers may open and still set no fruit, and it is during this period that the need for hand pollination becomes especially great.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;It is helpless against frost. The leaves die first, turning black, and the vines dry up. Fruit that is already ripe with a hardened rind, however, does not spoil right away from a single light frost. Even so, a squash that has been hit by frost loses much of its storability, so the rule is to harvest everything before the first frost whenever a first-frost forecast is issued.

### 3-2. Light

| Parameter | Standard | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Required sunshine | 6 hours or more a day | Needed for fruit set and sugar accumulation |
| Minimum sunshine | 4 hours a day | The vines grow, but there are few female flowers and the fruit is small |
| At 40% shade | Relative yield 102% | A value shared by all fruit vegetables and the basis for the S grade in the evaluation table |
| Leaf area | 20-25 leaves per fruit | Photosynthesis fills the fruit |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In squash, the number of leaves directly determines the size of the fruit. To grow one fruit properly, the vine that carries it needs 20 to 25 leaves. So if you remove too many leaves when pinching, the fruit becomes small, and conversely, if the leaves are too dense, they shade one another and the lower leaves cannot do their work. Striking this balance is what the pinching in Section 8 is about.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Tolerance of shade is useful for layout. If you raise the vines onto the top of a fence or onto a roof, they get sunlight without taking up field space. Letting the vines run among maize plants is also an old method, and squash grows even in the shade of maize. If sunshine falls below 4 hours a day, however, female flowers hardly appear.

### 3-3. Water and Soil

| Item | Standard | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Total evapotranspiration | About 550mm per growing season | On the low side among fruit vegetables, and the basis for Water use B in the evaluation table |
| Soil moisture | 60-70% of maximum water-holding capacity | Roots are deep, so it is relatively drought-tolerant |
| Soil texture | Well-drained sandy loam to loam | Not very particular about soil quality |
| Soil depth | 30cm or more | The taproot goes deep |
| pH | 5.5-6.8 | Relatively tolerant of acidity |
| Raised bed height | 20-30cm | Prevents excess moisture in the monsoon season (jangma, late June to July in South Korea) |
| Salt tolerance | Moderate | |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The saying that squash is not particular about soil is in fact true. The roots go deep and spread wide, and adventitious roots at every node increase the absorbing area. It grows even in places such as leftover scraps of poor soil, stony fields and spots beside compost heaps. The one place it cannot grow is where water collects. When the roots cannot breathe, late blight and Fusarium wilt set in.

> **Squash likes a spot rich in fertilizer, yet at the same time it is a crop that puts out only vines and no female flowers when nitrogen is excessive. The saying that it grows well when planted beside a compost heap is true, but if that spot is high only in nitrogen and low in phosphorus and potassium, you get nothing but lush leaves.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;A total evapotranspiration of 550mm is less than the 750mm of eggplant and chili pepper. The leaves are large and you would expect heavy evaporation, but the reason it actually uses less water is that its roots are deep. Even when the topsoil dries, it draws on water from the layers below. So it endures drought relatively well, and where water is scarce it holds a more favorable position than other fruit vegetables.
