## 15. Storage Without Refrigeration - Curing and Getting Through the Winter

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The Storability grade of A for winter squash comes close to being this crop's reason for existing. Apart from this, there is practically no fruit vegetable that lasts up to six months at ambient temperature with no electricity. But those six months do not come by themselves. Three steps must all be passed: the timing of harvest, curing treatment, and storage conditions.

### 15-1. Curing - Healing the Wounds

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Curing is the process of placing the fruit in a warm, humid place right after harvest so that tiny wounds in the rind heal and the rind becomes harder. It works on the same principle as in sweet potatoes, and this one step doubles the storage period.

| Item | Standard |
|---|---|
| Temperature | 25-30°C |
| Humidity | High (around 80%) |
| Duration | 10-14 days |
| Place | A sunny wooden-floored veranda (maru), under the eaves, the cooler end of an ondol (floor-heated) room, a shed without plastic sheeting |
| Layout | Lay the fruit out in a single layer so that the pieces do not touch each other |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The key is to create 25-30°C in autumn. In September in South Korea, leaving the fruit in a spot that gets daytime sun is enough, and at night a cover is put over it to trap the heat. In a house with ondol heating, the cooler end of the room is a good spot. When curing is done, the rind is visibly harder, and small wounds are sealed with cork-like tissue.

> **Squash that has not been cured does not last beyond three or four months, while squash that has been cured lasts six months. This difference of just over ten days decides the food available in the latter half of winter.**

### 15-2. Main Storage

| Item | Standard |
|---|---|
| Temperature | 10-15°C (12-14°C is best) |
| Humidity | 60-70% |
| Place | Cool, dry, and airy. A shed, an attic, under the floor of the maru, a north-facing room |
| Layout | In a single layer on a shelf or on straw, not touching each other |
| Care | Inspect once every 2-3 weeks and pick out any that have gone soft |
| Not allowed | Refrigerator. At 5°C or below, chilling injury occurs and the fruit actually spoils faster |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;When the temperature falls below 10°C, chilling injury sets in. This is a crop of tropical origin and is sensitive to cold, and even without freezing, if it is kept for long between 5-10°C, the cells are damaged and the inside goes soft. Conversely, above 20°C respiration becomes active, the flesh is used up quickly, and the fruit tries to sprout. The reason 12-14°C is best lies between these two limits. In South Korea's climate, a shed or north-facing room in winter falls roughly within this range. In a place that drops below freezing in midwinter, wrap the fruit in straw or straw sacks (gamani) to keep it from freezing.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Keeping the fruit from touching each other is also important. When one spoils, the rot passes to its neighbors through the touching surfaces, and when fruit is piled up, the lower ones are crushed and turn soft. Lay them in a single layer on a shelf, or put down straw and set them on it with gaps between. Check once every 2-3 weeks, pick out the ones that have begun to soften, and eat those first. If a single fruit is left alone, everything around it spoils.

### 15-3. Other Storage Methods

| Method | How | Duration | Characteristics |
|---|---|:---:|---|
| Whole fruit at room temperature | After curing, keep at 10-15°C | 4-6 months | No processing, and little nutrient loss |
| Drying (hobakgoji, dried squash strips) | Peel, slice thin, and dry in the sun | 6 months-1 year | Volume shrinks greatly and the sweetness deepens |
| Sliced, dried, then ground to powder | Dry until brittle and pound in a mortar | 1 year | Convenient to add to porridge or soup |
| Seed storage | Wash, dry, and seal | Several years | Both food and seed |
| Dried leaves and shoots | Blanch and dry | A few months | Used like namul (seasoned vegetable side dish) made from dried greens |
| Squash yeot and jocheong (taffy and thick syrup) | Simmer down thoroughly and reduce | A few months | Takes a lot of fuel |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Hobakgoji is a way of dealing with squash that cannot be stored because it is wounded, or that is unripe and does not keep. Peel the squash and scrape out the insides, slice it to a thickness of about 1cm, and dry it by spreading it in the sun or hanging it on straw rope. When completely dry, the volume shrinks to under 10%, and in winter it is soaked in water and used in namul or tteok (rice cake). A characteristic is that the sweetness deepens during drying.

> **Stored squash gets sweeter as time passes, because the starch in the flesh slowly turns into sugar. Unlike potatoes or sweet potatoes, there is a stretch during storage when quality improves, and one to two months after harvest is its peak.**

## 16. Cooking and Processing - Fruit, Leaves, Shoots, and Seeds

### 16-1. Uses by Plant Part

| Part | Season | Cooking | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ripe fruit (flesh) | Harvested September-October in South Korea, available all winter | Steaming, porridge, soup, beombeok (thick squash mash) | Needs 30 minutes of heating. Not suited to eating raw |
| Young fruit (aehobak, young Korean squash) | 7-10 days after flowering | Stir-frying, jjigae (stew), jeon (pan-fried patties) | A summer side dish |
| Leaves | All summer | Steamed for ssam (leaf wraps), blanched for namul | Tough veins and hairs are removed before use |
| Vine tip shoots | All summer | Blanched for namul | Tender and fragrant |
| Flowers | Flowering season | Jeon, added to soup | Male flowers are used. Picking female flowers means no fruit |
| Seeds | After harvest | Roasted and eaten as they are, ground into porridge, pressed for oil | Rich in protein and fat |
| Rind | After harvest | Edible if boiled thoroughly, otherwise livestock feed | Tough |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The Cooking & processing grade of B in the evaluation table means that 30 minutes of heating is needed and the fruit cannot be eaten raw. Compared with the SS of kale or the SS of chili pepper, there is a fuel burden. There is, however, a way to reduce that burden. Cutting the squash small greatly shortens the cooking time, and placing it over a fire that is already boiling other food uses no separate fuel. The most economical way is to lay sliced squash over the rice when cooking it and steam both together.

### 16-2. Leaves and Shoots

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The fact that squash leaves are eaten has a greater meaning for disaster preparedness than one might think. Squash gives its fruit once, in autumn, but the leaves and shoots keep coming all summer. If the Perenniality grade of D is an evaluation based on the fruit, the story changes once the leaves are counted.

| Item | Details |
|---|---|
| What to pick | Fully grown leaves from the middle of the stem. Leave the area near the growing tip at the end of the vine |
| How much to pick | Up to three or four leaves at a time from one plant. Overdoing it makes the fruit smaller |
| Preparation | Snap the leaf stalk and peel the skin off toward the stem side, and the tough fibers of the veins come off with it |
| Cooking | Steam on top of rice, and no extra fuel is needed |
| Nutrition | Contains beta-carotene, vitamin C, calcium, and iron |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Picking leaves requires balance. As seen earlier, growing one fruit takes 20-25 leaves, so picking many leaves makes the fruit that much smaller. Even so, a plant carries dozens of leaves, so picking a few fully grown leaves from the lower part has no great effect on the fruit. When there is nothing green on the summer table, those few leaves do their job.

### 16-3. Seeds

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Squash seeds are not something to throw away but a separate food. Per 100g they contain 25-30g of protein and 45-50g of fat, and they are rich in magnesium, zinc, and iron. They are the only channel within the same crop that makes up for the weakness of the Protein density grade of C in the fruit itself.

| Use | Method |
|---|---|
| Roasted seeds | Wash and dry, then roast over low heat. Eat in the shell or shelled |
| Powder | Roast and pound in a mortar. Added to porridge or soup, it gives a nutty flavor and adds protein |
| Oil | Roast and pound, wrap in cloth, and press. 30-40g from 100g of seed |
| Oil cake | The residue left after pressing out the oil can also be eaten or used as fertilizer |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The seed from one squash comes to 50-100g dried. Ten squash give 500g to 1kg, which is 150-300g in terms of protein. It is not a large amount, but it is not an amount to simply throw away when protein sources are scarce in winter. And if part of it is kept as seed, food and seed are secured in the same task.

## 17. Annual Work Calendar

| Month (South Korea) | Work |
|---|---|
| January | Check the condition of the stored squash and pick out any that have gone soft. Inspect the seed |
| February | Keep eating the stored squash. Decide on the planting spot and turn the compost |
| March | Start raising seedlings in late March. Apply lime to the field |
| April | Dig planting pits and put in compost. In mid-April, direct-sow or continue raising seedlings |
| May | Transplant from early to late May. Pinch the main vine at 5-6 true leaves |
| June | Train the vines and thin out the lateral vines. First top-dressing. Begin picking leaves to eat |
| July | Female flowers begin to open. Hand pollination in the early morning. Pick off the fruit of the first female flower |
| August | Fruit set management and laying bases under the fruit. Second top-dressing (potassium). Scouting for powdery mildew. Mark the fruit for seed saving |
| September | Reduce watering. From early September, harvest the ripe fruit and begin curing |
| October | Harvest everything before the first frost. Finish curing and move into main storage |
| November | Clear away the vines and leaves, chop them fine, and work them into the field. Take the seed from the seed-saving fruit |
| December | Check the temperature of the storage room and keep it from freezing. Clean, select, and store the seed |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The most important rows in this calendar are April and September. If it is not planted in April, it will not ripen in September, and if it is not harvested and cured on time in September, nothing is left over the winter. The summer months in between demand relatively little work. Apart from the July mornings for hand pollination and August for watching for powdery mildew, squash is close to a crop that is planted and then left to grow.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;It is also worth noting that the January and February rows list inspection, not harvest. Eating squash in winter is not a matter of harvesting from the field but of taking it out of the storehouse. So the winter work consists entirely of checking the storage room, and that simple task of picking out soft fruit in time decides whether anything to eat is left by March.
