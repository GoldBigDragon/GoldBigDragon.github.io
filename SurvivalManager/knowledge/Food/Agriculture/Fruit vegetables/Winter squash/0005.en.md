## 8. Crop Management - Pinching and Hand Pollination

### 8-1. How Pinching Works

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Squash vines have a hierarchy. The first vine that grows straight out of the seed is the main vine, the vines that emerge from the leaf axils of the main vine are lateral vines, and those that emerge from the lateral vines are secondary laterals. This hierarchy determines where the female flowers form, and pinching is the task that adjusts that position.

| Vine | How female flowers form |
|---|---|
| Main vine | Female flowers appear late and sparsely. For a long while it produces only male flowers |
| Lateral vine | Female flowers set well. Most of the fruit is taken from here |
| Secondary lateral | Female flowers appear, but the vine is weak, so the fruit is small |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;So the purpose of pinching is to cut the growing tip of the main vine early so that the lateral vines come out sooner. This is the basis for the reports that yield rises by about 30% compared with leaving the vines unmanaged.

| Method | Procedure |
|---|---|
| 1 main vine + 2 lateral vines | Cut the growing tip of the main vine when it has 5-6 true leaves. Of the lateral vines that emerge, keep only the 2 strongest and remove the rest |
| Main vine pinched at nodes 5-6 + 3-4 lateral vines | Cut the main vine at nodes 5-6 and train 3-4 lateral vines out in different directions |
| Leaving the vines alone | Do nothing. The yield is lower, but it takes no work |

> **Whether to pinch or not is a question of labor and space. If hands are scarce, squash will still set fruit when left alone, but the fruit is smaller and the count becomes uneven. Conversely, if space is tight, pinching becomes essential. Only by controlling the vines can several plants be fitted into a small area.**

### 8-2. Fruit Set Management - How Many to Grow

| Step | Details |
|---|---|
| Locate the female flowers | The first female flower appears at about node 8 of the lateral vine, the second at node 13, and the third around node 18 |
| Choose which to keep | Take fruit from female flowers that open after the plant has built up enough vigor, usually the second or third one |
| Remove the rest | Pick off fruit that set too early or that is poorly shaped while it is still young |
| Final target | 1 per vine, about 2 per plant |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The reason for picking off the fruit that sets on the first female flower is the same as for eggplant and chili pepper. If nutrients are drawn into a single fruit while the vine is still short and has few leaves, the whole plant fails to grow. As seen earlier, growing one fruit properly takes 20-25 leaves, so it makes sense to take fruit from female flowers that open after that many leaves have formed.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;How many fruit to leave on a plant depends on the purpose. To get two or three large squash, the rest must be picked off; to get many small squash, leave them as they are. If storage is the aim, the larger kind has the advantage. A large squash has more volume relative to its surface area, so it loses moisture more slowly, and its rind also hardens thicker. Small squash dry out quickly in storage.

### 8-3. Hand Pollination - When There Are No Bees

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Squash bears female and male flowers separately, and its pollen is heavy and sticky, so the wind cannot carry it. Insects must do the carrying, and if no insects come, the female flower opens, then withers yellow and drops. This is a structural weakness of this crop.

| Condition | Result |
|---|---|
| Rain keeps falling during the monsoon season (jangma, late June to July in South Korea) and bees cannot fly | All female flowers of that period drop |
| Few bees nearby | The fruit set rate falls sharply |
| Heat wave above 35°C | Pollen loses its vigor and fertilization fails |
| Rain in the early morning | Pollen gets wet and does not stick |

| Step | Method |
|---|---|
| 1. Timing | From just after sunrise until before 8 a.m. The flowers are open only at this time, and the pollen is most vigorous then |
| 2. Pick a male flower | Pick a fully open male flower together with its stalk |
| 3. Remove the petals | Fold back or tear off the petals, leaving only the stamens |
| 4. Transfer | Rub the stamens directly on the stigma of the female flower so that the pollen coats it evenly |
| 5. Check | If the ovary swells after three or four days, it has worked. If it withers yellow, it has failed |

> **One male flower can pollinate up to two or three female flowers. A brush or a cotton ball also works, but using the whole male flower is simpler and more reliable. There are reports that the fruiting rate more than doubles, and the difference is even larger in the monsoon season and during heat waves.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;One more point should be added. Hand pollination is a means of ensuring fruit set and, at the same time, a core technique of seed saving, because it means that people can control which pollen does the fertilizing. The isolated seed saving in Section 13 rests on this technique.

### 8-4. Protecting the Fruit

| Item | Method | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Laying a base | Lay rice straw, boards, or pieces of roof tile under the fruit | Keeps the underside from rotting in soil moisture |
| Turning | Gently turn the fruit once or twice during the ripening stage | Sunlight reaches the underside too, so the color develops evenly |
| Covering with leaves | During heat waves, cover the fruit with leaves or straw | Keeps the rind from being scalded by the sun |
| Not lifting the vines | Do not move the vines often | The adventitious roots at the nodes get torn |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Turning has to be done carefully. If the stalk is twisted, the passage for water and nutrients is damaged, and in severe cases that spot rots. Gently, a little at a time, and once or twice in the latter half of the ripening stage is enough.

## 9. Pests and Diseases - Prevention Without Chemicals

### 9-1. Main Diseases

| Disease | Symptoms | Conditions | Response without chemicals |
|---|---|---|---|
| Powdery mildew | A white powder covers the leaf surface and the leaves dry up | August-September in South Korea, when dry weather and high humidity alternate | Egg yolk-oil emulsion, diluted milk, diluted baking soda solution. Remove lower leaves |
| Downy mildew | Angular yellow spots bounded by the leaf veins appear, with mold on the underside | High humidity, low temperature | Ensure ventilation; preventive Bordeaux mixture |
| Fusarium wilt | The base of the stem splits lengthwise, sap oozes out, and the plant wilts | Excess moisture; soilborne | Cannot be treated. Remove infected plants, improve drainage |
| Late blight | Roots and stem soften and rot, and it spreads to the fruit | Excess moisture, spots where water collects | Raised beds and drainage. Remove infected plants at once |
| Anthracnose | Sunken spots appear on leaves and fruit | Hot and humid | Remove diseased parts, ventilation |
| Mosaic disease (virus) | Leaves become mottled and curl, and the fruit is deformed | Spread by aphids | Cannot be treated. Control aphids and remove infected plants |
| Southern blight | White mycelium and granules like millet grains form at the base of the stem | Hot and humid, excess organic matter | Remove infected plants, dry the soil |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The disease most often encountered on squash is powdery mildew. From late August in South Korea, a white powder, as if flour had been sprinkled, appears on the leaf surface, and as it spreads the leaves turn yellow, dry up, and die. The disease itself does not kill the plant, but when the leaves die, photosynthesis stops, and then the fruit does not ripen properly. What matters is whether the leaves are still alive during the final month, which determines storability, so slowing powdery mildew is directly a matter of the quality of the squash put into storage.

> **Powdery mildew is a fungus that grows on the leaf surface, so coating the surface with something works. This is why egg yolk-oil emulsion and diluted milk are effective. Because it is not a disease that burrows into the leaf, there is relatively much room to deal with it without chemicals.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Fusarium wilt is the opposite. A fungus in the soil enters through the roots and blocks the water-conducting vessels, the base of the stem splits lengthwise, and sap oozes out. There is no cure, and nothing can be done except to pull the plant out and get rid of it. However, winter squash is relatively resistant to this disease, which is why it is also used as rootstock for other cucurbit crops. This resistance lies behind the SS grade for Continuous-cropping tolerance.

### 9-2. Main Pests

| Pest | Damage | Response without chemicals |
|---|---|---|
| Aphids | Suck sap from new shoots and the undersides of leaves, and transmit mosaic disease | Soapy water spray, crushing by hand, protecting ladybugs |
| Two-spotted spider mite | Sucks sap from the underside of leaves, turning them white | Spray water hard on the underside of leaves. Worse in dry weather |
| Cucurbit leaf beetle | Chews round holes in young leaves and gnaws the roots too | Cover with netting in the seedling stage. Pick off by hand |
| Stink bugs | Suck sap from the fruit, and the spot turns hard | Pick off by hand in the early morning |
| Cucumber moth | Rolls the leaves and eats away inside them | Remove and destroy the rolled leaves |
| Root-knot nematode | Galls form on the roots and growth worsens | Intercropping with calendula, crop rotation |

### 9-3. Control Preparations Without Chemicals

| Preparation | How to make it | Use | Caution |
|---|---|---|---|
| Egg yolk-oil emulsion | Dissolve 1 egg yolk in 100mL of water, pour in 60mL of cooking oil, stir hard to emulsify, then mix into 20L of water | Powdery mildew, mites | Hard to use when oil is scarce. Sprayed at midday, it scorches the leaves |
| Diluted milk | Mix milk and water at 1:1 to 1:2 | Powdery mildew | As it dries it presses the mycelium flat and glues it down |
| Diluted baking soda solution | Dissolve 1g of baking soda in 1L of water | Powdery mildew | If too strong, it damages the leaves |
| Bordeaux mixture | Dissolve 1kg of copper sulfate and 1kg of quicklime separately, let them cool, combine, and make up to 100L with water | Prevention of downy mildew and bacterial diseases | It is a preventive only |
| Soapy water | Dissolve 20g of unscented soap in 1L of water | Aphids, mites | Must reach the underside of the leaves |
| Wood vinegar | Dilute 300-500 times | Repellent effect, microbial activity | The undiluted liquid is strongly acidic |
| Wood ash | Sift the ash and scatter it on leaves and soil | Repels cucurbit leaf beetles, supplies potassium | Washed off by rain |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Egg yolk-oil emulsion is singled out for squash because its effect on powdery mildew is relatively reliable. The oil forms a thin film on the leaf surface that covers the mycelium, and the lecithin in the yolk spreads that oil evenly in the water. However, oil is a precious resource in a disaster, so the fact that oil can be pressed from squash seeds becomes an unexpected link here. Part of the seed oil can be diverted to pest and disease control.

### 9-4. Cultural Control

| Method | Principle |
|---|---|
| Raised beds and drainage ditches | Remove the conditions for Fusarium wilt and late blight |
| Wide plant spacing | Ventilation dries the leaves quickly, so fungal diseases decrease |
| Straw mulching | Blocks rain splashing up from the soil and keeps the fruit from touching the ground |
| Removing lower leaves | Old, weakened lower leaves become a foothold for disease |
| Removing diseased leaves at once | Taking off a few leaves early can greatly slow powdery mildew |
| Disposing of crop residue after harvest | Clearing away the vines and leaves reduces the source of infection the next year |
| Intercropping with calendula | Suppresses root-knot nematodes |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;With powdery mildew, early action is exceptionally effective. The disease starts on one or two leaves and then spreads, so if the three or four leaves showing the first white spots are removed at once and carried out of the field, the spread is greatly delayed. It is a disease where results differ greatly between someone who walks the field every day and someone who does not.

## 10. Nutrient Deficiency and Excess

| Symptom | Cause | Remedy with natural materials |
|---|---|---|
| The whole leaf turns yellow, starting with the lower leaves, and the vines are thin | Nitrogen deficiency | During vine elongation, diluted urine at 10-15 times. Do not apply after fruit set |
| Leaf edges scorch brown and the fruit is small | Potassium deficiency | A handful of wood ash around each plant |
| Growth is slow, leaves are small, and they take on a purple tinge | Phosphorus deficiency | Bone meal, wood ash. In cold periods, uptake sometimes fails |
| Only the vines are vigorous, internodes are long, and no female flowers appear | Nitrogen excess | Stop nitrogen and supplement potassium with wood ash. Restrain growth by pinching |
| Female flowers open, but the fruit does not enlarge and drops | Pollination failure | Usually a pollination problem rather than a fertilizer problem. Pollinate by hand |
| Only the areas between the veins turn yellow, while the veins stay green | Magnesium deficiency | Dolomite powder, diversifying compost ingredients |
| The blossom end of the fruit softens and rots | Impaired calcium transport or excess moisture | Improve drainage and water evenly |
| Flesh color is pale and sweetness is low | Potassium shortage, insufficient sunlight, early harvest | Add wood ash, thin the leaves, delay harvest |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The most common misdiagnosis in squash is female flower drop. When a female flower opens, the fruit does not enlarge, and the flower withers yellow and drops, it is easy to think the fertilizer is insufficient, but in most cases pollination simply did not happen. The way to tell is to look at the ovary. If fertilization has occurred, the ovary visibly swells within three or four days. If it does not swell and the flower itself turns yellow first, fertilization has failed.

> **Without telling apart the cases that call for more fertilizer from those that call for moving pollen by hand, a grower falls into a vicious circle: more nitrogen is added, only the vines grow lush, and the female flowers become even fewer. If female flowers are appearing but no fruit sets, try hand pollination first.**

## 11. Intercropping and Crop Rotation

### 11-1. Compatibility

| Compatibility | Companion | Reason |
|:---:|---|---|
| Good | Maize (corn) | Maize grows upward and squash covers the ground, so the plants occupy separate layers |
| Good | Legume crops | They fix nitrogen and leave it in the soil |
| Good | Calendula | Suppresses root-knot nematodes |
| Good | Perilla and shiso | Their scent confuses pests |
| Poor | Other cucurbits (cucumber, watermelon, oriental melon (chamoe)) | They share pests and diseases, and there is also a risk of cross-pollination |
| Poor | Potato | They share late blight strains and compete for space |
| Poor | Low-growing leafy vegetables | Squash vines smother them |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Planting maize, beans, and squash together is a method that Native Americans have used for a long time. Maize raises its stalk, beans climb that stalk and fix nitrogen with their roots, and squash covers the ground to suppress weeds and hold soil moisture. The three crops use different heights and different rooting depths, so competition is low. On a small plot, this arrangement sometimes gives a larger total harvest than growing a single crop.

| Layer | Crop | Role |
|---|---|---|
| Top | Maize | Calories; support for the beans |
| Middle | Climbing beans | Protein; nitrogen fixation |
| Bottom | Squash | Calories and vitamins; ground cover; weed suppression |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This arrangement does come with conditions, though. The maize must be planted first and grown to knee height before the beans and squash go in. If all three are planted at once, the squash vines smother the young maize. And if the squash completely covers the ground between the maize plants, it becomes hard to step in at maize harvest. In practice it is better to train the squash vines in one direction, toward the outside of the field.

### 11-2. Crop Rotation

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The Continuous-cropping tolerance of winter squash is SS, and it belongs to the group in which continuous cropping does no harm. This is a very rare grade in this document's evaluation table, and it contrasts with cucumber and watermelon, which belong to the same cucurbit family but require a rotation break of 3 to 5 years. Therefore the same spot can be used for several years on a small plot.

| Year | Crop | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| Year 1 | Winter squash | |
| Year 2 | Winter squash (possible) or a legume | Continuous cropping is possible, but adding a legume restores soil fertility |
| Year 3 | Grain or leafy vegetables | Changes the nutrient makeup of the soil |
| Year 4 | Winter squash again | |

> **That continuous cropping is possible does not mean it is recommended. It only means that diseases build up less; if the same crop is planted year after year, the nutrients that crop uses heavily are drawn down in a lopsided way. It is better to put in a different crop once every 3 years.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The relationship with other cucurbit crops has to be looked at in two ways. In terms of pests and diseases, cucumber, watermelon, and oriental melon share powdery mildew, downy mildew, and Fusarium wilt, so planting them as neighbors is disadvantageous. In terms of cross-pollination the story is different. Cucumber (Cucumis sativus) and watermelon (CitrullusLanatus) belong to different genera and do not cross with squash. The problem arises only within the same genus Cucurbita, and even there, only members of the same species mix completely.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;What to plant after squash is also worth considering. Squash has a large amount of vines and leaves, so it leaves a considerable amount of organic matter after harvest. If healthy vines are chopped fine and worked into the soil on the spot, they become the next year's fertilizer. Diseased leaves are left out. An arrangement in which garlic or onions are planted after squash and overwinter keeps the ground from lying idle while reducing soil pathogens.
