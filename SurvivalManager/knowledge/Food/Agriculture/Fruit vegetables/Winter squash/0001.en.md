## Introduction

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Winter squash (Cucurbitamoschata) is an annual vine of the genus Cucurbita in the gourd family (Cucurbitaceae). It is also called neulgeun-hobak ("old pumpkin") or cheongdung-hobak, and its Korean name, maetdol-hobak ("millstone pumpkin"), comes from its round, flattened shape with deep grooves, like a millstone. It originates from Central and South America. The starting point for understanding this crop is that the young fruit picked and eaten as aehobak (tender young squash) and the fully ripened "old pumpkin" harvested at maturity are actually the same crop at different points in time.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Judged by the yardstick of disaster preparedness, winter squash earns an Overall grade of A, with a score of 48.4. Its Long-term grade of A (49.4) is higher than its Short-term grade of B (47.5), and its Supplementary grade is S (55.0). Fruit vegetables that earn an A in this table are rare, because of Storability. Fruit vegetables almost without exception stay at D for Storability, yet winter squash alone received an A. A squash that has ripened fully and been cured keeps for three to four months at room temperature without a refrigerator, and for up to six months under good conditions. It is a crop that is harvested in autumn, stacked beneath the maru (the raised wooden floor of a Korean house) for the whole winter, and taken out to eat one at a time, and it is effectively the only fruit vegetable with this trait.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Its calories are also respectable for a fruit vegetable. Calories per area is B: a yield of 4,000kg per 1,000m² works out to 1,520kcal per m², which is 0.58 times the self-sufficiency line. Compared with eggplant at 0.25 times and chili pepper at 0.10 times, it sits in a clearly different position. A large share of the fruit is starch, so a ripe fruit tastes sweet and fills the stomach to a fair degree. Micronutrients adds an S on top of that. It is especially rich in beta-carotene, which makes it a source of vitamin A in winter, a gap that grains and tuber crops (tubers/storage roots) alone can never fill.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Another point worth noting is that almost every part of this plant is edible. The ripe fruit becomes a side dish that comes close to a staple, the young fruit is eaten as aehobak, and the tender leaves and the shoots at the tips of the vines are steamed and used as ssam (food wrapped in a leaf). The seeds can be roasted and eaten or pressed for oil, and they are rich in protein and fat. The flowers are eaten too. Few crops give so many kinds of food from a single plant, and in a disaster this variety is valuable in itself.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;On top of that, Continuous-cropping tolerance is SS. Cucurbit crops mostly dislike continuous cropping, but winter squash is exceptionally classified in the no-harm group for continuous cropping. On a small plot where it is hard to move the crop to a new spot every year, this is a big advantage. Seed longevity is also S: the seeds last 6 to 10 years sealed at room temperature, the longest-lived seeds in this whole table. That means seeds saved well once can hold out for nearly ten years, and when the seed supply chain is cut off, the value of this trait is considerable.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The weaknesses are clear as well. First, Perenniality is D. It is harvested once a year, all at once in autumn, and that is the end of it. It is not a crop you pick from every week like eggplant or chili pepper, but one that fills the winter storehouse.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Second, Speed to harvest is C, with 120-150 days from sowing to full ripeness. It is a long season, sown in April and harvested in September (in South Korea), and if the sowing window is missed, the fruit will not ripen that year.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Third, Cold hardiness is E, and frost kills the plant.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Fourth, it takes up a lot of space. The vines run 3-5m, so the area a single plant occupies is large.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Fifth, Protein density is C, with a P:E of 9.3%, which falls short of the requirement line of 10%. Eating squash alone leaves you short of protein.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;And this crop has one more weakness, a peculiar one found nowhere else: it depends on insects for pollination. Squash bears female and male flowers separately, and the pollen is heavy and sticky, so the wind cannot carry it. Fruit sets only when bees come and go. In a year when the monsoon season (jangma, late June to July in South Korea) drags on and bees cannot fly, or in an environment where bees have declined, female flowers open but simply turn yellow, wither and drop off. Hand pollination, in which a person carries the pollen across by hand, is the answer to this problem, and Section 8 explains the principle.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In summary, winter squash is a crop that is planted in spring, forgotten all summer, and harvested all at once in autumn to see you through the winter. It needs little work as long as you give the vines room to run, but the room has to be wide. The arrangement that best suits the character of this crop is to plant a few plants in leftover spaces such as the edge of a field, beneath a fence, on a roof, or beside a compost heap, and let the vines run outward.
