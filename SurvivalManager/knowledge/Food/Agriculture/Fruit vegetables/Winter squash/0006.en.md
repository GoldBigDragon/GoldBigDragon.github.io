## 12. Harvest - How to Recognize Ripe Fruit

| Category | Standard |
|---|---|
| Time to ripe fruit | 50-60 days after flowering |
| Harvest season | Early September to mid-October in South Korea, before the first frost |
| Aehobak (young green squash) | 7-10 days after flowering, when a fingernail can be pressed into the rind |
| Weather for harvest | A clear, dry day. Avoid the time right after rain |
| How to cut | With a knife or pruning shears, leaving 3-5cm of stalk |
| How to handle | Lift and carry the fruit one at a time so that it is not wounded |

### 12-1. Three Signs of Ripeness

| Sign | Details | Importance |
|---|---|---|
| Rind color | Has changed completely from green to yellow-brown | Essential |
| White bloom | A white, powdery coating has formed evenly over the whole rind | Essential |
| Stalk | Dried hard like wood and cracked lengthwise | The most reliable sign |
| Firmness | A fingernail pressed into the rind leaves no mark | Supplementary |
| Sound | Gives a dull thud when tapped | Supplementary |

> **If only one of the three signs could be chosen, look at the stalk. If the stalk is still greenish and soft, the fruit is not yet ripe, however yellow the rind has turned. The fruit is ready for storage when the stalk has dried like wood and become corky.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The white bloom on the rind surface is a wax layer the plant puts out to prevent moisture loss. That it has formed evenly means the fruit has gone into storage mode on its own. This layer protects the rind during storage, so it is not wiped off. This is also the reason the fruit must not be washed before storing. If there is soil on it, it is enough to brush it off lightly with a dry cloth.

### 12-2. Why the Stalk Is Left On and the Fruit Is Cut with a Knife

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Squash must not be picked by twisting it off by hand. Twisting tears the stalk away from the fruit end and leaves an open wound there. That wound becomes the point where rot begins during storage. If a knife or pruning shears is used to cut on the vine side, leaving 3-5cm of stalk, the stalk stays attached to the fruit and acts as a stopper.

| Condition | Storage result |
|---|---|
| Stalk of 3-5cm attached and dried | Three or four months up to six months |
| Stalk attached but short | Two or three months |
| Stalk has come off, leaving a hole | Rots from that spot within a few weeks |
| Scratch wounds on the rind | Mold grows from that spot |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;That is why care is also needed when moving the fruit. Rolling it, throwing it, or stacking it so that pieces knock against each other causes wounds that are hard to see, and about a month later that spot turns soft. Lift and carry the fruit one at a time, and lay straw or grass on the floor to soften impacts. Squash with a wound is not stored but eaten first.

### 12-3. Squash Hit by Frost

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;When the first frost falls, the leaves and vines die and turn black. Fruit whose rind has already hardened does not spoil right away from a single light frost, but its cells are damaged and its storability drops greatly. Squash hit by frost is taken out of storage and eaten first or dried. So when a first-frost forecast comes, the decision is made to harvest everything, including unripe fruit. Unripe squash cannot be stored, so it is cooked and eaten like aehobak, or sliced and dried.

## 13. Seed Saving - The Cross-Pollination Barrier in the Cucurbit Family

### 13-1. What It Crosses With

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The first thing to know about saving winter squash seed is what it mixes with and what it does not. This distinction decides everything.

| Crop | Scientific name | Crossing with winter squash |
|---|---|:---:|
| Aehobak, green (unripe) squash, "old" (fully ripe) squash, butternut squash | Cucurbitamoschata | Crosses completely |
| Kabocha (chestnut squash, "sweet pumpkin"), yakhobak (medicinal squash) | Cucurbitamaxima | Almost never crosses (rarely, sterile hybrids) |
| Zucchini, spaghetti squash, ornamental squash | Cucurbita pepo | Almost never crosses |
| Cucumber | Cucumis sativus | Does not cross, because it is a different genus |
| Oriental melon (chamoe) and melon | Cucumismelo | Does not cross, because it is a different genus |
| Watermelon | CitrullusLanatus | Does not cross, because it is a different genus |
| Bottle gourd (calabash) | Lagenaria siceraria | Does not cross, because it is a different genus |

> **The claim that squash mixes with cucumber or watermelon is not true. When the genus is different, fertilization does not take place even if the pollen lands. What actually causes trouble is crossing within the same Cucurbitamoschata, that is, among winter squash, aehobak, and butternut squash.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This fact makes a big difference in practice. Even if cucumbers, oriental melons, and watermelons are growing in the same field, there is no problem for saving squash seed. Kabocha (C.maxima) and zucchini (C. pepo) are also safe in principle. The only things to guard against are other lines of the same species, and among these, aehobak varieties are the most common source of contamination. Aehobak and winter squash are both C.moschata, so they mix completely.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;However, telling the species apart is trickier than it seems. Squash sold at market is hard to identify down to species by outward appearance alone, and in many cases the cultivar name does not tell either. The most reliable method is to look at the stalk.

| Species | Shape of the stalk |
|---|---|
| C.moschata | A broadly flared pentagon, firmly woody |
| C.maxima | Round and thick, with spongy, cork-like tissue |
| C. pepo | Star-shaped with five distinct angles, and hard |

### 13-2. Preventing Cross-Pollination

| Method | Details | Practicality |
|---|---|---|
| Plant only one species | Do not plant other lines of the same species at all | The simplest and most reliable |
| Isolation distance | Keep at least 500m away from other lines | Difficult, because bees range widely |
| Staggered planting | Let only one line flower in a given year | Very practical, because seed longevity is 6-10 years |
| Hand pollination + bagging | The day before flowering, bag the female and male flowers; the next morning pollinate by hand and bag again | The most reliable, and feasible on a small scale |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The fact that squash seed lives for 6-10 years takes on strategic meaning here. One year is designated as the seed-saving year; in that year only winter squash is planted, and plenty of seed is set aside. For the next several years, aehobak or kabocha or anything else can be planted freely, because the seed already put aside does not change. In effect this discipline needs to be kept only once in five or six years, which is quite different in nature from the burden of chili pepper, which has to be saved every year.

### 13-3. Isolated Seed Saving by Hand Pollination

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This is the most reliable method. The steps follow one another at one-day intervals.

| Step | Timing | Details |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Check the flower buds | Afternoon of the day before flowering | Find the female and male flowers that will open the next morning. The buds are swollen and yellow, and their tips are about to part |
| 2. Bag the flowers | Afternoon of the day before flowering | Cover each female and each male flower with a paper bag or a cloth pouch and tie the opening shut |
| 3. Pollinate | The next day, from just after sunrise to 8 a.m. | Take the bag off a male flower and pick it, remove the petals, then take the bag off a female flower and rub the stamens on the stigma |
| 4. Bag again | Right after pollination | Cover the female flower with the bag again and take it off two days later |
| 5. Mark | Right after pollination | Tie a string or a scrap of cloth around the flower stalk to mark it as a seed-saving fruit |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Flowers are bagged to keep bees from visiting first. Squash flowers open before sunrise and bees start moving as soon as the sun is up, so unless the flowers are bagged the day before, other pollen has already landed on them before a person can touch them. Paper is the best material for the bags; plastic gets damp inside and spoils the flower. Cloth or non-woven fabric also works.

> **For seed-saving squash, one or two fruit are taken from each of several plants. Squash is a cross-pollinated crop, so if seed is taken continually from only one plant, inbreeding depression appears, with vigor weakening as the generations pile up. The rule is to take seed evenly from at least five plants, and preferably ten or more.**

### 13-4. Seed-Saving Procedure and Storage

| Step | Timing | Details |
|---|---|---|
| Choosing the seed fruit | August in South Korea | Choose fruit that is well shaped, free of disease, and clearly true to the cultivar's traits |
| Full ripening | September-October in South Korea | Leave it on longer than squash meant for eating, until it is completely ripe |
| After-ripening | 2-4 weeks after harvest | Leaving it another 2-4 weeks after harvest lets the seed fill out |
| Extracting the seed | After after-ripening | Split the fruit open and scrape out the seed together with the pulp |
| Washing | Right away | Put it in water and rub by hand to separate the pulp |
| Sorting | During washing | Seeds that float are empty duds and are thrown away |
| Drying | After washing | Spread in a single layer on cloth or a bamboo screen and dry in the shade for 1-2 weeks |
| Storage | After complete drying | Put in an airtight container with a desiccant and keep in a cool, dark place |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Seed must not be dried directly in the sun. The heat of direct sunlight damages the embryo and lowers the germination rate. Dry it slowly in airy shade, and spread it in a single layer, because if piled thick, mold grows inside the pile. Fully dry squash seed snaps when bent by hand and does not give when pressed with two fingernails.

| Item | Standard |
|---|---|
| Seeds per fruit | 200-500 seeds |
| Seed longevity | 6-10 years sealed at room temperature |
| Target moisture | 8% or less |
| Desiccant | Rice that has been roasted and cooled, charcoal, dry wood ash |
| Storage container | Glass jar, metal tin, or paper bag wrapped double |
| Germination check | Once every few years, place 20 seeds on a damp cloth and count the germination rate |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;One fruit yields 200-500 seeds, so two or three seed-saving squash secure several years' worth of seed. The flesh left after the seed is taken can simply be eaten. That seed saving does not eat into the food supply is another advantage of this crop. Squash with the seed removed is still whole food, and the seeds can also be roasted and eaten.

## 14. Toxicity and Compounds of Concern

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Winter squash has no general toxicity. The fruit, leaves, shoots, seeds, and flowers are all edible. There is, however, one substance that should be known: cucurbitacin.

| Compound | What it is | What the problem is | How to judge |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cucurbitacin | A bitter substance that plants of the cucurbit family make to deter herbivores | Eating a lot causes vomiting, abdominal pain, and diarrhea | If it tastes bitter, do not eat it |
| Mold | Grows on spoiled parts during storage | Toxins can form | If it is soft and smells, throw it away |

### 14-1. Bitter-Tasting Squash

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Cucurbitacin is a defensive substance that wild cucurbit plants originally carried, and in cultivated forms it has almost disappeared through long breeding. Under some conditions, however, it reappears.

| Condition | Explanation |
|---|---|
| Seed crossed with an ornamental squash | Ornamental squash still contains cucurbitacin, and the fruit of plants grown from seed fertilized with its pollen can be bitter |
| Feral squash | Squash that comes up by itself at the field edge has an unknown lineage |
| Extreme stress | There are reports that the content rises in severe drought or heat |

> **There is only one criterion: taste. If a bite of squash has a bitterness that stings the tongue, spit it out at once and throw away that whole squash. Cooking does not remove it, and if it is eaten out of reluctance to waste it, vomiting and diarrhea follow within a few hours. In severe cases, hospital treatment may be needed.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This risk grows especially when saving one's own seed. In the process of saving seed and replanting it, pollen from an ornamental squash can get mixed in from somewhere, and the result sometimes shows up several generations later. So for squash grown from saved seed, it is necessary to make a habit of tasting a small amount the first time it is eaten. If it is not bitter, it is safe.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The same goes for leaves and shoots. They are edible, but ones with a strong bitter taste are avoided. Normal squash leaves have only a slight green smell and are not bitter.
