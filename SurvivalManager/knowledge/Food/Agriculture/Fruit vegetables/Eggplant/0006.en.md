## 12. Harvest - Pick to Keep Them Coming

| Category | Standard | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Optimal harvest time | 10-20 days after flowering | Shorter in hot weather, longer when it is cool |
| Size standard | When it reaches the variety's standard length (15-20cm for long types) | Varies by variety, so judge by condition rather than length |
| Condition standard | The skin is glossy, and it springs back when pressed by hand | Once the gloss fades, it is already too late |
| First fruit | Pick it early, at egg size | To let the plant build its size first |
| Harvest interval | 2-3 times a week | In summer, two days is enough to notice growth |
| Time to harvest | Early morning | Fullest of moisture from overnight, so freshest and longest-lasting |
| Method | Cut above the calyx with scissors or a knife | Twisting it off tears the stem |

### 12-1. Why Harvest Early

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Eggplant is a crop whose quality drops once it ripens. What people eat is, botanically, an immature fruit — one whose seeds have not yet matured. Once the seeds begin to mature, the flesh turns spongy and dry, the skin toughens, and it develops a bitter taste. The skin color also shifts from dark purple to yellowish. A yellowed eggplant is not ripe — it has simply missed its eating window.

> **Eggplant only keeps fruiting if it is picked. Leave fruit on the plant, and it pours all its nutrients into making seed instead of making new flowers. Leave two or three overripe eggplants on a plant, and that plant's entire output stops.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Perenniality B in the evaluation table rests on this principle. Being able to keep picking until frost does not happen on its own — it is the result earned by diligent picking. Walk the plot two or three times a week, pick every fully grown fruit, and pick off and discard insect-damaged or misshapen ones too. Leave a spoiled fruit on the plant, and it only drains nutrients while becoming a foothold for disease.

### 12-2. Why Remove the First Fruit

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Picking the first fruit that sets at the crotch early, while it is still egg-sized, is an old technique. From the plant's point of view, the first fruit is its highest-priority organ, so a still-small plant concentrates its nutrients into that one fruit. The result is that the stem and roots fail to grow while only that one fruit enlarges, and afterward the plant lacks the strength to set fruit well. Removing the first fruit early sends those nutrients back into the stem and roots, letting the plant grow larger, and a larger plant produces far more fruit over the four months that follow. It is a trade: give up one fruit now to increase the total harvest.

### 12-3. Post-Harvest Handling

| Item | Details |
|---|---|
| Calyx | Cut leaving 3-5mm. Be careful not to prick yourself on the calyx spines |
| Washing | Wash just before eating. Washing ahead of time leaves surface moisture that becomes a starting point for spoilage |
| Storing | Stacking them causes bruising where they press together. Lay them out in a single layer |
| Temperature | 10-12°C is best. Below that, chilling injury sets in — pitting of the skin |
| Afterward | Keep only what will be eaten that day, and slice and dry the rest right away |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Chilling injury is a peculiar trait of eggplant. Being a tropical-origin crop, keeping it for long at temperatures below 5°C damages its cells, producing pitted marks on the skin and browning inside. Even with a refrigerator, tucking eggplant deep into the fridge is not good storage practice — and without a refrigerator, this problem simply does not arise. A spot at 10-15°C, such as under a cool floor or a north-facing eave, suits it best.

## 13. Seed Saving - The Advantage of Self-Pollination

### 13-1. Why Saving Eggplant Seed Is Easy

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Eggplant's S grade for seed saving rests on two grounds. First, self-pollination predominates. An eggplant flower has both pistil and stamens in one flower, and around the time it opens, its own pollen falls onto its own stigma and fertilization is complete. The cross-pollination rate is reported at 5-20% depending on conditions, rising where bees are plentiful — a contrast with kale, which, being self-incompatible, must always receive pollen from another plant. Second, a single fruit holds anywhere from several hundred to over a thousand seeds. Saving just two or three fruits yields enough seed for several years.

| Item | Standard |
|---|---|
| Required isolation distance | 30-50m from other eggplant varieties |
| Number of plants for seed saving | At least 3-5 plants |
| Seeds per fruit | Several hundred to over 1,000 |
| Seed longevity | 3-4 years sealed at room temperature |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Keeping 3-5 or more plants for seed saving is not about cross-pollination but about genetic breadth. Even in a self-pollinating crop, taking seed from only a single plant generation after generation narrows that line's diversity over time, and if some disease strikes that line's particular weakness, the whole thing can collapse at once. Taking seed evenly from several plants reduces that risk.

### 13-2. Seed-Saving Procedure

| Step | Timing | Details |
|---|---|---|
| Selecting seed plants | July | Choose plants that are disease-free, vigorous, and show clear varietal traits |
| Selecting seed fruit | Late July-August | Choose fruit lower on that plant, set while its vigor was strong |
| Marking | Right after selection | Tie a string around it to mark it and prevent it being picked by mistake |
| Full ripening | September-October | Leave it until the skin turns yellowish-brown and loses its gloss. 60-70 days after flowering |
| After-ripening | After harvest | 1-2 weeks in a cool, shaded spot lets the seed mature further |
| Separating the seed | After after-ripening | Split the fruit, soak the flesh in water, and knead it by hand to release the seed |
| Settling | Right after separation | In water, sound seed sinks while chaff and flesh float. Discard whatever floats |
| Drying | After separation | Spread on cloth and dry in the shade for 1-2 weeks. Done when a seed snaps cleanly when bent by hand |
| Storage | After fully dry | Put in a sealed container with a desiccant, in a cool, dark place |

> **Always choose seed fruit from lower on the plant — that is, fruit that set while the plant was at its strongest. Seed from fruit that sets just before frost has not had time to mature and germinates poorly.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Seed must not be dried in direct sun. The heat of direct sunlight damages the embryo and sharply lowers germination. The rule is to dry it slowly in a shaded spot with airflow, and it must be spread in a single layer, since piling it thickly lets mold grow inside the pile.

| Item | Standard |
|---|---|
| Target moisture | 8% or below |
| Desiccant | Roasted and cooled rice, charcoal, dry wood ash, roasted and cooled salt |
| Storage container | A glass jar, a metal tin, or a paper bag wrapped in a double layer |
| Storage temperature | The lower, the better, and it must stay steady |
| Germination check | Every year, place 20 seeds on a damp cloth and count the germination rate after a week |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The principles of seed storage are dryness, cold, and darkness. Of the three, dryness is the only one that can be reliably controlled in a disaster situation, so it deserves the most effort. Sealing in roasted, cooled rice equal to about a third of the seed's volume lets the rice absorb any remaining moisture. Charcoal serves the same role, and once used up it can be dried again over a fire and reused.

### 13-3. Seed Disinfection

| Method | Procedure | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Hot-water soak | Soak in 50-55°C water for 10-15 minutes, then cool immediately in cold water | Kills pathogens on the seed surface and just under the seed coat |
| Wood-ash coating | Coat dry seed with fine ash | Suppresses mold and blocks moisture |
| Sorting | Soak in water and discard whatever floats | Diseased seed and chaff usually float |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Temperature is the key factor in a hot-water soak. It must not stray outside 50-55°C. Above 60°C the seed dies, and below 45°C pathogens survive. Without a thermometer, mix boiling and cold water until it is hot enough that a hand can bear it for only about 3 seconds — that is roughly the right range — and use a large bowl with plenty of water, so the temperature drops less while the seed soaks.

## 14. Toxicity and Compounds of Concern

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Being a Solanaceae crop, eggplant contains trace amounts of steroidal alkaloids. These belong to the same family as potato's solanine, and in eggplant they mainly appear as solasonine and solamargine. The amount in a normally harvested eggplant is not a level that causes problems for people, but it is worth knowing under what conditions that amount rises.

| Compound | What It Is | What Problem It Causes | How to Avoid It |
|---|---|---|---|
| Solasonine and solamargine | Defense compounds Solanaceae plants make to fend off insects and fungi | Eating a lot causes stomach irritation and nausea | Do not eat strongly bitter fruit. Cooking reduces the amount somewhat |
| Nicotine | Present in trace amounts in Solanaceae plants | At about 0.01mg per 100g, has no practical effect | No action needed |
| Chlorogenic acid | The substance responsible for browning on a cut surface | Not toxic, but gives an astringent taste | Slice and soak in water or a weak salt solution |
| Histamine trigger | Itching or hives in some people | Varies greatly by individual | Start with a small amount the first time you eat it |

### 14-1. Why Bitterness Is a Warning Sign

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Alkaloids taste bitter. This is not an accident but a plant's defense strategy: it sends the signal "do not eat this" through taste, and animals remember that bitterness and avoid eating it again. The same signal works on people. If a bite of eggplant gives a sharp, tongue-stinging bitterness, that fruit has a high alkaloid content, and the right response is to stop eating it.

| Condition | Alkaloid Level | Explanation |
|---|:---:|---|
| A normally grown, in-season fruit | Low | Safe |
| A fruit that is too young | Somewhat high | Content is high because this is a stage that needs defense |
| A fruit that has missed its harvest window and turned yellow | High | Bitterness intensifies and the flesh turns spongy |
| A fruit grown under severe water shortage | High | Stress increases production of defense compounds |
| Leaves and stems | Very high | Not a part people eat |

> **Do not eat eggplant leaves and stems. Their alkaloid content is far higher than the fruit's. In a disaster situation, deciding to eat eggplant leaves as a vegetable side dish because there is nothing else is dangerous. Send the leaves and stems to compost instead.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In short, eggplant's toxicity is not a real danger as long as normal cultivation and a normal harvest time are followed. But in a disaster situation, where water is scarce and management grows lax, more fruit is likely to be stressed, and hunger can make people reluctant to waste even fruit that has missed its harvest window. Keeping to just one rule — discard it if it tastes bitter — is enough.

## 15. Storage Without Refrigeration - Dried Eggplant

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Eggplant's storability grade is D, and unpowered at room temperature it does not last a week. With 92% of the fruit being water, that is only to be expected. So there is only one way to carry eggplant through to winter: take the water out.

### 15-1. Comparing Storage Methods

| Method | Conditions | Duration | Characteristics |
|---|---|:---:|---|
| Short-term fresh storage | Wrapped in newspaper or paper, in shade at 10-15°C | 5-7 days | Keep unwashed. Chilling injury sets in below 5°C |
| Drying (dried eggplant) | Sliced thin and fully dried in sun and wind | 6 months-1 year | Volume and weight drop to a tenth, making it the most practical method |
| Salt-pickling | Cured with 10-15% salt | Several months | Must be soaked in water before eating |
| Jangajji | Buried in soy sauce, doenjang, or gochujang | Several months-1 year | Uses salt and fermentation together |
| Lactic fermentation | Brined at 2-3% salt, kept submerged under the brine | A few weeks-a few months | Prone to going soft, making it tricky for eggplant |

### 15-2. Making Dried Eggplant

| Step | Method | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Selecting | Use fruit that is unblemished and properly grown | Mold starts at a damaged spot |
| 2. Washing | Wash, then dry the surface completely | Leftover surface moisture lets mold grow early in drying |
| 3. Slicing | Cut into 5-7mm rounds, or split lengthwise into four pieces | Too thick, and the outside spoils before the inside dries |
| 4. Blanching (optional) | Dip in boiling water for 30 seconds to 1 minute, then remove | Kills enzymes, reducing browning and keeping the color |
| 5. Laying out | Spread in a single layer, without overlapping, on a basket or mat | Overlapping spots fail to dry |
| 6. Drying | 2-4 days in a sunny, airy spot | Bring it in at night to keep off the dew |
| 7. Finishing | Until it snaps cleanly when bent by hand | Any softness at all lets mold grow during storage |
| 8. Storage | In a sealed container with a desiccant, in a dark, cool place | Light fades its color |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Whether to blanch is where opinions split. Blanching keeps the color attractive and gives a softer texture once rehydrated, but it costs fuel. Skipping it lets the color turn brown, but costs no fuel and gives a stronger aroma. The traditional dried eggplant handed down as ogari is simply dried without blanching — dark brown in color, but with no problem in flavor. Where fuel is scarce, skipping the blanching step is the sensible choice.

> **Success or failure in drying is decided on the very first day. Surface water has to evaporate quickly on the first day it is laid out, or mold gets a foothold. Start laying it out on an overcast, humid day, and black mold appears by the third day, forcing the whole batch to be thrown out. Choose a clear, breezy day to begin.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;During the monsoon or on sunless days, there is a way to dry it over fire. Hanging a mat above the cooking or water-heating hearth, over the warmest spot of an ondol floor, or beside a stove lets the residual heat do the drying. This method suits a disaster situation well, since it needs no extra fuel and works even on humid days. Direct contact with smoke, however, leaves a sooty smell, so keep it out of the smoke's path.

| Item | Standard |
|---|---|
| Weight compared with fresh eggplant | Around 10% |
| Judging full dryness | Snaps with a sound and does not bend |
| Checking during storage | Open once a month to check for mold and dampness |
| If it turns damp | Lay it back out in the sun for half a day, then reseal |
| Insects | Indianmeal moth larvae can get in. Block them with sealing and a desiccant |

### 15-3. Salt-Pickling and Jangajji

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Where drying is hard — a long monsoon, or a spot with no sun — salt is the alternative. Split the eggplant lengthwise, layer it with salt at 10-15% of its weight, and press it under a heavy stone so the brine covers the material. At this concentration, most microbes cannot grow, so it keeps for several months. To eat it, soak it in water for a few hours to draw out the salt — a process that also draws out water-soluble nutrients along with it.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Jangajji adds fermentation to salt-pickling. Eggplant is salted in brine for a few days until it softens, then buried in soy sauce or doenjang. The high salinity and organic acids of doenjang and soy sauce prevent spoilage, and over time the eggplant absorbs their flavor, becoming a side dish in its own right. It tastes better and keeps longer than a plain salt pickle, but it depends on being able to make soy sauce or doenjang in the first place.

## 16. Cooking and Processing

| Method | Fuel | Characteristics |
|---|:---:|---|
| Steaming | Low (under 10 minutes) | Tends to float, so it needs a plate pressed on top to cook evenly. Uses no oil, making it the most economical method |
| Stir-frying | Low | Absorbs oil well, improving flavor, but uses a lot of oil |
| Grilling | Low | Grilled whole over embers and peeled, the inside turns soft. Uses leftover fire |
| Added to soup or stew | Combined with other cooking | Uses no extra fuel of its own |
| Rehydrating dried eggplant | Low | Soak in water 20-30 minutes, then stir-fry or dress it |
| Eating raw | None | Not recommended. Astringent, with alkaloids still present |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There is one property worth knowing when cooking eggplant. Its flesh has a spongy structure, so it absorbs oil extremely well. When oil is plentiful, this is an advantage, but in a disaster situation where oil is scarce, it becomes a drawback — stir-frying eggplant makes oil vanish in an instant. The way around this is to steam or blanch it first, filling the air inside the spongy structure with water. Eggplant filled with water absorbs less oil, so it can be stir-fried with only a little.

> **When oil is scarce, steaming is the right answer for eggplant. Tear the steamed eggplant apart and dress it with soy sauce or doenjang, garlic, and sesame salt, and it needs only 10 minutes of fuel and a few drops of oil. This is why the old method of gajinamul — dressed eggplant — became established.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Soaking dried eggplant in water for 20-30 minutes brings it back to nearly half its original volume. The soaking water carries out its aroma and water-soluble compounds, so it should not be discarded but used as stock. Rehydrated dried eggplant has a chewier texture and a stronger flavor than fresh eggplant, and it takes up less oil too. Dried eggplant namul eaten in winter is not a poor substitute — it is a different dish in its own right.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The rule is not to peel it. Nasunin, eggplant's signature compound, is in the skin, and dietary fiber is concentrated there too. If the skin is tough enough to make you want to peel it, that fruit has already missed its harvest window. For the thick skin of late-autumn fruit, cutting a few lengthwise slits improves the texture.

## 17. Annual Work Calendar

| Month | Tasks |
|---|---|
| January | Check seed stock and test germination rate. Gather materials for a fermentation-heated hotbed (rice straw, horse manure, rice hulls) |
| February | Sow mid-late in central South Korea, early-mid in the south. Build the hotbed and start it heating |
| March | Manage the seedlings. Prick out once at 3-4 true leaves. Apply lime to the field |
| April | Apply base fertilizer and compost, and build the ridge. Transplant in late April in southern South Korea. Begin hardening off |
| May | Transplant in early-mid May in central South Korea, staking at the same time. Remove side shoots below the crotch |
| June | Pick the first fruit while young. 1st top-dressing. Straw mulch. First harvest from late June |
| July | Harvest 2-3 times a week. 2nd top-dressing. Check drainage during the monsoon. Prepare for renewal pruning from late July |
| August | Renewal pruning and root pruning in early August, with the 3rd top-dressing. Fruit-set drops in this hot stretch. Start drying |
| September | Autumn eggplant sets on the new shoots after renewal. 4th top-dressing. Wait for the seed fruit to fully ripen |
| October | Finish harvesting, and slice and dry all the remaining fruit. Harvest the seed fruit and let it after-ripen |
| November | Pull up the plants and burn diseased ones. Cut healthy stems into the compost pile. Clean and store the seed |
| December | Review next year's rotation plan. Check the condition of the dried eggplant |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Two entries in this table deserve special attention: February and August. February is the month when 25°C must be created without heating, and August is when the decision on renewal pruning has to be made. Work in the other months is largely repetitive and mistakes can be recovered from, but getting either of these two wrong wipes out half that year's harvest. Failing at seedling-raising in February loses the whole season, and missing the pruning in August loses the September-October autumn crop.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The work of October and November is not light either. Gathering and drying all the remaining fruit before frost decides how much winter side dish there will be, and burning diseased plants while composting only the healthy ones decides how much disease pressure there will be next year. Eggplant is a crop where more is decided at the moment of clearing it out than at the moment of planting it.
