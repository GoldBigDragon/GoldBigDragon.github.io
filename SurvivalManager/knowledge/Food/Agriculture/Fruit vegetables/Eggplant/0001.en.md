## Introduction

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Eggplant (Solanum melongena) is an annual fruit vegetable in the genus Solanum, family Solanaceae. It originates from the subtropical belt stretching from eastern India to Myanmar, and this origin decides almost everything about its character. It loves heat, is helpless against cold, drinks a great deal of water, and a single frost ends an entire field at once. In South Korea's climate, eggplant is planted in late spring, bears fruit all through summer, and dies at the first frost — and across those four months it produces fruit with almost no rest.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Measured against disaster preparedness, the numbers are unsentimental. The overall grade is E, 31.7 points. The short-term score is also E, and so is the long-term score. Eggplant carries 19kcal per 100g, and even a yield of 3,500kg from 1,000m² works out to only 0.25× the self-sufficiency line in calories per area. Covering an adult's daily 2,100kcal with eggplant alone would take 11kg — far more than a stomach can hold. Eggplant is not a staple-calorie crop, and unless this fact is accepted first, a field plan will go wrong.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;And yet, in the same table, the supplementary-food grade is A, 50.2 points — 18.5 points above the overall score. That gap is the whole identity of eggplant. The very fact that it is low in calories is what pushes protein density to S and micronutrients to B. It means nutrient density per calorie is high, and that is exactly the definition of a supplementary crop. Once grains and tuber crops (tubers/storage roots) have built the calorie base, what a diet still lacks is variety and micronutrients — and eggplant addresses both at once. The tender fruit that comes off the plant every week in high summer sustains a diet's morale all by itself.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Eggplant's strength lies in its stability through the height of summer. A yield-stability grade of S, with year-to-year yield variation of only 5.7%, is one of the standout figures in this crop table. Hot, humid midsummer is the season when brassica leafy greens collapse and lettuce bolts, but for eggplant that same season is its main stage. Alongside this come a taste grade of S and a grade of S for prevalence in South Korea: seed and seedlings can be found in any region, and everyone eats it without hesitation. Its seed-saving grade is also S. Because self-pollination dominates, a variety stays true even with only a few plants isolated, so a line secured once can be carried forward for years.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The weaknesses gather into three clusters. The first is temperature. Cold hardiness is E: the minimum for growth is 15°C, and the plant dies below 10°C. Temperature range is also C — the fruit-setting range runs only 20-30°C, a span of just 10°C. Both ends of spring and autumn are cut away, and on top of that, raising seedlings alone takes 70-80 days. Converted to direct sowing, the crop needs 130-150 days, which makes raising seedlings without heating in a disaster situation a challenge in its own right.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The second is continuous-cropping damage. Continuous-cropping tolerance is D, an RDA break group of 5-7 years. Bacterial wilt and Verticillium wilt remain in the soil, and neither is a disease medicine can cure.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The third is storability, D. More than 90% of the fruit is water, so it does not last a week at room temperature.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;That is why Section 15 of this document puts its weight on drying. Slicing and drying eggplant cuts its weight to 10% and lets it keep for six months to a year at room temperature. Fresh eggplant is a midsummer side dish; dried eggplant is a winter one. What makes this crop useful without refrigeration is not the field but the drying rack under the eaves. In short, eggplant is not the centerpiece of a field but a handful of plants — five or six — kept to one side: picked and eaten fresh in summer, and whatever is left is sliced, dried, and carried into winter.
