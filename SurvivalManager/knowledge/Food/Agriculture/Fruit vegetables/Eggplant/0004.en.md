## 4. Varieties

### 4-1. Comparing the Main Varieties

| Variety | Shape | Maturity | Yield | Difficulty | Suited Environment | Characteristics |
|---|---|:---:|:---:|:---:|---|---|
| Sinheuksanho | Long fruit | Early | High | Easy | Open field or rain shelter | Sets fruit well, with stable early yield |
| Heukjinju | Long fruit | Early | High | Easy | Open field or rain shelter | The most widely used line in South Korea |
| Cheonhadaejang | Slender, long fruit (36-41cm) | Mid-season | High | Moderate | Hot, dry periods | Thin skin, glossy deep purple-black |
| Soeppulgaji | Thick, short fruit | Mid-to-late | Moderate | Easy | Open field | Thick-skinned and relatively tolerant of cool temperatures |
| Garakjanggaji | Slender, long fruit | Mid-season | Moderate | Moderate | Open field | Tender flesh, short cooking time |
| Paengpaengigaji | Plump, short fruit | Mid-season | Moderate | Easy | Cool-tolerant | Firm skin gives it a long post-harvest life |
| Traditional Joseon eggplant | Short, plump fruit | Mid-season | Low | Easy | Nationwide (South Korea) | Seed can be saved and replanted; strong aroma |

### 4-2. Choosing by Situation

| Situation | Choice | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Early in a disaster, fast harvest | Sinheuksanho, Heukjinju | Early varieties give a fast first harvest and set fruit reliably |
| Planning to save and carry seed forward | Traditional Joseon eggplant lines | Being an open-pollinated line, the saved seed's traits hold up the following year |
| Cool regions, short summers | Soeppulgaji, Paengpaengigaji | Growth slows relatively less at low temperatures |
| Planning to dry and store | Thin-skinned, long-fruited lines | Easy to slice thin and dries evenly |
| A few plants on a small plot | Sinheuksanho | High yield per plant and easy to manage |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In disaster preparedness, the axis of variety choice is not yield but the character of the seed. A large share of commercially sold varieties are F1 hybrids. F1 hybrids are uniform and high-yielding in their first year, but seed saved from those fruits and planted the next year segregates in its traits: large and small, long and round fruit come out mixed together, and yield drops. Where seed supply has been cut off, this property is fatal.

> **Eggplant's S grade for seed saving comes from the plant's own habit of self-pollination — it does not mean seed can be saved and used from every variety on the market. Seed saved from an F1 hybrid segregates the following year regardless of self-pollination.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;So, looking ahead over several years, securing one open-pollinated line is the practical form of preparedness. Traditional Joseon eggplant, or a line long kept in a local area, fits this role. Its yield falls short of commercial varieties, but its saved seed produces the same eggplant the following year too. The high yield of a commercial variety is a value that only holds while the seed supply chain is alive; once that chain breaks, it holds for only the first year.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Honesty is needed about resistance. There is effectively no eggplant variety fully resistant to bacterial wilt. Commercial growers avoid the problem by grafting onto resistant rootstock, most commonly turkey berry (Solanum torvum) and resistant eggplant lines. Grafting needs a sharp knife, fixing clips, and a hot, humid environment for the graft union to heal, which is not easy in a disaster situation. If grafting is not possible, the only path left is not resistance but rotation and sanitation, which is the content of Sections 9 and 11.

## 5. Field Preparation and Fertilizer

### 5-1. Field Preparation

| Item | Standard | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Tillage depth | 25-30cm | Thickens the topsoil to give the shallow root system a layer to spread through |
| Ridge height | 20cm or more | Prevents root oxygen shortage during the monsoon |
| Drainage channels | Field edges and row ends | Standing water triggers an outbreak of late blight |
| Lime application | 2-3 weeks before transplanting | Applying it at the same time as compost drives off nitrogen |
| Compost application | 1-2 weeks before transplanting | Use only fully matured compost |
| Continuous-cropping check | Whether Solanaceae were grown within the last 3-5 years | The first thing to check |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There is a chemical reason for not applying lime and compost on the same day. Lime is alkaline, and under alkaline conditions the ammonium nitrogen in compost turns into ammonia gas and escapes into the air — nitrogen carefully gathered simply turns into a smell and vanishes. The rule is to apply lime first, wait at least two weeks, and only then apply compost.

### 5-2. Fertilizer Amounts

| Fertilizer | Per 1,000m² | Per m² | Split |
|---|:---:|:---:|---|
| Nitrogen (N) | 19.0kg | About 19g | Base 10.2kg + top-dressing 8.8kg |
| Phosphorus (P) | 11.2kg | About 11g | All as base fertilizer |
| Potassium (K) | 15.0kg | About 15g | Base 9.0kg + top-dressing 6.0kg |
| Compost | 1,500-2,000kg | 1.5-2kg | Fully matured compost |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium together come to around 45g per m². Fertilizer demand grade D in the evaluation table comes from this requirement. Set beside kale's 24g or amaranth's 14g, it is two to three times as much. What matters is that nearly half of this amount goes in split as top-dressing. Since eggplant is a crop that keeps making fruit for four months, nutrients also have to keep coming in for four months. Apply only base fertilizer and leave it at that, and the fruit will visibly shrink from mid-July onward.

### 5-3. Timing of Base Fertilizer and Top-Dressing

| Stage | Timing | Details |
|---|---|---|
| Lime | 2-3 weeks before transplanting | Correcting soil acidity |
| Base fertilizer | 1-2 weeks before transplanting | All the compost, all the phosphorus, and 50-60% of the nitrogen and potassium |
| Top-dressing 1 | About 30 days after transplanting | Around when the first fruit sets |
| Top-dressing 2 | 20-25 days after 1st | As the harvest gets underway in earnest |
| Top-dressing 3 | 20-25 days after 2nd | Applied together with renewal pruning |
| Top-dressing 4 | 20-25 days after 3rd | The last feeding, for the autumn harvest |

### 5-4. Matching the Same Amounts Without Chemical Fertilizer

| Substitute | Nutrients Supplied | How to Use | Caution |
|---|---|---|---|
| Well-rotted compost | Nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, and micronutrients generally | 2-3kg per m² as base fertilizer | Under-rotted compost burns roots and attracts grubs |
| Diluted urine | Mainly nitrogen, with small amounts of potassium and phosphorus | Dilute 10-15× with water and pour into the furrow between plants | Burns leaves on direct contact. Stop 2 weeks before harvest |
| Wood ash | Potassium, calcium, phosphorus | Mix a handful (50-100g) per m² into the soil | Alkaline; overuse raises pH too far |
| Bone meal | Phosphorus, calcium | Mix into compost as base fertilizer | Breaks down slowly, so it is for base use only |
| Sesame/soybean oil cake | Nitrogen | Soak in water, ferment 2-3 weeks, then dilute 10× | Adding it raw straight into the soil damages roots with fermentation heat |
| Green manure | Nitrogen, organic matter | Cut grass from the field edge and cover the row with it | Grass that has already set seed will spread weeds |
| Fish and shellfish scraps | Nitrogen, phosphorus, calcium | Bury in the compost pile and let fully rot before use | Burying them raw in the field draws animals to dig them up |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Urine is the most reliable, most steady nitrogen source in a disaster situation. One adult's daily urine contains about 5-7g of nitrogen, which adds up to just over 2kg a year. Since a 20m² eggplant bed needs about 400g of nitrogen, two months of one person's urine covers a year's fertilizer supply. Freshly passed urine is nearly sterile and mainly urea; left for a few days, the urea turns to ammonia, gaining a smell and higher concentration, so it is better to dilute it right after collecting it.

> **Urine has plenty of nitrogen but is short on phosphorus. Feeding it alone leads to lush leaves and no fruit. Wood ash and bone meal need to fill in potassium and phosphorus together to balance it out.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Wood ash is the main source of potassium. Ash from broadleaf trees carries more potassium than ash from conifers, and fully burned white ash is best. However, wood ash is strongly alkaline, and applying a lot in one spot pushes pH above 8, which then actually blocks nutrient uptake. The trick is to spread no more than one handful around each plant and lightly scratch it into the soil. Because rain washes potassium away quickly, applying it in several smaller doses is more efficient.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The standard for judging how well compost has matured is smell, temperature, and form. Well-rotted compost smells like earth, is not warm to the touch even with a hand pushed into it, and no longer shows the shape of its original materials. An ammonia smell or a warm interior means fermentation is still underway, and adding it to the field in that state damages roots with fermentation heat and organic acids. In a shallow-rooted crop like eggplant, this damage is especially severe.

## 6. Sowing and Transplanting

### 6-1. Timing

| Task | Region | Timing | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sowing (start of seedling raising) | Central South Korea | Mid-late February | 70-80 days before transplanting |
| | Southern South Korea | Early-mid February | |
| Transplanting | Central South Korea | Early-mid May | After the last frost has fully passed |
| | Southern South Korea | Late April-early May | |
| First harvest | Across South Korea | Late June | 50-70 days after transplanting |
| End of harvest | Across South Korea | Mid-late October | Until the first frost |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The standard for setting the transplant date is not the calendar but the last frost. You need to know when the last frost falls in your area, and if you don't, it is better to plant late. Planting a week late costs only a week's delay in harvest, but planting a week early and getting caught by frost wipes out that year's crop entirely.

### 6-2. Raising Seedlings Without Heating

| Item | Standard |
|---|---|
| Pre-sowing treatment | Soak 10 hours in 30°C water, then wrap in a damp cloth and sprout at 25-30°C |
| Sowing depth | 0.5-1cm |
| Germination temperature | 25-30°C, 3-5 days |
| Seedling temperature | Day 22-30°C, night 15°C or above |
| Pricking out | Once, at 3-4 true leaves (35-40 days after sowing) |
| Seedling period | 70-80 days |
| Seedling ready for transplanting | 5-7 true leaves, around when the first flower bud appears |
| Hardening off | Starting 1 week before transplanting, set outside during the day to adapt to outdoor conditions |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Creating a germination temperature of 25-30°C in late February is the biggest hurdle with this crop. On the assumption that there is no electricity, there are broadly three usable methods.

| Method | Principle | Temperature Achieved | Limitation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fermentation-heated hotbed | Horse manure, rice straw, and rice hulls mixed with water and tramped in let microbes break them down and give off heat | Holds the bed surface at 20-30°C for 3-6 weeks | Gathering the materials and tramping them down is heavy labor |
| South-facing indoor windowsill | Sunlight coming through the glass by day warms the soil and water | Day 25-30°C, night 15-18°C | Securing night temperature is the key problem |
| Water-jug heat storage | A black water jug placed beside the seedling box stores the day's heat and releases it at night | Raises night temperature by 3-5°C | A supplementary method only |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The fermentation-heated hotbed is a method actually used before the age of electricity. Dig 50-60cm into the ground, wet horse manure or rice straw with water, tramp it in layer by layer, then cover it with 10cm of seed-starting soil on top. Within a few days microbial breakdown begins and the surface temperature rises, and that heat lasts 3-6 weeks. At night, cover it with a straw mat to trap the heat. Without horse manure, mixing rice straw, rice bran, and urine can start a similar fermentation, but it produces less heat for less time.

> **If raising seedlings proves too hard, there is the option of delaying the sowing date. Sowing in early April and transplanting in mid-June pushes the first harvest back to August, but seedlings can then be raised on outdoor temperature alone, without heating. The trade-off is a harvest period cut to a little over two months, but it prevents the crop from failing altogether.**

### 6-3. Transplanting

| Item | Standard | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Planting depth | Shallow, just enough that the seedling's soil ball barely shows | Planting too deep rots the stem |
| Single-row planting | Row spacing 80-90cm, plant spacing 45-50cm | 2.2-2.8 plants per m² |
| Double-row planting | Row spacing 150cm, plant spacing 45-50cm | Leave 60cm or more between the two lines for airflow |
| Planting method | Pour plenty of water into the hole and set the seedling in after it drains | Speeds up establishment |
| Staking | Set a 1.5m stake at the same time as transplanting | Staking later cuts the roots |
| Time of transplanting | Afternoon on an overcast day | Transplanting at midday on a clear day causes severe wilting |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;A plant spacing of 45-50cm looks wide, but it is what eggplant needs. Because of its structure of continuous forking at the crotch, a single plant eventually spreads to nearly 1m across, and planting densely darkens the interior and blocks airflow. Blocked airflow lets leaf mold and gray mold take hold, and fruit that gets no light fails to color. The loss from disease outweighs the gain from packing more plants into a small plot.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The reason for staking at the same time as transplanting is the roots. Driving a stake in about a month later cuts through fine roots that have already spread out, and bacterial wilt pathogens enter through the cut. A stake about 2cm thick and 1.5m long, of wood or bamboo, is enough, and in a disaster situation a straight branch cut and dried from around the field will do. Infrastructure needs C in the evaluation table refers to the burden of having to prepare fresh stakes and tying twine every year.

## 7. Water Management

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;92% of an eggplant fruit is water. This one sentence explains the whole of water management. What a person harvests is, in effect, water, so if water is short, there is nothing to harvest. This is why total evapotranspiration reaches 750mm per season, and why water use sits at C in the evaluation table.

| Stage | Water Management | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Right after transplanting-establishment | Pour plenty into the planting hole, then keep it from drying out for the next 3-4 days | Absorption is weak until new roots emerge |
| After establishment-before flowering | Every 4-5 days, when the topsoil dries | Keeping it slightly dry at this stage drives roots deeper |
| Flowering and fruit-set | Every 2-3 days, generously | Water shortage causes flowers and young fruit to drop |
| Fruit enlargement and harvest | Every 2-3 days, generously | Most of the fruit's weight is water taken in at this stage |
| Monsoon season | Stop watering; check drainage channels | Root oxygen shortage is where late blight begins |
| Right after renewal pruning | Generously | New roots and new shoots emerge at the same time |

### 7-1. Signs of Water Shortage

| Order | Symptom | Explanation |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Flowers and pea-sized young fruit drop | The first sign to appear, and loss has already begun by this point |
| 2 | The proportion of short-styled flowers (short pistil) rises | They open but fail to fertilize and drop |
| 3 | Fruit stops thin and short, and the skin toughens | The result of cells failing to swell with water |
| 4 | Leaves droop by day and recover by evening | Still a recoverable stage |
| 5 | Leaves no longer recover even by evening, and lower leaves yellow and drop first | Even watering at this stage will not save that week's harvest |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The order is worth paying attention to. Wilting leaves are the fourth sign, and by then the flowers have already dropped. In other words, the sign a person notices most easily comes last. That is why, in an eggplant bed, you watch the soil rather than the leaves. Push a finger in 5cm, and if it does not feel cool and moist, it is time to water.

### 7-2. Methods for When Water Is Scarce

| Method | Principle | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Straw/grass mulch | Covers the soil surface to block evaporation | Extends the watering interval 1.5-2× |
| Water vessel around the plant | Push a bottle or bamboo tube with a hole in the bottom into the soil and fill it with water | Water seeps slowly into the root layer, reducing loss |
| Evening watering | Water after the sun sets | Evaporation loss is much lower than watering by day |
| Furrow watering | Run water only into the furrow between rows | Water never touches the leaves, reducing disease spread |
| Reusing dishwater and rice-rinse water | Collect and use water low in oil and detergent | Brings organic matter along with it |

> **Sprinkling water onto the leaves is the worst way to water eggplant. Leaves that stay wet overnight create ideal conditions for leaf mold and gray mold spores to germinate. Always water the soil, never the leaves.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;What matters for mulching in a disaster situation is doing it without plastic sheeting. Rice straw, barley straw, cut and dried grass, fallen leaves, and rice hulls can all be used. A thickness of 5-10cm is right; too thin has no effect, and too thick cools the soil and delays early growth. Rather than covering it right after transplanting, it is better to wait until early June, once the soil has warmed enough. Besides blocking evaporation, straw mulch also stops splashing rain from carrying soil-borne pathogens up onto the lower leaves, and tilling it under in autumn turns it into organic matter.
