## 1. Disaster-Preparedness Evaluation

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Eggplant's overall grade is E, 31.7 points. Broken down by scenario, its short-term ability to survive the early stage of a disaster is E (31.9), its long-term ability to sustain itself over several years is E (31.6), and its value as a supplementary side-dish resource beyond staple food is A (50.2). There is an 18.5-point gap between the overall and supplementary scores, and that gap sums up the character of eggplant in one line: it does not work as a staple food, but it ranks near the top as a side dish.

### 1-3. Why Overall E and Supplementary A Sit Side by Side

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The key to reading this table is that Calories per area D and Supplementary A sit side by side in it. The two values are not a contradiction but two faces of the same fact. Eggplant carries 19kcal per 100g. Set beside 130kcal for the same weight of cooked rice or 401kcal for dried soybeans, it is close to plain water. In fact, 92% of an eggplant fruit is water. Even harvesting 3.5kg from 1m² converts to only 665kcal, just 0.25× the self-sufficiency line.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;But it is that very low calorie count that pushes up the grades in the other columns. The 23.8% P:E behind the S grade for protein density does not come from eggplant being rich in protein. At 1.13g per 100g, the absolute amount is minor; the ratio is only large because the denominator, calories, is so small. The same is true of the B grade for micronutrients. It means this is a food with high nutrient density per calorie — the kind eaten not to fill the stomach but to balance out a diet.

> **The point of planting eggplant is not to fill the rice bowl, but to secure a side dish that comes off the plant every week through the four months of high summer.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;One thing should be added here. The B grade for micronutrients applies a cap of eating 2,500g a day, while actually covering a full day's calories with eggplant alone would take 11.1kg. The table's figure of meeting 13 nutrients is therefore a value for "eating a very large amount of eggplant." In reality, eggplant is a side dish eaten at around 100-300g a day, and at that amount what it offers is mainly potassium, dietary fiber, and the polyphenols in the skin. Vitamin C, at 2.2mg per 100g, is nearly absent, so eggplant cannot take the place of leafy greens. This distinction matters: eggplant is not a crop that competes with leafy greens, but one that fills the high summer when leafy greens cannot hold on.

### 1-4. How to Read the Strengths

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The criteria graded S are six: protein density, sunlight response, yield stability, taste, prevalence in South Korea, and seed saving. A goes to cooking & processing and seed longevity; B goes to micronutrients and perenniality.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The most practical of these is the S for yield stability. Year-to-year yield variation of 5.7% means the harvest does not swing wildly from year to year. In a disaster situation, what matters more than the average yield is the range of variation: a crop with a high average that can drop by half in a bad year is impossible to plan around. Because eggplant's main stage is precisely the hot part of summer, that condition is guaranteed almost every year in South Korea's climate. The monsoon and typhoons are variables, but far milder ones than the collapse that leafy greens or brassica crops suffer in summer.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The S for sunlight needs careful interpretation. The figure of 102% relative yield at 40% shade is a value assigned across fruit vegetables generally; it does not mean yield actually rises in shade, but that moderate shading does no harm because excessive direct sun and heat in high summer become a stress in themselves. In fact, under strong midday light above 35°C, pollen loses vigor, fertilization fails, and flowers drop. The fact that placing eggplant where a wall casts shade for half the day, or to the east of a tall crop, does not much reduce yield is useful when dividing up a small plot of land.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Seed saving S and seed longevity A form a pair. An eggplant flower has both pistil and stamens together, and in most cases fertilization is completed by the flower's own pollen reaching its own pistil around the time it opens. So even planting just a few plants apart keeps a variety pure across generations. Seed stays viable for 3-4 years sealed at room temperature, so one well-kept packet of seed becomes insurance for several years. In a situation where the seed supply chain has broken down, this combination carries no small value.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Taste S and cooking & processing A go together too. Eggplant has little off-putting smell or texture, and can be eaten after cooking for under 10 minutes. Compared with soybeans, which need eight hours of soaking and an hour of boiling, the fuel burden is very low. Eating it raw is not recommended, however, and Section 14 explains why.

### 1-5. How to Read the Weaknesses

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The criteria graded E are two: cold hardiness and non-food value. D goes to calories per area, fertilizer demand, storability, and continuous-cropping tolerance. C goes to speed to harvest, water use, infrastructure needs, temperature range, pest & disease pressure, pest-control self-reliance, yield retention without synthetic inputs, and tillage & digging energy. The sheer number of Cs and Ds is itself what drags down this crop's overall score.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Cold hardiness E is the most fundamental constraint. A minimum for growth of 15°C, with death below 10°C, means that in South Korea's climate the period when eggplant can actually grow is only five months, from mid-May to mid-October. On top of this comes temperature range C: the range in which fruit can set runs only 20-30°C, a span of just 10°C, and outside it, flowers may open but no fruit forms. Set beside kale, which spans 25°C from 5-30°C, this is extremely narrow. Eggplant is a crop that picks its season.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Continuous-cropping tolerance D is the heaviest item when looking several years ahead. By RDA standards it is a break group needing 5-7 years of rest, and in this table it belongs to the group most affected by continuous cropping. The cause is bacterial wilt and Verticillium wilt. Both leave pathogens in the soil, neither can be cured with medicine, and once a plant is infected it simply dies. Where a field is too small to run a five-year rotation, eggplant can effectively become a crop that cannot be planted at all. Section 11 deals with this problem.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Fertilizer demand D and yield retention without synthetic inputs C are one and the same issue. Eggplant is a heavy feeder that needs nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium combined at about 40g per m², and cutting off chemical fertilizer drops the yield to 40.8% — less than half. Making fruit continuously for four months requires that much nutrition to keep flowing in, and if the flow breaks, the fruit gets smaller, its color fades, and its skin toughens. This is the exact opposite position from beans, which supply their own nitrogen through root nodules. Section 5 covers how to build this flow.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Of the 130-150 days behind speed to harvest C, 60-80 days are spent raising seedlings. This seedling stage is especially difficult in a disaster situation. The optimal germination temperature for eggplant seed is 28-30°C, and outdoor temperatures in South Korea in late February come nowhere close. Without an electric seedling heater, alternatives such as a hotbed heated by fermentation or a south-facing indoor windowsill have to be used, and if even those fail, there is no eggplant crop that year. Section 6 deals with this problem.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Storability D follows directly from the fact that 92% of the fruit is water. It does not last a week at room temperature, does not keep long even with refrigeration, and cold storage actually causes chilling injury — pitting of the skin. Section 15's drying method addresses this weakness head-on.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Non-food value E means eggplant meets none of the eight criteria. It is not a fiber, medicinal, feed, fuel, or dye crop. Eggplant has no use beyond eating. Its stems and leaves are thick and plentiful enough, however, to be worth turning into compost once harvest is over.
