## 8. Crop Management - Side Shoots and Renewal Pruning

### 8-1. Training - Why Grow Three Stems

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;At the crotch, eggplant forks in two, and each of those stems forks in two again. Left alone, it multiplies into 4, 8, then 16 branches. As the stems increase, so do the leaves, so total photosynthesis rises — but the nutrients reaching any one fruit fall. The result is a mass of small fruit with seeds forming early inside them. On top of that, the interior grows so dense that light and air can no longer get in.

| Step | Details |
|---|---|
| 1. Locate the crotch | Find the point where the stem forks in two at the spot the first flower opened |
| 2. Remove side shoots below it | Snap off by hand every side shoot that grows out below the crotch |
| 3. Select the main stems | To the 2 stems forking at the crotch, add the strongest side shoot just below it, growing the plant as 3 stems |
| 4. Ongoing care | Let these 3 stems keep forking further, but thin out only the branches that grow inward and overlap |
| 5. Clearing lower leaves | Once harvest begins, remove leaves within 30cm of the ground one by one, in sequence |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Removing the side shoots below the crotch matters especially. These shoots carry almost no flowers while still drawing off nutrients, and growing dense near the ground, they block airflow. Leaves touching the soil become a direct channel for pathogens carried up by splashing rain. Snapping off a side shoot by hand while it is still fingernail-sized leaves the smallest wound; cutting it with a knife after it has thickened lets disease enter through that cut.

| Item | Technique |
|---|---|
| Time to remove | Morning on a clear day | 
| Reason | The wound dries within the day. Removing shoots on a rainy day lets bacteria enter through the wound |
| Tool | By hand if at all possible. If a knife is used, heat it over a flame or dip it in boiling water between plants |
| Reason | Bacterial wilt and viruses travel from plant to plant on a blade |

### 8-2. The Three-Leaf Rule

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Roughly three leaves are needed to properly grow a single eggplant fruit: one above it and two below feed that fruit through photosynthesis. Fewer leaves than this makes the fruit small; more, and the extra leaves only cast shade. This is why the technique arose of removing two or three leaves beneath a fruit together with the fruit at harvest. Those leaves have already finished their job and are now only obstacles shading the fruit that will grow next.

> **Removing leaves from eggplant does not harm the plant — it redirects its nutrients. Removing too many at once, however, collapses its photosynthetic capacity itself, so never take more than 20% of the total leaves at one time.**

### 8-3. Staking and Tying

| Method | Details | Characteristics |
|---|---|---|
| Single-stake tying | Set one stake per plant and tie the main stem to it | Simplest, but branches spreading sideways sag |
| Two-line tying | Set stakes on both sides of the row and run two tiers of line between them, enclosing the plants | Spreads the stems apart, improving airflow |
| X-stake tying | Cross two 1.8m stakes and lay a crossbar at the point where they meet | Good airflow and support together, and the stems spread apart |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Staking is not only about preventing the plant from falling over. The bigger purpose is spreading the stems outward so light and air can reach inside the plant. Once fruit begins to set, a plant's weight increases greatly, and summer downpours and typhoons can split the stems; a split becomes an entry point for disease. When tying, loop the string in a figure-eight around the stem and the stake so the stem is not pinched, and leave slack since it will thicken as it grows. Tying twine can be replaced with straw rope, kudzu vine, or paper mulberry bark.

### 8-4. Renewal Pruning - How to Get an Autumn Crop

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;From late July to early August, eggplant's vigor drops noticeably. There are two causes: one is that pollen loses its power above 35°C and fertilization fails; the other is that two months of nonstop fruiting have exhausted the roots and stems. Left as it is at this point, the plant heads into autumn lush with leaves but bearing no fruit.

| Step | Details |
|---|---|
| Timing | Late July-early August, once the monsoon ends and vigor breaks |
| Cutting the stems | Cut each main stem back, leaving only two or three nodes from the bottom. A bud must remain below the cut |
| Root pruning | Drive a spade in 20cm deep, 25-30cm from the plant, to cut the old roots |
| Top-dressing | Add compost and diluted urine into the cut and cover with soil |
| Watering | Water generously, then keep it from drying out afterward |
| Recovery | New shoots emerge and grow within 2-3 weeks, and fruit sets again from mid-September |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Root pruning may sound strange. Roots are cut on purpose to induce new ones. Old roots have lost much of their absorptive capacity, and vigorous new roots grow out from the cut. Since more than half the leaves and stems have already been cut away, the burden the roots have to carry is also reduced. Adding top-dressing and water at this point makes the plant build new stems and new roots at the same time — in effect, a person is artificially creating a second spring.

> **The old saying that autumn eggplant tastes best is not sentiment but physiology. As temperatures fall, fruit grows more slowly, and slowly grown fruit has densely packed cells, giving tender flesh and few seeds. Renewal pruning is the preparation for getting that autumn eggplant.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Renewal pruning does have conditions, though. First, at least two months must remain before frost; cutting past mid-August in central South Korea brings frost before the new shoots can set fruit. Second, the plant must not already be diseased — a plant with bacterial wilt or Verticillium wilt will not recover from cutting and simply dies. Third, water and fertilizer must be available to follow up; cutting and then leaving the plant alone prevents recovery. If even one of these three is not met, it is better to leave the plant unpruned.

## 9. Pests and Diseases - Managing Without Chemicals

### 9-1. Major Diseases

| Disease | Symptoms | Conditions | Response Without Chemicals |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bacterial wilt | Wilts suddenly at midday and revives by morning, repeating for several days before dying. Cutting the stem releases a milky white bacterial ooze | Hot and humid; soil-borne | No cure. Pull up and burn the whole plant, roots included, as soon as it is found, and remove the soil from that spot too. 5-7-year rotation |
| Late blight | The stem base touching the ground turns dark brown, pinches in, and the plant wilts rapidly | Excess moisture, poor drainage, monsoon | Raised beds and drainage channels are the only real defense. Preventive spraying with Bordeaux mixture |
| Verticillium wilt | Only one half of the leaf turns yellow and wilts | Cool and humid; soil-borne | No cure. Remove infected plants and rotate over the long term |
| Gray mold | Gray mold grows on flowers or wounds, and fruit turns soft | Cool and humid, poor airflow | Shake off spent flowers and ensure airflow |
| Leaf mold | Gray-brown mold appears on the underside of leaves, and the top side yellows | Humid, poor airflow, excess nitrogen | Clear lower leaves; avoid excess nitrogen |
| Powdery mildew | White powder covers the leaf surface | When dry and humid conditions alternate | A dilution of 1 part water to 1-2 parts milk; diluted baking soda |
| Viral disease | Leaves become mottled and curled, and fruit is deformed | Spread by aphids and thrips | No cure is possible. Block the carrier insects and remove infected plants |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Bacterial wilt deserves separate understanding. Its cause is a bacterium, Ralstonia solanacearum, which survives in the soil for years. It enters through root wounds, blocks the xylem, and once the xylem is blocked the plant cannot draw up water and wilts. This is where the characteristic symptom comes from — wilting at midday and reviving by morning, repeated for several days. Because it is a bacterial disease rather than a fungal one, preventives like Bordeaux mixture do not work well against it, and once it has entered a plant there is no way to reverse it.

> **If a plant pulled up with bacterial wilt is left in the field or put into the compost pile, the bacteria spread across the whole field. Pull it up and burn it, or bury it deep, far from the field. Always wash your hands and tools afterward.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Pest-control self-reliance C in the evaluation table reflects this situation. Even with treatments available, there is nothing to use against bacterial wilt or Verticillium wilt, and without treatments the losses from the other diseases grow larger too. The estimated yield loss without pest control, 49.8%, means losing about half if nothing is done, and that figure should be taken at face value. Growing eggplant without chemicals means planting on the assumption that half will be lost, so the numbers only work out if twice the needed amount is planted.

### 9-2. Major Pests

| Pest | Damage | Response Without Chemicals |
|---|---|---|
| Aphids | Cling to the underside of leaves and new shoots, sucking sap and spreading viruses | Soapy-water spray; crush by hand; protect ladybugs and lacewings |
| Two-spotted spider mite | Clings to the leaf underside sucking sap, turning leaves white with fine webbing | Spray water hard onto the leaf underside. Populations explode in dry conditions, so increase watering |
| Thrips | Cling to flowers and young fruit, scarring them, and spread viruses | Yellow and blue sticky traps; straw mulch to block pupae from moving |
| Tobacco cutworm | Larvae bore into the fruit and feed inside it | Pick and dispose of fruit with holes bored into it. Remove larvae by hand |
| Stink bugs | Suck sap from the fruit, leaving the spot hard and misshapen | Catch by hand in early morning when they are sluggish |
| Flea beetles | Chew small holes in the leaves of young seedlings | Cover with netting early after transplanting |

### 9-3. Homemade Treatments Without Chemicals

| Treatment | How to Make | Use | Caution |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bordeaux mixture | Dissolve 1kg copper sulfate and 1kg quicklime separately in water, let cool, combine, and make up to 100L with water | Prevents late blight and bacterial spot | A preventive, not a cure. Using it too concentrated in hot weather burns leaves |
| Soapy water | Dissolve 20g of unscented, additive-free soap in 1L of water | Aphids and mites | Only effective where it touches the leaf underside. Spraying at midday burns leaves |
| Diluted milk | Mix milk and water 1:1 to 1:2 | Powdery mildew | As it dries, it physically pins the fungal threads down |
| Wood ash | Lightly dust sifted ash onto leaves and soil | Repels pests; supplies potassium | Washes away when it rains |
| Wood vinegar | Cool and collect the smoke from charcoal-making, then dilute it 300-500× | Repellent; activates soil microbes | The undiluted liquid is strongly acidic, so it must always be diluted |
| Tobacco leaf or chili steep | Chop finely, soak in water for a day, then strain and use | Repels soft-bodied insects | Also irritating to people. Do not use just before harvest |

### 9-4. Cultural Control - Prevention Through Planting Practices

| Method | Principle |
|---|---|
| Rotation | Cycling Solanaceae crops on a 5-7-year interval lowers the density of soil-borne pathogens. The most reliable measure |
| Raised beds | Keep water from pooling in the root layer, reducing late blight and root rot |
| Wide plant spacing | Secures airflow, removing the very conditions fungal diseases need |
| Straw mulch | Stops splashing rain from carrying soil-borne pathogens onto the lower leaves |
| Clearing lower leaves | Removes leaves near the ground, cutting off the infection route |
| Burning diseased material | Infected debris left in the field becomes next year's source of infection |
| Healthy seedlings | Do not plant seedlings that were already diseased at the seedling stage |
| Tool disinfection | Heat knives and shears over a flame or dip them in boiling water between plants |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Of these eight, rotation and drainage practically do the largest share of the work. The rest are means of trimming losses bit by bit, while these two remove the very conditions a disease needs to take hold. This means that growing eggplant without chemicals requires putting effort into layout rather than treatment.

## 10. Nutrient Deficiency and Excess

| Symptom | Cause | Action with Natural Materials |
|---|---|---|
| Yellowing starting from the lower leaves and spreading over the whole plant, which stays small | Nitrogen deficiency | Dilute urine 10-15× and pour into the furrow, or work rotted compost around the base of the plant |
| Leaf edges scorch brown, and fruit stays small | Potassium deficiency | Mix a handful of wood ash per plant into the soil. Fruit-peel compost is also rich in potassium |
| Slow growth, with the underside of leaves and the stem turning purplish | Phosphorus deficiency | Add bone meal or wood ash. In cold weather, cold soil can sometimes block uptake regardless |
| Plenty of flowers but no fruit sets, and flowers simply drop | Boron deficiency or heat stress | Diversify the materials going into compost. If heat is the cause, lower it with shading |
| Yellowing between the veins while the veins themselves stay green | Magnesium deficiency | Dolomite powder, or diversify the materials going into compost |
| New leaves are small and malformed, and the growing point dies | Calcium deficiency | Burned, ground eggshell or shell powder. Usually calcium simply failed to move due to a lack of water |
| Leaves grow large and lush, deep green, but flowers are few | Excess nitrogen | Cut off the nitrogen supply and top up potassium with wood ash. Water generously to flush it out |
| Leaf edges curl inward and growth stops | Salt accumulation | Water generously to flush it out, and reduce fertilizer amounts the following year |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The most common problem in eggplant is excess nitrogen. Urine and compost are relatively rich in nitrogen but low in phosphorus and potassium, so feeding only these leads to lush leaves but few flowers. If the leaves are deep green and thick, the stem is thick, but flowers are scarce, this is almost certainly the case. Adding more nitrogen at this point only makes it worse. The answer is to add potassium and phosphorus with wood ash and bone meal to restore the balance.

> **When calcium-deficiency symptoms appear, it is rare for the soil to actually lack calcium. Calcium is an element that moves with water, so when water is short, it cannot reach new leaves and fruit even if the soil holds plenty of it. Most symptoms that look like calcium deficiency are actually water shortage.**

## 11. Intercropping and Crop Rotation

### 11-1. Companion Compatibility

| Compatibility | Partner | Reason |
|:---:|---|---|
| Good | Legumes (kidney beans, cowpeas, peas) | Nitrogen fixed by root-nodule bacteria remains in the soil |
| Good | Marigold | A substance released from its roots suppresses root-knot nematodes |
| Good | Basil, perilla (chajogi) | Their strong scent confuses aphids and thrips |
| Good | Welsh onion, garlic | Sulfur compounds lower the density of soil pathogens |
| Bad | Tomato, chili pepper, potato (Solanaceae) | Share pests and diseases wholesale. Avoid planting even next to them |
| Bad | Maize | Casts shade and competes heavily for nutrients |
| Bad | Winter squash | Its vines cover the plants and block airflow |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Planting beans between eggplants is especially valuable in a disaster situation. Eggplant is a heavy feeder that consumes a lot of nitrogen, while beans make their own nitrogen through root nodules. The nitrogen beans make does not go straight to the eggplant that same year, but it remains in the soil the following year as the bean roots decompose. Beans are also low-growing, so they do not block eggplant's sunlight, and they cover the ground to suppress weeds. Climbing beans, however, will climb up the eggplant, so bush-type kidney beans or cowpeas are the better fit.

### 11-2. Crop Rotation

| Year | Crop | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| Year 1 | Eggplant | |
| Year 2 | Legumes (soybean, kidney bean, cowpea) | Restoring nitrogen; cutting off the food supply for Solanaceae pathogens |
| Year 3 | Leafy greens (napa cabbage, lettuce) or alliums (Welsh onion, garlic) | Different rooting depths use the soil layers evenly |
| Year 4 | Grain (maize, sorghum) or root vegetables (radish, carrot) | Returning organic matter; improving soil structure |
| Year 5 | Legumes or green manure | Restoring soil fertility |
| Year 6 onward | Eggplant resumes | |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The 5-7-year break period the evaluation table calls for is hard to keep on a small plot, since this cycle only works with five or more sections of field. There are three practical compromises.

| Method | Details | Limitation |
|---|---|---|
| Divide the plot finely | Split the field into 6 sections and move one section over each year | Yield varies unevenly since sun and water conditions differ by section |
| Shifting cultivation | Plant a few plants each year in a different spot, along a field edge or around the house | Care becomes scattered and the harvest spread thin |
| Soil replacement | Remove 30cm of soil from a badly diseased spot and refill with new soil | Heavy labor, and a source of soil is needed |

> **Plant eggplant in the same spot for three years or more, and it is common for the first two years to look fine, only for half the plants to suddenly wilt and die in the third. Soil pathogens build up quietly and then break out all at once. Rotation is not something you start after disease appears — it is something you do before it appears.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;What to plant after eggplant is also worth thinking through. Since eggplant draws heavily on nutrients for four months, the crop that follows needs to be able to withstand that depletion. This is why legumes are the first candidate, and buckwheat and sorghum, which grow even in poor soil, are the second. Planting another heavy feeder right after eggplant, such as napa cabbage or regular cabbage, on the other hand, leaves both crops unsatisfying. Chopping up the stems and leaves after clearing the eggplant and tilling them into that spot turns them into organic matter for the following year — but diseased plants must always be excluded, leaving only healthy ones to go in.
