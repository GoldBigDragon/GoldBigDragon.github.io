## 7. Water Management

### 7-1. Standards by Growth Stage

| Stage | Management | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Sowing to germination | Keep the soil from drying out | The kernels are large and take up a lot of water |
| Seedling stage | Moderate. Avoid excess moisture | Encourages the roots to go deep |
| Vegetative growth stage | Plenty | Stems and leaves grow rapidly |
| Silking and flowering | Absolutely plenty | These 2 weeks decide the whole yield |
| Grain-filling stage | Plenty | The stage when starch fills the kernels |
| Maturity stage | Cut off | Makes drying after harvest easier |
| Monsoon season (jangma, late June to July in South Korea) | Draining water is the management | 4 days of flooding wrecks the yield |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Maize's water requirement is greater than that of other grains. Its total evapotranspiration of 550mm is more than double that of sorghum. South Korea's summer rainfall generally covers this through natural precipitation, but in years when the monsoon ends early and heat waves continue, it falls short.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;When water is limited, it should be concentrated at silking. If water is cut off during the 2 weeks when maize silks emerge to receive pollen, the silks come out late and are out of step with the pollen. Pollen is all shed within a few days, so if the silks are late the result is an empty ear. This loss cannot be recovered no matter how much water is applied later. Drought in the vegetative stage ends with shorter plants, but drought at silking wipes out the harvest itself.

### 7-2. Symptoms of Water Shortage and Excess

| Symptom | Cause | Judgment and action |
|---|---|---|
| Leaves curl inward at midday | Temporary water shortage | If they flatten out again in the evening, it is within the normal range |
| Leaves are still curled in the morning | Real drought | If it is the silking stage, irrigate immediately |
| Silks do not emerge or come out very late | Drought or heat at silking | Cannot be recovered |
| Kernels are missing at the tip of the ear | Water shortage or pollen shortage | Revise next year's layout and irrigation plan |
| Leaves turn pale yellow and dry up from the lower leaves | Flooding or nitrogen deficiency | Dig up the base and tell them apart by root color |
| Leaves take on a purple tinge | Impaired phosphorus uptake in cold weather | Recovers when temperatures rise |
| The base turns soft and the plant falls over | Root rot from flooding | Drain immediately and hill up |

> **Maize is a crop that uses a lot of water and at the same time collapses the moment it is submerged. Both supplying and draining water are needed.**

## 8. Crop Management

### 8-1. Weeding - The Biggest Cause of Maize Loss

| Timing | Work | Key point |
|---|---|---|
| 2 weeks after emergence | First weeding | Pull weeds while they are young |
| 5-7 leaf stage | Second weeding, hilling and top-dressing | Do all three jobs at once |
| After knee height | As needed | Maize casts shade and suppresses weeds |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Of maize's potential loss without pest control of 68.5%, 40.3 percentage points come from weeds. Weeds are a far larger source of loss than pests and diseases. This is good news under conditions without pesticides, because weeds are a problem solved not by chemicals but by human hands. Weeding diligently alone can prevent most of maize's loss.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Timing matters, however. The period before maize passes knee height, that is, 30-45 days after sowing, is when competition with weeds is fiercest. After that, the broad leaves of maize cover the ground and weeds cannot grow. It is efficient to concentrate labor in this one month.

### 8-2. Hilling

| Timing | Work | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| 5-7 leaf stage | Gather soil around the base to a height of 20cm | Brace roots sink into the soil and lodging is greatly reduced |
| Knee height | One more time if needed | Helps root development and improves drainage |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Hilling is an especially valuable job in maize. For a crop that reaches 2-3m in height to withstand a typhoon, its roots must be firmly anchored, and in maize the brace roots that emerge from the base of the stem play that role. Hilling soil up so that these brace roots sink well into the ground noticeably reduces lodging. At the same time the furrows between rows deepen and drainage improves. It is a job that raises both yield and stability without costing anything.

### 8-3. Other Management

| Item | Details |
|---|---|
| Side shoots (tillers) | Removing them does not increase yield, so leave them |
| When side shoots appear | They come out when the plant population is low or fertilizer is plentiful |
| Hand pollination | If kernel set is poor, shake the tassels to drop pollen |
| Bird damage | Birds peel back the husks and eat the kernels. Cover ears with netting or paper bags when they are ripening |
| After lodging | Stand the plants up and press soil around the base. Ears that touch the ground rot |
| Removing lower leaves | Do not do it. It reduces the photosynthetic area |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Hand pollination is a useful technique in a small field. When maize is planted in few rows or windless days continue, pollen does not settle properly. After the morning dew has dried, shaking the tassels by hand, or cutting off a tassel and shaking it over the silks of the plants below, noticeably improves kernel set. It is best to do it several times over a few days, because the silks do not all emerge at once.

## 9. Pests and Diseases

### 9-1. Major Diseases

| Disease | Conditions | Symptoms | Response without pesticides |
|---|---|---|---|
| Smut | Hot and humid conditions, wounded sites | White galls form on the ears or stalks and burst into black powder | As soon as found, cut off before they burst and burn them; improve drainage; rotate crops |
| Southern leaf blight (sesame-spot disease) | Hot and humid conditions | Spots the size of sesame seeds form on the leaves | Ensure airflow, remove and burn diseased leaves, rotate crops |
| Stalk rot | Water contaminated with fungi or bacteria | The inside of the stalk turns soft and the plant falls over | Switch to furrow irrigation and reduce water splash |
| Root rot | Excess moisture, prolonged flooding | Roots rot black | Nothing can be done except improve drainage |
| Black-streaked dwarf | Virus carried by the small brown planthopper | Leaves become stiff and plants become stunted | Control the vector, remove weeds around the field |
| Sheath blight | Dense planting, high humidity | The leaf sheaths turn soft and dry up | Secure planting spacing, drainage |
| Ear rot | Rain during grain filling | Mold grows on the ears | Early harvest, husk and dry the ears, discard diseased ears |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The most dangerous in a disaster is ear rot. The problem is toxicity more than yield loss. Some of the molds that grow on the ears produce toxins such as aflatoxin and fumonisin, and these toxins do not disappear with heating. Aflatoxin in particular is a powerful liver toxin and is carcinogenic. The hungrier people are, the more they are tempted to eat moldy grain out of reluctance to waste it, so this decision needs to be settled in advance.

> **Moldy maize does not become safe by washing, boiling, or roasting. Discarding it is the only way, and do not feed it to livestock either.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Prevention depends on drying. If rain continues late in grain filling, mold has already started on the ears. Maize is wrapped in husks and does not dry well once the inside gets wet, so it is more dangerous. In that case it is better not to wait until fully ripe but to pick, husk and dry the ears.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Smut is different in nature. If the white gall is cut off before it bursts into black powder, that is the end of it on the spot. After it bursts, the spores spread through the whole field and persist for several years. Going around the field to find the galls and remove them before they burst is the only practical response to this disease. Incidentally, in Mexico these galls are eaten as huitlacoche, but unless you can tell them apart with certainty, it is safer not to eat them.

### 9-2. Major Pests

| Pest | Timing | Damage | Response without pesticides |
|---|---|---|---|
| Asian corn borer | Late May-early October, 3 generations a year | Larvae eat into leaves, the inside of stalks, and ears | Remove and burn damaged stalks, deal with crop residue after harvest, remove egg masses |
| Armyworm | Sudden outbreaks | Strips the leaves in no time | Catch by hand; harvest early if severe |
| Small brown planthopper | Growing season | Carries viruses | Remove weeds around the field |
| Aphids | Growing season | Suck sap and invite sooty mold | Spray soapy water, protect natural enemies |
| Birds | Grain-filling stage | Peel back the husks and eat the kernels | Cover with paper bags or old cloth, nets |
| Rats | Grain-filling stage and storage | Eat the ears and gnaw through storehouses | Clean up the field, use metal containers |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The Asian corn borer is maize's representative pest. It occurs three times a year in South Korea: the first generation flies from late May to early June, the second from early July to mid-August, and the third from mid-August to early October. Once larvae bore into the stalk, sprays do not reach them, so even in ordinary times control is timed to the moment the eggs hatch. Under conditions without pesticides, the only means available are physical ones.

| Method | Details | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Residue disposal | After harvest, gather the stalks and burn them or chop them finely and bury them | Eliminates the larvae that overwinter inside the stalks |
| Egg mass removal | Find the scale-like egg masses on the undersides of leaves and rub them off | Most effective because it is done before hatching |
| Removing damaged stalks | Cut and burn stalks that expel sawdust-like droppings | Eliminates the larvae inside |
| Adjusting sowing time | Sow to avoid the first-generation flight, or sow early to avoid it | Throws the generations and the growing season out of step |
| Protecting natural enemies | Do not kill parasitic wasps and spiders | Density declines gradually |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Of these, the most effective is disposing of residue after harvest. Corn borer larvae overwinter inside maize stalks and emerge the following spring. If cut stalks are left in the field, the larvae survive intact inside them and become the starting point of next year's population. Cutting and burning them, or, if burning is not possible, crushing them finely and burying them deep in the compost pile, kills most of them. It is a job that can cut next year's damage by half or more without a single drop of pesticide.

### 9-3. Natural Control Agents

| Material | How to make | Target | Caution |
|---|---|---|---|
| Soapy water | 5-10g of plain soap per 1L of water | Aphids, mites | Sprayed at midday it scorches the leaves |
| Wood ash | Sprinkle on the leaves and leaf axils on a dewy morning | Young larvae | Effective during corn borer hatching |
| Wood vinegar | Dilute 500-1,000 times | Fungal disease prevention, repellent | Too strong a mix harms the plants |
| Garlic and chili infusion | Chop finely, soak for a day and strain | Moth larvae, aphids | Irritating to human eyes too |
| Bordeaux mixture | Dissolve copper sulfate and quicklime in water | Fungal disease prevention | Possible only when the materials are available |

### 9-4. Five Principles of Pest Control Without Pesticides

| Principle | Details | Application to maize |
|---|---|---|
| Weeding | Eliminate weeds early | 40 percentage points of the loss come from weeds |
| Residue disposal | Clear the stalks after harvest | Eliminates the corn borer's overwintering sites |
| Drainage | Keep water from pooling | Removes the conditions for root rot and stalk rot |
| Crop rotation | Alternate with legumes | Gets recovery of soil fertility and lower disease levels together |
| Mixed planting | Plant mixed with other crops | Viral disease fell by 72% when mixed with paprika |

## 10. Nutrient Deficiency and Excess

| Symptom | Cause | Remedy with natural materials |
|---|---|---|
| Lower leaves turn yellow in a V shape along the midrib | Nitrogen deficiency. The typical maize symptom | Urine diluted 10 times, well-rotted compost |
| Leaves take on a deep purple color | Phosphorus deficiency or cold | Bone meal, wood ash. If due to cold, recovers as temperatures rise |
| Leaf edges scorch | Potassium deficiency | Scatter wood ash between the plants |
| White stripes form on new leaves | Zinc deficiency. Common with excess phosphorus | Diversify the compost materials |
| Only the areas between veins are yellow | Magnesium deficiency | Dolomite powder, diversified compost |
| No kernels at the tip of the ear | Shortage of nutrients or water at silking | Move top-dressing earlier and increase irrigation |
| Tall but with dark blue-green leaves and small ears | Excess nitrogen | Rare in maize, but appears in extreme cases |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Nitrogen deficiency symptoms in maize are characteristic and easy to recognize. They start at the tip of the lower leaves and spread inward in a yellow V shape along the midrib. This is because maize pulls scarce nitrogen out of the lower leaves and sends it to the new leaves above. If you see this symptom, apply diluted urine or well-rotted compost as top-dressing immediately, and if it is before silking, recovery is still possible.

## 11. Intercropping and Crop Rotation

### 11-1. The Three Sisters Method - The Core of Disaster Maize Growing

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There is a traditional answer to the problem of maize exhausting the soil. It is a method Native Americans have used for hundreds of years, in which maize, beans and squash are planted together in one spot.

| Crop | Role | Specific action |
|---|---|---|
| Maize | Support | Tall, it becomes a pole for climbing beans |
| Beans | Nitrogen supply | Root-nodule bacteria (rhizobia) fix nitrogen from the air and put it into the soil |
| Squash | Ground cover | Its broad leaves cover the ground, suppress weeds and prevent evaporation |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This combination has three kinds of value in a disaster. First, from one piece of land you obtain carbohydrate (maize), protein (beans) and vitamins (squash) at the same time. Second, beans return the nitrogen that maize took, easing the drain on soil fertility. Third, squash covers the ground and weeding labor falls greatly. Considering that weeds are the biggest cause of maize loss, this effect alone makes it worthwhile.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;More important is nutritional complementarity. Maize is short of lysine and tryptophan, and beans are rich in both. Eating only maize leads to pellagra, but eating it together with beans does not. That people in Central and South America have eaten maize and beans together is no coincidence but wisdom created by thousands of years of experience.

| How to plant | Details |
|---|---|
| Order | Plant maize first, beans when it reaches knee height, and squash after that |
| Spacing | One or two bean seeds between maize plants, squash sparsely at the edge of the ridge |
| Bean variety | Climbing beans are suitable. They climb up the maize |
| Caution | Beans planted too early twine around the maize and pull it over |

### 11-2. Compatibility

| Compatibility | Partner | Effect |
|:---:|---|---|
| Very good | Beans, adzuki bean, cowpea | They fix nitrogen and share it, and complement the nutrition |
| Very good | Squash | Covers the ground and reduces weeds and evaporation |
| Good | Chili pepper, paprika | The shade of maize reduced viral disease by 72% |
| Good | Potato (preceding crop) | The growth periods are offset |
| Poor | Sorghum, foxtail millet | Being in the same grass family, they share pests and diseases and compete heavily for nutrients |
| Poor | Continuous maize cropping | Soil fertility is exhausted and corn borers accumulate |

### 11-3. Rotation Order

| Year | Crop | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| Year 1 | Maize (mixed with beans) | Secures maximum calories |
| Year 2 | Sole legume crop (soybean, adzuki bean, cowpea) | Restores soil fertility |
| Year 3 | Leafy or root vegetable | Uses the remaining nutrients |
| Year 4 | Grain (sorghum, foxtail millet, oat) | A buffer before returning to maize |
| Year 5 | Return to maize | Fertility and disease levels have recovered |

> **Continuous maize cropping shows no sign of trouble for the first two years and then collapses in the third. Rotation with legumes is not an option but a prerequisite.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The Continuous-cropping tolerance A in the evaluation table is a value for the pest and disease side. On the soil fertility side, maize is one of the crops least suited to continuous cropping. On land with originally thick organic matter, such as the black soils of the US Midwest, continuous cropping is possible, but the upland field soils of South Korea have no such margin. This is all the more so without chemical fertilizer.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;If land is too small for a four-year rotation, at minimum keep to a two-year rotation, alternating maize and beans every year. Adding to this the planting of beans together with maize in the Three Sisters method means nitrogen is replenished to some degree even in the year maize is planted. Combining these two is the realistic way to keep growing maize under conditions where fertilizer is scarce.
