## Introduction

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Maize (Zea mays) originated in Central America. It is the product of thousands of years of human selection acting on teosinte, a wild grass from Mexico, and the extent of that transformation has no parallel in crop history. A grass that once bore only a few seeds per ear became a grain bearing hundreds. In exchange, maize became a plant that cannot survive without people: its husk-wrapped seeds cannot scatter on their own. Maize exists only because people plant it, and that fact explains about half of its character.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Under disaster conditions, maize's greatest strength is calories per area: 600kg from 1,000m², or 2,148kcal/m², which is 0.82× the self-sufficiency line. Except for potatoes, this is the highest level on this list, and it is overwhelming among grains. Meeting one person's annual calorie needs from maize alone takes only 120m², or 36 pyeong (about 119 m²). On top of this, the storability of dried kernels is SS, exceeding 3 years at room temperature; seed can be obtained anywhere across South Korea; and the taste is not off-putting. When the goal is to extract as many calories as possible from a small plot, no other grain can replace maize.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The cost is high, though. First, maize is a heavy feeder: it requires 40g/m² of nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium (N+P+K) combined, more than twice as much as sorghum and over three times as much as foxtail millet. Once chemical fertilizer is gone, this demand has to be met with compost and diluted urine, and simply producing enough of them is heavy labor in itself — the reason zero-input yield retention drops to D. Second, maize depletes soil fertility heavily; a field planted with maize year after year turns visibly poor within just a few years. Third, seed saving is difficult: as a cross-pollinating crop it needs an isolation distance of at least 200m, and much of the seed sold commercially is F1, so replanting saved seed collapses the harvest through inbreeding depression. Fourth, maize is extremely vulnerable to shade and frost.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The overall grade is B, and both the short-term and long-term scores are also B. This is a flat profile, tilted neither toward the short term like potatoes nor toward the long term like sorghum. What this flatness means is clear: maize pulls its weight in any phase once the right conditions are met, but meeting those conditions is far harder than for other crops. This document focuses on how to meet them — in particular, intercropping with legumes to protect soil fertility, removing and grinding kernels without tools, drying to a moisture content of 13-14%, and the alkaline treatment (nixtamalization) and pellagra problem that anyone relying on maize as a staple food must understand.
