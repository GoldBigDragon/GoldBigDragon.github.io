## 15. Storage Without Refrigeration

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The Storability of maize is SS. In the 1,577 storage-stage observations of the FAO Food Loss Index, the median loss rate is 3.00%, and dried grain lasts more than 3 years at room temperature. However, this grade applies only to completely dried grain. Green maize has a limit of three days at room temperature, and 1-2 weeks even with refrigeration. For the same crop, that is a thousandfold difference in storage time, and what makes that difference is drying.

### 15-1. The Three Enemies of Storage

| Enemy | Conditions | Result | Axis of response |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mold | Moisture of 15% or more | Aflatoxin production, discard all of it | Drying |
| Insects | Temperature of 20°C or more, moisture of 12% or more | Eat out the kernels, leaving only powder | Airtight sealing and low temperature, control |
| Rats | Accessible containers | The kernels are large, so they eat them especially readily | Physical barrier |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Rats are especially fond of maize because the kernels are large. Sacks and plastic are easily gnawed through, and once there is a hole, moisture and insects come in through it together. This is why metal tins and earthenware jars are worth more for storing maize than for other grains.

### 15-2. Storage Conditions

| Condition | Target | When it is missed |
|---|---|---|
| Moisture | 13-14%, 12-13% for seed | Above 15%, the aflatoxin risk rises sharply |
| Temperature | 15°C or less is ideal, as low as possible | Above 25°C, insects breed faster |
| Humidity | 65% or less in the surrounding air | Dry grain absorbs moisture again |
| Light | Block it completely | Heat builds up and quality falls |
| Form | On the cob or as grain | On the cob is better for ventilation, grain is better for space efficiency |
| Inspection | Once every two months | Finding problems early prevents loss |

### 15-3. Storing on the Cob and Storing as Grain

| Method | Advantages | Disadvantages | Suitable situation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Storing on the cob | Good ventilation, so less mold. The kernels are hard to spoil | Volume is three times or more. Rats can reach it easily | When there is plenty of space and drying is incomplete |
| Storing as grain | Small volume and easy to seal | If not completely dry, mold spreads rapidly | When complete drying has been confirmed |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The traditional method was to hang the ears whole. Braided and hung under the eaves or in a barn, they keep drying all winter, and when needed a few ears are taken down and shelled for use. The biggest advantage is that it is safe even if drying is not complete. The drawbacks are volume and rats.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The safest compromise is to split the two. Keep the portion to be eaten by spring hanging on the cob, and the portion that must last past summer into the next year is dried thoroughly, shelled, and put into airtight containers. In this way, what will be used soon keeps drying while it is used, and the long-term reserve is protected by complete sealing.

### 15-4. Storage Containers

| Container | Airtightness | Rodent-proof | Duration | Notes |
|---|:---:|:---:|---|---|
| Metal tin | Very good | Complete | 3 years or more | Best suited to maize |
| Earthenware jar (onggi) | Good | Complete | 2-3 years | Constant temperature, and rats cannot gnaw through |
| Glass jar | Very good | Complete | 3 years or more | Must be kept in a dark place |
| Thick plastic | Fair | Poor | 1 year | Rats gnaw through it easily |
| Straw sack (gamani) or burlap sack | Poor | Poor | 6 months | Defenseless against rats and insects |
| Hanging | None | Fair | 6 months-1 year | Good ventilation, so less mold |
| Underground storage pit | Good | Good | 2-3 years | Constant temperature. If water seeps in, everything is lost |
| Corn crib | None | Fair | 1 year | A traditional structure built with sparse slats for ventilation |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The corn crib is a structure long used in the United States and Latin America. The walls are built of sparse wooden slats for ventilation, the floor is raised off the ground, and tray-shaped guards are fitted on the posts so that rats cannot climb. Ears stacked in whole keep drying all winter while being stored. If you handle a large quantity of maize in a disaster, it is a facility worth trying to build, and with just planks and posts it can be put up in a day or two.

### 15-5. Controlling Insects in Storage - Without Pesticides

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The real chief enemy in grain storage is insects. Maize weevils, Indian meal moths and flour beetles are already present as eggs in grain right after harvest and hatch inside the storehouse. The maize weevil lays its eggs inside the kernel, so a kernel that looked sound on the outside gets a hole one day and an insect comes out. One insect becomes dozens in two months, and in half a year a whole container is filled with powder and shells.

| Method | Principle | Application | Effect |
|---|---|---|---|
| Complete sealing | Cutting off oxygen suffocates the insects | Fill the container to the brim and seal it | The most reliable. The less empty space, the better |
| Mixing in wood ash | The fine ash particles scratch the insects' exoskeleton and dehydrate them | Mix in 2-5% of the grain volume evenly | A long-proven method. Wash it off before eating |
| Diatomaceous earth | Fine silica shells damage the exoskeleton | Mix 1-3g into 1kg of grain | More effective than ash. Works only when dry |
| Fine sand or yellow loess | The particles fill the gaps and block the insects' movement | Put in as alternating layers | Maize kernels are large and leave many gaps, so it is very effective |
| Dried chili peppers | Capsaicin acts as a repellent | Put several in the container | Traditionally used for storing maize |
| Mugwort, sweet flag and Japanese pagoda tree leaves | Essential-oil compounds suppress egg-laying | Dry them and put them in between layers | A supplementary measure |
| Roasted salt | Holds moisture and removes the conditions insects need to survive | Wrap in cloth and put in | Doubles as a desiccant |
| Charcoal | Absorbs moisture and odors | Wrap baked, dried charcoal in cloth and put in | Also helps prevent mold |
| Cold treatment | Eggs and larvae die below freezing | Put outdoors for a few days in midwinter, then bring back in | Beware of condensation when bringing it back in |
| Heat treatment | Eggs and larvae die at 60°C | Spread thin in the sun for a few hours | Not used for seed |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The most important is the first one. Complete sealing is the most powerful measure that can be used even without pesticides. Insects breathe too, so when grain and insects consume the oxygen in a sealed container, carbon dioxide builds up, oxygen falls, and the insects die. The more completely you fill the container and minimize empty space, the faster the effect. Because maize kernels are large and leave a lot of air between them, the container must be packed more firmly than for other grains. After filling it to the brim, shake it to settle the grain and fill it again, repeating two or three times.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The principle of wood ash and diatomaceous earth is the same. Both kill physically rather than chemically. When fine, sharp particles scrape off the wax layer covering an insect's body, the insect loses water and dries out. This method has two advantages. Insects cannot develop resistance, and it is harmless to people. Just wash it off with water before eating. The drawback is that it does not work in the presence of moisture. If the grain turns damp, neither ash nor diatomaceous earth is effective, so in the end drying comes first.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;When using ash, sift it and use only the part as fine as wheat flour. Mix in 2 to 5% of the grain volume, that is, about one doe (about 1.8 L) of ash per mal (about 18 L) of maize, and turn it by hand so that a thin coat covers the surface of every kernel. Since maize kernels are large and their surface area relatively small, little ash is needed, and it is easy to wash off later. Moreover, in the case of maize this ash has another use: the ash used for alkali treatment when cooking is the same ash.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Dried chili peppers were also traditionally widely used for storing maize. Sticking several chilies in among the grain makes insects reluctant to approach. Alone their effect is limited, but used together with sealing or ash they are a worthwhile supplementary measure.

### 15-6. Regular Inspection

| When | What to check | Action if abnormal |
|---|---|---|
| Every two months | Condensation on the outside of the container, moldy smell | Open immediately and spread out to dry |
| Every two months | Small holes in the kernels, powder at the bottom | It is weevils. Dry everything in the sun and sift them out |
| Every two months | Cobweb-like threads on the surface | It is the Indian meal moth |
| Every two months | Warmth when held in the hand | Heating is a sign of excess moisture |
| Every two months | Signs of rats around the container | Check for droppings or gnaw marks |
| Spring and autumn | Open the whole thing and check the smell and color | If there is an odd smell, check it in portions |

## 16. Cooking and Processing

### 16-1. Alkali Treatment (Nixtamalization) - The Core of Cooking Maize

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;If you make maize your staple, this section is the most important in the whole document. It is a treatment in which dry maize is boiled in water containing ash or lime, and it solves three things at once.

| Effect | Details |
|---|---|
| Niacin release | Bound niacin changes to an absorbable form, preventing pellagra |
| Skin removal | The outer skin of the kernel softens and comes off when rubbed |
| Easier milling | The kernels soften and are ground far more easily with a maetdol (hand-turned stone mill) or mortar |

| Step | Details | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Measure 3 doe (about 1.8 L each) of water for every 1 doe of dry maize kernels | Make sure they are well covered |
| 2 | Add wood ash at about 5% of the maize volume | If quicklime is available, 1% is enough |
| 3 | Bring to a boil, then lower the heat and simmer thoroughly for 30-50 minutes | The kernels swell and the skins lift |
| 4 | Turn off the heat and leave overnight | This time is the heart of the alkali action |
| 5 | Pour off the water and rub by hand to remove the skins | The yellow skins come off |
| 6 | Rinse three or four times until the water runs clear | Wash off the ash and alkali completely |
| 7 | Cook as is, or pound into flour | If dried, it keeps for a long time |

> **This one step, boiling with ash, turns maize into a safe staple. It is the reason people in Central and South America stayed healthy for thousands of years eating nothing but maize.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There are several alkali materials that can be used. Wood ash from burning broadleaf trees is the easiest to obtain, and lime made by burning seashells or bones can also be used. If slaked lime or quicklime is available it works with the smallest amount, but if the amount is wrong it gives a bitter taste, so be careful. When using ash, add enough that the ash is visible to the eye, and when the cooking water feels slippery, the alkali is sufficient.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Treated maize can be eaten as porridge or rice as is, and when pounded and kneaded it becomes the dough for traditional maize pancakes. If dried it keeps for a long time, and when needed you only need to soak it in water and pound it. The only burden is fuel, but fuel can be saved by processing a large quantity at once and drying it.

### 16-2. Milling - Making Flour

| Method | Tool | Characteristics |
|---|---|---|
| Maetdol | Two stones, upper and lower | Produces the finest flour |
| Mortar | Mortar and pestle | Very hard work, because maize kernels are hard |
| Stone slab grinding | A flat stone and a rolling stone | Same principle as the metate of Central and South America |
| Pounding after alkali treatment | Any tool | The kernels soften and it is far easier |
| Pounding after roasting | Any tool | The tissue breaks down, so it is less work and tastes nutty |
| Soaking and grinding | Maetdol | Grinding with water produces dough right away |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Dry maize is among the hardest of grains, so pounding it as it is is extremely hard. If you try to pound it in a mortar, the kernels fly out and your arms tire first. That is why maize milling always requires pretreatment. Alkali treatment softens the kernels and cuts the effort to half or less, and roasting breaks down the tissue so it grinds much more easily. Soaking in water overnight and grinding with water in a maetdol is also good. Dough comes out immediately instead of dry flour, but this is no problem if it will be eaten on the spot.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;If you have no maetdol, you can make one. Pick two hard stones, chip the faces that meet to roughen them, drill a hole in the center of the upper stone so the grain can enter, and fit a handle. It works even if it is not precise. If the disaster looks likely to last, it is a tool well worth making in advance.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Flour spoils much faster than grain. This is especially so for maize flour, because the fat from the germ gets mixed in. Grind only as much as will be eaten in a month and keep the rest as grain.

### 16-3. Cooking That Saves Fuel

| Method | Fuel | Procedure | Notes |
|---|:---:|---|---|
| Boiling green maize | Little | Add just a little water and steam for 20 minutes | Steaming uses less fuel than boiling |
| Boiling dry grain | A lot | Soak overnight, then boil for more than 1 hour | Uses the most fuel |
| Cooking after alkali treatment | Medium | Already cooked during the treatment process | Efficient if processed all at once |
| Flour porridge | Very little | Stir flour into water and boil for 10 minutes | The most efficient |
| Pancakes | Little | Fry the dough thin | If thick, the inside does not cook |
| Residual-heat cooking | Very little | Boil for 10 minutes, turn off the heat, wrap in straw or a blanket and leave for 1 hour | Cuts fuel to half or less |
| Popcorn and puffed maize | Very little | Put in a heated pot, cover and shake | Takes a few minutes |
| Baking in ashes | Very little | Bury a lump of dough in hot ashes | Uses the leftover heat of other cooking |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Boiling dry maize grain as it is is the worst choice in terms of fuel. It must be boiled for more than an hour and still does not soften completely. Alkali treatment or pounding it into flour saves much more fuel. Flour porridge in particular takes only 10 minutes, making it the best form for eating maize when fuel is extremely scarce.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Popcorn is a surprisingly sensible way of cooking. If you have planted a popping variety (a maize variety used to make popcorn), just putting it in a heated pot, covering it and shaking it produces food that has swollen to dozens of times its volume in a few minutes. It uses almost no fuel, needs neither hulling nor milling, and can be eaten right away. Among maize, it is the form with the best cooking efficiency. However, popping varieties must be planted separately.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Heat-retention cooking is especially valuable with dry maize. If soaked grain is boiled for just 10 minutes, taken off the fire and wrapped whole in a box filled with straw or rice husks, or in a thick quilt, the internal temperature stays above 80°C for over an hour and cooking continues. What would have to be boiled for an hour is cut to 10 minutes.

### 16-4. Processing That Doubles as Storage

| Processing | Procedure | Storage | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Drying after alkali treatment | Dry the treated maize | Sealed, 1 year | Can be soaked in water and used right away |
| Roasted flour | Roast, then pound | Sealed, 3-6 months | Mix with water and eat right away |
| Maize starch | Soak, grind, let settle, and dry the sediment | Several years | Volume shrinks and insects do not get in |
| Dried green maize | Blanch, take the kernels off and dry them | 1 year | Soak in water and add to soup or porridge |
| Maize liquor | Mix nuruk (Korean fermentation starter) into alkali-treated maize and ferment | Stored sealed | Serves both calorie preservation and water disinfection |
| Maize jocheong (grain syrup) | Saccharify with malt (yeotgireum) and boil down | 1 year in a cool place | Provides sweetness and concentrated calories |
| Corn silk tea | Dry the silks and keep | Sealed, 1 year | Makes use of a part that would be thrown away |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Extracting maize starch is especially useful in a disaster, because every bit of kernels slightly eaten by insects, broken kernels and scraps that are hard to store can be turned into starch. Grind maize soaked overnight, wrap it in cloth and knead it in water, and white water comes out. Let it settle, discard the water on top, pour in fresh water, repeat three or four times, and dry the sediment that remains: that is maize starch. Volume and weight drop greatly, almost no insects get in, and it keeps for years. Stirred into boiling water it cooks immediately, so it needs almost no cooking fuel.

### 16-5. Using By-Products

| By-product | Use |
|---|---|
| Cob | Firewood with strong heat. The ash is potassium fertilizer. Crushed fine for compost |
| Husk | Substitute for string, material for straw sandals and mats, for wrapping dumplings or tteok (rice cake) |
| Stalks and leaves | Livestock forage, compost material. However, because of corn borers it is better to burn them |
| Silks | Dried and used as tea |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Maize was rated E for Non-food value, but in an actual disaster the usefulness of its by-products is not small. The cob in particular burns well and is invaluable as cooking firewood, and the ash it leaves is used as it is for alkali treatment and for insect protection in storage. Within the single crop of maize, grain, fuel, alkali and insect repellent all come out together.

## 17. Annual Work Calendar

| Month (South Korea) | Work | Key point |
|---|---|---|
| January | Inspect storage, check seed, maintain tools | Check for moldy smell and weevil holes |
| February | Plan the field, turn compost, germination test | Calculate how much compost to put on the maize field |
| March | Deep plowing (18cm or more), drainage ditches, base fertilizer, scattering wood ash | Hauling manure is the biggest job |
| April | Sowing (early April in southern South Korea, mid-April in central South Korea), staggered at 1-2 week intervals | Confirm a soil temperature of 12°C or more |
| May | Thinning (at 15cm height), first weeding, watch for first-generation corn borer | Weeds are the biggest cause of loss |
| June | Top-dressing at the 5-7 leaf stage, second weeding, hilling, sowing beans | Do three jobs at once |
| July | Concentrated irrigation at silking and flowering, hand pollination, second-generation corn borer | These 2 weeks decide the whole yield |
| August | Harvest green maize, manage grain filling, scare off birds | Green maize is 22-25 days after silking |
| September | Start of grain harvest, husk and hang to dry | Check the black layer, then pick |
| October | Finish harvest, drying, dispose of stalk residue | Burn the stalks to prevent corn borer overwintering |
| November | Threshing, final drying, storage, keep seed separately | Seal after confirming 13-14% moisture |
| December | Alkali treatment and milling, check storage, plan next year | Process only as much as will be eaten in a month |

### 17-1. Five Things Not to Forget

| Item | Details |
|---|---|
| Fertilizer | Maize is a heavy feeder. Plant without a compost plan and there will be no harvest |
| No continuous cropping | Rotation with legumes or the Three Sisters method is a prerequisite. It collapses in the third year |
| Layout | Do not plant in one long line; plant several rows in a block. This is because of wind pollination |
| Seed | F1 has no next year. Always secure open-pollinated varieties or landraces |
| Alkali treatment | Eating only maize brings pellagra. Boil with ash or eat with beans |
