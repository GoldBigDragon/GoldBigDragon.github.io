## 3. Growing Conditions

### 3-1. Thresholds and Optimal Values

| Condition | Optimal | Tolerable Range | Outside This Range |
|---|---|---|---|
| Growing temperature | 20-30°C | 12-40°C | Below 12°C growth stops, and frost kills the plant |
| Germination temperature | 32-34°C | minimum 8-11°C | At soil temperature 13°C, emergence takes 18-20 days; at 15-18°C, 8-10 days |
| Silking-stage temperature | 25-28°C | 35°C or below | Above 35°C pollen dies and ears come out empty |
| Grain-filling temperature | 20-25°C | 18-30°C, range 12°C | Excessive heat prevents kernels from filling properly |
| Sunlight | 8-10 hours of direct sun a day | 6 hours or more | At 40% shade, yield drops to 45% |
| Total water requirement | around 550mm | 400-700mm | Drought at silking is the most damaging |
| Soil | Well-drained sandy loam to loam | Most soil types | Aeration matters especially |
| Soil pH | pH 6.0-6.8 | pH 5.5-7.5 | Under strongly acidic conditions, phosphorus becomes locked up |
| Waterlogging | None | Up to 3 days | 4+ days of flooding reduces height by 12% and delays silking by 5 days |
| Frost | None | None | A single frost kills the plant |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;What stands out in this table is the range of germination temperatures. The optimum is 32-34°C, yet the minimum is 8-11°C. Germination still happens at low temperatures, but very slowly. At a soil temperature of 13°C, sprouting takes 20 days, and during those 20 days the seed is exposed to the risk of rotting in the soil or being eaten by insects. This means that sowing early is not necessarily an advantage.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The waterlogging entry is worth recording in detail, too. If maize sits in water for 4 days or more, height and stem thickness decrease and leaves age quickly. In poorly aerated soil, height drops by 12% and silking is delayed by 5 days; and once silking is delayed, it falls out of sync with pollen shed and the ear comes out empty. This is why drainage during the monsoon season (jangma, late June to July in South Korea) decides whether a maize crop succeeds or fails.

### 3-2. Choosing a Site Under Disaster Conditions

| Site | Suitability | Reason |
|---|:---:|---|
| South-facing gentle slope, sun all day | Best | As a C4 crop, sunlight directly determines yield |
| Deep, fertile flat field | Best | As a heavy feeder, naturally nutrient-rich land is an advantage |
| Nearby field where compost can be concentrated | Good | Reduces the labor of carrying fertilizer |
| Near livestock housing or a latrine | Good | Easy access to manure resources |
| Field converted from a rice paddy | Caution | Deep tillage of 18cm or more and drainage channels are essential |
| Poor, gravelly ground | Unsuitable | Better given over to sorghum or foxtail millet |
| Shaded location | Unsuitable | Shade damage is far greater than for other crops |
| Low ground where water pools | Unsuitable | 4 days of flooding collapses the yield |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The principle for allocating fields under disaster conditions is clear. Give the most fertile, sunniest land to maize, and turn poor, marginal land over to sorghum or foxtail millet. On good land, maize produces overwhelming calories, but on poor land it becomes merely average. Sorghum and foxtail millet, by contrast, hold their own even on poor land. Exploiting this asymmetry is the layout that maximizes the total harvest.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The distance for hauling fertilizer is also a practical consideration. Maize demands a large amount of compost, and compost is heavy. Without a cart, carrying a ton of compost to 1,000m² is days of labor. Simply assigning maize to a field near the compost pile or the livestock shed saves a considerable amount of work.

## 4. Varieties

### 4-1. Classification by Use

| Type | Characteristics | Use | Disaster Perspective |
|---|---|---|---|
| Waxy corn | Sticky and good for boiling | Eaten as fresh corn | South Korea's leading variety type. Widely liked |
| Sweet corn and super-sweet corn | High in sugar; can be eaten raw | Eaten fresh, right away | Hard to store; kernels shrivel |
| Grain and feed corn | Kernels are hard and starchy | Dried for storage, milled | Essential for long-term stockpiling and use as a staple |
| Popping corn (for popcorn) | Kernels are small and hard | Popped | Can be cooked with almost no fuel |

> **This is the fork in the road for disaster preparedness. Fresh-corn varieties are food for one summer season; only grain-type varieties become a stockpile that gets you through winter.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Missing this distinction causes trouble. Waxy corn and sweet corn are varieties meant to be eaten fresh, so once fully ripened their kernels shrivel and their starch content is too low to make good storage grain. Grain-type varieties, on the other hand, taste poor eaten fresh, but once left to fully ripen and dried, they become a grain that lasts 3 years. Maize's A grade for calories per area is a figure that assumes grain-type varieties.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The practical layout is to plant both. Plant some waxy corn to eat right away in summer, and fill the rest with grain-type corn set aside as a winter stockpile. However, if the two types are planted close together, cross-pollination will make the waxy corn lose its stickiness, so they need to be at least 200m apart or their sowing dates staggered by at least 3 weeks.

### 4-2. Comparison of Major Varieties

| Variety | Days to Maturity | Yield | Characteristics |
|---|:---:|:---:|---|
| Waxy corn | 80-90 days | Medium | South Korea's leading type; sticky texture |
| Super-sweet corn | 70-80 days | Medium | High in sugar and grows quickly |
| Godangok | About 80 days | High | 10 days shorter than waxy corn, allowing two crops a year |
| Grain & feed corn | 90-100 days | High | Hard kernels, ideal for dry storage |

### 4-3. Selection Criteria by Situation

| Situation | Recommendation | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Goal is long-term stockpiling | Grain & feed corn | Keeps 3+ years when dried |
| Securing summer food | Waxy corn | Can be boiled and eaten right away |
| Need a fast harvest | Super-sweet corn, Godangok | Ready to harvest in 70-80 days |
| Limited land available | Godangok | Allows two crops a year |
| Fuel is extremely scarce | Popping corn | Popping uses almost no fuel |
| Need to save your own seed | Landraces / open-pollinated varieties | F1 collapses the following year |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The last item is the most important. Much of the maize seed sold commercially is an F1 hybrid. F1 gives an excellent yield in its first year, but if you plant seed saved from it, inbreeding depression appears — height and ears become uneven, and the harvest falls to less than half. If you assume a situation where supply chains are cut and seed cannot be bought, securing landraces or open-pollinated varieties in advance is far safer, even if their yield is a bit lower. Landraces let you save seed and keep replanting them year after year.

## 5. Field Preparation and Fertilizer

### 5-1. Preparing the Ground

| Item | Details | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Tillage depth | 18cm or more, ideally 25-30cm | Roots must spread deep and wide to meet the plant's heavy nutrient demand |
| Drainage channels | Essential around the field and in the furrows | 4 days of flooding collapses the yield |
| Raised bed | 15-20cm high | Prevents flooding during the monsoon |
| Soil pH | pH 6.0-6.8 | Strong acidity locks up phosphorus |
| Organic matter | Compost 1,000-1,500kg/1,000m² | As a heavy feeder, maize needs a large amount |
| Lime | For correcting acidity | Wood ash can be used instead |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;What sets preparing a maize field apart most from other crops is the sheer amount of organic matter needed. As a heavy feeder, maize needs plenty of compost worked in, and that compost has to break down slowly and keep supplying nutrients into the later part of the growing season. When chemical fertilizer is available, compost is a supplement; when it is not, compost has to do everything. That is why preparing for a maize crop starts not in spring but in the autumn of the year before. When the compost pile was built decides next year's maize harvest.

### 5-2. Standard Fertilization Rates (per 1,000m²)

| Fertilizer | Rate | Timing |
|---|:---:|---|
| Nitrogen (N) | 10-15kg | 50% as base fertilizer, 50% as top-dressing |
| Phosphorus (P) | 8-10kg | All as base fertilizer |
| Potassium (K) | 8-10kg | Split between base fertilizer and top-dressing |
| Compost | 1,000-1,500kg | All as well-rotted base fertilizer |

| Type | Timing | Details |
|---|---|---|
| Base fertilizer | 1-2 weeks before sowing | All the compost, all the phosphorus, half the potassium, half the nitrogen |
| Top-dressing | 5-7 leaf stage | The remaining nitrogen and potassium. Combine with hilling |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Unlike other grains, maize loses out if you skimp on fertilizer. Sorghum and foxtail millet lodge if given more nitrogen, but maize's stem is thick and sturdy enough to handle a fair amount of nitrogen, and it pays that back in the ear. If there is any surplus fertilizer, directing it to maize increases the total harvest.

### 5-3. When There Is No Chemical Fertilizer - Disaster Substitute Fertilizers

| Substitute Resource | Nutrients Supplied | How to Make and Use It | Caution |
|---|---|---|---|
| Well-rotted compost | All-around, especially nitrogen | Layer fallen leaves, grass, and manure and let it rot for 3-6 months | For maize, quantity is what matters. Build up a large amount in advance |
| Diluted urine | Nitrogen, potassium | Dilute 10× with water and pour into the furrows at the 5-7 leaf stage | Becomes the main top-dressing |
| Wood ash | Potassium, phosphorus, calcium | Scatter 40-60kg over 1,000m² | Also corrects soil acidity |
| Bone meal | Phosphorus, calcium | Burn, crush, mix into compost, and let sit for two months | Breaks down slowly, so prepare it in advance |
| Legume green manure | Nitrogen | Turn under soybeans, Chinese milk vetch, or hairy vetch as a preceding crop | The most effective method for maize |
| Intercropping with soybeans | Nitrogen | Plant soybeans together between the maize plants | The core of the Three Sisters method |
| Livestock bedding | All-around | Use rotted bedding that mixes rice straw and manure | Avoid bedding with a lot of salt |
| Composted human waste | Nitrogen, phosphorus | Mix with sawdust or rice hulls and let it fully rot for 6 months or more | If undercomposted, parasites and pathogens remain |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The fertilizer problem is heavier for maize than for any other crop. Converting 10-15kg of nitrogen into compost comes to about 2 to 3 tons of well-rotted compost, and all of it has to go into a single 1,000m² plot. Realistically, securing this amount every year is difficult. That is why the strategy for feeding maize splits into three approaches.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;First, plant it together with soybeans. Some of the nitrogen fixed by the soybean's root-nodule bacteria flows to the maize through the soil. It cannot fill the gap completely, but it covers a good share of the shortfall. Second, plant a legume green manure as a preceding crop and turn it under when it flowers. Turning under hairy vetch or Chinese milk vetch supplies 8-12kg of nitrogen per 1,000m² — an amount that nearly meets maize's standard nitrogen requirement. Third, use urine as a top-dressing. One adult's urine contains 6-10g of nitrogen a day, so collecting a month's worth from a family of four yields enough nitrogen for a full top-dressing of 1,000m².

> **Half of growing maize happens not in the field but at the compost pile and in the green-manure plot. Planting maize without a fertilizer plan is only going through the motions of planting.**

## 6. Sowing

### 6-1. Timing

| Region | Optimal Sowing Time | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Southern South Korea | Early April | After the risk of frost has passed |
| Central South Korea | Mid-April | The benchmark is a soil temperature of 12°C or above |
| Second crop (double-cropping) | Early July | Use an early-maturing variety and harvest before frost |
| Sowing deadline | Late June | Later than this, and grain-filling runs into frost |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The benchmark for sowing time is soil temperature. It must exceed 12°C, and it is far better above 15°C. At 13°C, sprouting takes 20 days, but at 15-18°C it takes only 8-10 days. Sowing hastily and having the seed spend 20 days in the soil gives a worse result than waiting 1 more week and having it come up in 10 days.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In a disaster situation, it is better not to sow everything at once but to split sowing at 1-2 week intervals. This spreads out the harvest so you avoid the problem of not being able to process it all at once, and it also keeps a single typhoon or disease outbreak from wiping out the whole crop. In particular, whatever portion will be eaten as fresh corn must be sown in stages. If it all ripens at once, it all has to be eaten or thrown away within a few days.

### 6-2. Preparing Seed

| Item | Details | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Selection | Use the thick, even kernels from the middle of the ear | Kernels from the tip and base germinate unevenly |
| Water sorting | Put in water and remove any that float | Maize kernels are large enough to sort by eye, too |
| Ash coating | Coat kernels with wood ash | Disinfects and dries at the same time, and attracts fewer birds and ants |
| Pre-germination | Wrap in a damp cloth and let sit for a day to sprout | Speeds up germination. Take care not to damage the roots |
| Seeds per hole | 2 kernels | Insurance against poor germination |

### 6-3. Seed Disinfection - Without Chemicals

| Method | Procedure | Effect | Caution |
|---|---|---|---|
| Wood ash coating | Lightly moisten the seed with water, then coat it with fine ash | Disinfects the surface and also repels birds and ants | The most practical method |
| Hot-water soak | Soak in 50-55°C water for 10 minutes, then cool in cold water | Kills surface pathogens | Above 60°C the seed itself dies |
| Wood vinegar soak | Soak for 6-12 hours in a 500× dilution | Disinfects the surface and promotes germination | Undiluted, it kills the seed |
| Selecting healthy seed | Save seed only from plants that had no corn smut | The most fundamental method | Mark the plants during the growing season |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Coating with wood ash is especially useful for maize seed. Because the kernels are large, birds, mice, and ants often dig them up and eat them after sowing, but coating them with ash makes them less visible and less appealing, reducing the damage. It disinfects at the same time.

### 6-4. Planting

| Item | Standard | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Sowing depth | 3-5cm | Kernels are large, so they are planted deeper than other grains |
| Row spacing | 70cm | Wide enough to walk between rows for weeding and hilling |
| Plant spacing | 25cm | Too dense, and ears become smaller |
| Per hole | 2 kernels | Thinned to 1 plant later |
| Layout | Several rows grouped together rather than one long row | Improves wind pollination |
| After covering with soil | Press down gently | Brings the soil into close contact with the seed |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Layout matters especially. Because maize carries its pollen on the wind, planting it in one long row lets the pollen blow out of the field, leaving the ears sparse. Arranging at least four rows side by side in a block lets the plants pollinate each other, giving even kernel set. A common mistake when growing maize in a small garden is planting a single row along a fence, and the result is an ear with scattered, sparse kernels.

### 6-5. Thinning

| Timing | Task | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Height 15cm | Thin to leave only 1 plant | Cut at the base rather than pulling it out |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;When thinning, it is better to cut than to pull. Maize roots are tangled together, so pulling one plant out also disturbs the roots of the plant you mean to keep. Cutting close to the ground with a fingernail or a small knife leaves the remaining plant undamaged. Young maize plants that are removed can be used as livestock feed or compost material.
