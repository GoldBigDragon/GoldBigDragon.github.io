## 12. Harvest and Threshing

### 12-1. Two Kinds of Harvest - Green Maize and Grain

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Harvesting maize is completely different depending on the purpose. Green maize to be boiled and eaten must be picked while the kernels are still soft, while grain to be stored must be left until it is completely dry. The timing differs by more than a month.

| Purpose | Timing | Criterion |
|---|---|---|
| Green maize | 22-25 days after silking | A pressed kernel gives out white juice and the contents are cheese-like |
| Grain (mature kernels) | 50-60 days after silking | Kernels harden and a black layer forms at the base |

| Category | State | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| Early state | The contents of the kernel are watery | Still too early |
| Right time for green maize | Paste- or cheese-like contents, the variety's own color | Pick now |
| Overripe | The contents have hardened and the skin is tough | Too late for green maize. Turn it to grain |
| Right time for grain | A fingernail cannot dent it and the husk is bone dry | Harvest |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The right time for green maize is only a few days. Even a day or two late and the sweetness drops and the skin turns tough. So for green maize you must sow in batches at 1-2 week intervals, and picking in the early morning is best, because the sugar made overnight has not yet turned into starch.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The criterion for grain is the black layer. Remove one kernel and look at its base: a black dot forms where it was attached to the cob. Once this is visible, the kernels no longer grow larger even if left in the field. All that remains is drying, and that drying is safer under the eaves than in the field.

> **If rain continues during grain filling, pick the ears right after the black layer forms, husk them and dry them. Maize is wrapped in husks, so once wet it does not dry well.**

### 12-2. Harvesting Tips

| Step | Details | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Start on a clear morning | Wet ears are the starting point of mold |
| 2 | Bend the ear downward and twist it off | Pulling damages the stalk |
| 3 | Husk the ear but leave two or three layers | The remaining husks can be used to tie and braid the ears together |
| 4 | Sort out and discard ears with visible mold | Left mixed in, they contaminate the entire storehouse |
| 5 | Tie two or three ears together with the remaining husks | Preparation for hanging to dry |
| 6 | Hang them under the eaves or in a barn | A posture that allows airflow decides the drying |
| 7 | Gather and burn the stalks of the harvested field, or chop and bury them | Eliminates the corn borer's overwintering sites |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Leaving two or three layers of husk is a traditional method. Braiding or tying the remaining husks together strings several ears into one line and makes them easy to hang. The scene of maize bundles hung in rows under the eaves is just that, and this posture is the ideal drying form that satisfies ventilation and drainage at the same time. Using the maize's own husks as string, in a situation where string is scarce, is also an advantage.

### 12-3. Drying

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Everything about grain storage is decided by moisture. Above 15% moisture, mold grows, and below 13%, both mold and insects almost stop being active. Freshly picked maize has 25-35% moisture. Bringing this down to 13-14% is the one and most important task right after harvest. Maize kernels are large, so it takes longer to dry than other grains.

| Step | Method | Duration | Moisture reached |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Husking | Remove the husks, leaving only two or three layers | - | Changes the drying rate greatly |
| 2. Hang-drying | Hang the ears whole under the eaves or in a barn | 3-6 weeks | 16-18% |
| 3. Sun-drying | Spread the ears whole on a meongseok (straw mat) in the sun | 3-5 days | 15% |
| 4. Threshing | Shell the kernels off when they come loose easily | 1 day | - |
| 5. Final drying | Spread the grain and dry it in the sun | 2-4 days | 13-14% |
| 6. Resting | Leave in the shade for a day to even out the moisture | 1 day | Uniform |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The key in drying maize is the husk. If the husks are left on, the inside does not dry, moisture is trapped, and mold grows. More than half of the mold in storage comes from husks not being removed properly. Even if you leave two or three layers for tying, the rest must be removed, and especially the part covering the tip of the ear must be opened up completely.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Hang-drying takes 3-6 weeks. It is much longer than for other grains. This is because the kernels are large and attached to the cob, so the area touched by air is small. Do not hurry; give it enough time, and all you have to do in the meantime is keep it out of the rain. Anywhere that has a roof and is open on all sides is good, whether under the eaves, in a barn, or in a wondumak (raised open-sided field shelter).

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There are ways to gauge moisture without a meter.

| Method | Procedure | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| Fingernail test | Press a fingernail into a kernel | If no mark is left, it is dry |
| Twisting | Twist the ear with both hands | If the kernels fall off in a rush, it is dry enough to thresh |
| Biting | Bite a kernel with the molars | If it cracks crisply and powder comes out, it is 13-14% |
| Salt test | Put dry salt and grain in a jar, shake, and leave for 15 minutes | If salt sticks to the jar wall, the moisture is high |
| Jar test | Fill a glass jar halfway, seal it and leave for a day | If the inside of the jar fogs up, dry it more |
| Weight test | Pick one ear and weigh it every day | When the weight stops dropping, it is fully dry |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The twisting test is a convenient method that can be used only on maize. A fully dry ear drops its kernels in a shower just from being twisted with both hands. If the kernels stay attached to the cob even when you apply force, it is still wet, and stored in that state it will certainly spoil.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;When using fire for drying, do not exceed 50°C. Above that, the grain becomes unusable as seed, and the starch changes, which also lowers the taste. Place it in a loft above the hearth or at the cooler far end of an ondol (floor heating) room, but raise the heat only to the point where it feels warm to the hand.

### 12-4. Threshing

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Threshing maize is different from other grains. The kernels are packed tightly into the cob, so beating or trampling does not work. On the other hand, each kernel is large, so they can be removed by hand. The knack is to clear the first row to make space.

| Method | Procedure | Scale | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pushing with the thumb | Push off one row with the thumb to make an empty groove, then push the rest off sideways | Small amounts | Once the first row is off, the rest push off easily |
| Rubbing two ears | Press two dry ears together and rub them against each other | Medium | Fastest without tools |
| Rubbing inside a sack | Put ears in a sack and roll them by stepping on them | Large amounts | Kernels do not scatter |
| Bottle-cap board | Rub the ear on a board studded with bottle caps or nails | Medium | A thresher that is easy to make |
| Pounding in a wooden mortar | Put the ears in whole and pound lightly | Medium | Drawback: the cob breaks and mixes in |
| Stripping with an iron rod | Push an iron rod through the center of the ear and turn it | Medium | Bores a hole in the cob and pushes the kernels off |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The most practical method is to clear the first row with the thumb and push off the rest. Maize kernels sit in lines, so once space opens beside them they fall off as they are pushed. If you pick off only the first row one by one with your thumbnail, after that you can push with the palm and remove them much faster. If your hand hurts, wear thick gloves or switch to the method of pressing two ears together and rubbing.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The bottle-cap board is a simple tool worth making in advance. Nail several crown-type bottle caps onto a plank with the teeth facing up, or make it by nailing thick nails closely together. Press a dry ear onto it and turn it, and the kernels are scraped off. The amount one person can process in a day is three or four times what is done by hand.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Do not throw away the cob. Dried cobs are good-burning firewood, and the ash from burning them becomes potassium-rich fertilizer. Crushed finely, they serve as a carbon source for compost, and in the old days they were also used as stoppers or as a substitute for brushes. In a disaster, maize cobs are a resource no less valuable than the grain.

### 12-5. Winnowing Out the Chaff

| Method | Procedure | Conditions |
|---|---|---|
| Pouring in the wind | On a windy day, pour slowly from a height | Maize kernels are heavy and are not blown away by an ordinary wind |
| Winnowing basket | Put in a winnowing basket and toss it by pushing forward and pulling back | Removes husk fragments and broken cob |
| Sieving | Separate cob pieces with a coarse sieve and crumbs with a fine sieve | Sieving is especially easy because maize kernels are large |
| Hand sorting | Pick out moldy kernels and insect-eaten kernels | The last gate before storage |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Maize kernels are large and heavy, so sorting is easier than for other grains. In exchange, hand-picking is especially important, because a few moldy kernels can contaminate the entire storehouse. Pick out discolored kernels, kernels with black tips, and kernels with insect holes without feeling reluctant.

## 13. Seed Saving

### 13-1. F1 Hybrids and Open-Pollinated Varieties - The First Thing to Check

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;What must be checked before anything else in saving maize seed is whether the variety planted is an F1 hybrid or an open-pollinated variety.

| Category | Saving your own seed | Result the next year |
|---|---|---|
| F1 hybrid | Not possible | Because of inbreeding depression, heights and ears vary widely and the harvest falls to half or less |
| Open-pollinated varieties and landraces | Possible | If isolation is kept, the same traits are maintained |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Inbreeding depression appears most severely in maize. F1 seed is made by crossing two lines with different characters, and heterosis appears only in that first generation. If you plant seed saved from it, the genes segregate, large and small, early and late plants get mixed up, and the whole harvest collapses.

> **If you assume the disaster will be prolonged, you must secure open-pollinated varieties or landraces, even if their yield is a little lower. With F1 there is no second year.**

### 13-2. Isolation

| Method | Details | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Distance isolation | Keep 200m or more from other varieties | The most reliable, but difficult on small land |
| Time isolation | Stagger sowing dates by 3 weeks or more | If flowering periods do not overlap, there is no mixing |
| Bagging | Cover the ear with a paper bag before the silks emerge | Suited to small scale |
| Hand pollination | Shake pollen from the tassel of the same variety into the bag | Used together with bagging |
| Barrier | Plant a tall other crop in between | Effective only partially |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Maize is a cross-pollinating crop that moves its pollen by wind. If a neighboring field has a different variety, its pollen drifts in and mixes, and the result shows up directly in that year's ears. If sweet corn is next to a field of waxy (glutinous) maize, non-waxy kernels end up mixed into the waxy ears.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The most practical method on small land is bagging. Cover an ear just before the silks emerge with a paper bag, shake in pollen taken from a tassel of the same variety, and tie it again. Managing just three or four ears this way is enough for next year's seed. This is because a single maize ear carries several hundred kernels.

### 13-3. Selecting and Storing Seed Plants

| Criterion | Details | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Selecting individuals | Plants with no disease symptoms, that did not fall over, and with large ears | The traits are passed on |
| From many plants | Collect evenly from at least 20-30 plants | It is a cross-pollinating crop, so genetic diversity is needed |
| Kernel position | Use only kernels from the middle of the ear | Kernels at the tip and base germinate unevenly |
| Harvest timing | After it is fully ripe and the black layer has formed | Immature seed has lower germination ability |
| Drying | Dry the ears thoroughly whole, then remove the kernels by hand | Avoids mechanical damage |
| Moisture | 12-13% | Slightly drier than for food |
| Storage | Airtight container in a cool, dark place | Put in ash or dried chili peppers as well |
| Longevity | 2-3 years sealed at room temperature | Lasts longer if cool |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;What differs in saving maize seed from self-pollinating crops such as foxtail millet and sorghum is that seed must be taken from many plants. Maize is a cross-pollinating crop, so a genetically diverse population must be maintained to stay healthy. If you keep taking seed from only the single best ear, inbreeding weakness appears within a few generations. The rule is to mix ears from at least 20-30 plants evenly and use them as next year's seed.

### 13-4. Germination Test

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Before planting old seed, check its germination ability. Wrap 20 kernels in a wet cloth, put them in a warm place and wait 5-7 days. If 18 or more of the 20 germinate, use the seed as is; if 14-17, increase to 3 seeds per hole; if fewer than 12, you must obtain new seed. The standard for maize is to plant 2 seeds per hole, so the germination rate is reflected directly in the number of plants.

## 14. Toxicity and Precautions

### 14-1. Pellagra - The Greatest Risk of Making Maize a Staple

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Maize itself is not toxic. But there is a problem that arises when maize is made the staple food, and historically it has killed hundreds of thousands of people. It is pellagra, a niacin deficiency disease.

| Item | Details |
|---|---|
| Cause | The niacin in maize exists in a bound form that cannot be absorbed |
| Aggravating factor | Maize is also short of tryptophan. Tryptophan is the raw material the body converts into niacin |
| Symptoms | Inflammation of skin exposed to sun, diarrhea, mental disturbance, and death |
| Onset conditions | When a diet with almost no protein source other than maize continues for several months |
| History | Occurred on a large scale in southern Europe and the southern United States in the 19th century |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The reason this disease is frightening is that maize is an excellent food. You feel full and get plenty of calories, and then the disease comes months later. If the cause is unknown, nobody thinks it is because of maize. In fact, in 19th-century Europe it was long thought to be an infectious disease.

> **If you make maize the staple but have no beans and do no alkali treatment, that diet will make people sick after a few months.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There are two responses, and neither is hard. First, eat it together with beans. Beans are rich in tryptophan and lysine and fill the shortage exactly. If maize and beans are eaten together, pellagra does not come. Second, alkali treatment. When maize is boiled in water containing ash or lime, the bonds are broken and the niacin becomes absorbable. This is called nixtamalization, and it is a method indigenous peoples of Central and South America have used for thousands of years. The specific procedure is in Section 16.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The cause of the great pellagra epidemic was that the people who took maize to Europe took the crop but not this cooking method. In a disaster, the presence or absence of this knowledge can produce the same result.

### 14-2. Mycotoxins

| Risk factor | Risk level | Action |
|---|:---:|---|
| Normal dry grain | None | Safe |
| Ears with mold | Very high | Discard all of it. Do not give it to livestock either |
| Grain that has gone damp and smelly in storage | Very high | Discard. Washing or roasting leaves the toxin |
| Discolored and clumped grain | High | Remove it generously, including the surrounding part |
| Kernels with only the tips blackened | High | Pick them out |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Maize is among the grains most prone to aflatoxin contamination. The kernels are large and take a long time to dry, and because it is wrapped in husks it does not dry well once the inside gets wet. Aflatoxin is a powerful liver toxin, is carcinogenic, and does not disappear with heating. It is especially dangerous for children.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The response is entirely prevention. Husk and dry the ears thoroughly, store only after confirming 13 to 14% moisture, and check the smell once every two months during storage. However reluctant you are, never put anything that has already molded into a person's mouth. Put it in the compost pile and it will decompose and become fertilizer for next year.

### 14-3. Other Precautions

| Item | Details |
|---|---|
| Raw maize | Sweet corn and supersweet corn can be eaten raw |
| Dry grain | Must be soaked and cooked. It cannot be digested as is |
| Corn silk | Can be drunk as tea. It has a diuretic effect |
| Smut galls | Eaten in Mexico, but not to be eaten unless you can tell them apart with certainty |
| Single-item diet | Short of lysine and tryptophan. Always eat with beans |
