## 1. Disaster-Preparedness Evaluation

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Maize's overall grade is B, with a score of 46.8 points. Broken down by scenario, its short-term ability to get through the early stage of a disaster is B (48.0), its long-term ability to sustain itself over several years is B (45.6), and its value as a supplementary food beyond the staple itself is A (49.1). The short-term and long-term scores differ by little more than 2 points. This means maize can be used evenly in any phase, but also that it is not overwhelming in any single phase.

### 1-3. How to Read the Strengths

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Four criteria carry a grade of S or higher: infrastructure needs, storability, taste, and prevalence in South Korea. A grade of A goes to calories per area, pest & disease pressure, continuous-cropping tolerance, and seed saving. Of these, the one that matters most for disaster preparedness is the A for calories per area.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;At 2,148kcal/m², maize is the highest among the grains on this list — 1.6× that of sorghum, 2.3× that of foxtail millet, and 3.8× that of buckwheat. The energy return factor of 16.8× tells the same story: it means the crop returns sixteen times the energy that was spent tilling the field. Where the field has to be tilled by human labor alone, this efficiency is decisive. The smaller the land and the fewer the people, the higher maize's relative value climbs.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The A for pest & disease pressure is also worth noting. A yield loss of 28.2% without any pest control is low among grains. Maize is not especially disease-resistant, but the loss when no pesticide at all is used is smaller than for other crops. Of maize's 68.5% potential loss without control, 40.3 percentage points come from weeds, and pests and diseases account for the rest. In other words, weeding matters more than pesticide in growing maize. Once pesticides are gone, this structure works in maize's favor.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The SS for storability and the S for prevalence in South Korea also matter a great deal in practice. Dried kernels last more than 3 years at room temperature, and seed can be obtained anywhere across South Korea. When deciding what to stock up on just before a disaster, maize seed is among the easiest things to get hold of.

> **If you need to extract as many calories as possible from a small plot, no other grain can replace maize. The problem is the price that comes attached to those calories.**

### 1-4. How to Read the Weaknesses

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Three criteria carry a grade of E: response to sunlight, cold hardiness, and non-food value. D goes to perenniality, fertilizer demand, yield retention without synthetic inputs, and yield stability. Of these, the combination that is most damaging for disaster preparedness is the D for fertilizer demand together with the D for synthetic-input-free yield retention.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Maize is a heavy feeder. It requires 40g/m² of nutrients, with a particularly large nitrogen demand. In normal times a single bag of chemical fertilizer takes care of this, but once supply chains are cut, the whole amount has to be made up with compost and diluted urine. Per 1,000m², 10-15kg of nitrogen converts to several tons of well-rotted compost, and producing that much and carrying it to the field is itself considerable labor. A zero-input yield retention of 50.4% means the harvest is cut in half if no fertilizer at all is applied, and that drop directly cuts into maize's biggest strength — calories per area.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The problem of depleted soil fertility does not show up directly in the evaluation table, but it is more serious. Maize draws large amounts of nutrients out of the soil without giving anything back. Unless the land starts out unusually rich — like the American Midwest or Ukraine's black soil (chernozem) — a field visibly turns poor after just two or three years of continuous maize cropping. The A for continuous-cropping tolerance is an evaluation of pest and disease pressure, not of soil fertility. That is why adopting maize as a disaster crop must go hand in hand with a plan to restore soil fertility. The Three Sisters method and rotation with legumes, covered in Section 11, are that plan.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The E for sunlight response is a weakness shared by all C4 crops. The figure of 45% relative yield at 40% shade was measured with maize as the representative species. Shade from trees at the edge of a field, tall neighboring crops, and long stretches of cloudy weather all cut yield directly. A maize field should be placed where it gets the best sun.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The A for seed saving is trickier than it looks. Maize is a wind-pollinated, cross-pollinating crop, so it cross-breeds with any other variety within 200m. A bigger problem is that much of the seed sold commercially is F1 hybrid. Planting seed saved from an F1 brings on inbreeding depression, so height and ears become uneven and the harvest falls to less than half. If a disaster is expected to run long, landraces or open-pollinated (fixed) varieties must be secured in advance. Section 13 covers this in detail.

## 2. What Kind of Plant Is This?

### 2-1. Taxonomy and Origin

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Maize is an annual grass in the genus Zea, family Poaceae. It belongs to the same family as sorghum, foxtail millet, and proso millet, and among them it is the largest and most widely eaten crop. It originated on the highlands of southern Mexico, descending from a wild grass called teosinte.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Maize's domestication was extreme. Teosinte's few seeds scattered on their own once they ripened, but today's maize holds hundreds of kernels tightly fixed to a cob and wrapped in husks. This structure made harvesting easy for people, but it cost maize its ability to reproduce on its own. Without a person to strip the husk, remove the kernels, and plant them, maize disappears. It is a crop that depends entirely on people, which is why managing seed matters more for maize than for almost any other crop in a disaster.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Its Central and South American origin also explains other things. Coming from tropical highlands, it has never had to face frost, it has C4 photosynthesis optimized for strong sunlight, and it has no ability to cope with waterlogged conditions.

### 2-2. What It Looks Like

| Organ | Characteristics |
|---|---|
| Leaf | Broad, long, and strap-shaped (linear). Alternate arrangement; the leaf sheath wraps around the stem |
| Stem | Grows upright, 1.5-3m tall. Nodes are distinct, and the stem is solid inside (not hollow) |
| Root | In addition to fibrous roots, brace roots (prop roots) emerge from the base of the stem |
| Flower | Monoecious. Male flowers form at the top of the stem, female flowers separately in the leaf axils |
| Silk | The style of the female flower. Each strand of silk connects to a single kernel |
| Fruit | A caryopsis (grain). Densely packed onto the cob and wrapped in husks |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The fact that male and female flowers form separately matters in practice. A kernel only sets once pollen shed from the male flower at the top of the stem drifts down on the wind and reaches a strand of silk on a female flower below. Because each strand of silk corresponds to one kernel, any silk that never receives pollen leaves an empty spot. That is why some ears of maize come out with a bare patch or scattered, sparse kernels.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There is a practical conclusion to draw from this. Maize sets kernels far better when several rows are planted together in a block than when it is planted in one long row. In a single row, wind carries the pollen away out of the field, but planted in a block, plants pollinate each other. This is a layout principle to remember when planting maize on a small plot.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The brace roots are also worth knowing about. Thick roots emerge at an angle from the nodes at the base of the stem and anchor into the ground, and these are what support a plant nearly 3m tall. Hilling (earthing up) to help these brace roots anchor well into the soil greatly reduces lodging.

### 2-3. Growth Stages

| Stage | Timing | Key Physiology | Key Nutrients |
|---|---|---|---|
| Germination & seedling stage | 3 weeks from sowing | Germinates and the first leaf emerges | Phosphorus, nitrogen |
| Vegetative growth stage | 3-6 weeks | Stem and leaves grow rapidly | Nitrogen |
| Silking & flowering stage | 6-8 weeks | Male flowers appear, silk emerges, and pollination occurs | Phosphorus, potassium |
| Grain-filling stage | 8-12 weeks | Starch fills the kernels | Potassium |
| Maturity stage | After 12 weeks | Kernels harden and a black layer forms at the base | - |

> **The two weeks around silking decide everything for maize. Drought, high heat, or nutrient shortage during this period shows up directly as barren ears.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Maize's water and nutrient needs are concentrated at the silking stage. If the soil dries out during this period, silk emerges late and arrives after the pollen has already all shed, leaving the ear empty. Heat above 35°C produces the same result, because pollen dies in the heat. If there is only one chance to water a maize crop, that chance has to be at silking.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The one thing you must understand about maize nutrition is the niacin problem. Maize contains niacin (vitamin B3), but it exists in a bound form that people cannot absorb. As a result, relying on maize as a staple without another protein source leads to pellagra, the niacin-deficiency disease. It is an illness that progresses through dermatitis, diarrhea, and dementia, and can end in death. This disease spread widely in the 19th century among people in southern Europe and the American South who depended on maize.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The native peoples of Central and South America found the solution thousands of years ago: boiling maize in water mixed with ash or lime. The alkali breaks the bond and converts niacin into a form the body can absorb. This process is called nixtamalization. The people who carried maize to Europe took the crop but not this preparation method, and pellagra spread as a result. Anyone planning to make maize a staple food must read the alkaline treatment section in Section 16.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In terms of protein, too, maize is on the weak side among grains. It is short in both lysine and tryptophan, and because tryptophan is also a raw material the body converts into niacin, this shortage makes pellagra worse still. There are two separate reasons to eat maize together with legumes: legumes supply both the lysine and the tryptophan that maize lacks. It is no coincidence that people in Mesoamerica have long eaten maize together with beans.
