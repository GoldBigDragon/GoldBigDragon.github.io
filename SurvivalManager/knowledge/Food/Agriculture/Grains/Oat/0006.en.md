## 15. Storage Without Refrigeration

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Oat's Storability grade is SS. In the storage-stage observations of the FAO Food Loss Index, the median loss rate for oat is a low 1.30%, and dried kernels in the husk keep for 3 years or more at room temperature. This grade, however, comes with an important proviso: it applies only to grain in the husk. Oat's fat content, 3.8 to 6.9%, is among the highest of any grain, so once the grain is milled and the germ is exposed, rancidity comes quickly. Milled oat groats last 3-6 months at most.

### 15-1. The Four Enemies of Storage

| Enemy | Conditions | Result | Main line of defense |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mold | Moisture of 15% or more | Spoilage, toxin production, discard everything | Drying |
| Insects | Temperature of 20°C or more, moisture of 12% or more | Eat into the kernels | Airtight sealing, low temperature, pest control |
| Rats and mice | Containers they can reach | They eat and defecate, contaminating the grain | Physical barriers |
| Rancidity | Oxygen, light, heat | Stale smell and bitter taste | Storing in the husk, light exclusion, low temperature |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Other grains have only three enemies; oat has four. The fourth, rancidity, is the particular challenge of storing oat. Unlike mold or insects, rancidity is invisible and progresses gradually, so that one day when you open the container the smell has changed. The only way to prevent it is to block out oxygen, light, and heat.

### 15-2. Storage Conditions

| Condition | Target | If outside the target |
|---|---|---|
| Moisture | 13-14%, 12-13% for seed | Mold grows above 15% |
| Temperature | 15°C or lower, the lower the better | If higher, rancidity and insects both speed up |
| Humidity | Surrounding air at 65% or less | Dry grain reabsorbs moisture |
| Light | Complete exclusion | Light directly promotes fat oxidation |
| Oxygen | Exclude as much as possible | The direct cause of rancidity |
| Form | Hulled oat in the husk; naked oat consumed as soon as possible | Milled grain has less than half the lifespan |
| Inspection | Once every two months, check the smell | Rancidity can be detected only by smell |

> **Store oat in the husk and mill only as much as will be eaten. The husk is nature's packaging that blocks oxygen.**

### 15-3. Storage Containers

| Container | Airtightness | Light exclusion | Duration | Notes |
|---|:---:|:---:|---|---|
| Metal tin | Very good | Complete | 3 years or more | Best suited to oat. Blocks both light and oxygen |
| Hangari (onggi earthenware jar) | Good | Complete | 2-3 years | Stable temperature, and rats cannot gnaw through it |
| Glass jar | Very good | None | 2-3 years | Always keep it in a dark place or wrap it in cloth |
| Thick plastic | Fair | Partial | 1-2 years | Rats gnaw through it and oxygen also seeps through slowly |
| Woven straw sacks (gamani) and burlap sacks | Poor | Partial | 6 months | Defenseless against rancidity and insects |
| Underground storage pit | Good | Complete | 2-3 years | Low, stable temperature slows rancidity |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;For oat, containers that block light completely, such as metal tins or hangari, are especially suitable. Glass jars seal well but let light through and promote fat oxidation, so they must be kept in a dark place or wrapped in cloth or paper. The advantage of a glass jar, being able to check the condition by eye, becomes more of a disadvantage with oat.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;An underground storage pit suits oat well, because the temperature is stable and low all year, which slows the rate of rancidity. Choose a high spot where water does not seep in, dig down 1m or more, line the floor and walls thickly with dry straw, bury the airtight containers whole, and cover them with soil. Once opened, a pit is hard to reseal, so make several pits, each sized for the amount to be taken out at one time.

### 15-4. Controlling Insects in Storage - Without Chemicals

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The real main enemy of grain storage is insects. Pests such as the Angoumois grain moth, Indian meal moth, rice weevil, and flour beetle are already present in the grain as eggs right after harvest and hatch inside the storehouse. A single insect becomes dozens in two months, and in half a year a whole container is filled with powder and droppings. The methods that can be used where there are no insecticides are as follows.

| Method | Principle | Application | Effect |
|---|---|---|---|
| Complete sealing | Cutting off oxygen suffocates the insects | Fill the container to the brim and seal it | The most certain. Also prevents rancidity |
| Mixing in wood ash | Fine ash particles scratch the insects' exoskeleton and dehydrate them | Mix evenly at 2-5% of the grain volume | A long-proven method. Wash it off before eating |
| Diatomaceous earth | The tiny silica shells damage the exoskeleton | Mix 1-3g per 1kg of grain | More effective than ash. Works only when dry |
| Fine sand or loess (yellow clay) | The particles fill the gaps and block the insects' movement | Put in layers alternating with the grain | Increases the volume |
| Dried chili peppers or sancho (Korean prickly-ash pepper) | The pungent compounds act as a repellent | Put a few in the container | A supplementary measure |
| Mugwort, sweet flag, and Japanese pagoda tree leaves | Essential-oil components inhibit egg laying | Dry them and layer them between the grain | Traditionally widely used |
| Roasted salt | Holds moisture and removes the insects' conditions for survival | Wrap in cloth and put in | Doubles as a desiccant |
| Charcoal | Absorbs moisture and odors | Wrap baked, dried charcoal in cloth and put in | Also absorbs some of the rancid smell |
| Cold treatment | Eggs and larvae die below freezing | Put outside for a few days in midwinter, then bring in | In oat, it even has the effect of suppressing rancidity |
| Heat treatment | Eggs and larvae die at 60°C | Spread thinly in the sun for a few hours | Oat is high in fat, so keep the temperature lower |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The most important is the first. Complete sealing is the most powerful measure that can be used even without chemicals, and with oat it has one more special advantage. Cutting off oxygen does not just kill insects; it also stops rancidity. Since it blocks insects and rancidity at once, sealing is worth more for oat than for other grains. The more completely the container is filled and the empty space minimized, the greater the effect. A fully filled tin lasts far longer than one filled halfway. It is better to pack several small containers full than to put half a load in one large tin.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Wood ash and diatomaceous earth work on the same principle. Both kill physically, not chemically. When fine, sharp particles scrape off the wax layer covering an insect's body, the insect loses moisture and dries out. This approach has two advantages: insects cannot develop resistance, and it is harmless to people. Washing with water before eating is all that is needed. The drawback is that it does not work when there is moisture. Once the grain turns damp, neither ash nor diatomaceous earth has any effect, so in the end drying comes first.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;When using ash, sift it and select only the portion as fine as flour. Mix it in at 2-5% of the grain volume, that is, about one doe (about 1.8 L) of ash per mal (about 18 L) of oat, and turn it with your hands so that a thin coat covers the surface of every kernel. With oat, ash does not coat evenly if awns remain, so it is best to do this after screening out the awns thoroughly. The grain looks cloudy gray, but rinsing three or four times in water cleans it.

### 15-5. Ways to Slow Rancidity

| Method | Principle | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Storing in the husk | The husk blocks oxygen | The greatest effect. The difference is 3 years versus 6 months |
| Roasting in advance | Heat kills the fat-splitting enzymes | Even milled oat keeps several months longer |
| Steaming or blanching | Kills the enzymes | The method used for commercial oatmeal |
| Light exclusion | Prevents oxidation by light | Use a metal tin or hangari |
| Low temperature | Slows the chemical reaction rate | Lowering the temperature by 10°C slows the reaction to half |
| Milling in small batches | Shortens exposure time | Pound only enough for one month |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Roasting in advance is especially useful in a disaster. Oat fat spoils not only because of oxygen but also because of an enzyme in the oat itself, lipase. This enzyme breaks down the fat and creates the stale smell. Roasting over low heat until a nutty aroma appears kills the enzyme and greatly delays rancidity. Commercially made oatmeal is steamed for the same reason. Roasted oat can even be mixed into water and eaten as is, so it also saves cooking fuel.

### 15-6. Regular Inspection

| When | What to check | Action if abnormal |
|---|---|---|
| Every two months | Smell. Whether it smells stale | It is rancid. Consume it first |
| Every two months | Condensation on the outside of the container, moldy smell | Open at once, spread out, and dry |
| Every two months | Powder or cobweb-like threads on the surface | It is the Indian meal moth. Dry all of it in the sun and sift it out |
| Every two months | Warmth when gripped by hand | Heat generation is a sign of excess moisture |
| Spring and autumn | Open the whole container and check smell and color | Set an order and eat the fastest-spoiling first |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In oat storage, managing the order of consumption is important. If you decide in advance to eat the forms that go rancid quickly, such as milled, ground, and naked oat, first, and the hulled oat in the husk last, losses drop greatly. If you simply eat what is most pleasant first, you end up consuming what should be kept longest, so it is better to mark things in advance.

## 16. Cooking and Processing

### 16-1. Milling - Removing the Husk Without Tools

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;If you planted naked oat, you can skip this section, because the husk comes off at the same time as threshing. Hulled oat needs milling, and because the oat husk clings to the kernel more tightly than in other grains, it takes particular effort.

| Method | Tool | Procedure | Efficiency |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pounding in a mortar | Mortar (jeolgu) and pestle | Put in dry grain and pound. Take it out every 5 minutes and winnow | The most common, but hard work |
| Moistened pounding | Mortar | Sprinkle a little water before pounding and leave for 30 minutes | The husk comes off easily |
| Pounding after roasting | Pot and mortar | Roast over low heat to make the husk crisp, then pound | Best suited to oat |
| Husking with a millstone | Hand millstone (maetdol) | Raise the upper stone and turn slowly | Adjusting the gap is the key |
| Foot mortar | Treadle pounder (didilbang-a) | Step on it to lift and drop the pestle | Far less effort |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The most effective trick in milling oat is to roast it before pounding. A few minutes over low heat drives off the moisture in the husk and makes it crisp, and the gap between kernel and husk opens so that the husk comes off much more easily. In addition, the roasting kills lipase and thus delays rancidity. It solves milling and storage together. However, roasted oat cannot be used as seed, so seed must be set aside separately.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The knack of milling is not to try to finish in one go. Pounding hard for a long time breaks the kernels before the husks come off. Pound for 5 minutes, take the grain out and winnow off the bran, then put it back and pound again, repeating four or five times. Only if the bran is removed along the way does the force reach the remaining kernels properly.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The bran is not thrown away. It is rich in dietary fiber and minerals and good as livestock feed, and if there are no livestock it goes into the compost.

### 16-2. Rolling - Pressing Flat

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The best form in which to eat oat is not whole kernels but pressed flat. This is called rolled oats or oatmeal. Pressing widens the surface area so water soaks in quickly, cooking time drops greatly, and digestion is easier too. In a disaster where fuel is scarce, this difference is not small.

| Method | Tool | Procedure |
|---|---|---|
| Rolling with a rolling pin | Rolling pin, bottle, round piece of wood | Wrap milled oat in cloth and press and roll with the pin |
| Pressing between two stones | Two flat stones | Spread a single layer and press and rub from above |
| Pounding with a mallet | Wooden mallet | Wrap in cloth and tap lightly |
| Turning the millstone with a wide gap | Millstone | Raise the upper stone high and turn; the grain comes out pressed |
| Steaming, then pressing | Steamer (siru) and rolling pin | Steam for 10 minutes, then press; it flattens much more easily |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Steaming and then pressing is the best method. Steaming for about 10 minutes softens the kernels so they flatten with less effort, and the steaming kills lipase and delays rancidity. After pressing, the flakes must be dried again. This is the same principle as the way commercial oatmeal is made.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Rolled oats cut cooking time to 1/3 or less of that for whole kernels. Whole oat must be boiled for 40 minutes to an hour, but rolled oats are cooked in 5-10 minutes. If pressed very thin, they can be eaten just by pouring boiling water over them and covering with a lid. Considering fuel consumption, it makes sense to turn the grain into rolled oats in advance after harvest. However, rolled oats spoil much faster than whole kernels, so make only enough for one or two months.

### 16-3. Grinding - Making Flour

| Method | Tool | Characteristics |
|---|---|---|
| Millstone | Two stones, upper and lower | Produces the finest flour |
| Mortar | Mortar and pestle | Produces coarse flour. Sift and pound again |
| Grinding on a stone slab | A wide flat stone and a rolling stone | Suited to small amounts |
| Roasting, then grinding | Anything | Far less effort, and rancidity is also delayed |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Because oat is high in fat, the flour tends to clump when ground. It can even gum up a millstone. Roasting to drive off moisture before grinding makes it grind much better, and the flour turns fine and loose.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Oat flour has no gluten, so dough made from it does not become viscous. It is hard to make into bread or noodles, and is used for porridge, buchim (pan-fried pancakes), and dough mixed with other flours. In return, because of beta-glucan it thickens when stirred into water, which makes it best suited to porridge.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Flour spoils much faster than whole kernels, and oat even more so. Grind only enough for one month and keep the rest as whole kernels.

### 16-4. Fuel-Saving Cooking

| Method | Fuel | Procedure | Notes |
|---|:---:|---|---|
| Boiling whole kernels | A lot | Soak overnight, then boil 40-60 minutes | Uses the most fuel |
| Rolled-oat porridge | Little | Add water and boil 5-10 minutes | The most practical form |
| Flour porridge | Very little | Stir the flour into water and boil 5 minutes | Uses the least fuel |
| Soaked, eaten as is | None | Soak rolled oats in water or broth for several hours | Can be eaten without fire |
| Residual-heat cooking | Very little | Boil 10 minutes, turn off the fire, wrap in straw or a blanket, and leave 40 minutes | Cuts fuel to half or less |
| Roasted flour | Almost none | Stir pre-roasted flour into water | No cooking fuel needed |
| Mixed-grain rice (honban) | Medium | Mix in 2-3 parts per 10 (20-30%) with rice or barley | Less off-putting |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Oat is one of the rare grains that can be eaten without fire. Thinly pressed rolled oats, soaked in water for a few hours, can be eaten as they are. The taste is lesser, but when fuel has run out completely, when a fire cannot be lit, or when on the move, this is a big advantage. Other grains must be cooked, but with oat there is an option.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Insulated cooking is also very effective. If whole oat is boiled for only 10 minutes, taken off the fire, and wrapped whole in a box filled with straw or rice hulls, or in a thick quilt, the internal temperature stays above 80°C for over an hour and cooking continues. What would take 40 minutes of boiling is cut to 10.

### 16-5. Processing That Doubles as Preservation

| Processing | Procedure | Storage | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Roasted flour | Roast, then grind | Airtight, 6 months | Stir into water and eat right away |
| Rolled oats | Steam, press, and dry | Airtight, 2-3 months | Greatly reduces cooking fuel |
| Malt (yeotgireum) | Sprout the grain, dry, and grind | Airtight, 1 year | Used to make jocheong (grain syrup) and liquor |
| Oat jocheong | Saccharify with malt and reduce by boiling | In a cool place, 1 year | Yields sweetness and concentrated calories |
| Oat liquor | Mix steamed oat with nuruk (Korean fermentation starter) and ferment | Stored sealed | Preserves calories and doubles as water disinfection |
| Fermented porridge | Mix flour with water and leave 2-3 days | A few days | Improves nutrient absorption |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Malt can also be made from oat. Soak the grain in water, let it sprout slightly, then dry and grind it. Because the enzymes produced as the grain sprouts convert starch into sugar, with this malt you can make jocheong and liquor from any grain. It is the key to self-sufficiency in sweetness and concentrated calories when sugar has disappeared.

## 17. Annual Work Calendar

| Month (South Korea) | Work | Key points |
|---|---|---|
| January | Inspect stored grain, check seed, maintain tools | Check for stale and moldy smells |
| February | Plan fields, turn compost, germination test, prepare for spring sowing | Prepare in advance so that sowing can happen the moment the ground thaws |
| March | Sow spring oat (early to mid-month), tread autumn-sown plots | Sowing even one day earlier decides the yield |
| April | Weeding, removing wild oat, tillering management | Weed control in the first month is important |
| May | Top-dressing (at 20cm plant height), heading, watching for disease | Top-dressing has no effect if the timing is missed |
| June | Harvest spring oat, drying, threshing | The goal is to finish before the monsoon season (jangma, late June to July in South Korea) |
| July | Tidy up storage, sow following crops (soybean, foxtail millet), summer sowing in cool highlands | Do not leave harvested plots idle |
| August | Sow autumn oat (mid to late month), prepare fields | Have tillering finish before winter |
| September | Manage growth of autumn oat, weeding | Build up vigor before overwintering |
| October | Growth management, start harvesting early varieties | Have grain filling finish before frost |
| November | Harvest autumn oat, dry, thresh, store | Seal airtight after confirming 13-14% moisture |
| December | Milling and rolling, storage inspection, planning for next year | Process only enough for one or two months |

### 17-1. Five Things Not to Forget

| Item | Details |
|---|---|
| Avoiding summer | Oat is weak against heat and humidity. Choose one of three: early spring sowing, autumn sowing, or cool highlands |
| Sowing time | For spring, right after the thaw, and by mid-March at the latest in South Korea. A single day changes the yield |
| Top-dressing | Time it to a plant height of 20cm. If late, it only invites lodging |
| Harvest timing | If rain is forecast, cut without waiting for full ripeness |
| In the husk | Being high in fat, milled oat does not last half a year. Keep it in the husk and pound only as much as will be eaten |
