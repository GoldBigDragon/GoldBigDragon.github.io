## Introduction

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Oat (Avena sativa) is a grain that took hold in northern Europe. It began as a weed that crept into wheat fields. Again and again, in the cold, wet, acidic soils where wheat could not grow, only the oat survived, and eventually people accepted it as a crop. That is why Scotland and Scandinavia became oat country, and on the Korean Peninsula, too, oat served as a staple food in high mountain regions such as the Gaema Plateau. Its origin as a weed explains its character. It does not demand good land, it grows whether the soil is acidic or poor, and it withstands temperatures that kill other grains.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Evaluated under disaster conditions, oat earns an overall grade of A, and its long-term self-reliance is graded S. It has three standout strengths. The first is cold hardiness, grade S: sown in autumn, it overwinters at 10°C below zero. A grain that gets through the winter means the field is not left idle even in a year when summer crops are wiped out, and it means the first harvest arrives in spring, just when grain stocks are at their lowest. The second is resistance to shade. As a C3 cereal, it keeps 88% of its yield even under 40% shade. In a disaster where volcanic ash or huge fires darken the sky and sorghum or maize collapse to half their yield, oat is nearly unaffected. The third is the ability to hold up without fertilizer. Its zero-input yield retention of 51.8% is high even among grains.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The weaknesses are just as clear. The biggest are heat and humidity. Oat is a cool-climate crop: above 30°C its growth is impaired, and in the hot, humid weather of the monsoon season (jangma, late June to July in South Korea) Fusarium head blight spreads. To grow oats in central and southern South Korea, where summers are hot and the rainfall is concentrated in one season, you have to steer around the summer. There are only three ways: sow in spring right after the thaw and harvest in June, sow in autumn and harvest in early summer of the following year, or move up to cool high-elevation highlands.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The second weakness is yield: 300kg per 1,000m², or 1,164kcal per m², which is 0.44 times the self-sufficiency line.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Third, its fat content, at 3.8 to 6.9%, is high for a grain, so it goes rancid quickly once milled.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In sum, oat is not the mainstay of calories but a crop that fills the empty slots in the calendar. The slot of sowing in autumn, overwintering, and harvesting in spring has long been held by barley and wheat in South Korea's cropping systems, and oat takes over that slot on poorer and colder land. On top of that, its taste grade is S, the least off-putting of any grain. In a disaster, where the same food must be eaten for a long time, not finding it unpleasant is a more important quality than it sounds. This document puts particular weight on how to thresh, dehull, and roll oats flat without tools; how to dry them down to 13-14% moisture; and how to store a fat-rich grain without letting it go rancid.
