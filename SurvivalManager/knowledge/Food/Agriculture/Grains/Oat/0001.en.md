## Introduction
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Oat (Avena sativa) is a grain that took hold in northern Europe. It began as a weed in wheat fields, and it was accepted as a crop because, again and again, it was the only plant left alive on cold, wet, acidic ground where wheat would not grow. It therefore asks for no good land, grows on acidic or poor soil, and tolerates temperatures that kill other grains. Sown in autumn, it overwinters at -10℃ and is the first harvest of spring, so the field is not left idle even in a year when the summer crops are lost. As a C3 cereal it keeps 88% of its yield under 40% shading, and its yield retention without inputs is high at 51.8%. Its weak points are heat and humidity. Growth is impaired above 30℃ and Fusarium head blight spreads in the rainy season, so summer must be avoided. It yields 300 kg per 1,000㎡ and 1,164 kcal per ㎡, which is 0.44 times the self-sufficiency line, and because it is rich in fat, it goes rancid quickly once milled. Oat is not the backbone of calorie supply; it is the crop that fills the gaps in the calendar.

## 2. Plant Overview
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Oat is an annual of the grass family (genus Avena). The stem stands upright, is hollow, is 60-150 cm tall and lodges easily. The roots are shallow and spread widely. The flower is a panicle, with one spikelet hanging at the tip of each branch, and it is self-pollinating. The fruit is a caryopsis; in hulled oat it is enclosed in the husk, and in naked oat the husk falls off easily. Wild oat (Avena fatua) belongs to the same genus.

| Stage | Timing | Key point |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Germination and emergence | 3 weeks after sowing | Phosphorus, nitrogen |
| Tillering | 3-6 weeks after emergence | Side shoots branch out and the plant count is set |
| Elongation | 4-6 weeks after tillering | The stem lengthens rapidly |
| Heading | 60-80 days after sowing | The head emerges |
| Grain filling and maturity | 30-40 days after heading | Starch fills the kernels |

> **Oat has a short growing period, so early nutrient supply decides the harvest. If top dressing comes late, there is no time to recover.**

| Nutrition per 100 g | Content |
|:---|:---|
| Calories | 334-389 kcal (dry grain) |
| Carbohydrates | 66.3-70.4 g (a large share is beta-glucan, a soluble dietary fiber) |
| Protein | 9.9-14.3 g (about twice that of brown rice) |
| Fat | 3.8-6.9 g (among the highest of the grains) |
| Others | Dietary fiber, iron, B vitamins, vitamin E |

## 3. Growing Environment and Varieties
| Condition | Optimum | Limit and effect |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Growing temperature | 18-25℃ | 4-30℃. Growth is impaired above 30℃ |
| Germination | 15-20℃ | Minimum 1-3℃. Early spring sowing is possible |
| Grain filling | 10-22℃ | Kernels shrivel in high heat |
| Cold tolerance | - | Overwinters at -10℃ when sown in autumn. Dies if it stays below -15℃ for long |
| Light | 6 hours or more a day | 4 hours or more. 88% of yield even under 40% shade |
| Water | 400 mm in total | 300-500 mm. Weak to excess moisture and to severe drought |
| Soil | Sandy loam, well drained | pH 5.8-6.5 (5.0-7.5). The best acid tolerance among small grains. Clay soil damages the roots |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Mountain highlands at 500 m or more above sea level are best (summer cultivation is even possible there), followed by gently sloping south-facing fields and flat fields. Because oat tolerates shade, partial shade where no other crop can be planted can be used. Fields converted from paddies need drainage, and low-lying land is avoided because of waterlogging and Fusarium head blight.

| Type | Characteristics | Suitability for disasters |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Hulled oat | The husk is firmly attached and needs milling | Keeps 3 years or more in the husk. Takes much work to eat |
| Naked oat | The husk falls off by itself when threshed, so no milling is needed | Easy to eat, but storage life is 6 months to 1 year |

> **It is reasonable to secure both: hulled oat for long-term stockpiling and naked oat for what will be eaten soon.**

| Variety | Maturity | Yield (t/ha) | Suitable cropping season |
|:---|:---|:---:|:---|
| Samhan oat | Early to medium | 7.1 | Both spring and autumn. High growing stability, suitable if you settle on one variety |
| Hi-Speed | Very early | 6.3 | Spring and summer. Summer cultivation in highlands |
| Cayuse | Medium to late | 8.4 | Spring and autumn |
| Foothill | Late | 9.3 | Spring only. Highest yield |
| Suwan, West | Very early, early | 5.7, 4.3 | Autumn only |

## 4. Field Preparation and Sowing
| Item | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Soil preparation | The seeds are small, so break up the clods finely and level the soil. Dig drainage ditches around the field and in the furrows. 1,000-1,500 kg of well-rotted compost. Firm the soil after sowing |
| Fertilizing | Per 1,000㎡: 6.6 kg nitrogen (50% basal, 50% top dressing), 5.3 kg phosphate, 2.8 kg potassium (phosphate and potassium entirely as basal fertilizer) |
| Top dressing | Judge by height, not by date. Apply at 20 cm plant height. Past that it does not come back as yield and only invites lodging |
| Substitute resources | 20-40 kg of wood ash (also corrects acidity), urine diluted 10 times (around 20 cm plant height) |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Oat tolerates acid soil, so it has an advantage over other small grains when lime cannot be applied.

| Cropping season | Sowing | Harvest |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Spring cultivation | Early to mid March. As early as possible right after the thaw | Early to mid June |
| Autumn cultivation | Mid to late August | Early to mid November. In the south, an early-summer harvest the following year after overwintering is also possible |
| Summer cultivation (highlands) | Late July to mid August | October-November. Works only at 500 m or more above sea level |

| Sowing | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Seed | Sieve out plump, even kernels and remove small ones and chaff. Pick out wild oat (long awns, darker kernels). Drill sowing 15-16 kg per 1,000㎡, broadcast sowing 20 kg |
| Disinfection | Soak in 50-55℃ water for 10-15 minutes (against smut), coat with fine ash, or use seed from plants that showed no disease |
| Planting | Row spacing 20-25 cm, depth 2-3 cm. After covering, tread it down with the feet or press with a board (germination rises greatly). After broadcast sowing, cover the seed with soil using a rake |

## 5. Water, Cultivation Management, and Pests and Diseases
| Period | Water management |
|:---|:---|
| Sowing to germination | Keep from drying out |
| Tillering | Leave it to natural rainfall |
| Elongation, heading and grain filling | Supplement if dry |
| Harvest | Cut off completely. Rain makes the crop lodge and sprout on the head |
| Rainy season | Draining the water is the management |

> **Water management in oat farming is mostly about draining. Rain at harvest in particular is the greatest cause of loss.**

| Management | Details |
|:---|:---|
| Weeds | Early growth is slow, so plow before sowing to bury the weeds, and weed for the first time 2-3 weeks after emergence. Once tillering ends, the oat covers the ground. Wild oat is the most troublesome |
| Lodging prevention | Nitrogen at or below the standard amount, the sowing rate observed, top dressing at 20 cm plant height, short and stiff varieties, drainage. There is little that can be done after the crop has fallen |
| Overwintering | Autumn-sown plants are heaved by frost, so press the plants down by treading when the frozen ground thaws in early March |
| Others | The heads hang down and birds perch on them easily, so string up threads. Oat straw is used for mulch, livestock bedding, feed and compost |

| Pest or disease | Response without chemicals |
|:---|:---|
| Fusarium head blight (humid conditions at flowering and grain filling; heads turn red or pink) | Drainage and ventilation, early harvest, discard diseased heads |
| Smut (seed-borne) | Hot-water seed treatment, selection of sound seed, burn diseased heads |
| Leaf spot | Observe the sowing rate, restrain fertilizing, remove diseased leaves |
| Crown rust, powdery mildew | Resistant varieties, ensure ventilation, diluted milk |
| Armyworm, aphids, mites | Hand picking, soapy water, protect natural enemies, spray water |
| Birds, field mice | String up threads, nets, tidy the field's surroundings, manage storage containers |

> **Oat whose heads have turned red or pink does not become safe by washing or boiling. Discarding it is the only way.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The key to control is to avoid the season when disease spreads. Avoiding the summer rainy season is half of oat pest and disease control, and restrained nitrogen, ventilation and drainage are the rest. Wood ash and garlic infusion are also used.

| Symptom | Cause and action |
|:---|:---|
| Yellowing overall and small plants | Nitrogen deficiency. Rotted compost, or urine diluted 10 times at around 20 cm plant height |
| Leaf margins scorched | Potassium deficiency. Wood ash |
| Yellow between the veins, veins stay green | Magnesium deficiency. Dolomite powder |
| New leaves turn white | Manganese or iron deficiency. Stop overusing wood ash, add leaf mold |
| Leaf tips dry gray | Copper deficiency (peat or sandy soil). Diversify compost materials |
| Only the leaves thrive and the crop lodges | Excess nitrogen. Cut off the nitrogen |

## 6. Intercropping and Crop Rotation
| Compatibility | Crop |
|:---|:---|
| Very good | Soybean, adzuki bean, cowpea (following crop, restores nitrogen), potato and sweet potato (following crop; oat's allelopathic compounds lower nematode density) |
| Good | Clover and Chinese milk vetch (green manure and fodder), summer vegetables on ground cleared in June |
| Fair | Barley, wheat (same cultivation method but they share diseases) |
| Poor | Continuous oat (it tolerates continuous cropping but disease builds up) |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Rotate oat in year 1, a legume in year 2, potato or a root vegetable in year 3, and return to oat in year 4. After spring oat is harvested in June, four months remain until autumn, so follow it with soybean, foxtail millet or summer vegetables so the field is not left idle.

## 7. Harvest, Threshing, and Seed Saving
| Step | Details |
|:---|:---|
| Timing | Spring cultivation early to mid June (finish before the rainy season), autumn cultivation early to mid November (before frost). When the stems and leaves turn yellow, the heads droop with the kernels pointing down, and a fingernail pressed into a kernel leaves no mark. If kernels fall when the head is shaken, cut at once |
| Cutting | Cut close to the base with a sickle without shaking the plants. Bind about ten plants at a time, stand them leaning against each other in the field, and move them to a barn or under the eaves after a few days. Pick out reddened heads separately and discard them |
| Drying | Preliminary drying 2-4 days (18-20%) → hang to dry 1-2 weeks (15-16%) → threshing → finishing drying → 13-14%. The fat content is high, so keep the drying temperature low |
| Judging | A kernel snaps crisply when bitten and leaves flour; dry kernels shaken in both hands knock together with a clear sound; sealed in a jar, no condensation forms in a day or two |
| Threshing | Beat with a flail or stick on a straw mat, strike inside a sack, or rub inside a tub. Naked oat is easy |
| Chaff | Let it blow away in a steady wind of 2-4 m/s, fan it, or sieve it. If the awns are long, rub by hand or foot to break them off, then winnow |

| Seed saving | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Selection | Walk the field from heading and mark plants. Choose plants with no sign of disease, not lodged, and with large heads. Exclude plants that showed smut or Fusarium head blight. Let them ripen fully for 45 days or more after heading |
| Wild oat | Pick out the long-awned, dark kernels. If it mixes in, it increases every year |
| Storage | Always in the husk (milling sharply reduces germination ability and lifespan), moisture 12-13%, 10℃ or below, light completely blocked. Twice the required amount |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Oat is a self-pollinating crop, so pure lines are easily maintained. Oat seed tends to have a shorter life than other grains, so run a germination test on old seed before planting.

## 8. Storage
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Storability is SS, but because of the high fat content, rancidity is a fourth enemy after mold, insects and rats. Rancidity can be detected only by smell.

| Item | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Conditions | Moisture 13-14%, temperature 15℃ or below (the lower the better), surrounding humidity 65% or below, light and oxygen blocked as far as possible, smell check every two months |
| Form | In the husk (3 years or more). Eat milled naked oat as soon as possible (its life is less than half). Manage the order of use so the ones that go rancid fastest are eaten first |
| Container | Metal drum 3 years or more (blocks light and oxygen together), glass jar 2-3 years (in a dark place or wrapped in cloth), underground storage pit 2-3 years, thick plastic bag 1-2 years, burlap sack 6 months |

| Way to slow rancidity | Principle and effect |
|:---|:---|
| Store in the husk | The husk blocks oxygen. The difference between 3 years and 6 months |
| Roast in advance | Heat kills the fat-degrading enzymes. Even milled oat lasts several months longer |
| Steam or blanch | Kills the enzymes. The method commercial oatmeal uses |
| Shade, low temperature | Lowering the temperature by 10℃ halves the reaction rate |
| Mill in small batches | Pound only a month's worth at a time |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;For insect control, completely airtight storage filled to the top is the surest (it also stops rancidity); also used are wood ash 2-5%, diatomaceous earth 1-3 g per 1 kg of grain, fine sand or yellow clay, charcoal, and putting the grain outside for a few days in midwinter (cold). Because the fat content is high, keep heat treatment temperatures low.

## 9. Cooking and Precautions
| Preparation | Method |
|:---|:---|
| Milling (hulled oat) | Pound in a mortar; sprinkle a little water, leave for 30 minutes, then pound; roast over low heat to make the husk crisp and then pound (the best fit). Do not try to finish in one go: take it out every 5 minutes and winnow. Do not throw away the bran |
| Rolled oats | The best form for eating oat. Steaming milled oat for 10 minutes and pressing it with a rolling pin works best; wrapping it in cloth and pressing with a rolling pin, a bottle or a hammer, or pressing between two flat stones, also works. Cooking time is a third or less of whole kernels |
| Flour milling | Pounding after roasting is much less work and also delays rancidity. It has no gluten, so the dough has no stickiness. Flour spoils much faster than whole kernels |

| Cooking | Fuel and tips |
|:---|:---|
| Soak and eat as is | Soak rolled oats in water or broth for several hours. Can be eaten without fire |
| Flour porridge | Stir the flour into water and cook for 5 minutes. Uses the least fuel |
| Rolled-oat porridge | Add water and cook for 5-10 minutes. The most practical |
| Residual-heat cooking | Boil for 10 minutes, wrap in straw or a blanket for 40 minutes. Fuel use drops by more than half |
| Mixed rice | Mix 20-30% into rice or barley |
| Boiling whole kernels | Soak overnight, 40-60 minutes. Uses the most fuel |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Processing that also serves as storage includes rolled oats (airtight, 2-3 months), malt (airtight, 1 year), oat syrup (in a cool place, 1 year), oat liquor and fermented porridge.

| Precaution | Details |
|:---|:---|
| Toxicity | None at ordinary intake |
| Moldy oat | The toxin of Fusarium head blight does not disappear with heating. Discard all of it and do not give it to livestock either |
| Rancid oat | Has a stale smell and leaves a bitter taste. Oxidized fat products are harmful, so do not eat it |
| Gluten | Oat itself has no gluten, but it can be cross-contaminated with wheat. People who are severely sensitive should be careful |
| Excessive intake | The large amount of dietary fiber upsets digestion. Increase the amount gradually |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Oat grain is a good feed for horses and cattle, oat straw is soft and usable as roughage, and green oat is not toxic and can be fed to livestock as it is.

## 10. Annual Work Schedule
| Month | Work |
|:---|:---|
| Jan-Feb | Check stored grain (stale smell, moldy smell), check seed, turn the compost, germination test, prepare for spring sowing |
| March | Sow spring oat (early to mid month), tread the autumn-sown plants. Sowing even one day earlier decides the yield |
| April | Weeding, remove wild oat. Weed control in the first month matters |
| May | Top dressing (20 cm plant height), heading, watch for disease |
| June | Harvest spring oat, dry and thresh. Finish before the rainy season |
| July | Tidy storage, sow following crops (soybean, foxtail millet), highland summer sowing |
| August | Sow autumn oat (mid to late month), prepare the field |
| Sep-Oct | Manage autumn oat growth, weeding, begin harvesting early varieties |
| November | Harvest autumn oat, dry, thresh and store (seal after confirming moisture of 13-14%) |
| December | Mill and roll (only a month or two's worth), check storage, plan for next year |

> **Five things not to forget: avoid summer, sow right after the thaw, top-dress at 20 cm plant height, harvest early when rain is forecast, and store in the husk.**
