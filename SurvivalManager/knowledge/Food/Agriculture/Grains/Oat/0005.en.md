## 12. Harvest and Threshing

### 12-1. Judging the Timing

| Criterion | Details | Judgment |
|---|---|---|
| Spring crop | Early to mid-June (South Korea) | The goal is to finish before the monsoon season (jangma, late June to July in South Korea) arrives |
| Autumn crop | Early to mid-November (South Korea) | Harvest before frost falls |
| For seed | 45 days or more after heading | Must be fully ripened for good germination |
| Stem color | Stems and leaves turn yellow | Maturity is near |
| Kernel hardness | A fingernail pressed into a kernel leaves no mark | Fully ripe |
| Head posture | Head branches droop and kernels point downward | Harvest time |
| Shattering | Kernels fall when the head is shaken | Past the best time. Cut immediately |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The most important variable in deciding when to harvest oat is not the condition of the kernels but the weather. Oat has a hollow stem with drooping heads, so it lodges easily in wind and rain, and fallen heads touch the ground and sprout in the head. If rain is forecast, the right answer is to cut and bring the crop in even if it is a little early. Slightly underripe kernels fill out to some extent as they dry indoors, but kernels that have lodged and sprouted do not recover.

> **With oat, far more is lost by waiting for it to ripen than by cutting a little early.**

### 12-2. Harvesting Tips

| Step | Details | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Start on a clear morning, after the dew has dried | Wet heads are a starting point for mold |
| 2 | Cut with a sickle close to the base | The straw is harvested along with it and used for mulch and fodder |
| 3 | Do not shake the heads while cutting | Oat is a grain that shatters easily |
| 4 | Tie the cut stalks into bundles | A little over ten plants per bundle is suitable |
| 5 | Stand the bundles leaning against each other in the field | Keeps them out of the rain and ensures ventilation |
| 6 | After a few days, move them to a barn or under the eaves | They must be kept from dew and rain |
| 7 | Pick out heads that have turned red and discard them separately | Fusarium head blight produces toxins |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Standing cut oat in the field is a method long used for small-grain cereals. If the bundles are stood leaning against each other in a cone shape, rain runs down the outside, and the inside is ventilated and dries. However, in South Korea you never know in June when the monsoon rains will arrive, so it is safer not to leave them long and to move them indoors within a few days.

### 12-3. Drying the Grain - Down to 13-14% Moisture

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Everything in grain storage is decided by moisture. Above 15% moisture, mold grows; below 13%, both mold and insects almost stop their activity. Freshly cut oat has a moisture content of 20-30%. Bringing this down to 13-14% is the one and most important task right after harvest.

| Stage | Method | Duration | Moisture reached |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Preliminary drying | Stand the cut bundles in the field | 2-4 days | 18-20% |
| 2. Hang drying | Hang or stack in a barn or under the eaves | 1-2 weeks | 15-16% |
| 3. Threshing | Thresh once sufficiently dry | 1 day | - |
| 4. Final drying | Spread on a straw mat (meongseok) or cloth and dry in the sun | 2-3 days | 13-14% |
| 5. Tempering | Leave one day in the shade to even out the moisture | 1 day | Evened out |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Because oat is high in fat, particular care must be taken with drying temperature. If dried above 50°C, the fat begins to oxidize and a stale smell develops, and the grain can no longer be used as seed. Sun drying is the safest; if fire must be used, raise the heat only to the point where the grain feels warm to the touch. If it feels hot, it is already too high.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The principles for sun drying are the same for every grain. First, do not spread the grain directly on the ground. Moisture from the soil wets the underside again. Second, do not pile it deeper than 3-5cm, and turn it three or four times a day. Third, when the sun sinks, gather it in or cover it. If it takes the night dew, the moisture removed during the day returns. Fourth, do not rush the drying. If only the outside dries and moisture is trapped inside, mold will grow later.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There are ways to gauge moisture without a meter.

| Method | Procedure | Judgment |
|---|---|---|
| Biting | Bite a kernel with a molar | If it snaps apart with a crack and gives off powder, it is 13-14%. If it squashes, it is still wet |
| Salt test | Put a spoonful of dry salt and a handful of grain in a jar, shake, and leave for 15 minutes | If the salt sticks to the jar wall, the moisture is high |
| Feel in the hand | Grip a handful and open the hand | If it slides apart, it is dry; if it clumps, it is wet |
| Sound | Hold it in both hands and shake | Dry kernels knocking together give a clear sound |
| Jar test | Fill a glass jar halfway, seal it, and leave for a day | If the inside of the jar fogs up, it needs more drying |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The jar test is the most reliable as a final check before going into storage. In the sealed jar the grain and air reach moisture equilibrium, and if the moisture is above 14%, the humidity inside the jar exceeds 70% and fog forms at dawn when the temperature drops.

### 12-4. Threshing - Without Tools

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Oat is an easy grain to thresh. The kernels hang loosely at the tips of the head branches, so when dry they fall even under a light impact. If anything, the problem is that they fall too easily, causing losses during harvest and transport.

| Method | Procedure | Scale | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hand stripping | Hold the stalk of the head and strip downward with a gloved hand | Small amount | Least loss. Used for seed |
| Beating | Spread the heads on a straw mat and beat them with a flail (dorikkae) or a stick | Large | The most common |
| Striking inside a sack | Put them in a sturdy sack and slam the whole sack against the floor | Medium | Kernels do not scatter |
| Treading | Spread on a straw mat and tread as if rubbing with the feet | Large | Takes less effort |
| Rubbing in a tub | Put in a large tub and rub while stirring with a stick | Medium | Easier on the hands |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;A flail can be made by hand. Simply hang a short stick loosely from the end of a long handle with a leather strap or tough cord. When the handle is swung, the stick at the end spins around and strikes the grain. It is three to four times more efficient than beating with arm strength alone and tires you less even when used all day. It is a tool especially well suited to threshing small-grain cereals, and one of the first farm tools to make in a disaster.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;If naked oat has been planted, a big advantage shows at this stage. The husk comes off at the same time as threshing, so after winnowing alone the grain is ready to eat. With hulled oat, one more step, milling, remains after threshing.

### 12-5. Winnowing the Chaff - Fan Mills and Winnowing Baskets

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Threshed oat is mixed with head branches, awns, leaf fragments, and dust. Oat awns are long and stiff and get mixed in particularly heavily, and if the grain is stored as it is, the moisture and insect eggs clinging to the chaff ruin the entire storage.

| Method | Procedure | Conditions |
|---|---|---|
| Pouring in the wind | On a windy day, pour the grain slowly from a height | A steady wind of 2-4m per second |
| Winnowing | Put it in a winnowing basket (ki) and push forward and pull back to toss it into the air | No wind needed |
| Fanning | One person pours while another makes wind with a fan or a board | Two people are needed |
| Sieving | Separate awns and branches with a coarse sieve and dust with a fine sieve | Sieving is especially effective for oat because of its awns |
| Rubbing off awns | Rub by hand or foot to break off the awns, then winnow | Must be done first if the awns are long |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Oat awns are a real problem in storage. Long and stiff, they create gaps between the grains in the container, and those gaps become hiding places for insects. On top of that, the awns themselves hold moisture. Even if it means winnowing one more time, screening out as many awns as possible extends storage life. Rubbing with the feet to break off the awns and then winnowing makes the grain much cleaner.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;A winnowing basket can be made at home. Weaving split bamboo or bush-clover twigs, or even building one from thin plywood with only three sides raised, works. The knack of winnowing is not to shake it up and down but to push forward and pull back, tossing the grain briefly into the air. While it is airborne, the lighter material lags behind and collects toward the front.

## 13. Seed Saving

### 13-1. Selecting Plants for Seed

| Criterion | Details | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Selection timing | Walk the field from heading onward and mark plants | They cannot be told apart after harvest |
| Plant conditions | No disease symptoms, not lodged, with large heads | The traits are passed on |
| Harvest timing | 45 days or more after heading, after fully ripening | Immature seed has a lower germination rate |
| Plants to exclude | Plants that showed smut or Fusarium head blight | Prevents seed transmission |
| Wild oat | Pick out kernels with long awns and darker color | If mixed in, it increases every year |
| Form | Always store in the husk | Milling sharply reduces germination ability and lifespan |
| Quantity | Twice the amount needed | The seeding rate is high, so set aside plenty in advance |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Oat is a self-pollinating crop, so pure lines are maintained well. Even when another variety is nearby the chance of crossing is low, and the seed you collect carries the same traits as the parent. This is why the Seed saving grade is S. From the standpoint of disaster preparedness, this is a major asset. A line once secured can be carried on for many years.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;What to watch out for is wild oat. It is almost indistinguishable from cultivated oat, so if it gets mixed in during seed saving, its proportion grows year after year. Because wild oat drops its seed on its own when ripe, harvest losses become large, and because its germination is irregular, the field becomes uneven. The surest approach is to tell it apart by head shape during the growing season and pull it out; when selecting seed, pick out kernels with long, twisted awns and kernels of an unusually dark color.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Oat seed must always be stored in the husk. Even when growing naked oat, for seed use choose individuals whose husk remains, or, in the case of naked oat, leave it as it is, unmilled. Because it is a high-fat grain, once the germ is exposed oxidation is fast and germination ability falls sharply.

### 13-2. Seed Storage

| Condition | Standard | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Moisture | 12-13% | The lower the moisture, the longer seed lives |
| Temperature | 10°C or lower is ideal | Rich in fat, it goes rancid fast at higher temperatures |
| Container | Glass jar, metal tin, earthenware jar (hangari) | Blocks moisture and insects at once |
| Light | Complete exclusion | Light promotes fat oxidation |
| Form | In the husk | Milling sharply shortens lifespan |
| Lifespan | 2-3 years sealed at room temperature | Lasts longer if kept cool |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Oat seed tends to have a shorter lifespan than other grains, because of its fat content. After 2-3 years the germination rate falls noticeably, so it is safer to collect fresh seed every year and renew the line. If you plan to skip several years and use old seed, it must be kept in the coolest place.

### 13-3. Germination Test

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Before planting old seed, check its germination ability. Wrap 20 kernels in a wet cloth, keep them in a warm place, and wait 5 days. If 16 or more of the 20 germinate, use the seed as is; if 12-15 do, increase the seeding rate by 1.5 times; if fewer than 10 do, new seed must be obtained. Because oat is a crop with a high seeding rate, a drop in germination rate leads directly to a lower harvest.

## 14. Toxicity and Precautions

### 14-1. Toxicity of the Grain - None

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Oat is not toxic at normal intake. Unlike sorghum, its leaves do not contain cyanogenic glycosides in problematic amounts, and there is nothing in the grain that has to be removed. Washed and cooked, it is safe as it is. If anything, it is among the gentler grains to digest, so it suits children, the elderly, and convalescent patients.

### 14-2. Cases That Call for Caution

| Item | Details | Action |
|---|---|---|
| Moldy grain | The toxins of Fusarium head blight do not disappear with heating | Discard all of it. Do not feed it to livestock either |
| Rancid grain | Smells stale and tastes bitter | Do not eat it. Fat oxidation products are harmful to the body |
| Gluten sensitivity | Oat itself has no gluten, but it can be cross-contaminated with wheat | Those who are severely sensitive should take care |
| Excessive intake | Plenty of dietary fiber can cause digestive discomfort | Increase the amount gradually |
| Undercooked grain | The starch is not digested | Soak thoroughly and cook well |

> **Moldy grain does not become safe by washing, boiling, or roasting. Throwing it away is the only way.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The problem actually encountered more often with oat is not mold but rancidity. Because it is a high-fat grain, milled or ground oat starts to smell stale within a few months. The stale smell comes from oxidized fat, and the oxidation products are not good for the body. Unlike mycotoxins, however, they are not an acute danger, so in a truly starving situation it can be eaten. It is enough to know that the criterion for judging is different.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Ways to prevent rancidity are covered in detail in Section 15. The key points are to store in the husk, mill only as much as will be eaten, and roast the grain to inactivate the enzymes.

### 14-3. Feeding Livestock

| Item | Details |
|---|---|
| Oat grain | A good feed for horses and cattle. In particular, it is the standard feed for horses |
| Oat straw | Soft among small-grain straws and usable as roughage |
| Green oat | Widely used as a forage crop. Not toxic |
| Caution | Moldy grain is poison for livestock too |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Oat does not have the cyanogenic glycoside problem that sorghum has, so young green oat can be fed to livestock as is. That is why it is widely used as a forage crop. If you want to keep livestock going in a disaster, oat becomes a rare option that supplies both grain and roughage from a single crop.
