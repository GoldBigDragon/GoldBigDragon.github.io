## 3. Growing Conditions

### 3-1. Limits and Optimum Values

| Condition | Optimum | Tolerated range | Outside the range |
|---|---|---|---|
| Growing temperature | 18-25°C | 4-30°C | Growth is impaired above 30°C |
| Germination temperature | 15-20°C | Minimum 1-3°C | Germinates even at very low temperatures, so early-spring sowing is possible |
| Grain-filling temperature | 10-22°C | Range 12°C | Heat during grain filling shrivels the kernels |
| Cold hardiness | - | Overwinters at 10°C below zero when sown in autumn | Plants freeze to death if temperatures stay colder than 15°C below zero for long |
| Light | 6 hours or more a day | 4 hours or more | Keeps 88% of its yield even under 40% shade |
| Total water requirement | About 400mm | 300-500mm | Weak against waterlogging and also against severe drought |
| Soil | Sandy loam, well drained | Most soil textures | Roots are damaged in clay soil |
| Soil acidity | pH 5.8-6.5 | pH 5.0-7.5 | Best acid tolerance among the small-grain cereals |
| Growing period | 75-110 days | Varies by variety | Spring sowing takes 100-120 days |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The narrowest item in this table is the grain-filling temperature. A range of 10-22°C occurs in South Korea only in May and early June, and in October and November. Every plan for growing oats starts from fitting grain filling into these two windows. There is no cropping type that runs through the summer.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Conversely, the widest margin is in cold hardiness. Oat sown in autumn withstands temperatures down to 10°C below zero. When snow cover provides insulation, it survives even lower temperatures. This trait makes it possible to use the winter field, and that is oat's greatest value in a disaster.

### 3-2. Choosing a Site in a Disaster

| Site | Suitability | Reason |
|---|:---:|---|
| Mountain land at 500m or more above sea level | Best | Summers are cool, so even summer cultivation is possible |
| Gently sloping south-facing field | Good | Favors overwintering with autumn sowing |
| Flat-land field | Good | Used for early spring sowing or autumn sowing |
| Partial shade | Usable | A C3 cereal, so it tolerates shade. Suits spots where other crops cannot be planted |
| Field converted from a rice paddy | Caution | Workable if drainage is secured |
| Low-lying ground where water collects | Unsuitable | Waterlogging damage and Fusarium head blight occur |
| Valley on a north-facing slope | Caution | Coolness is good, but late frost and humidity are problems |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Oat tolerates shade well, so it can use partially shaded spots where other crops are hard to plant. Even beside a fence, on the north side of a large tree, or where a building's shadow falls for about half the day, oat keeps a good part of its yield. When dividing limited land, giving the sunny spots to sorghum or maize and assigning the shaded spots to oat increases the total harvest. This is the practical reason to mix C3 and C4 in the grain lineup.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Cool highlands are the best place for oat. North Korea's Gaema Plateau was a major oat-producing area because it stays cool even in summer, so the grain-filling temperature fits. In mountain areas above 500m elevation, even summer cultivation becomes possible, which widens the choice of cropping types.

## 4. Varieties

### 4-1. Hulled Oat and Naked Oat - The Most Important Distinction in a Disaster

| Type | Husk | Milling | Storage | Suitability for disaster |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Hulled oat (covered oat) | Tightly attached to the kernel | Absolutely necessary | 3 years or more in the husk | Good for storage, but takes a lot of work before it can be eaten |
| Naked oat (hull-less oat) | Falls off by itself when threshed | Not needed | 6 months to 1 year, since it is already in a milled state | Easy to eat, but storage life is short |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This distinction matters more than any other varietal trait. Without electricity or a milling machine, eating hulled oat requires pounding in a mortar, and because the oat husk clings to the kernel more tightly than in other grains, this is especially hard work. With naked oat, that whole process is skipped. Once it is threshed, it can be eaten right away.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In storage, however, the situation reverses. Naked oat has no husk from the start, so its germ is exposed, and because it is a high-fat grain, it goes rancid quickly. At room temperature, it starts to develop a stale smell in 6 months to a year. In hulled oat the husk blocks oxygen, so it keeps for 3 years.

> **A sensible disaster-preparedness arrangement is to secure hulled oat for long-term stockpiling and naked oat for the portion to be eaten right away.**

### 4-2. Comparison of Main Varieties

| Variety | Maturity | Yield (t/ha) | Suitable cropping type | Notes |
|---|:---:|:---:|---|---|
| Samhan | Early to mid-season | 7.1 | Both spring and autumn | Heading date May 15. High cultivation stability |
| High Speed | Very early | 6.3 | Spring and summer | A rare variety suited to summer cultivation |
| Cayuse | Mid to late | 8.4 | Spring and autumn | Heading date May 26 |
| Foothill | Late | 9.3 | Spring only | Heading date June 10. Highest yield |
| Suwan | Very early | 5.7 | Autumn only | Heading date October 15 |
| West | Early | 4.3 | Autumn only | Heading date November 2 |

### 4-3. Selection Criteria by Situation

| Situation | Recommendation | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| When no tools are available | Naked oat types | No milling is needed |
| When the goal is long-term stockpiling | Hulled oat types | Keeps 3 years or more in the husk |
| When planning to use the winter field | Suwan, West | Varieties dedicated to autumn cultivation |
| When wanting an early harvest in spring | Samhan | Early to mid-season and highly stable |
| In cool highlands, through the summer | High Speed | Very early, so summer cultivation is possible |
| For maximum yield | Foothill | Highest, at 9.3 t/ha |
| When unifying on one cropping type | Samhan | Can be used in both spring and autumn |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Assuming the disaster will be prolonged, it is realistic to build around a single variety suited to both spring and autumn. That way only one kind of seed has to be managed, while the cropping type can be changed to fit the situation. Varieties dedicated to a particular cropping type lead in yield, but the seed must be managed as several separate kinds, and the risk of mixing or losing them rises accordingly.

## 5. Field Preparation and Fertilizer

### 5-1. Preparing the Land

| Item | Details | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Plowing | Break up clods finely and level evenly | The kernels are small, and any that fall between clods cannot emerge |
| Drainage ditches | Always around the field and in the furrows | In excess moisture, oat suffers lodging and disease together |
| Soil acidity | pH 5.8-6.5 | Best acid tolerance among the small-grain cereals, so less lime is needed |
| Organic matter | Well-rotted compost 1,000-1,500kg/1,000m² | Supplies water-holding capacity and nutrients together |
| Firming | Press down after sowing | Raises the germination rate greatly |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Oat tolerates acidic soil well, so it has an advantage over other small-grain cereals when lime cannot be applied. If the supply of liming materials is cut off in a long-term disaster, South Korea's soils, which are acidic to begin with, grow steadily more acidic as rainfall leaches out the base cations. Under those conditions, where barley and wheat lose strength first, oat holds out relatively longer. If wood ash is available, it can be used for partial correction of acidity.

### 5-2. Standard Fertilizer Rates (per 1,000m²)

| Fertilizer | Rate | Timing |
|---|:---:|---|
| Nitrogen (N) | 6.6kg | 50% as base fertilizer, 50% as top-dressing |
| Phosphorus (P) | 5.3kg | All as base fertilizer |
| Potassium (K) | 2.8kg | All as base fertilizer |
| Compost | 1,000-1,500kg | All as base fertilizer, as well-rotted compost |

| Type | Timing | Details |
|---|---|---|
| Base fertilizer | Before sowing | All the compost, all the phosphorus and potassium, half the nitrogen |
| Top-dressing | When plants are 20cm tall | The remaining half of the nitrogen. It has no effect if the timing is missed |

> **Judge top-dressing by height, not by date. Once the plants pass 20cm, it does not come back as yield and only invites lodging.**

### 5-3. When There Is No Chemical Fertilizer - Substitute Fertilizers for a Disaster

| Substitute resource | Nutrients supplied | How to make and use | Caution |
|---|---|---|---|
| Wood ash | Potassium, phosphorus, calcium | Scatter 20-40kg per 1,000m² and rake it in | Alkaline, so it also corrects soil acidity |
| Well-rotted compost | All-round | Pile leaves, grass, and manure in layers and let them rot for 3-6 months | Under-rotted compost burns roots |
| Diluted urine | Nitrogen, potassium | Dilute 10-fold with water and pour into the furrows when plants are about 20cm tall | If the timing is missed, it only invites lodging |
| Bone meal | Phosphorus, calcium | Burn and crush the bones, mix into compost, and let it mature for two months | Breaks down slowly, so prepare it in advance |
| Legume green manure | Nitrogen | Plow under soybean or Chinese milk vetch as a preceding crop | Sow 2-3 weeks after plowing under |
| Livestock bedding | All-round | Rot down a mix of rice straw and manure before use | Avoid material high in salt |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Oat's zero-input yield retention of 51.8% is high even among grains. Even with no fertilizer at all, it yields more than half of a normal year's harvest. Moreover, the requirement itself is low, 18g per m², so compost alone can cover a good part of it.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;However, oat is sensitive to nitrogen. Its requirement is small, but that does not mean its response is dull: with even a little too much nitrogen, the stems overgrow and lodge. Because the stems are hollow, once down they do not stand up again, and fallen heads touch the ground and rot or sprout. Even when fertilizer is plentiful, it is better to hold back with oat.

## 6. Sowing

### 6-1. Timing by Cropping Type - Avoiding Summer Is Everything

| Cropping type | Sowing (South Korea) | Harvest (South Korea) | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Spring crop | Early to mid-March | Early to mid-June | Sow as early as possible right after the thaw |
| Autumn crop | Mid to late August | Early to mid-November | In southern South Korea, overwintering and harvesting in early summer of the following year is also possible |
| Summer crop (cool highlands) | Late July to mid-August | October-November | Works only at 500m or more above sea level |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There is just one rule for spring sowing: plant as early as possible, as soon as the ground thaws. It is no exaggeration to say that every day of delay reduces the harvest. The reason is the grain-filling period. The later the sowing, the more the grain-filling period runs into early-summer heat, and above 22°C the kernels do not fill out and shrivel. In South Korea, the goal is to finish sowing by mid-March, and once April arrives it is better to give up on a spring crop and wait for autumn.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;From the standpoint of disaster preparedness, autumn sowing is more valuable. The field from which a summer crop has been harvested is not left idle all winter, and the harvest comes in the following spring, when grain stocks are at their lowest. If sown in mid to late August in South Korea, the crop completes enough tillering before winter and goes into dormancy. Sown too early, it overgrows before winter and becomes vulnerable to cold; sown too late, tillering falls short and winter survival drops.

### 6-2. Seed Preparation

| Item | Details | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Selection | Choose large, uniform kernels | Sift through a sieve to remove small kernels and empty husks |
| Water flotation | Put in water and skim off whatever floats | Brine flotation works poorly with oat because of the husk |
| Removing wild oat | Pick out kernels with long awns and darker color | If mixed in, it increases every year |
| Seeding rate (drilled in rows) | 15-16kg per 1,000m² | That is 150-160kg per ha |
| Seeding rate (broadcast) | 20kg per 1,000m² | Work is quick, but a lot of seed is needed |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Oat requires a higher seeding rate than other grains, because the kernels are large and tillering is relatively limited. At 15kg per 1,000m², 5% of the 300kg harvest has to be set aside as seed, so this ratio must be kept in mind when making a seed-saving plan.

### 6-3. Seed Disinfection - When There Are No Chemicals

| Method | Procedure | Effect | Caution |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hot-water treatment | Soak in 50-55°C water for 10-15 minutes, then cool in cold water | Kills surface and near-surface pathogens such as smut | Above 60°C the embryo is killed |
| Wood-ash dusting | Coat dried seed with fine ash | Makes the surface alkaline and dry | Coarse ash makes sowing uneven |
| Wood vinegar soak | Soak for 6-12 hours in a 500-fold dilution | Surface disinfection and faster germination | Undiluted vinegar kills the seed |
| Selecting healthy seed | Take seed only from plants that showed no symptoms | Smut is seed-borne | The most fundamental method |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Oat smut is seed-borne. It is a disease in which the head turns into black powder, and once it enters a field it increases a little each year. Hot-water treatment is the most effective response, but temperature control is difficult. If there is no thermometer, mixing boiling water and cold water at a ratio of 2 to 3 gives roughly 50°C. That is hot enough that you can still keep a hand in it for about 3 seconds. If you are not confident, it is safer to rigorously exclude any plants that produced diseased heads from seed collection.

### 6-4. Planting

| Item | Standard | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Row spacing | 20-25cm | Fairly dense even among small-grain cereals |
| Sowing depth | 2-3cm | If deeper, emergence is poor |
| Order of operations | Plowing, sowing and fertilizing, covering with soil, firming | Firming comes last |
| Firming | Press down with feet, a plank, or a log | Raises the germination rate greatly |
| After broadcasting | Rake to cover the seed with soil | Exposed seed is eaten by birds |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Firming is especially effective with oat. Spring sowing takes place in a dry season, and autumn sowing has the problem of seed being lifted by winter frost heave; firming eases both. Pressing soil and seed into close contact lets water rise from the lower layer by capillary action, so germination becomes even, and in winter roots are less often heaved up by frost. Rolling a log or pressing a plank down by stepping on it is enough, and this half-day job makes a clear difference in the germination rate.
