## 7. Water Management

### 7-1. Standards by Growth Stage

| Period | Management | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Sowing to germination | Keep the soil from drying out | Germination needs moisture |
| Tillering | Leave it to natural rainfall | The moisture in this period governs the tiller count |
| Stem elongation | Supplement if dry | The stem grows rapidly in this period |
| Heading and grain filling | Supplement if dry | It determines how well the kernels fill |
| Harvest | Stop completely | Rain makes plants lodge and causes pre-harvest sprouting |
| The monsoon season (jangma, late June to July in South Korea) | Draining water is the management | Excess moisture brings Fusarium head blight and lodging |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Oat's water requirement, 400mm in total, is moderate. It neither uses extremely little, like sorghum or foxtail millet, nor a great deal, like rice. With South Korea's rainfall, natural precipitation alone is generally enough for both spring and autumn cultivation. When problems arise, they are usually from excess, not shortage.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Rain at harvest time is especially dangerous. Oat has a hollow stem, so when wet it cannot bear the weight and lodges, and when fallen heads touch the ground, pre-harvest sprouting occurs, in which the kernels sprout in the head. Sprouted kernels are hard to eat and cannot be stored. If rain is forecast, the right answer is to cut in a hurry rather than wait for full ripeness.

### 7-2. Symptoms of Water Shortage and Excess

| Symptom | Cause | Assessment and action |
|---|---|---|
| Leaves curl inward from the tips | Water shortage | Supplement water if it is the stem elongation or grain-filling stage |
| Few tillers and sparse plants | Water shortage in the tillering stage | Does not recover. Sow earlier the following year |
| Kernels shrivel and are light | Drought or heat during grain filling | Harvest earlier to salvage what remains |
| Lower leaves turn yellow and the base is dark | Excess moisture, root oxygen shortage | Dig furrows at once to drain the water |
| Heads turn red | Fusarium head blight. Humidity is the cause | Remove diseased heads and ensure ventilation |
| Stems lie down before ripening | Excess moisture combined with excess nitrogen | Recovery is difficult. Reduce fertilizer the following year |

> **In growing oats, water management is mostly a matter of draining water. Rain at harvest time in particular is the biggest cause of loss.**

## 8. Crop Management

### 8-1. Weed Management - The Early Stage Is Everything

| Timing | Method | Key point |
|---|---|---|
| Before sowing | Plow the field to bury existing weeds | Resets the starting line |
| 2-3 weeks after emergence | 1st weeding | Oat grows slowly at first and is crowded out by weeds |
| After tillering is complete | If needed | Once the plants spread, oat covers the ground |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Oat is rather slow in early growth. For two to three weeks after sprouting it is thin and tender and is easily smothered by weeds. Once it gets past that hurdle, tillering begins, the plants spread and cover the ground, and after that weeds can barely grow. This is why the weeding labor in oat growing is concentrated in the first month.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The most troublesome weed is wild oat. It is almost indistinguishable from cultivated oat, and when it ripens it drops its seed on its own and comes up again the next year. The key to telling them apart is the awn. Wild oat has long, twisted awns and a dark color. If you spot it after the heads emerge, always pull it up and take it out of the field. Leaving a single plant scatters hundreds of seeds over the field.

### 8-2. Preventing Lodging

| Item | Method | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Restraint with nitrogen | Do not exceed the standard rate | The most fundamental countermeasure |
| Following the seeding rate | Avoid over-seeding | Dense stands make stems thin |
| Top-dressing timing | Time it to a plant height of 20cm | Late top-dressing is the main culprit of overgrowth |
| Variety | Choose short, sturdy varieties | Consider this first if typhoons are frequent in the area |
| Drainage | Keep water from collecting | Roots loosen in wet ground |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Oat is prone to lodging even among small-grain cereals. The stems are hollow, the plant is tall, and the heads hang from the tips of the branches, so the center of gravity is high. At this scale, staking or tying is not possible, so prevention is everything. Of the five measures above, restraint with nitrogen is the most effective.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Once the crop has already gone down, there is not much that can be done. Still, even just propping up the parts where heads touch the ground with sticks can reduce pre-harvest sprouting and rot. If rain looks likely to continue, it is better to cut and bring in the lodged parts first.

### 8-3. Other Management

| Item | Details |
|---|---|
| Overwintering care | Autumn-sown crops have roots lifted by frost heave, so tread them down in early spring |
| Treading (stepping on plants) | In early March in South Korea, when the frozen ground thaws, tread on the plants to press them down |
| Bird scaring | After heading the heads droop and birds perch easily. Stretch lines of string |
| Residue handling | Use cut straw as mulch, livestock bedding, or compost material |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Treading is a long-standing operation for autumn-sown small-grain cereals. When the ground freezes and thaws repeatedly over winter, frost heave rises and lifts the roots, and if left alone, they dry up and die in spring. When the ground begins to thaw in early spring, pressing the roots back into the soil by walking on them or rolling them restores the plants. It is a job that protects post-winter yield with no tools or fertilizer.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Oat straw is never thrown away. It is soft among small-grain straws, so it can be used as livestock feed, it makes an excellent mulching material when spread on the field, and it is also good as a carbon source for a compost pile. In a disaster, every by-product other than the grain is a resource.

## 9. Pests and Diseases

### 9-1. Major Diseases

| Disease | Conditions | Symptoms | Response without chemicals |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fusarium head blight | Humidity during flowering and grain filling | Parts of the head turn red or pink | Drainage and ventilation, early harvest, discarding diseased heads |
| Smut | Seed-borne | The head turns into black powder | Hot-water treatment, selecting healthy seed, burning diseased heads |
| Leaf spot | Dense planting, excess nitrogen | Spots form on leaves and spread | Following the seeding rate, restraint with fertilizer, removing infected leaves |
| Crown rust | Cool, damp weather | Orange powder sticks to leaves | Resistant varieties, ensuring ventilation |
| Powdery mildew | Poor ventilation | White powder covers the leaf surface | Spraying diluted milk, keeping proper plant spacing |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The most dangerous disease in a disaster is Fusarium head blight. The yield loss is large, but the real problem is the toxin. This fungus produces mycotoxins such as deoxynivalenol, and this toxin does not disappear when heated. Eating infected oats causes vomiting and abdominal pain, and eating them over a long period leads to more serious problems.

> **Oats whose heads have turned red or pink do not become safe by washing or boiling. Throwing them away is the only way.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Prevention comes down to two things. First, set the cropping type so that flowering and grain filling do not overlap with the monsoon (jangma). Sowing the spring crop early and harvesting in early June in South Korea, or switching to the autumn crop, avoids this period. Second, if rain continues, do not wait for full ripeness; cut the crop, bring it in, and dry it indoors. Half of the reason oat has to avoid summer lies in this disease.

### 9-2. Major Pests

| Pest | Timing | Damage | Response without chemicals |
|---|---|---|---|
| Armyworm | Sudden outbreaks | Chews up leaves and stems in no time | Catch by hand as soon as an outbreak is confirmed; harvest early if severe |
| Aphids | Growing season | Suck sap and transmit viruses | Spraying soapy water, protecting natural enemies, restraint with nitrogen |
| Spider mites | Dry periods | Leaves turn yellow | Spraying water, soapy water |
| Birds | Grain filling | Perch on the drooping heads and eat the kernels | Stretching string, nets, noise-making devices |
| Field mice | Storage and overwintering | Eat kernels and roots | Tidying the area around the field, managing storage containers |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Oat is a crop with low pest and disease pressure. The A for pest-control self-reliance means that most problems can be avoided with cultural control alone, without chemicals. Because it grows in the cool season, it is out of step with the active period of insect pests, and because the growing period is short, there is no time for diseases to spread widely. In practice, the labor that goes into pest control in growing oats is very small.

### 9-3. Natural Pest-Control Materials

| Material | How to make | Target | Caution |
|---|---|---|---|
| Soapy water | 5-10g of pure soap per 1L of water | Aphids, spider mites | Spraying at midday burns the leaves |
| Diluted milk | Dilute with water 5-10 times | Powdery mildew | Spray on a clear morning |
| Wood vinegar | Dilute 500-1,000 times | Preventing mold diseases | If too strong, it causes chemical injury |
| Wood ash | Sprinkle on a morning with dew | Young larvae | Reapply if washed off by rain |
| Garlic infusion | Chop finely, soak for a day, and strain | Aphids | Strong smell |

### 9-4. Five Principles of Pest Control Without Chemicals

| Principle | Details | Application to oat |
|---|---|---|
| Avoidance | Sow and harvest so as to avoid the period when disease circulates | Avoiding the summer monsoon (jangma) is half of pest control in oat |
| Healthy seed | Do not take seed from diseased heads | Smut is seed-borne |
| Proper fertilization | Do not apply nitrogen beyond the standard rate | Excess nitrogen invites both leaf spot and lodging |
| Ventilation | Follow the seeding rate and plant spacing | Dense planting is a hotbed of mold diseases |
| Drainage | Keep water from collecting | Removes the conditions for Fusarium head blight |

## 10. Nutrient Deficiency and Excess

| Symptom | Cause | Remedy with natural materials |
|---|---|---|
| The whole plant turns yellow and plants are small | Nitrogen deficiency | Well-rotted compost, or urine diluted 10-fold, at about 20cm plant height |
| Leaf edges scorch | Potassium deficiency | Scatter wood ash |
| Growth is slow and leaves are dark bluish-green | Phosphorus deficiency | Mix bone meal into compost and apply |
| Only the tissue between the veins is yellow, while the veins stay green | Magnesium deficiency | Dolomite powder, diversifying compost materials |
| New leaves turn white | Manganese or iron deficiency | Add leaf mold, stop overuse of wood ash |
| Leaf tips dry out gray | Copper deficiency. Common in peat or sandy soils | Diversify compost materials |
| Only the foliage is lush, and plants lodge | Nitrogen excess | Stop nitrogen. If already too late, reduce it the next year |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Manganese deficiency is something to be aware of with oat in particular. Oat has a relatively high manganese requirement even among small-grain cereals, and when the soil tips toward alkaline, manganese is bound and cannot be absorbed. This problem can appear in fields where a lot of wood ash has been scattered year after year. If new leaves turn white and fold as if broken in the middle, suspect manganese deficiency. The response is to reduce the use of wood ash and to lower the soil pH by adding leaf mold or fallen-leaf compost.

## 11. Intercropping and Crop Rotation

### 11-1. Compatibility

| Compatibility | Crop | Effect |
|:---:|---|---|
| Very good | Soybean, adzuki bean, cowpea (following crop) | Restores nitrogen and complements the amino acid profile |
| Very good | Potato, sweet potato (following crop) | Oat's allelochemicals lower nematode density beforehand |
| Good | Clover, Chinese milk vetch | Planted together, they serve as both green manure and fodder |
| Good | Vegetables (following crop) | Summer vegetables use the plot harvested in June |
| Fair | Barley, wheat | Same cultivation methods make management easy, but they share diseases |
| Poor | Oat after oat | Tolerates continuous cropping, but disease density builds up |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Oat's special value lies in its effect on the following crop. Oat roots release allelochemicals that noticeably lower the density of soil nematodes. Nematodes do heavy damage to root crops such as potato, sweet potato, and carrot, and where chemicals cannot be used there is almost no way to respond. Putting in oat for one year to reduce nematodes before planting root crops is one of the few soil-management tools that can be used without chemicals. Plowing under forage oat as green manure is even more effective.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;After spring oat is harvested in June in South Korea, the field has four months left until autumn. If soybean, foxtail millet, or autumn vegetables are planted there, two harvests are taken from the same land in a year. Double cropping thus partly offsets oat's weakness of Calories per area C.

### 11-2. Rotation Order

| Year | Crop | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| Year 1 | Oat | Reduces nematodes and suppresses weeds |
| Year 2 | Legumes (soybean, adzuki bean, cowpea) | Restores nitrogen |
| Year 3 | Potato or root vegetables | Takes advantage of land with reduced nematodes |
| Year 4 | Back to oat | Disease density has dropped |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Oat's Continuous-cropping tolerance is SS, so it is robust against repeated planting itself. The reason rotation is still recommended is that the density of smut and leaf spot builds up year after year. If land is too limited to allow rotation, it is better to observe at least the minimum of not putting spring oat and autumn oat in the same spot in succession.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Combining cropping types within a single year is also useful. The scheme is to plant oat in autumn, let it overwinter and harvest it in early summer of the following year, then plant foxtail millet or soybean in the same spot and harvest in autumn. The winter field is not left idle, two harvests, in spring and in autumn, come in, and because the crop changes, disease density is also managed. It is an arrangement that draws the maximum calories from the limited land available in a disaster.
