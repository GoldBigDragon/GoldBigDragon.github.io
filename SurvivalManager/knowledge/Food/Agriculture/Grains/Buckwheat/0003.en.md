## 3. Growing Conditions

### 3-1. Limits and Optimal Values

| Condition | Optimal | Tolerable Range | Outside That Range |
|---|---|---|---|
| Growing temperature | 15-25°C | 5-30°C | Growth stops below 5°C and frost kills the plant |
| Flowering temperature | 17-19°C | Below 30°C | Above 30°C, fertilization fails and only flowers appear |
| Germination temperature | 25-31°C | Minimum 0-4.8°C | Germinates even at very low temperatures |
| Grain-filling temperature | 17-29°C | Range 12°C | Seed doesn't fill properly at high temperatures |
| Light | 6-8 hours a day | 4 hours or more | Retains 88% of yield even at 40% shade |
| Precipitation | 70mm from germination to flowering, 20mm from full bloom to maturity | Total 250mm | Very vulnerable to waterlogging |
| Soil | Well-drained sandy loam to loam | Most soil types | Fails in wet, clayey soil |
| Soil acidity | Very wide tolerance | Grows well in acidic soil | Struggles in alkaline soil high in limestone |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The narrowest item in this table is flowering temperature. The optimum is 17-19°C, and fertilization fails above 30°C. Every plan for growing buckwheat starts with fitting the flowering stage into this temperature window. That's why sowing time is set so that summer buckwheat flowers in early summer and fall buckwheat flowers in early fall.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The acidity item is unusual. Buckwheat grows well in acidic soil and struggles instead in alkaline soil high in limestone — the opposite of most crops. In a long-term disaster, when the supply of lime fertilizer is cut off and soil gradually turns acidic, the very condition that saps other crops is not a problem for buckwheat. That's why buckwheat is well suited as the first crop to revive an old field that has turned acidic.

> **Do not apply lime to buckwheat. The treatment that helps other crops harms this one.**

### 3-2. Choosing a Site in a Disaster

| Site | Suitability | Reason |
|---|:---:|---|
| An already-tilled empty field | Best | Energy return is low, so breaking new ground is a loss |
| A spot where another crop failed | Best | Already tilled, and fits a situation where it's too late for other crops |
| An old field that's turned acidic | Very good | Excellent tolerance for acidity |
| Poor, gravelly land; cleared burn plots | Good | Low fertilizer demand |
| Partial shade | Usable | A C3 crop, so it tolerates shade well |
| Limestone-rich land | Unsuitable | One of the rare conditions where buckwheat struggles |
| Wet, clayey soil; low-lying land | Unsuitable | Dies from waterlogging right after sowing and in early growth |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The core principle for choosing a site for buckwheat differs from other crops. Planting buckwheat on the best land is usually a loss, because maize or sorghum on that same spot would yield three to four times the calories. Buckwheat is a crop for the leftover spot, the late spot, the bad spot — that is its reason for existing.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Waterlogging is the most common cause of buckwheat failure. It's especially vulnerable right after sowing and in early growth. If the seed sits submerged during several days of rain, it simply rots, and young seedlings die because their roots can't breathe. Drainage channels must be finished before sowing, without exception.

## 4. Varieties

### 4-1. Ecotype - The Most Common Mistake

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Buckwheat divides into two ecotypes based on its response to day length and temperature. Plant it without knowing this, and you can end up with flowers blooming in profusion while not a single seed sets.

| Ecotype | Characteristics | Representative Varieties | Optimal Sowing Time |
|---|---|---|---|
| Summer buckwheat (spring-sown type) | Sown in spring, harvested in summer | Yangjeolmemil, Yangjeolmemil No. 2 | Central-northern South Korea: late April-early May; southern South Korea: early-mid April |
| Fall buckwheat (summer-sown type) | Sown in summer, harvested in fall | Daesanmemil | Central-northern South Korea: mid-late July; southern South Korea: early-mid August |

> **Sow fall buckwheat in spring, and it flowers but sets almost no seed. This is the most common, and most costly, mistake in growing buckwheat.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Fall buckwheat is built to set seed under short days. Sown in spring, it coincides with the days growing longer, so it never shifts into reproductive growth and just keeps building leaves and stems. Flowers open, but seed doesn't set. Summer buckwheat, by contrast, has been bred to set seed even under long days, which is what makes spring sowing possible.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In a disaster, if the source of the seed is unknown, this problem can genuinely occur. When you're not sure, the safe approach is to sow small amounts at both the summer and fall timings, see which one sets seed, and match that timing from the following year on. You lose one season, but it's a cheap price to pay for knowing which type you have.

### 4-2. Comparison of Major Varieties

| Variety | Ecotype | Days to Maturity | Plant Height | Flower Color | Seed Color | 1,000-Seed Weight (g) | Yield (kg/1,000m²) |
|---|---|:---:|---|---|---|:---:|:---:|
| Yangjeolmemil | Summer type | 68 days | Short stem | White | Black | 26.3 | 121 |
| Yangjeolmemil No. 2 | Summer type | 68 days | Short stem | White | Blackish brown | 29.5 | 155 |
| Daesanmemil | Fall type | 73 days | Medium stem | White | Brown | 23.7 | 130 |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Yangjeolmemil was designated a nationally recommended variety across South Korea in 1995. Because of its determinate growth habit, it ripens more evenly than indeterminate varieties, and it's early-maturing, short-stemmed, high-yielding, and high in rutin. Even after maturing, it sheds little seed and resists lodging well. It's a variety that substantially improves on buckwheat's classic weaknesses: shattering and uneven ripening.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Daesanmemil is a fall-buckwheat variety bred in 1999, and it's also well suited to growing sprouts. Its sprout yield is a high 970%, which makes it the better choice if you plan to sprout it indoors in winter as a stand-in for vegetables.

### 4-3. Selection Criteria by Situation

| Situation | Recommendation | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Sowing in spring | Yangjeolmemil, Yangjeolmemil No. 2 | Summer type; sets seed from a spring sowing |
| Sowing in summer | Daesanmemil | Fall type; sets seed as days grow shorter |
| Maximizing yield | Yangjeolmemil No. 2 | Highest at 155kg per 1,000m² |
| Concerned about shattering | Yangjeolmemil | Determinate growth gives even ripening and little seed drop |
| Growing sprouts to eat | Daesanmemil | Sprout yield is 970% |
| Seed saving is difficult | Tartary buckwheat | Self-pollinating, so no isolation is needed |
| Rutin is the goal | Tartary buckwheat | 70-100× that of common buckwheat |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The last two entries are worth noting. Tartary buckwheat is barely grown in South Korea because of its taste, but from a disaster-preparedness standpoint it has two major advantages. First, it self-pollinates and sets seed even without insects — a decisive difference in a situation where bees have declined. Second, having no self-incompatibility, it needs no isolation when saving seed, and the variety stays stable. Its bitterness is reduced considerably by hulling it well and rinsing it several times.

## 5. Field Preparation and Fertilizer

### 5-1. Preparing the Ground

| Item | Details | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Tilling | Shallow tilling is fine. 20cm is enough | Energy return is low, so deep tilling is a loss |
| Drainage channels | Must be dug before sowing | Waterlogging right after sowing is the biggest cause of failure |
| Raised bed | Can be low, but keep a channel for water | Just needs to prevent standing water |
| Leveling | Break the soil up evenly | The seed is small; if it sinks too deep, it can't emerge |
| Organic matter | Well-rotted compost, 1,000-1,500kg/1,000m² | No need to apply a lot |
| Lime | Don't apply | Buckwheat struggles in alkaline soil |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Field preparation for buckwheat is far lighter than for other crops. Shallow tilling is fine, and there's no need to build up a high raised bed. This is a real, practical advantage of buckwheat, because in a situation where you have to sow quickly on ground where another crop failed, there's no time to break new ground properly. Scratch the surface once where the previous crop was cleared, sow, and buckwheat will grow.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The one thing that cannot be skipped, though, is drainage. Most buckwheat failures trace back to this. If rain continues for several days right after sowing, the seed sits submerged and rots, and young seedlings die within days. Every other step can be skipped, but not drainage.

### 5-2. Standard Fertilization Rate (per 1,000m², 300 pyeong)

| Fertilizer | Rate (kg/1,000m²) | Notes |
|---|:---:|---|
| Nitrogen (N) | 5.4 | The full amount as base fertilizer is enough |
| Phosphorus (P) | 3.1 | Full amount as base fertilizer |
| Potassium (K) | 3.9 | Full amount as base fertilizer |
| Borax | 2-3 | Raises the seed-set rate |
| Compost | 1,000-1,500 | Well-rotted compost |
| Silicate | As needed | Reduces lodging |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Buckwheat has very strong nutrient uptake. Its taproot, with abundant root hairs, thoroughly draws in whatever nutrients remain in the soil, and it can even use forms of phosphorus other crops cannot. That's why it grows even with almost no fertilizer applied.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;But this strong uptake comes at a price. When buckwheat strips the soil of nutrients, the crop that follows it suffers a shortage. Saying buckwheat needs fertilizer is really about the crop after it, more than about buckwheat itself. If you plan to plant another crop after buckwheat, you need to either fertilize the buckwheat or add fertilizer after harvesting it.

### 5-3. When There's No Chemical Fertilizer - Disaster Substitutes

| Substitute | Nutrients Supplied | How to Make and Use It | Caution |
|---|---|---|---|
| Well-rotted compost | Comprehensive | Layer fallen leaves, grass, and manure and let it rot for 3-6 months | A small amount is enough for buckwheat |
| Diluted urine | Nitrogen, potassium | Dilute 10× with water and pour into the furrow during early growth | Too much causes leggy growth and lodging |
| Wood ash | Potassium, phosphorus, calcium | Use only a small amount | Alkaline, so overuse must be avoided with buckwheat |
| Bone meal | Phosphorus, calcium | Burn, crush, and mix into compost | Buckwheat uses phosphorus efficiently, so little is needed |
| Boron from ash | Boron | Present in trace amounts in seaweed ash and various plant ashes | Hard to measure an exact dose |
| Legume green manure | Nitrogen | Turn under a legume as the preceding crop | Can be excessive for buckwheat |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The thing to watch for with buckwheat is wood ash. For other crops it's an excellent substitute fertilizer supplying potassium and phosphorus, but since buckwheat struggles in alkaline soil, applying a lot of it actually does harm. It's safer to limit it to leftovers from other crops, no more than 10kg per 1,000m².

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Boron has a noticeable effect on buckwheat's seed-set rate. If borax is available, mix 2-3kg into the base fertilizer or apply it as a foliar spray before flowering. If borax can't be obtained, the next-best option is to diversify your compost materials. Legume stems and leaves, seaweed, and fruit peels are relatively rich in boron.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Buckwheat's zero-input yield retention is 45.1%. Even with no fertilizer applied at all, it still yields nearly half of normal. Since its baseline requirement is already among the lowest of any crop, at 12g/m², compost alone can in practice cover most of it. Buckwheat is the crop you can still plant when fertilizer has run out completely.

## 6. Sowing

### 6-1. Timing

| Cropping Type | Region | Sowing | Harvest |
|---|---|---|---|
| Summer buckwheat | Central-northern South Korea | Late April-early May | Early-mid July |
| Summer buckwheat | Southern South Korea | Early-mid April | Late June-early July |
| Fall buckwheat | Central-northern South Korea | Mid-late July | Early-mid October |
| Fall buckwheat | Southern South Korea | Early-mid August | Late October-mid November |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The benchmark for timing fall buckwheat's sowing is the first frost. It must be sown 10 to 12 weeks before the first frost arrives. Sow too early, and flowering falls into midsummer heat, so fertilization fails, and heat combined with humidity also brings disease. Sow too late, and frost catches the grain while it's still filling. This window is narrower than it looks.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In a disaster, buckwheat is for sowing in haste on ground where another crop has failed. In that case, the rule is simple: are there 70 days left before the first frost? If yes, sow; if not, don't. In central South Korea, if the first frost is taken as mid-October, the cutoff is early August.

### 6-2. Sowing Methods

| Method | Sowing Rate (kg/1,000m²) | Spacing | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|:---:|---|---|---|
| Broadcast sowing | 8-10 | None | Fastest to do | Uses more seed and is harder to manage |
| Narrow-row sowing | 6-8 | 30cm between rows | Allows weeding | Takes more labor |
| Hill sowing | 0.5-2 | 30cm between rows, 15cm between plants | Uses the least seed | Takes the most labor |

| Item | Standard | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Sowing depth | 2-5cm | Emergence rate drops beyond 5cm |
| Soil covering | Light | The seed is large; just covering it with soil is enough |
| Firming | Press down lightly | Brings the seed into close contact with the soil |
| Density | Not too sparse | Self-incompatible, so pollination needs many plants |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;If you have to sow in a hurry, broadcast sowing is the right answer. It uses more seed, but it's fast, and buckwheat sorts itself out even when crowded. Scratch the field once, scatter the seed, then rake it over again to cover it with soil — that's all. In half a day you can sow a considerable area.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;That density can't be too low is a caution specific to buckwheat. Because it's self-incompatible, it can only set seed by receiving pollen from another plant. Plant just a few scattered stems and there's no partner to carry pollen to, so no seed sets. The rule is to sow in a clustered block, even if the area is small.

### 6-3. Seed Preparation and Disinfection

| Method | Procedure | Effect | Caution |
|---|---|---|---|
| Water sorting | Put seed in water and skim off what floats | Screens out empty seed | Buckwheat's hull is large and floats easily; judge carefully |
| Hot-water treatment | Soak 10-15 minutes in 50-55°C water, then cool in cold water | Kills surface pathogens | Above 60°C, the seed dies |
| Wood-ash dusting | Coat the seed with fine ash | Disinfects and dries at the same time | Use only a small amount |
| Germination check | Always do this before sowing | Buckwheat seed has a short lifespan | Essential for seed more than 1 year old |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;For buckwheat, checking germination matters more than disinfecting the seed. At room temperature, germination rate starts declining around 8 months and drops sharply by 1-2 years. Unlike other grains, you can't stockpile it for several years and then use it. When using old seed, you must test it beforehand and adjust the sowing rate.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Water sorting needs care. Because buckwheat's hull is large and structured with hollow space, even a sound, well-filled seed can sometimes float. It's safer to skim off only what floats completely and keep what stays suspended in between.

### 6-4. Mulching and Other Preparations

| Item | Details | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Straw mulching | After sowing, spread a thin layer of dry grass or straw | Retains moisture, suppresses weeds, prevents soil from splashing in rain |
| Plastic mulching | Use it if available | Raises soil temperature, speeding emergence and early growth |
| Drainage channels | Must be maintained before and after sowing | Prevents waterlogging in early growth |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In a disaster, plastic is hard to obtain, so straw mulching is the realistic option. Buckwheat germinates fast and comes up in three to four days, so the covering must be very thin — too thick, and the sprout can't push through. If you're sowing where a previous crop was cut down, simply leaving its residue in place is enough.
