## 15. Storage Without Refrigeration

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Buckwheat's storability is SS. Based on the median of three FAO-measured species in the same order (Caryophyllales), the storage loss rate is a low 1.25%, and the whole seed dried in its hull keeps for more than three years at room temperature. Its fat content is a low 2.2%, so there's less risk of rancidity than with oats, and the hard triangular hull provides physical protection on top of that. As grains go, it's on the easy-to-store end.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;For seed stock, though, it's a different matter. Storability for eating and longevity as seed are separate things, and buckwheat seed's germination rate drops sharply after one to two years. That means grain for eating can be kept for three years, but seed for planting needs to be freshly saved every year.

### 15-1. Three Enemies of Storage

| Enemy | Condition | Result | Response axis |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mold | Moisture 15% or more | Spoilage, toxin production, total loss | Drying |
| Insects | Temperature 20 degrees or more, moisture 12% or more | Eat into the kernels | Sealing and cold, pest control |
| Rats | Accessible containers | Eat and defecate, contaminating them | Physical barriers |

### 15-2. Storage Conditions

| Condition | Target | If out of range |
|---|---|---|
| Moisture | 13-14%, seed 12-13% | Above 15%, mold grows |
| Temperature | Ideally 15 degrees or below; lower is better | Seed longevity is especially sensitive to temperature |
| Humidity | Surrounding air 65% or below | Dry seed reabsorbs moisture |
| Light | Complete block | Rutin breaks down in light |
| Form | Always keep in the hull | Hulling sharply shortens longevity |
| Checks | Once every two months | Catching problems early prevents loss |

> **Store buckwheat in the hull and only hull as much as you'll eat. The triangular hull is nature's sturdiest grain packaging.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Buckwheat's hull is different in nature from other grains' bran. It isn't attached to the kernel but a hard shell loosely fitted over it, so it physically blocks impact, moisture, and insects. Stored in the hull, it lasts three years, but hulled buckwheat groats keep far less time, and ground into flour, a few months is the limit. This gap is wider than in other grains.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The requirement to block light is also specific to buckwheat. Rutin breaks down in light, so leaving it in a bright place for a long time reduces the compound that gives buckwheat its characteristic properties. The calories and protein stay the same, but the part that makes buckwheat distinctively buckwheat disappears.

### 15-3. Storage Containers

| Container | Sealing | Duration | Advantage | Disadvantage |
|---|:---:|---|---|---|
| Earthenware jar (onggi) | Good | 2-3 years | Steady temperature, blocks light completely | Heavy and breakable |
| Metal can | Very good | 3 years or more | Completely blocks rats and insects | If condensation forms, that spot spoils |
| Glass jar | Very good | 3 years or more | Condition can be checked by eye | Must be wrapped to block light |
| Thick plastic | Moderate | 1-2 years | Light and cheap | Rats chew through it |
| Straw sack/burlap bag | Poor | 6 months-1 year | Allows ventilation | Buckwheat's round seeds roll out through gaps |
| Underground storage pit | Good | 2-3 years | Low, steady temperature | If water seeps in, the whole stock is lost |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Because buckwheat is triangular and rolls easily, it leaks out more readily than other grains. It escapes through the stitching gaps of sacks and gaps around jar lids, and those same gaps become passages for insects and rats. Choosing a seamless container and sealing the lid securely matters especially here.

### 15-4. Pest Control During Storage - Without Chemicals

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The real main enemy of grain storage is insects. Pests like the Angoumois grain moth, the Indian meal moth, rice weevils, and the foreign grain beetle are already present as eggs in the grain right after harvest and hatch inside storage. One insect becomes dozens within two months, and within half a year, a single jar fills with powder and droppings. Here are methods that work without pesticides.

| Method | Principle | Application | Effect |
|---|---|---|---|
| Total sealing | Cuts off oxygen and suffocates insects | Fill the container fully and seal it | Most reliable. Less empty space is better |
| Mixing in wood ash | Fine ash particles scratch insects' exoskeletons and dehydrate them | Mix in evenly at 2-5% of the grain volume | A long-proven method. Wash off before eating |
| Diatomaceous earth | Fine silica shells damage the exoskeleton | Mix in 1-3g per 1kg of grain | More effective than ash. Only works when dry |
| Fine sand/loess | Grains fill the gaps and block insect movement | Put in as alternating layers | Buckwheat's seeds are large, so it's easy to sieve apart later |
| Dried chili/Sichuan pepper | The pungent compounds act as a repellent | Place a few inside the container | A supplementary measure |
| Mugwort, sweet flag, pagoda tree leaves | Essential-oil compounds suppress egg-laying | Dry and layer them in | Traditionally widely used |
| Roasted salt | Traps moisture and removes insects' living conditions | Wrap in cloth and place inside | Doubles as a desiccant |
| Charcoal | Absorbs moisture and odor | Wrap dried, fired charcoal in cloth and place inside | Also helps prevent mold |
| Cold treatment | Eggs and larvae die below freezing | Set outside for a few days in midwinter, then bring back in | Watch for condensation when bringing it back in |
| Heat treatment | Eggs and larvae die at 60 degrees | Spread thin in the sun for a few hours | Do not use for seed stock |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The most important one is the first. Total sealing is the most powerful tool available without any chemicals. Insects breathe too, so inside a sealed container, as the grain and insects use up oxygen, carbon dioxide builds up and oxygen falls until the insects die. The fuller the container and the less empty space, the faster it works. Because buckwheat is triangular, a lot of space remains between the grains, so it's best to fill it, shake it down, and refill it two or three times.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Wood ash and diatomaceous earth work on the same principle. Both kill physically, not chemically. Fine, sharp particles scratch off the waxy layer covering an insect's body, and the insect loses moisture and dries out. This approach has two advantages: insects cannot develop resistance, and it's harmless to people. Washing it off with water before eating is enough, and eating a little of it causes no problem. The drawback is that it doesn't work in the presence of moisture. If the grain gets damp, both ash and diatomaceous earth lose their effect, so drying always comes first.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;When using ash, sieve it and use only the fine part, like flour. Mix in about 2-5% of the grain's volume — roughly one doe (about 1.8 L) of ash per mal (about 18 L) of buckwheat — and stir by hand so a thin coat covers every grain's surface. Buckwheat's smooth hull doesn't hold ash well, so it helps to stir it a few more times after mixing. It looks gray and dusty on the surface, but since the hull is removed before eating anyway, almost none of the ash reaches the kernel itself. That's why ash pest control is especially clean for buckwheat.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There is a caution, though. Using a lot of ash on buckwheat meant for seed can let its alkalinity work against germination, since buckwheat is a crop that prefers acidic soil. For seed stock, it's safer to use dried chili or charcoal instead of ash, or if ash is used, to keep it to a very small amount.

### 15-5. Regular Checks

| Timing | What to check | Action if abnormal |
|---|---|---|
| Every two months | Condensation on the container's outside, moldy smell | Open immediately and spread out to dry |
| Every two months | Powder or spiderweb-like threads on the surface | This is the Indian meal moth. Sun-dry the whole batch and sieve it out |
| Every two months | Warmth when grasped by hand | Heat is a sign of excess moisture |
| Spring and autumn | Germination test for seed stock | Buckwheat's germination rate drops quickly |
| Before the monsoon season (jangma, late June to July in South Korea) | Recheck the seal | Reseal if it has loosened |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The check that buckwheat needs more often than other grains is the seed germination test. Because germination rate starts to drop at around eight months, checking once each in autumn and spring — even outside the sowing season — can head off losing a seed line altogether.

## 16. Cooking and Processing

### 16-1. Milling - Making Flour

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;After hulling, buckwheat is mostly eaten as flour. The kernel breaks easily, so it isn't suited to cooking whole like rice, but milling it into flour widens its uses to noodles, muk (a savory starch jelly), pan-fried cakes, and more.

| Method | Tool | Characteristics |
|---|---|---|
| Millstone | Two stone disks, upper and lower | Produces the finest flour. Buckwheat grinds easily |
| Mortar | Mortar and pestle | Produces coarse flour. Sieve and pound again |
| Grinding on a flat stone | Flat stone and a rolling stone | Suited to small amounts |
| Roasting then pounding | Any tool | Less effort, and a nutty aroma comes through |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Among grains, buckwheat is relatively easy to mill. Its kernel isn't hard like maize but has a structure that breaks easily, so it turns to flour fairly easily even with just a mortar. This is a real practical advantage of buckwheat. Its Cooking & Processing grade is D, but that reflects the fact that a milling step is required, not that the milling itself is especially hard. A millstone-level tool can be made by hand, and that tool handles it well enough.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;To make a millstone, choose two hard stones, chip the faces that meet each other rough, drill a hole in the center of the upper stone for the grain to go in, and fit a handle. It works even without fine craftsmanship. With the same millstone, widening the gap between the upper and lower stones does hulling, and narrowing it does milling, so one tool handles both steps.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Buckwheat flour's character changes with the degree of milling. Flour milled fine and white tastes mild but has less rutin and minerals, while coarse flour ground to include the parts near the hull is darker in color and stronger in taste but far better in nutrition. In a disaster, there's no time to spare for refining, so the coarse kind results naturally, and that's actually the better one nutritionally.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Flour spoils far faster than whole grain. The rule is to grind only as much as you'll eat in a month and leave the rest in the hull.

### 16-2. Having No Gluten

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Buckwheat flour has no gluten. This cuts both ways.

| Aspect | Details |
|---|---|
| Advantage | Even people who cannot eat wheat can eat it. It digests gently |
| Limitation | The dough doesn't become sticky, making it hard to form into noodles or bread |
| Solution | Mix in wheat flour or starch, or scald the dough with hot water |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;When making buckwheat noodles, wheat flour is mixed in not for flavor but to hold the dough together. Pure buckwheat dough breaks apart and can't be drawn into noodles. If there's no wheat flour, mix in potato starch or sweet potato starch. If there's none of that either, there's the method of scalding the dough with hot water. Pouring in boiling water partially gelatinizes the starch, giving the dough enough stickiness to hold together after a fashion. This is the traditional method used for makguksu (cold buckwheat noodles).

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;It's often easier to make it into a completely different form instead. Porridge, pan-fried cakes, and muk don't need the dough to be sticky. In a disaster, there's no reason to insist on noodles specifically.

### 16-3. Fuel-Saving Cooking

| Method | Fuel | Procedure | Notes |
|---|:---:|---|---|
| Flour porridge | Very little | Mix flour into water and boil 5-10 minutes | Most fuel-efficient |
| Buckwheat muk | Medium | Mix flour into water, boil while stirring, then cool and let it set | Keeps for several days once made |
| Pan-fried cakes | Little | Fry the batter thin | If too thick, the inside won't cook through |
| Whole-grain rice-style | A lot | Soak, then cook | Rarely used because the grain breaks apart |
| Noodles | Medium | Knead, draw out, and boil | Boiling time is short, so fuel use is low |
| Residual-heat cooking | Very little | Boil, then turn off the heat and wrap to retain warmth | Cuts fuel use by more than half |
| Roasted flour | Almost none | Stir pre-roasted flour into water | No cooking fuel needed |
| Buckwheat tea | Little | Steep roasted grain in water | Do it while boiling water anyway |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Buckwheat is a crop that needs little cooking fuel. As flour, it cooks in 5 to 10 minutes, and even noodles need only around 2 minutes of boiling. That's a big difference compared to having to boil whole maize kernels for an hour. In a situation with extremely scarce fuel, this trait of buckwheat partly makes up for its weak yield.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Buckwheat muk is an especially useful form in a disaster. Made once, it keeps for several days, it can be stretched a great deal by using more water, and it's very filling. The method is simple. Mix buckwheat flour into water to a thick consistency, then boil over low heat while stirring continuously. Once it turns translucent and thickens, pour it into a bowl and let it cool to set. It's the most sensible way to cook when grain is scarce and needs to be stretched further.

### 16-4. Buckwheat Sprouts - Something Green in Winter

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Buckwheat has one more use that other grains don't: sprouting the seed and eating it like a vegetable.

| Step | Details | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Soak buckwheat still in the hull in water for 6-12 hours | Sprouting works better with the hull on |
| 2 | Drain and spread on a dish or cloth | If water pools, it rots |
| 3 | Sprinkle water two or three times a day to keep it moist | Just don't let it dry out |
| 4 | Kept in a dark place, it sprouts in 3-4 days | The sprouts come up yellow |
| 5 | Exposed to light, it turns green within two days | Rutin and vitamins increase |
| 6 | At 5-7 days, once it's 5-10cm, cut and eat it | Pick out the hulls before eating |

> **This is a way to get fresh greens in winter in five days, with no soil and no field. In a season with no vitamins otherwise, knowing this makes a real difference.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Buckwheat sprouts contain far more rutin and vitamins than the seed does. When fresh vegetables disappear completely in winter, vitamin deficiency sets in gradually — bleeding gums, wounds that won't heal, a loss of energy. Along with soybean sprouts and radish sprouts, buckwheat sprouts are one of the few ways to get something green without soil in that season. All that's needed is seed, water, and a container, and five days. Daesan buckwheat has a sprout yield of 970%, making it especially well suited to this use.

### 16-5. Processing That Doubles as Storage

| Product | Procedure | Storage | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Roasted grain | Hull it and roast over low heat | Sealed, 6 months | Steep as tea or eat as is |
| Roasted flour | Roast, then grind | Sealed, 3-6 months | Mix into water and eat right away |
| Dried muk | Slice buckwheat muk thin and dry it | 1 year | Soak in water and add to soup or stew |
| Buckwheat starch | Soak in water, grind, and dry the settled sediment | Several years | Volume shrinks and insects don't get in |
| Buckwheat honey | If you keep hives, harvest it during flowering | Several years | Dark in color and high in iron |
| Buckwheat hulls | Dry and collect them | Indefinite | Pillow filling, insulation, kindling |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Dried muk is a method long used in Gangwon Province. Sliced thin and fully sun-dried, buckwheat muk hardens and keeps for a year. Soaked in water before eating, it returns to its original form. Its reduced volume and weight make it easier to store and transport, and it lets muk that would otherwise spoil within days be kept for a long time instead.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Buckwheat honey is a side income from growing buckwheat. Buckwheat is a prime nectar crop, and bees gather in large numbers during its flowering period. If you keep hives, a spot near a buckwheat field is the best location. Buckwheat honey is dark in color, strong in aroma, and rich in iron and antioxidants. In a situation where sugar has disappeared, honey is nearly the only concentrated sweetener available, and it keeps indefinitely without spoiling.

### 17-1. Five Things Not to Forget

| Item | Details |
|---|---|
| Ecotype | Sowing autumn buckwheat in spring produces flowers only, with no seed. Always check this |
| Drainage | Waterlogging right after sowing is the most common cause of failure. Drainage comes first |
| No treatment during flowering | Self-incompatible, so without bees there's no fruit-set. Spray nothing |
| Harvest timing | Cut when 75-80% is ripe. Waiting for full ripeness loses the lower grains |
| Allergy | Anyone eating it for the first time should start with a very small amount. Without medical care, anaphylaxis is fatal |
