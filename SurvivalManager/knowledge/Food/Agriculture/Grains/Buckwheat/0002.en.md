### 1-3. How to Read the Strengths

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Seven criteria carry a grade of S or higher: protein density S, speed to harvest S, fertilizer demand S, water use S, infrastructure needs SS, pest-control self-reliance SS, storability SS, continuous-cropping tolerance SS. For a crop with an overall grade of C, having this many top-tier grades is unusual. The reason is a single criterion: calories per area at D drags everything else down.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Speed to harvest at S is buckwheat's defining trait. At 60-75 days, it's faster than any other crop in this document. Foxtail millet comes close at 65-80 days, but buckwheat is a bit faster, and more importantly, it can be sown on the spur of the moment, in a spot no one had planned for. Sow buckwheat in July after summer crops were wiped out by drought, in May after a late frost killed the spring planting, or on a field just plowed under after disease, and you harvest something that same year. No crop fits the term "famine-relief crop" more precisely than this one.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Protein density at S is a grade grains rarely earn. Protein makes up 13.9% of calories, 1.4× the requirement line, and it earned a bonus for an essential amino-acid score above 90. That last point is decisive. Every grass grain, without exception, falls short on lysine, so its protein profile is never complete without legumes — but buckwheat, being in the knotweed family, escapes that limit. It has enough lysine. Without legumes, buckwheat on its own comes close to delivering complete protein.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Pest-control self-reliance at SS is also substantial. Buckwheat is never sprayed. Its growing period is too short for disease to spread, the list of known pests and diseases is short to begin with, and none of them is deadly. Being a crop with nothing to attend to even when pesticides have disappeared entirely cuts both labor and risk at once.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Continuous-cropping tolerance at SS has a special story behind it. Buckwheat is not only strong under repeated planting — simply growing it cleans up the field. Substances released from its roots lower soil nematode populations, and its fast growth covers the ground and smothers weeds. That's why it's called a cleaning crop in the West and used to reclaim fields. It's one of the few tools for reviving a field when neither pesticides nor plastic mulch are available.

> **Buckwheat's value isn't yield — it's timing and protein. It's a crop that delivers something in two months, in the very place where every other plan has fallen apart.**

### 1-4. How to Read the Weaknesses

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;No criterion received an E. The lowest are calories per area D, perenniality D, cold hardiness D, and tillage & digging energy D.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Calories per area at D determines everything else. 561kcal/m² is 26% of maize's and 43% of sorghum's. This number alone means buckwheat cannot be a staple. However much land you have, if maize or sorghum can grow on it, that's the better choice. There are only two situations where buckwheat is the right pick: when it's too late in the season to sow anything else, and when the land is too poor for anything else to grow.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Tillage & digging energy at D is another expression of the same problem. An energy return of 3.9× means you get back less than four times the energy you spent tilling the field. Compared with maize's 16.8×, that's a fourfold gap. Under conditions where the field has to be tilled by human muscle, this efficiency is seriously poor. Breaking new ground for buckwheat is a loss in most cases; sowing it into a field that's already been tilled and left idle is the sensible use.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Cold hardiness at D narrows the growing window. Growth stops at 5°C or below, and it's frost-tender. Fall buckwheat must be sown 10 to 12 weeks before the first frost; any later, and it gets caught by frost while the grain is still filling.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Seed longevity C and seed saving A need to be considered together. Buckwheat seed loses germination noticeably within 1-2 years at room temperature. Some reports say the decline starts as early as 32 weeks, or about 8 months. Unlike other grains, you can't stockpile several years' worth — you have to save fresh seed every year. But buckwheat is self-incompatible, so it can't set seed alone; insects or wind have to carry pollen from another plant. As a nectar-source crop it draws bees well, but if you assume a situation where bees have disappeared, this dependence becomes a weakness.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Finally, there's one weakness the evaluation table doesn't capture: allergy. In some people, buckwheat triggers a serious allergic reaction, and rarely, anaphylaxis. In a disaster where medical care is cut off, that's a real danger. Section 14 covers this in detail.

## 2. What Kind of Plant Is This?

### 2-1. Taxonomy and Origin

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Buckwheat is an annual dicot in the knotweed family (Polygonaceae), genus Fagopyrum. Since it isn't in the grass family, it isn't, strictly speaking, a grain — it's called a pseudocereal because its seed is used the way grain is. Dock, smartweed, sorrel, and rhubarb are in the same family.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Its native range is the highlands stretching from southwestern China to the Himalayas — places with short, cool summers and shallow, acidic soil. The reason buckwheat sets seed in just 60-75 days is that it had to complete its life cycle within a short summer, and its tolerance for poor acidic soil is likewise a product of that environment. Conversely, hot, humid conditions are something buckwheat never encountered, which is why fertilization fails above 30°C.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There are two cultivated species: common buckwheat (Fagopyrum esculentum) and tartary buckwheat (F. tataricum). Tartary buckwheat's rutin content reaches 70-100× that of common buckwheat, but true to its name, it has a strong bitter taste. On the other hand, it self-pollinates, which gives it the advantage of being much easier to save seed from.

### 2-2. Appearance

| Organ | Characteristics |
|---|---|
| Leaf | Triangular, heart-shaped. Alternate arrangement, with long petioles |
| Stem | Grows upright and is hollow inside. Sometimes tinged red |
| Root | Taproot. Many root hairs, strong nutrient uptake |
| Flower | White or light-pink raceme. Self-incompatible; insects carry the pollen |
| Fruit | Triangular achene. Wrapped in a hard black or brown hull |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Buckwheat flowers have a special structure. Within the same variety, two flower types are mixed together: long-styled flowers, with a long pistil and short stamens, and short-styled flowers, the reverse. Long-styled flowers are fertilized only by pollen from short-styled flowers, and short-styled flowers only by pollen from long-styled ones. That's what self-incompatibility means here: fertilization fails not only with a flower's own pollen but between flowers of the same form. So buckwheat absolutely needs insects, and there must be enough plants in the field. Plant just a few and the flowers bloom in profusion, but almost no seed sets.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The seed's triangular hull matters in practice too. It's thick and hard, and sits loosely around the kernel. Thick and hard means it favors storage; loose-fitting means it's relatively easy to remove. The hull itself is used as pillow filling and insulation, so it isn't thrown away.

### 2-3. Growth Stages

| Stage | Timing | Key Physiology | Key Nutrients |
|---|---|---|---|
| Germination and seedling stage | Sowing to 2 weeks | Germination is very fast — it emerges in three to four days | Phosphorus, nitrogen |
| Vegetative growth stage | 2-5 weeks | Stems and leaves increase rapidly | Nitrogen |
| Flowering stage | 5-7 weeks | Flowers open and insects pollinate them | Phosphorus, boron |
| Grain-filling and ripening stage | 7-10 weeks | Seeds fill and ripen, maturing from the bottom up | Potassium |

> **On a single buckwheat plant, flowers keep opening while seeds ripen one after another. Wait for all of them to ripen, and the ones that ripened first will have already shattered and fallen.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Buckwheat's biggest agronomic trait is indeterminate growth. While flowers lower on the plant open and their seeds ripen, new flowers keep opening higher up. So at harvest time, a single plant carries fully ripe seed, seed still filling, and flowers that haven't set seed yet, all at once. Wait for everything to ripen and the ripe seed lower down will have already shattered; cut too early and the upper portion will be empty chaff. That's why buckwheat's harvest timing is judged by proportion, not by everything being ripe. The right time is when 75-80% of the grain has ripened.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Temperature matters during flowering. The optimal temperature for flowering is 17-19°C, and above 30°C fertilization fails. This is where the phenomenon of flowers blooming without setting seed comes from. It happens when summer buckwheat runs into midsummer heat, so sowing time is timed so that flowering falls in early summer or early fall.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The core of buckwheat's nutrition is protein quality. A content of 13g is already high for a grain, but the real value lies in its amino-acid profile. Grass grains, without exception, are short on lysine. So however much rice, wheat, maize, sorghum, or foxtail millet you eat, the protein profile is never complete without legumes. Buckwheat, being in the knotweed family, escapes that limit and has enough lysine. That's what the essential amino-acid score above 90 means.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This difference matters in a disaster. In a year when the legume harvest fails, or when legumes simply can't be obtained, eating grain alone fills the stomach but brings on protein deficiency little by little. Muscle wastes away, wounds stop healing, and immunity drops. Buckwheat, on its own, solves much of this problem. It delivers few calories, but the protein quality attached to those few calories is different.

### 2-5. Rutin

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Rutin is a component that can't be left out of any discussion of buckwheat. It's a flavonoid, also called vitamin P, and it's the point where buckwheat stands clearly apart from other grains.

| Item | Details |
|---|---|
| Action | Strengthens capillary walls and lowers vascular permeability |
| Relevance | Prevention of hypertension and arteriosclerosis; reduced bleeding tendency |
| Location | Concentrated in the hull and the layer near it, and in young sprouts and leaves |
| Varietal difference | Tartary buckwheat has 70-100× the level of common buckwheat |
| Loss | Decreases the more it's milled and the longer it's stored |
| Heat | Not significantly destroyed by cooking |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Rutin has two kinds of significance in a disaster. First, it helps protect vascular health when fresh vegetables are cut off. When vitamin C runs short, capillaries weaken, gums bleed, and bruising comes easily — rutin partially compensates in that direction. Second, buckwheat sprouts can be put to use. Soak the seed in water and leave it in a dark place, and it sprouts within days, with far more rutin and vitamins in the sprout than in the seed. It's a way to get fresh greens in winter, in days, with no soil and no field. Section 16 covers this.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Rutin is concentrated near the black hull. There's far more of it in coarse flour ground with the hull left in than in flour that's been milled white. In a disaster, there's no time to spare for fine milling anyway, so, naturally, more rutin ends up being eaten.
