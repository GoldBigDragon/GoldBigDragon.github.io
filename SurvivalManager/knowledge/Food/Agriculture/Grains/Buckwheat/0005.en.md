## 12. Harvest and Threshing

### 12-1. Judging the Time - The 75-to-80% Rule

| Criterion | Details | Judgment |
|---|---|---|
| Ripening ratio | When 75-80% of the grain has turned black and ripe | The right time to harvest |
| Days | 60-75 days after sowing | Varies with the variety and that year's temperatures |
| Seed color | Changes from green through brown to black | Ripens from the bottom up |
| Seed firmness | Doesn't dent when pressed with a fingernail | Fully ripe |
| Moisture content | 16% or below | The harvest benchmark |
| Shattering | Black seed comes off easily when stripped by hand | Time to hurry |
| Weather | After a run of clear days | Cutting it wet makes drying hard |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The difficulty of harvesting buckwheat comes from its indeterminate growth. Within a single plant, the seed at the bottom has already turned black and is ready to shatter, while flowers are still opening at the top. You can't wait for everything to ripen, but cut too early and everything up top becomes empty chaff. So the standard is a proportion. The point of least loss is when 75-80% has turned black and ripe.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Making this judgment means walking the field and checking individual plants. Pick a few plants, count the seed, and estimate the ratio of black to not-yet-black. Once you're used to it, you can tell just by the color of the whole field. When a buckwheat field as a whole starts turning brown, that's roughly the time.

> **The most common loss with buckwheat is waiting for it to ripen and shedding all the bottom seed in the process. Cut it once 75% has ripened.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Weather has to be watched too. Harvest on a rainy or humid day and the extra moisture makes drying hard, and mold sets in during that time. If rain is forecast, it's better to cut a little early. Conversely, if several clear days look likely, you can afford to wait another day or two.

### 12-2. Harvest Technique

| Step | Details | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Cut early in the morning before the dew dries | Seed sheds less while damp |
| 2 | Cut at the base with a sickle | The stem is soft and cuts easily |
| 3 | Lay the plants down gently without shaking them as you cut | Buckwheat shatters very easily |
| 4 | Spread cloth or a straw mat on the ground and lay the plants on it | Recovers any seed that falls off |
| 5 | Tie into bundles | If too large, the inside won't dry |
| 6 | Air-dry in the field or yard for 3-5 days | During this time, the still-unripe seed at the top ripens too |
| 7 | Keep it out of the rain | If it gets wet, mold and pre-harvest sprouting follow |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Cutting in the early morning is a technique specific to buckwheat. Buckwheat seed sheds very easily, but when it's wet with dew, it sheds less. Once the sun is up and it dries, just touching a sickle to it makes the seed pour down. This difference decides more than 10% of the yield.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The 3-to-5-day air-drying process after cutting matters. During this period, nutrients remaining in the stem move into the unripe seed, so the seed higher up ripens to some degree as well. Thresh it right after cutting, and the still-unripe seed simply becomes chaff. These few days are there to gain that after-ripening effect.

### 12-3. Drying the Grain - Down to 13-14% Moisture

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Everything about grain storage comes down to moisture. Above 15%, mold grows; below 13%, both mold and insects nearly stop being active. At harvest, buckwheat's moisture is around 16%. It starts out lower than most other grains, but it still has to be brought down further, to 13-14%.

| Stage | Method | Duration | Moisture Reached |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Air-drying | Spread or stand the cut plants in the field or yard to dry | 3-5 days | 15-16% |
| 2. Threshing | Thresh once sufficiently dry | 1 day | - |
| 3. Final drying | Spread on a mat or cloth and dry in the sun | 2-3 days | 13-14% |
| 4. Tempering | Rest in the shade for a day to even out the moisture | 1 day | Equalized |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The principles for sun-drying are the same across grains. First, don't spread it directly on bare ground — moisture from the soil re-wets the bottom layer. Second, don't pile it thicker than 3-5cm, and turn it three or four times a day. Third, bring it in or cover it once the sun goes low — night dew puts back the moisture removed during the day. Fourth, don't rush the drying. If only the outside dries while moisture stays trapped inside, mold appears later.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There's a caution specific to buckwheat. Its triangular seed rolls easily and scatters in the wind, and its smooth hull slides on any slope. It helps to put up a low border around the drying area or fold up the edges of the mat.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There are ways to judge moisture without a meter.

| Method | Procedure | Judgment |
|---|---|---|
| Biting | Bite a hulled kernel with a molar | If it snaps cleanly and turns to powder, it's 13-14% |
| Hull sound | Grab a handful and rub it between your hands | If the hulls rustle and crumble, it's dry |
| Salt test | Put a spoonful of dry salt and a handful of grain in a jar, shake, and let sit 15 minutes | If salt sticks to the jar walls, moisture is high |
| Hand feel | Grip a handful, then open your hand | If it slides apart freely, it's dry |
| Jar test | Fill a glass jar halfway, seal it, and let it sit for a day | If the inside fogs up, it needs more drying |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;If using heat, don't exceed 50 degrees. Above that, the grain can no longer be used as seed, and buckwheat, with its already short seed lifespan, is even more sensitive to this. Keep it only up to the temperature your hand can touch and still feel just warm.

### 12-4. Threshing - Doing It Without Tools

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Buckwheat is very easy to thresh. Its seed is loosely attached to the stem, and once dry, it drops off at the lightest impact. If anything, the problem runs the other way — it can shed so easily that you lose some of it.

| Method | Procedure | Scale | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Stripping by hand | Grip the stem and strip the seed off with your hand | Small amounts | Least loss. Used for seed stock |
| Beating | Spread on a mat and beat lightly with a stick or flail | Large amounts | Most common method. No need to hit hard |
| Beating in a sack | Put it in a sack and strike the whole sack against the ground | Medium amounts | Seed doesn't scatter |
| Treading | Spread on a mat and tread on it with your feet | Large amounts | Takes less effort |
| Shaking | Hold the bundle upside down and shake it inside a container | Medium amounts | Especially well suited to buckwheat |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The trick with threshing buckwheat is not to hit it hard. The seed comes off so readily that a light beating is enough, and striking hard cracks the triangular hull, damaging the kernel and producing more chaff. Just holding a bundle upside down and shaking it inside a large container brings down a considerable amount on its own.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;After threshing, the stems and leaves are left on the field or used for compost. Buckwheat stems are soft and break down quickly, returning the phosphorus and potassium buckwheat drew from the soil.

### 12-5. Winnowing Out the Chaff

| Method | Procedure | Conditions |
|---|---|---|
| Pouring in the wind | On a windy day, pour it slowly from a height | Buckwheat seed is light, so a gentle breeze works best |
| Basket winnowing | Load a winnowing basket and toss it forward and back to lift the chaff | Most reliable |
| Fanning | One person pours while another fans air | Lets you control the strength |
| Sieving | A coarse sieve for stem bits, a fine sieve for fragments | Buckwheat's large kernel makes sieving easy |
| Water sorting | Pour into water and skim off floating chaff | Must be dried again immediately afterward |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Buckwheat seed is on the light side, so wind strength needs care. Pour it into a strong wind and even sound, well-filled seed blows away. A method where you can control the strength, like fanning, or basket winnowing, is safer.

### 12-6. Hulling - Removing the Triangular Hull

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Buckwheat has one more step after threshing: hulling, removing the hard triangular hull. This hull is too tough to eat, and without removing it, you can't even mill the grain into flour.

| Method | Tool | Procedure | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mortar pounding | Mortar and pestle | Pound lightly, then winnow; repeat | Pounding hard breaks the kernel too |
| Spread-set millstone grinding | Millstone | Raise the upper stone to about kernel-size and turn it | Only the hull cracks; the kernel stays intact |
| Dampening before pounding | Mortar | Sprinkle a little water, let sit 30 minutes, then pound | Hull comes off easily |
| Roasting then pounding | Pot and mortar | Roast over low heat until the hull turns crisp, then pound | Best suited to buckwheat |
| Rubbing with a stone mortar | A hollow stone and a round stone | Rub by hand to crack the hull | Suited to small amounts |
| Rubbing between wooden boards | Two boards | Place between them and press and rub | Easy to make |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The trick to hulling buckwheat is the opposite of other grains. Because the hull isn't tightly bonded to the kernel but sits loosely around it, you crack it open rather than rub it off. That's why raising the millstone's upper stone to about the kernel's diameter and turning it cracks only the hull, leaving the kernel inside intact. This is the most efficient method, and commercial buckwheat hullers work on the same principle.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Roasting before pounding also works well. A few minutes over low heat drives the moisture out of the hull, making it crisp and much easier to crack, and at the same time brings out buckwheat's characteristic nutty aroma. Roasted grain can't be used as seed, though, so seed stock must be set aside separately.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The hulls that come off aren't thrown away. Buckwheat hulls are light, breathe well, and don't hold moisture, which is why they've long been used as pillow filling. They also serve as insulation, make good kindling when burned, and their ash becomes fertilizer. For a crop like buckwheat, whose grain yield itself is small, the usefulness of its by-products is relatively large.

## 13. Seed Saving

### 13-1. Constraints From Self-Incompatibility

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Saving buckwheat seed is trickier than for the other crops in this document, because it's a self-incompatible, cross-pollinating crop.

| Item | Details | Implication |
|---|---|---|
| Fertilization mode | Long-styled and short-styled flowers must receive each other's pollen | Cannot set seed alone |
| Vector | Carried by bees and other insects | No insects means no seed set |
| Isolation | Must be kept apart from other varieties | Bagging doesn't work |
| Maintaining a population | Must collect evenly from many plants | Cannot take seed from just one plant |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Bagging, the isolation method used for other crops, doesn't work for buckwheat. Enclosed in a bag, only flowers of the same form meet each other, and fertilization fails. It would work if you could pick out long-styled and short-styled flowers and pair them by hand, but that isn't practical. So maintaining buckwheat varieties relies on distance isolation and timing isolation instead.

| Method | Details | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Distance isolation | Keep at least 500m from other varieties | Set generously, since it's within bee flight range |
| Timing isolation | Stagger flowering by 3 weeks or more | Summer and fall types naturally fall out of sync |
| Single variety | Grow only one variety in the area | Most practical |
| Using tartary buckwheat | Capable of self-pollination | No isolation needed |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In a small area, the most practical approach is growing just one variety. Even if you grow both summer and fall buckwheat, their flowering times fall out of sync naturally, so they don't cross. The problem is a neighbor growing a different variety, which is beyond any individual's control. In a disaster, the best solution is an agreement at the village level to standardize on one variety.

### 13-2. Selecting and Storing Seed Plants

| Criterion | Details | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Plant selection | Plants with no disease symptoms, that haven't lodged, and that set abundant seed | Traits carry forward |
| From many plants | Collect evenly from at least 30 plants | A cross-pollinating crop needs diversity |
| Harvest timing | Leave a bit longer than usual to fully ripen | Immature seed has poor germination |
| Bottom seed | Use the bottom seed, which ripened first | Best-filled |
| Form | Always store with the hull on | Hulling sharply shortens seed life |
| Moisture | 12-13% | Seed stock should be a bit drier |
| Lifespan | 1-2 years at room temperature | Shorter than other grains |
| Quantity | At least twice the amount needed | Accounts for declining germination |

> **Buckwheat seed germination starts declining at around 8 months. It cannot be stockpiled for several years the way other grains can — fresh seed must be saved every year.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Seed longevity at C is a genuine weakness of buckwheat. Sorghum or foxtail millet lasts 2-3 years, longer still under good conditions, but buckwheat only lasts 1-2 years. If a bad year causes seed saving to fail even once, the line can be lost right away. There are two responses. First, save seed every single year without fail. Second, store it in the coolest, driest spot available. Lowering the temperature by 10°C roughly doubles seed lifespan.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;One consolation is that buckwheat needs little seed to begin with. For hill sowing, 0.5-2kg per 1,000m² is enough. Setting aside two or three times what's needed isn't a heavy burden.

### 13-3. Germination Testing

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Germination testing matters especially for buckwheat. Because seed life is short, the germination rate can change greatly after just a single year. Wrap fifty seeds in a damp cloth, leave it somewhere warm, and wait 3-4 days. Buckwheat germinates fast, so the result comes sooner than with other grains. If 40 or more out of 50 germinate, use the seed as is; at 30-40, increase the sowing rate by 1.5×; below 30, new seed must be obtained.

## 14. Toxicity and Precautions

### 14-1. Buckwheat Allergy - The Most Important Caution

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Buckwheat itself isn't toxic. But it does carry one danger that other crops don't: allergy.

| Item | Details |
|---|---|
| Cause | Some of buckwheat's proteins act as strong allergens |
| Frequency | Not common, but relatively frequently reported in Korea, Japan, and China |
| Symptoms | Hives, itching of the mouth and throat, abdominal pain, vomiting, difficulty breathing |
| Severe cases | Anaphylaxis. Blood pressure drops and the airway closes, putting life at risk |
| Onset speed | Appears within minutes to tens of minutes after eating |
| Heat | Cooking barely reduces its allergenicity |
| Trace amounts | A reaction can occur even from a very small amount |
| Contact | Reactions can occur without eating it, from inhaling buckwheat flour or from a buckwheat-hull pillow |

> **Buckwheat allergy can progress to anaphylaxis. In a disaster where medical care is cut off, this is a real threat to life.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This problem carries a different weight in a disaster than in normal times. In normal times you could be treated in an emergency room, but when hospitals aren't functioning, there's almost nothing that can be done about anaphylaxis. A preventive approach is therefore necessary.

| Situation | Action |
|---|---|
| Someone eating it for the first time | Start with a very small amount, watch for a reaction, and increase over several days |
| Young children | Take special care. Start with less than a spoonful |
| Anyone with a history of allergy | Do not eat it. Substitute another crop |
| If a reaction appears | Stop immediately and do not try again |
| Cooking utensils | If anyone has the allergy, use separate utensils |
| Buckwheat-hull pillows | Don't keep them near anyone with the allergy |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;When food is being shared at the community level, it's worth knowing in advance who reacts to buckwheat. Accidents genuinely happen where someone unknowingly eats food containing buckwheat noodles or buckwheat jelly. It's safer to label or set aside any food that contains buckwheat.

### 14-2. Rutin in Tartary Buckwheat

| Item | Details |
|---|---|
| Content | 70-100× that of common buckwheat |
| Safety | No problem at ordinary intake levels |
| Taste | As rutin breaks down, it produces a strong bitterness |
| Caution | If taking anticoagulants, be careful with large amounts |
| Processing | Hulling well and rinsing several times reduces the bitterness |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The rutin in tartary buckwheat is less a toxin than a component with strong pharmacological activity. It strengthens capillary walls and lowers vascular permeability, which works to your benefit under normal circumstances. However, if you're taking medication related to blood clotting, there's a possibility of interaction, so it's better to avoid large amounts. Continuing such medication through a disaster would be rare, but it's worth noting for the record.

### 14-3. Other Precautions

| Item | Details | Action |
|---|---|---|
| Moldy grain | Mycotoxins don't disappear with heating | Discard all of it |
| Fagopyrism | Buckwheat's fagopyrin compound can cause photosensitivity | Occurs when livestock eat large amounts of buckwheat leaves and are then in the sun for a long time |
| Hull | Indigestible and catches in the mouth | Always hull it before eating |
| Undercooked grain | Starch isn't digested | Cook thoroughly |
| Ordinary consumption | Safe | No problem without an allergy |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Fagopyrism is a phenomenon that mainly appears in livestock. When fagopyrin, a pigment found in buckwheat's leaves and stems, enters the body, the skin becomes sensitive to sunlight, causing inflammation and ulcers on white-haired areas. It can occur when livestock fed a large amount of buckwheat leaves are then left in the sun all day. When feeding buckwheat residue to livestock, limiting the amount and providing shade prevents any problem. The seed that people eat contains almost none of this compound.
