## Introduction
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Buckwheat (Fagopyrum esculentum) is a dicot of the knotweed family, not a grass, and what is eaten like a grain is a seed, not a kernel. It yields 150 kg per 1,000㎡, 561 kcal per ㎡, only 0.21 times the self-sufficiency line, so it cannot be the mainstay of a staple. It is nevertheless counted as a famine-relief crop because it is the fastest, at 60-75 days from sowing to harvest. It can be sown where other crops have been destroyed by drought, flood, or late frost, and still give something within the same year. It also grows on poor land and acid soil, needs only 12 g of fertilizer per ㎡ and 250 mm of water, needs no pest control, and has protein of a quality far above grains. Its weaknesses are frost, self-incompatibility, short seed life, the hard hull, and allergy.

## 2. Plant Overview
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;An annual of the genus Fagopyrum in the knotweed family, native to the highlands from southwestern China to the Himalayas. The cultivated species are common buckwheat (F. esculentum) and Tartary buckwheat (F. tataricum). The leaves are triangular and heart-shaped, the stem is straight and hollow, and the straight taproot absorbs nutrients strongly. The flowers are white or pale pink racemes; being self-incompatible, long-styled and short-styled flowers must receive each other's pollen, so insects (bees) carry the pollen. The fruit is a hard triangular achene. It has indeterminate growth, so flowers keep opening and seeds ripen in turn from the bottom; if you wait for everything to ripen, the first-ripened seed falls.

| Stage | Timing | Key points |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Germination and seedling stage | 2 weeks after sowing | Emerges in three or four days |
| Vegetative growth stage | Weeks 2-5 | Stalk and leaves increase |
| Flowering stage | Weeks 5-7 | Insects pollinate. Phosphorus and boron are important |
| Grain-filling and maturity | Weeks 7-10 | Ripens in turn from the bottom |

| Nutrition per 100 g | Content |
|:---|:---|
| Calories | 364-374 kcal (dry seed) |
| Protein | 12.96-13.2 g (13.9% of calories). No lysine shortage |
| Carbohydrate / fat / dietary fiber | 72.8 g / 2.2 g / 5.0 g (low fat, so little risk of rancidity) |
| Others | Calcium, iron, vitamin K, B vitamins |
| Rutin | A flavonoid that strengthens capillary walls. Abundant near the hull and in sprouts and leaves; Tartary buckwheat has 70-100 times as much as common buckwheat. Not much destroyed by cooking, and it decreases the longer the grain is stored |

## 3. Growing Environment and Varieties
| Condition | Optimum | Limits and effects |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Growing temperature | 15-25℃ | 5-30℃. Growth stops below 5℃ and frost kills the plant |
| Flowering temperature | 17-19℃ | Above 30℃ fertilization fails and only flowers appear |
| Germination | 25-31℃ | Germinates even at 0-4.8℃ |
| Light | 6-8 hours a day | 4 hours or more. 88% of yield is kept even under 40% shading |
| Water | 250 mm in total | 70 mm from germination to flowering, 20 mm from full bloom to maturity. Very weak against excess moisture |
| Soil | Well-drained sandy soil to loam | Tolerates acid soil and does poorly in limy or alkaline soil. Fails in damp clay |

> **Do not put lime on buckwheat. That prescription, so good for other crops, harms buckwheat.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Good places are empty fields that are already tilled, spots where other crops failed, old fields leaning acid, and poor gravelly ground. Avoid chalky ground and damp low land.

| Ecotype | Representative varieties | Sowing |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Summer buckwheat (spring-sown type) | Yangjeol (68 days, 121 kg per 1,000㎡), Yangjeol 2 (68 days, 155 kg, the highest yield, little shattering) | Spring |
| Autumn buckwheat (summer-sown type) | Daesan (73 days, 130 kg, sprout yield 970%) | Summer |

> **If autumn buckwheat is sown in spring, it flowers but sets almost no seed. This is the most common and most fatal mistake in growing buckwheat, so if the origin of the seed is unknown, check the ecotype first.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Tartary buckwheat can self-pollinate, so it needs no isolation, and its high rutin favors seed saving and medicinal use.

## 4. Field Preparation and Sowing
| Item | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Preparing the soil | Shallow tilling to 20 cm is enough (the calorie recovery multiple is low, so deep tilling is a loss). Always dig drainage channels before sowing and break the soil fine. Do not add lime |
| Fertilization | Per 1,000㎡: nitrogen 5.4 kg, phosphorus 3.1 kg, potassium 3.9 kg, fully matured compost 1,000-1,500 kg. Borax at 2-3 kg raises seed set. Siliceous material reduces lodging |
| Substitutes | A little rotted compost, urine diluted 10 times (early growth), bone meal; wood ash is alkaline, so only a little |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Buckwheat absorbs nutrients very strongly and leaves little in the field after harvest, so the field must be restored with fertilizer or legumes. Yield retention without inputs is 45.1%.

| Type | Region | Sowing | Harvest |
|:---|:---|:---|:---|
| Summer buckwheat | Central and north | Late April to early May | Early to mid-July |
| Summer buckwheat | South | Early to mid-April | Late June to early July |
| Autumn buckwheat | Central and north | Mid to late July | Early to mid-October |
| Autumn buckwheat | South | Early to mid-August | Late October to mid-November |

| Sowing method | Seed rate (per 1,000㎡) | Notes |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Broadcasting | 8-10 kg | Fastest. For hurried planting |
| Drilling in rows | 6-8 kg, 30 cm between rows | Weeding possible |
| Spot sowing | 0.5-2 kg, 30 cm between rows, 15 cm between plants | Uses the least seed |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Sow 2-5 cm deep, cover lightly, and press down. Because it is self-incompatible, do not plant too thinly. Sow autumn buckwheat 10-12 weeks before the first frost. Seed life is short, so a germination test is essential (always for seed more than a year old), and the seed is soaked in 50-55℃ water for 10-15 minutes or coated with fine ash. A straw mulch is useful for holding moisture and suppressing weeds.

## 5. Water, Cultivation Management, and Pests and Diseases
| Stage | Water management |
|:---|:---|
| Germination | Keep the soil from drying |
| Early growth | Preventing excess moisture is everything. Waterlogging right after sowing is the most common cause of failure, so dig furrows to drain |
| Flowering | Plenty is needed |
| Grain filling | Little |
| Maturity | Cut off completely. If it rains, the seed falls and sprouts |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The reason flowers open but no seed sets is usually one of four: high temperature, water shortage, too few pollinating insects, or a mistake in ecotype.

| Management | Content |
|:---|:---|
| Weeds | Hardly needed. Just pull the conspicuous large weeds and buckwheat suppresses the rest itself |
| Lodging | The hollow stem falls over easily. Hill up to flowering, and avoid dense planting and too much nitrogen |
| Pollination | Do not plant thinly, spray nothing at flowering, leave flowers at the field edge to keep bees, and shake the plants in the morning for hand pollination |
| Other | Pinching and top dressing are unnecessary. Just before harvest, hang up shiny things to keep birds off |

| Pest or disease | Response without pesticides |
|:---|:---|
| Stem rot, gray mold | Drainage and airflow, remove diseased parts |
| Powdery mildew | Milk diluted 5-10 times or baking soda solution (5 g baking soda and a little soap per 1 L of water), airflow |
| Moths, aphids | Hand-picking, soapy water (5-10 g of pure soap per 1 L of water), protect natural enemies |
| Birds | The largest real loss. Scarecrow, shiny scraps, nets |

> **Spray nothing during flowering. If the bees die, there is no harvest that year.**

| Deficiency or excess | Symptoms and remedy |
|:---|:---|
| Nitrogen shortage | Pale yellow, short plants. Rotted compost, a little diluted urine |
| Boron shortage | Flowers open but no seed sets. Borax foliar application |
| Iron shortage | New leaves turn white. Too much alkali, so stop wood ash and lime |
| Nitrogen excess | Plants grow tall and lodge. Stop fertilizing and hill up |

## 6. Companion Planting and Rotation
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Buckwheat is a crop that keeps the field from lying idle. Plant autumn buckwheat after potatoes dug in June, after barley and wheat cut in June, and after harvested sesame; plant Chinese cabbage and radish where April-sown summer buckwheat was harvested in July, or plant late rice on rain-fed paddy. Continuous cropping of buckwheat is not harmful, but yield declines gradually.

| Cleansing-crop effect | Use |
|:---|:---|
| Weed suppression | Rapid growth and allelochemicals clear a field taken over by weeds in a single year |
| Nematode suppression | Plant before potatoes or root vegetables |
| Phosphorus solubilization | Takes up phosphorus other crops cannot use, and returns it to the soil when plowed under |
| Green manure | Plow under before the seed ripens, when in full bloom |

| Rotation | Crop |
|:---|:---|
| Year 1 | Buckwheat |
| Year 2 | Legumes (restore nitrogen) |
| Year 3 | Potato or root vegetables |
| Year 4 | Grains, or return to buckwheat |

## 7. Harvest, Hulling, and Seed Saving
| Step | Content |
|:---|:---|
| Timing | 60-75 days after sowing. Cut when 75-80% of the seeds have ripened black (moisture 16% or less). If you wait for all to ripen, you lose the lower seed |
| Cutting | In the early morning before the dew dries, cut at the base with a sickle and lay it down without shaking |
| Drying | Place on a cloth or mat, tie in small bundles, and dry in the sun for 3-5 days. Keep it out of the rain. Then thresh it and dry on a mat for another 2-3 days to 13-14% moisture |
| Judging | A hulled kernel cracks with a snap and leaves powder when bitten, hulls crumble with a rustle when rubbed, and sealed in a jar for a day it shows no fog |
| Threshing | Strip by hand (for seed), beat lightly with a stick on a mat (do not hit hard), strike the sack on the ground, trample, or hold upside down and shake |
| Chaff | Pour in a light wind, winnow, fan, sieve, or separate in water (dry immediately) |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;For hulling (removing the triangular husk), you can pound lightly in a mortar and winnow, repeating; run a millstone with the upper stone raised to the size of the kernel; or roast gently so the husk turns crisp and then pound, and roasting before pounding suits it best. The husk is used for pillow stuffing, insulation, and kindling.

| Seed saving | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Constraint | Self-incompatible, so covering with a bag does not work |
| Isolation | At least 500 m from other varieties, or offset flowering by at least 3 weeks. Planting only one variety in an area is the most realistic |
| Selection | Evenly from 30 or more disease-free, unlodged plants. Use the seed that ripened first, lower down |
| Storage | In the husk, moisture 12-13%. 1-2 years at room temperature, and germination starts to fall from 8 months, so take new seed every year. Keep twice the amount needed |

## 8. Storage
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Storability is SS. Store in the husk and hull only as much as you will eat. Once hulled, seed life drops sharply and rutin is broken down by light.

| Item | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Conditions | Moisture 13-14% (12-13% for seed), temperature 15℃ or less, surrounding humidity 65% or less, light completely blocked, check every two months, and germination test seed in spring and autumn |
| Containers | Metal drum or glass jar 3 years or more / earthenware jar 2-3 years / thick plastic 1-2 years / sack 6 months to 1 year (the triangular seeds roll easily and leak out) |

| Insect control | Method |
|:---|:---|
| Complete sealing | Fill the container to the brim and close it. The surest |
| Wood ash | Mix fine ash into 2-5% of the grain volume. Wash it off before eating |
| Diatomaceous earth | 1-3 g per 1 kg of grain. Works only when dry |
| Supplements | Dried chili and Sichuan pepper, mugwort and sweet flag leaves, roasted salt, charcoal. Leave it outdoors for a few days in midwinter (cold), or spread thinly in the sun at 60℃ (not for seed) |

## 9. Cooking and Cautions
> **Buckwheat allergy can lead to anaphylaxis. In a disaster where medical care is cut off, this is a real danger to life.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Part of buckwheat protein is a strong allergen that is not reduced by heating, and reactions can occur from a very small amount, from inhaling the flour, or from a buckwheat-hull pillow. Symptoms are hives, itching of the mouth and throat, abdominal pain, vomiting, and difficulty breathing, and in severe cases a fall in blood pressure and airway blockage, appearing within minutes to tens of minutes after eating. A person eating it for the first time should start with a very small amount (a spoonful or less for a child) and increase it over several days; a person with a history of allergy should not eat it; and if a reaction occurs, stop at once and do not try again. Use separate cooking utensils, keep hull pillows away, and if you share food as a community, find out in advance who reacts.

| Caution | Content |
|:---|:---|
| Tartary buckwheat | Rutin is 70-100 times higher, so it tastes strongly bitter. Normal intake is no problem, but take care with large amounts if taking anticoagulants. Hulling well and rinsing several times reduces the bitterness |
| Moldy grain | Mycotoxins are not destroyed by heat. Discard it all |
| Hulls, undercooked | The hull is indigestible, so hull before eating, and cook the starch thoroughly |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Buckwheat has no gluten, so people who cannot eat wheat can eat it, but the dough has no stickiness, which makes noodles or bread hard to make. Mix in wheat flour or starch, or knead with hot water. After hulling, grind with a millstone or mortar; roasting before pounding makes it less work and brings out the aroma. Flour spoils much faster than the grain, so grind only a month's worth.

| Cooking | Fuel and tips |
|:---|:---|
| Roasted flour | Mix into water and eat at once. Needs no cooking fuel |
| Flour porridge | Stir flour into water and cook 5-10 minutes. The most efficient |
| Buckwheat jelly (muk) | Cook flour in water while stirring, then cool to set. Keeps for a few days |
| Pancakes, noodles | Fry thin (thick ones stay raw inside). Noodles boil quickly |
| Residual-heat cooking | After boiling, turn off the heat and keep wrapped, which cuts fuel by more than half |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;For buckwheat sprouts, soak the seed in its hull in water for 6-12 hours, drain, spread it in a bowl or on cloth, and sprinkle with water two or three times a day. In the dark, yellow sprouts appear in 3-4 days; in light they turn green in two days; and at 5-10 cm after 5-7 days, remove the hulls and eat them. This gives fresh greens within five days in winter with no soil or field, and has more rutin and vitamins than the seed. Processing that also serves storage includes roasted kernels (sealed, 6 months), roasted flour (3-6 months), dried jelly (1 year), and starch (several years). During flowering you can also get buckwheat honey from bees.

## 10. Annual Work Schedule
| Month | Work |
|:---|:---|
| January-February | Check storage, seed germination test, plan the field and turn the compost |
| March | Tilling, drainage channels, basal fertilizer |
| April | Sow summer buckwheat (early to mid-month in the south, late in the central and north). Check the ecotype |
| May | Hilling, flowering begins. Spray nothing at flowering |
| June-July | Harvest and sun-dry summer buckwheat (cut when 75-80% is ripe). In mid to late July, sow autumn buckwheat in the central and north (10-12 weeks before the first frost) |
| August-September | Sow autumn buckwheat in the south. Flowering management and protecting the bees |
| October-November | Harvest, sun-dry, thresh, and dry autumn buckwheat (moisture 13-14%), store, secure seed |
| December | Hulling and milling, growing sprouts, check storage |

> **Five things not to forget: check the ecotype, drainage, no spraying during flowering, harvest when 75-80% is ripe, and beware of allergy.**
