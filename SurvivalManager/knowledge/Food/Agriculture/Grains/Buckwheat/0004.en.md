## 7. Water Management

### 7-1. Standards by Growth Stage

| Stage | Management | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Sowing to germination | Keep the soil from drying out | Germination is fast; it emerges in three to four days |
| Early growth | Preventing waterlogging is everything | Waterlogging at this stage is the most common cause of failure |
| Flowering stage | Needs adequate moisture | About 70mm of rainfall is needed from germination to flowering |
| Grain-filling stage | Little. 20mm from full bloom to maturity is enough | Excess moisture causes pre-harvest sprouting and quality loss |
| Maturity stage | Cut off water completely | Rain causes seed to shatter and sprout |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;What's distinctive about buckwheat's water management is how clearly its needs split by stage. From germination to flowering it needs 70mm, but from full bloom to maturity, 20mm is enough. It's front-loaded: more early, less late. The fact that it can complete its life cycle on as little as 250mm total is also because the later requirement is so small.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The most important part of water management for buckwheat isn't giving water — it's removing it. Buckwheat is highly vulnerable to waterlogging right after sowing and in early growth. Freshly sown seed rots if it sits submerged, and a young plant that has just put out its cotyledons dies after only three days underwater. The half-day it takes to dig drainage channels before sowing decides whether the buckwheat crop succeeds or fails.

### 7-2. Symptoms of Water Shortage and Excess

| Symptom | Cause | Diagnosis and Action |
|---|---|---|
| Leaves wilt and stems droop | Water shortage | If it's flowering, add water |
| Flowers bloom heavily but seed doesn't set | High heat, water shortage, or a shortage of pollinating insects | Several causes can overlap; covered later |
| Seed is small and shriveled | Drought during grain-filling | Does not recover |
| Young plants soften at the base and collapse | Waterlogging | Dig a furrow immediately to drain the water |
| Leaves turn pale yellow and roots turn black | Excess moisture, oxygen-starved roots | Fix the drainage at the root of the problem |
| Sprouting occurs in ripe seed | Wet conditions during maturity | Harvest without delay |

> **It's common for buckwheat to flower without setting seed. The cause is usually one of four things: high heat, water shortage, a lack of pollinating insects, or the wrong ecotype for the season.**

## 8. Crop Management

### 8-1. Weed Management - Mostly Unnecessary

| Stage | Method | Key Point |
|---|---|---|
| Right after sowing | No special action | Buckwheat comes up fast |
| Early growth | Pull only the conspicuous large weeds | Buckwheat suppresses weeds on its own |
| After that | Not needed | The leaves fully cover the ground |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Buckwheat is one of the rare crops that needs almost no weed management. Germination is very fast, in three to four days, and its broad triangular leaves quickly cover the ground and deny weeds light. On top of that, substances released from its roots suppress the germination of weed seeds. Just two weeks after sowing, the field is covered in buckwheat leaves and no other plant can grow.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Because of this trait, buckwheat is used to clean up fields. Sow and harvest it once in an old, weed-choked field, and weed density drops noticeably. In the West this use is called a cleaning crop. It's one of the few ways to reclaim a weed-overrun field when herbicides aren't available, and in the process it also produces food.

### 8-2. Hilling and Preventing Lodging

| Item | Timing | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Hilling | Up to flowering | Mounds soil at the base to prevent lodging |
| Adjusting sowing rate | At sowing | Overcrowding thins the stems and they fall over |
| Restrained fertilization | At base-fertilizer stage | Too much nitrogen causes leggy growth |
| Silicate | Base fertilizer | Strengthens the stem |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Buckwheat's stem is hollow inside, so it lodges easily. On top of that, indeterminate growth keeps adding flowers and seed higher up the plant, which raises its center of gravity. The most effective countermeasure is hilling — mounding soil at the base up through flowering. If you sowed in rows, simply raking soil up from the furrow is enough.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Once it has lodged, there's almost nothing to be done. Buckwheat's stem is delicate — try to stand it back up and it breaks, and even if you prop it up, it falls over again. Seed that touches the ground rots or sprouts. So lodging is entirely a matter of prevention.

### 8-3. Pollination - Management Unique to Buckwheat

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Buckwheat is a self-incompatible, cross-pollinating crop, so insects or wind absolutely must carry its pollen. This is a management task unique to buckwheat that other grains don't have.

| Method | Details | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Ensuring density | Don't sow too sparsely | There must be a partner to exchange pollen with |
| Attracting bees | Buckwheat is a leading nectar source | Bees gather on their own during flowering |
| No pesticides | Spray nothing at all during flowering | If pollinators die, there's no seed set |
| Surrounding flowers | Leave other flowers along the field edge | Keeps the bee population sustained |
| Hand pollination | Walk between the rows during flowering and shake the plants | Helps to some extent when insects are scarce |
| Morning hours | Buckwheat releases pollen in the morning | Morning is the best time to shake the plants |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Buckwheat's status as a leading nectar source cuts both ways. When buckwheat flowers, bees gather and pollination happens, and at the same time, if you have hives, you get honey. Buckwheat honey is dark, strongly flavored, and rich in iron. When sugar has disappeared in a disaster, honey is nearly the only concentrated sweetener available, and buckwheat is a crop that helps produce it. This is one reason its non-food value scores C on the evaluation table, higher than other grains.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;On the other hand, if you assume a scenario where insects have disappeared, buckwheat becomes vulnerable. If bees decline because of a large-scale pesticide accident or a sudden environmental shift, buckwheat's seed-set rate falls. In that case, tartary buckwheat should be considered. It self-pollinates and sets seed even without insects.

### 8-4. Other Management

| Item | Details |
|---|---|
| Pinching | Not done. Buckwheat is better left alone |
| Top-dressing | Usually not needed. The growing period is too short for it to have an effect |
| Scaring off birds | Birds eat the seed right before harvest. Hang up shiny objects |
| Residue handling | Leave cut stems on the field or use them as compost |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Buckwheat stems make good green manure. Soft and moisture-rich, they break down quickly and return the phosphorus and potassium buckwheat drew from the soil back to the field. It's a way to partly offset the problem of buckwheat's strong nutrient uptake working against the crop that follows. Leave them lying where they were cut, or till them in shallowly.

## 9. Pests and Diseases

### 9-1. Major Pests and Diseases

| Pest/Disease | Conditions | Damage | Response Without Chemicals |
|---|---|---|---|
| Stem rot | Wet conditions | Stems soften and the plant dies | Improve drainage, rotate crops |
| Powdery mildew | Poor air circulation | White powder covers the leaf surface | Spray diluted milk or baking-soda solution, improve airflow |
| Gray mold | Wet conditions during flowering | Flowers and young seed turn soft and rot | Improve drainage and airflow, remove diseased parts |
| Moths (caterpillars) | Growing season | Chew on leaves and stems | Early detection and hand-picking |
| Aphids | Growing season | Suck sap and bring on sooty mold | Spray soapy water, protect natural predators |
| Birds | Right before harvest | Eat the seed | Scarecrows, shiny objects, netting |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Among the crops covered in this document, buckwheat has the simplest response to pests and disease. Its pest-control self-reliance grade of SS means there is practically nothing that causes real trouble even without chemicals. The list of known pests and diseases doesn't exceed six kinds, none of them is deadly, and the growing period is too short for disease to spread very far.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;However, the evaluation table's pest-and-disease pressure score of C (estimated yield loss 50.6%) does need explaining. This figure isn't measured from buckwheat itself; it's inferred from the average across the entire class Magnoliopsida (dicots). Because there's little research data specific to buckwheat, the value was borrowed from a higher taxonomic group, which makes the assessment somewhat more conservative than reality. In actual cultivation, buckwheat's pest and disease damage isn't very large.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The most substantial loss comes not from disease or insects, but from birds. Buckwheat seed is large and its hull is smooth, which makes it easy for birds to eat. If birds aren't kept off during the ten days to two weeks right before harvest, a considerable amount disappears. Without netting, stringing several lines of thread over the field, hanging shiny objects, or setting up something that makes noise can all still be effective.

### 9-2. Natural Control Agents

| Material | How to Make It | Target | Caution |
|---|---|---|---|
| Diluted milk | Dilute 5-10× with water | Powdery mildew | Spray on a clear morning |
| Baking-soda solution | 5g baking soda and a bit of soap in 1L water | Powdery mildew | Too concentrated damages the leaves |
| Soapy water | 5-10g pure soap in 1L water | Aphids | Spraying at midday scorches the leaves |
| Wood vinegar | Dilute 500-1,000× | Preventing fungal disease | Too concentrated causes phytotoxicity |

> **Spray nothing at all during flowering. If the bees die, there is no harvest that year.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This principle matters especially for buckwheat. Being self-incompatible, it cannot set seed at all without insects. Even soapy water is harmful to bees. Even if disease appears, it's better to hold off during flowering and either treat it after the flowers have finished or simply leave it alone. Buckwheat barely needs pest control anyway.

### 9-3. Principles of Pest Control Without Chemicals

| Principle | Details | Application to Buckwheat |
|---|---|---|
| Drainage | Prevent standing water | Most of buckwheat's disease problems come from excess moisture |
| Airflow | Don't sow too densely | Reduces powdery mildew and gray mold |
| Avoidance | Keep flowering from overlapping midsummer heat and humidity | Choosing the right cropping type is itself pest control |
| No treatment during flowering | Protects the bees | Seed set itself depends on bees |
| Blocking birds | Physical barriers | The single biggest source of real loss |

## 10. Nutrient Deficiency and Excess

| Symptom | Cause | Natural Remedy |
|---|---|---|
| Whole plant turns pale yellow and stays short | Nitrogen deficiency | Well-rotted compost, a small amount of urine diluted 10× |
| Leaf edges turn brown | Potassium deficiency | Apply only a very small amount of wood ash |
| Growth is slow | Phosphorus deficiency | Mix bone meal into compost. Buckwheat uses phosphorus efficiently |
| Flowers bloom but seed doesn't set | Boron deficiency | Foliar-apply borax. If unavailable, diversify compost materials |
| Leaves turn red | Phosphorus deficiency or low temperature | If it's low temperature, it recovers on its own |
| New leaves turn pale white | Iron deficiency, excess alkalinity | Stop applying wood ash or lime |
| Leggy growth and lodging | Excess nitrogen | Stop fertilizing and hill the plants |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The one thing especially worth knowing about buckwheat is boron. When boron is short, flowers open normally but seed doesn't set. This symptom is common in buckwheat, and because it has several possible causes, diagnosis is difficult. High heat, water shortage, a lack of pollinating insects, the wrong ecotype, and boron deficiency all produce the same symptom. If you can rule out the first four, suspect boron. Adding 2-3kg of borax per 1,000m², or applying it as a foliar spray just before flowering, noticeably raises the seed-set rate.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Iron deficiency arises from a context opposite to that of other crops. Because buckwheat prefers acidic soil, applying a lot of wood ash or lime and tipping the soil toward alkaline locks up iron so it can't be absorbed. If new leaves turn pale white, suspect excess alkalinity and stop applying ash.

## 11. Intercropping and Crop Rotation

### 11-1. Compatibility

| Compatibility | Partner | Effect |
|:---:|---|---|
| Very good | Potato (preceding crop) | Follow with fall buckwheat where potatoes were dug in June |
| Very good | Barley, wheat (preceding crop) | Fills the spot cut in June |
| Very good | Sesame (preceding crop) | Followed by fall buckwheat after harvest |
| Good | Maize, tobacco | Used as a preceding or following crop |
| Good | Rice (rain-fed paddy) | Harvest summer buckwheat in spring, then transplant rice late |
| Good | Fall vegetables (following crop) | Planted where summer buckwheat was harvested |
| Poor | Buckwheat after buckwheat | Continuous cropping itself isn't harmful, but yield declines |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Buckwheat's compatibility table has a different character from other crops. It centers not on mixed cropping planted together, but on sequential cropping, planted one after another. The reason lies in buckwheat's short growing period. Since it finishes in 60 to 75 days, it's well suited to filling the gap between other crops, and that's its most rational use.

| Cropping Combination | Sequence |
|---|---|
| Fall buckwheat after spring potatoes | June: harvest potatoes; July: sow buckwheat; October: harvest buckwheat |
| Fall vegetables after summer buckwheat | April: sow buckwheat; July: harvest; napa cabbage and radish planted in its place |
| Fall buckwheat after barley and wheat | June: harvest the grain crop; July: sow buckwheat |
| Fall buckwheat after sesame | Sown right after the sesame harvest |
| Late rice after summer buckwheat | In a rain-fed paddy, harvest buckwheat in spring, then transplant rice late |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;What these combinations have in common is that buckwheat keeps the field from lying idle. A field that opens up in June or July is too early for fall crops and too late for summer ones. Buckwheat fills that two- to three-month gap. This creates a structure where the same land is harvested twice in one year, which partly offsets buckwheat's weakness of D-grade calories per area.

### 11-2. Buckwheat as a Cleaning Crop

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Buckwheat has one use that other crops don't: cleaning up a field.

| Effect | Mechanism | Application |
|---|---|---|
| Weed suppression | Fast growth covers the ground and releases allelopathic substances | Sow one season in a weed-overrun field to clean it up |
| Nematode suppression | Substances from the roots lower nematode populations | Sown before potatoes or root vegetables |
| Phosphorus solubilization | Absorbs phosphorus other crops can't use | Turning it under returns that phosphorus to the soil |
| Improving soil structure | The taproot loosens the soil | Helps break up compacted ground |
| Green manure | The soft stems break down quickly | Turned under at flowering, it makes good green manure |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;With no pesticides, no plastic, and no herbicides available, the means of reviving a field are extremely limited. Buckwheat is one of the few, and above all, it produces food while it cleans up. If you have a field so overrun with weeds and nematodes that potatoes won't do well, putting buckwheat into it for a season is a thoroughly reasonable choice.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;If you plan to use buckwheat purely as green manure, turn it under before the seed ripens, when it's in full bloom. That's when stem and leaf mass are at their greatest and softest, so they break down fastest. Turn it under after the seed has ripened, and the following year buckwheat will come up on its own across the whole field and become a weed.

### 11-3. Rotation Order and the Follow-Crop Problem

| Year | Crop | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| Year 1 | Buckwheat | Clean up weeds and nematodes |
| Year 2 | Legumes | Restore nitrogen and replenish nutrients buckwheat stripped out |
| Year 3 | Potatoes or root vegetables | Make use of the cleaned-up land |
| Year 4 | Grain, or return to buckwheat | - |

> **Buckwheat has very strong nutrient uptake. A field where buckwheat has been harvested must be restored, either by adding fertilizer or by planting legumes.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Buckwheat's nutrient uptake is a double-edged sword. It's the reason buckwheat grows even in poor land, and also the reason the following crop runs short on nutrients. Buckwheat itself needs almost no fertilizer, but the advice to fertilize the field you plant it in is really for the sake of the crop that follows. Skip fertilizing buckwheat and move on, and the next crop pays the price.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Buckwheat's continuous-cropping tolerance is SS, so it holds up well to repeated planting, but sow it on the same field for several years running and yield gradually declines. It's not disease — it's nutrient depletion. The rule is to leave at least a 2-year gap, or to put legumes in between.
