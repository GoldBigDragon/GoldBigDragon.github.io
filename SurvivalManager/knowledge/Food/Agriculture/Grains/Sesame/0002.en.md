## 1. Disaster-Preparedness Evaluation

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Sesame and perilla received an Excluded verdict because their Calories per area grade is E, that is, they fall short of 15% of the self-sufficiency line. This means they do not qualify as a food crop. Had this gate not been applied, the score would have been 35.7 points, which corresponds to rank 162 among the 183 valid species. The task of this section is to sort out what the verdict of exclusion says and what it does not say.

### 1-3. What Does the E for Calories per Area Measure?

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In this table, the exclusion verdict comes from a single line, the first. 80kg per 1,000m², 38kcal per 100g, 30kcal per m², 0.01× the self-sufficiency line. One has to read what this calculation was performed on. The composition data are for perilla leaves (raw), the taxonomy is the mint family (Lamiaceae), genus Perilla, and the light-response group is set to leafy vegetables. In other words, this evaluation measures the leaves, not the seeds.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Measured by the leaves, the result is only natural. 100g of perilla leaves is 38kcal, perilla leaves are not a vegetable eaten in large amounts at one sitting, and the weight that comes from a leaf-only harvest is not large either. A value of 1% of the self-sufficiency line is inevitable. The verdict of exclusion is accurate. You cannot fill your stomach on perilla leaves.

> **That sesame and perilla rank at the bottom in calorie grade is a completely different matter from judging them useless in a disaster. What this crop provides is not calories but oil, and the value of oil is not captured in a calorie table.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;So the numbers for the seeds have to be looked at separately. Sesame seed is 539-573kcal per 100g, and perilla seed also exceeds 550kcal. This is 1.5 times that of cereals and seven times that of potatoes, and it is among the highest of all foods. The yield of sesame is small, about 60-100kg per 1,000m², but converted to calories it comes to around 350kcal per m². That is more than ten times the leaf-based 30kcal.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Even so, it amounts to only 13% of the self-sufficiency line of 2,616kcal, so it still cannot become a staple food. The verdict does not change. What differs is the nature of that 13%. It is calories made of fat, a kind of calorie that no other crop can produce in its place.

### 1-4. The Value of Oil

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The evaluation table has no criterion that measures oil. But if we lay out what oil does in a household during a disaster, it becomes clear why this crop must not be erased from the field.

| Use | What Changes |
|---|---|
| Cooking | Stir-frying, pan-frying and deep-frying become possible. Ingredients that cannot be eaten when only boiled or steamed become edible |
| Absorption of fat-soluble vitamins | Vitamins A, D, E, K and beta-carotene enter the body only when fat is present. This is why namul (seasoned vegetable side dishes) are dressed with oil |
| Essential fatty acids | The body cannot make linoleic acid and alpha-linolenic acid, so they must be obtained from food |
| Calorie concentration | Fat gives 9kcal per 1g, more than double that of carbohydrate. It is of great value when you must eat little and still gain a lot |
| Lamp light | Put a wick in it and burn it, and it becomes a flame. It is the only light on a night when the power is cut |
| Soap | Combined with ash lye, it becomes soap. It keeps hygiene from collapsing |
| Waterproofing and preservation | Worked into wood, leather and paper, it keeps water from soaking in |
| Lubrication | Applied to metal, hinges and tools, it reduces rust and friction |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Of these eight, none can be replaced by cereals, tuber crops (tubers/storage roots) or vegetables. If you have livestock, animal fat can replace some of them, but in a situation where there is not even enough feed for the animals, it is hard to use animals as a source of oil. The only way to produce oil in the field is through oilseed crops, and among them sesame is in the group with the highest oil content in the seed.

### 1-5. How to Read the Strengths

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The criteria graded SS are three: Protein density, Micronutrients and Infrastructure needs. S went to Storability, Prevalence in South Korea and Seed saving, and A went to Fertilizer demand, Water use, Cooking & processing, Yield retention without synthetic inputs and Yield stability.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The most practical strength is the S for Prevalence in South Korea. Because it is grown commercially across all eight provinces of South Korea, seeds can be obtained in any region. This is very different from water spinach and amaranth, which have a prevalence grade of B and whose seeds are hard to find outside their main growing areas. On the question of whether you can get hold of it at the moment a disaster begins, this difference is decisive.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The A for Yield retention without synthetic inputs, 56.0%, is also a value worth noting. It means that more than half of the yield is kept when chemical fertilizer is cut off, and compared with the 40.8% of leafy vegetables, that is much better. Because this is a crop that originally grew on poor soil, it does not depend much on fertilizer. The small requirement of 18g per m² behind the A for Fertilizer demand tells the same story.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Look at the A for Water use and the A for Yield stability together as well. Sesame is the very model of a drought-tolerant crop, and its total evapotranspiration of 400mm is about 1/3 that of rice. A year-to-year variation of 6.3% means the harvest does not swing widely from year to year. This stability comes with a caveat, however. In a year when a long monsoon season (jangma, late June to July in South Korea) leaves the field flooded, this figure does not hold at all.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The S for Seed saving rests on the fact that the plant is mainly self-pollinating. Varieties do not mix easily, so even with several varieties in one field, seed can still be collected separately. In a situation where you cannot buy packets of seed, this is a major safeguard.

### 1-6. How to Read the Weaknesses

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The criteria graded E are two: Calories per area and Cold hardiness. D went to Tillage & digging energy and Taste, and C went to Speed to harvest, Temperature range, Pest & disease pressure, Pest-control self-reliance, Continuous-cropping tolerance and Seed longevity.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The C for Continuous-cropping tolerance is the heaviest item in practical terms. The pathogens of Fusarium wilt and late blight stay in the soil for a long time, so sesame needs a break of 3-4 years. When the field is small, this constraint is considerable. If you plant in the same spot every year, the first year or two are fine, but from the third year it suddenly collapses. The crop rotation plan in Section 11 deals with this problem.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The C for Seed longevity must also be taken to heart. At room temperature, germination ability declines within 1-2 years. This is the fate of oil-rich seeds. Oil oxidizes when it meets air, and the oxidized oil damages the embryo inside the seed. This is very different from amaranth seed, which lasts 3-5 years.

> **Sesame and perilla are crops whose seed must be saved fresh every year. If you trust old seed and skip a year, you will have nothing to plant the following year.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The caveat attached to the S for Storability tells the same story. It states that the seeds keep for 1 year at room temperature, and it spells out that the reason they fall short of 3 years is oil rancidity. While other grains hold out for several years, one year is the limit for sesame and perilla. However, sesame contains natural antioxidants called sesamin and sesamolin, so its oil goes rancid more slowly than other vegetable oils. This difference is put to practical use in Sections 15 and 16.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The D for Tillage & digging energy, with an energy return of 2.8×, means that the calories coming back are small compared with the effort spent on tilling the field. In a situation without machines, where the field has to be tilled by human strength, this figure is a real burden. However, this calculation too rests on calories based on the leaves, and converted to oil, the picture changes.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The D for Taste came from the caveat "off-putting smell and aroma." The score was assigned for the strong aroma of perilla leaves, and in reality people differ in how they feel about this aroma. Yet the same aroma is also the basis for the C in Non-food value, because the strong aroma drives away animals and insects. If sesame or perilla is planted along the edge of a field or mixed in among other crops, fewer Korean water deer and wild boars come in. A trait that is a burden for the person eating becomes useful for protecting the field.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Finally, there are two burdens that do not show up in the evaluation table at all. One is waterlogging. Sesame is a crop that can lose a whole field within days if water stands, yet this risk is not captured in any cell. The other is seed shattering. When the capsules ripen, they split open by themselves and spill the seeds, so if the harvest moment is missed, the seeds are spilled in the field. These two are the causes that produce losses most often in actual cultivation, and they are covered in Sections 7 and 12 respectively.
