## 14. Toxicity and Compounds of Concern

### 14-1. There Is No Toxicity

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Sesame and perilla contain no toxic compounds that are harmful to people. There is nothing like the solanine of potatoes or the cyanogenic glycosides of cassava, and eating the seeds raw is not a problem. There are, however, a few things worth knowing.

### 14-2. Allergy

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Sesame is classified as one of the major allergenic foods. Depending on the person, reactions such as hives, a swollen feeling in the mouth, and difficulty breathing occur, and rarely they lead to a serious whole-body reaction. In a disaster, when medical care is hard to get, this point becomes more important than usual.

| What to Do | Details |
|---|---|
| Give a small amount to anyone eating it for the first time | Especially when giving it to a child for the first time, start with a small amount and watch the reaction |
| Stop if there is a reaction | Stop everything, down to sesame oil and kkaesogeum (sesame salt). Even trace amounts can cause a reaction |
| Substitute perilla | A sesame allergy does not necessarily appear with perilla |

### 14-3. Oxalic Acid and Phytic Acid in the Hull

| Compound | Where It Is | What the Problem Is | What to Do |
|---|---|---|---|
| Oxalic acid | The seed hull, especially in black sesame | It binds with calcium and interferes with its absorption. This is why sesame's abundant calcium cannot be fully used | Roasting reduces it somewhat. Removing the hull reduces it greatly, but the calcium goes down along with it |
| Phytic acid | Throughout the seed | It binds with iron, zinc and calcium and interferes with their absorption | Soaking in water, roasting or fermenting reduces it |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Whether to remove the hull is a matter of weighing. Most of the calcium is in the hull, so removing it loses calcium, and eating it with the hull means oxalic acid blocks the absorption of that calcium. In practice, roasting and eating it with the hull is better. This is because some of the oxalic acid breaks down during roasting, and even if some calcium is not absorbed, it is better than having none at all.

### 14-4. Rancidity of the Oil

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;What is actually a danger in sesame and perilla is not a toxic compound but spoiled oil. When oil meets air and oxidizes, peroxides and aldehydes form, and these not only smell bad but are harmful to the body and irritate the digestive tract.

| How to Tell | Details |
|---|---|
| Smell | The nutty aroma disappears and a sour or paint-like smell appears |
| Taste | A bitter or acrid taste appears |
| Color | The color deepens and becomes cloudy |
| Stickiness | The mouth of the bottle or the bottom of the container becomes sticky and gummed up |

> **Do not eat oil whose smell has changed. But do not throw it away either. Use it as lamp oil or for making soap. Oil that can no longer be eaten still has its uses.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Sesame oil goes rancid slowly thanks to sesamin and sesamolin. Perilla oil, with more than 60% alpha-linolenic acid, spoils much faster, but in return it has the property of hardening in air, so it is used to treat wood and paper. This is the principle behind oiled paper and wood finishes.

### 14-5. Other Cautions

| Item | Details |
|---|---|
| Excess fat | Sesame exceeds 500kcal per 100g. This is an advantage when calories are short, but eating a lot at once causes diarrhea |
| Nitrate in perilla leaves | It accumulates in leaves that received a lot of fertilizer. Discard the blanching water, and do not feed large amounts to infants |
| Eating perilla leaves raw | They can be washed and eaten as they are. However, if feces were used as fertilizer in the field, cook them before eating |
| Storing roasted sesame | Roasting improves the aroma but speeds up rancidity. Roast only as much as will be eaten |

## 15. Storage Without Refrigeration

### 15-1. The Rule Is to Keep It as Seed

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There is one rule for storing sesame and perilla. Kept as seed, they last far longer than if pressed into oil and stored.

| Form | Storage Period at Room Temperature | Why |
|---|:---:|---|
| Unroasted seed | About 1 year | The oil is locked inside the cells, so it contacts less air |
| Roasted seed | 3-6 months | The cells are broken and the oil is exposed to air |
| Ground sesame | Within 1 month | The surface area increases greatly and it spoils rapidly |
| Pressed sesame oil | 6 months to 1 year | Thanks to sesamin, it keeps relatively long among vegetable oils |
| Pressed perilla oil | 2-3 months | It spoils quickly because of its high alpha-linolenic acid content |

> **Sesame and perilla are crops to be stored in the storehouse as seed and roasted and pressed only as needed, each time. If you press it all at once, it becomes inedible within half a year.**

### 15-2. Storing Seed

| Step | Method | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Drying completely | Dry to a moisture content of 12% or less. When squeezed in the hand the seeds rustle dryly, and when bitten between the teeth they snap with a crack, they are ready | If moisture remains, mold grows and rancidity speeds up |
| Cooling | After sun-drying, cool completely in the shade before packing | If packed while warm, dew forms inside the container |
| Sealing | Put in an earthenware jar, glass bottle or metal tin and close it | Keeps out oxygen and moisture |
| Filling completely | Fill the container as full as possible | The air in the empty space in the container spoils the oil |
| Desiccant | Wrap roasted, cooled salt or charcoal in cloth and put it in | Absorbs the remaining moisture |
| Location | Keep in a cool, dark place with little temperature fluctuation | An earth cellar, a basement or a north-facing room is suitable |
| ÷ | Divide among several containers | Even if one container is lost, not everything is lost |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The instruction to fill the container full is what differs from the storage of other grains. For oil-rich seeds, reducing the surface in contact with air is advantageous. If a jar is only half filled, the air above spoils the oil, so it is better to divide the seed among several small containers and fill each one completely.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Grain insects are also a problem. Sealing comes first, and putting dried chili peppers, sancho (Zanthoxylum pepper) or ash in with the seed reduces infestation. If you find insects in it, spread it out in the sun to drive them off, then dry it again and repack it.

### 15-3. Storing Oil

| Method | Details |
|---|---|
| Divide into small bottles | Air gets in every time a bottle is opened, so open only a bottle holding as much as will be used |
| Fill to the top and stopper tightly | Reduces the empty space in the bottle |
| Use dark bottles or jars | Light promotes oxidation |
| Keep in a cool place | Do not keep it near the fire in the kitchen |
| Keep oil pressed without roasting separately | The aroma is weak but it keeps longer |

### 15-4. Storing Perilla Leaves

| Approach | Method | Duration |
|---|---|:---:|
| Leave as is | Unwashed, with surface moisture shaken off, in a cool place | 2-3 days |
| Drying | Dry completely in shade and wind, then seal | Several months |
| Jangajji (pickled vegetables in soy sauce or paste) | Layer the leaves in soy sauce or doenjang (Korean soybean paste) | Several months |
| Salting | Salt them and keep them under a weight | Several months |
| Kimchi | Season and pack into a jar | 2-3 months |
| Freezing | Blanch, squeeze out the water, and freeze | Several months |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Perilla leaves have a strong aroma and suit jangajji and pickling well. Leaves layered in soy sauce become a side dish (banchan) in themselves, and the soy sauce also takes on the aroma, so you get two things. Dried perilla leaves lose their aroma, but put into soup or porridge they are still useful.

## 16. Cooking and Processing - Pressing Oil

### 16-1. To Roast or Not to Roast

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The thing to decide before pressing oil is whether to roast or not. This choice completely changes the character of the oil that comes out.

| Item | Oil Pressed from Roasted Seed | Oil Pressed from Unroasted Seed |
|---|---|---|
| Aroma | A strong nutty aroma | Almost no aroma |
| Color | Dark brown | Clear yellow |
| Yield | More comes out | Less comes out |
| Smoke point | Low. Not suited to stir-frying | High. Can be used for deep-frying |
| Keeping quality | Sesamol forms during roasting, so it actually keeps longer | Average |
| Main uses | Dressing seasoned dishes (muchim), drizzling over at the end | Stir-frying, deep-frying, lamps, soap |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The reason roasting yields more oil is that heat softens the cell walls and the oil droplets inside the cells merge with one another. The oil that was trapped gains a path to come out when pressed, and for the same reason, if pressed without roasting, more oil is left behind in the seed.

| Roasting Tips | Details |
|---|---|
| Heat | Low to medium heat |
| Stirring | Stir without stopping. If just one side burns, it tastes bitter |
| Signs | The seeds pop with a crackling sound, turn light brown, and give off a nutty smell |
| When to finish | As soon as the color begins to develop, take it off the heat at once. It keeps cooking in the residual heat |
| Cooling | Spread out wide to cool. If piled up, they keep cooking inside and burn |

### 16-2. How Oil Pressing Works

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The principle of oil pressing is simple. It is to bring the oil droplets inside the cells outside, gather them together, and let them flow out. For this you crush, heat and press.

| Step | What You Do | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Crushing | Grind the seeds in a mortar or millstone | The cell walls have to be broken for the oil to come out. The finer the grind, the better it comes out |
| Heating | Steam the ground seed in a siru (earthenware steamer) or warm it in a pan | The viscosity of the oil drops so it flows well. The protein sets and the residue clumps together |
| Wrapping | Put it in a hemp or cotton sack | It holds back the residue and lets only the oil through |
| Pressing | Press hard with a lever or wedges | The greater the pressure, the more comes out |
| Collecting | Catch the oil that flows out in a container | |
| Settling | Leave it for a day or two so the sediment settles | If sediment remains, the oil spoils quickly |
| Decanting | Pour off only the clear upper part | |

| Pressing Method | How to Build It | Yield |
|---|---|---|
| Lever press | Hook one end of a long log onto a post and hang a heavy stone from the other end. Place the sack in the middle | 30-40% of the seed weight |
| Wedge press | Put the sack inside a log frame and press by hammering in wooden wedges | 35-45% |
| Screw press | A thick screw presses a plate down. Iron is needed | 40-50% |
| Hand twisting | Place the sack between two rods and wring it by twisting | 20-30% |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Since the oil content of sesame seed is 45-55%, traditional pressing extracts 2/3 to 75% of the oil it contains. From 1kg, 350-450g comes out, and the rest remains in the oil cake. The remaining oil raises the feed value of the oil cake, so it is not necessarily a loss.

### 16-3. Extracting Oil Without Pressing

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There is a method for situations where a pressing tool cannot be built. It uses water to float the oil out.

| Step | Method | Principle |
|---|---|---|
| Grinding | Grind roasted sesame finely into a thick paste | Breaks the cells |
| Adding water | Add water a little at a time while continuing to grind | It becomes a thick mixture of oil and water |
| Boiling | Pour into a wide pot and boil over low heat | As the water boils, the oil droplets merge with one another |
| Skimming | Skim off the oil that has risen to the top with a ladle | Oil is lighter than water, so it floats |
| Boiling down | Warm the skimmed oil again over low heat to drive off the remaining water | If water remains, the oil spoils |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This is a method that has long been used in Africa and India, and its advantage is that almost no tools are needed. Its drawbacks are that the yield is lower than pressing and that it consumes fuel, and what is left over is still used as oil cake.

### 16-4. Uses of Oil Cake

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The oil cake left after pressing oil is not something to throw away. More than half of the original seed remains as oil cake, and it contains 35-40% protein.

| Use | Details | Caution |
|---|---|---|
| Human food | Grind it and mix into porridge or tteok (rice cake) | It is rich in protein and so highly valuable. It is coarse, so too much makes it hard to eat |
| Livestock feed | A good protein source for chickens, pigs and cattle | If given in large amounts at once, the oil causes diarrhea |
| Fertilizer | Around 5-6% nitrogen, 2% phosphorus, 1% potassium | Always ferment it before applying. Raw cake generates heat in the soil and damages the roots |
| Fishing groundbait and attractant | The strong smell draws in fish and insects | |
| Fuel | Dry it and burn it | The remaining oil makes it burn well. If it has another use, do not burn it |

> **A sack of sesame divides into three things: oil, feed and fertilizer. Throwing away the oil cake means throwing away, once more, a value as great as the oil that was pressed out.**

### 16-5. Lamp Oil

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Vegetable oil is used to light the dark on nights when the power is out. Sesame oil has been used as lamp oil since ancient times.

| Item | Details |
|---|---|
| Wick | Twisted from cotton thread, hemp thread or cloth. The thicker it is, the brighter it burns and the faster the oil runs down |
| Container | Pour oil into a shallow dish or small cup and dip one end of the wick in |
| Principle | Oil is drawn up through the gaps between the wick's threads, and the flame burns the oil at its tip |
| Which oil is better | Sesame oil gives little soot and climbs well. Perilla oil gives much soot and a crust forms on the wick |
| Which oil to use | Use oil that has become hard to eat, or oil whose smell has changed, first |
| Brightness | One lamp is about as bright as one candle. Gather several and they light a room |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;An order has to be set. Eat the oil first, use what has become hard to eat in the lamp, and once that stage has passed, make it into soap. Burning perfectly good sesame oil from the start means burning calories.

### 16-6. Making Soap

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;When oil meets a strong alkali, it becomes soap. This reaction means a great deal in a disaster. When hygiene collapses, diarrheal diseases and skin diseases spread, and it is common for these to claim people before hunger does.

| Step | Method | What Happens |
|---|---|---|
| Leaching the lye | Stir hardwood ash into water, leave it for a day, then collect the liquid on top | The potassium carbonate in the ash dissolves into the water. This is lye |
| Causticizing | Add quicklime made by burning seashells or limestone to the lye, stir, and let it settle | Potassium carbonate turns into potassium hydroxide. The white sediment is calcium carbonate and is left to settle and discarded |
| Testing strength | Float a bird feather or a potato in it | If the feather begins to dissolve, or the potato floats with a patch about the size of a coin showing above the surface, the strength is sufficient |
| Mixing | Warm the oil and pour in the lye a little at a time, stirring without stopping | The fat in the oil meets the alkali and splits into soap and glycerin |
| Boiling | Boil over low heat for several hours, stirring | It continues until the reaction is finished. It thickens and changes color |
| Hardening | Add salt and the soap clumps together and floats to the top | The soap that was dissolved in water separates because of the salt |
| Finishing | Scoop it out and let it set in a mold, or use it as is as soft soap | Soap made with ash lye becomes soft soap, and adding salt makes it hard |

| Caution | Details |
|---|---|
| Lye damages flesh | Keep it off the eyes and skin. If it touches them, wash for a long time with water and rinse with diluted vinegar |
| Avoid metal vessels | Aluminum vessels corrode. Use cast iron or onggi (earthenware) |
| Under-finished soap is harsh | If alkali remains, it damages the skin. Let it cure for a few weeks before use |
| Softwood ash is weak | Hardwood ash has more alkaline content |

> **The order of oils to use for soap is clear. Oil that can no longer be eaten, animal fat, and residual oil from oil cake come first, and perfectly good sesame oil comes last. A doe (about 1.8 L) of oil should first become side dishes before it becomes soap.**

### 16-7. Eating the Seeds as They Are

| Method | Details | Fuel |
|---|---|---|
| Roasted, as is | Sprinkle roasted sesame on rice or namul (seasoned vegetable side dishes) | A few minutes |
| Kkaesogeum (sesame salt) | Pound roasted sesame together with salt | Only the roasting time |
| Kkaejuk (sesame porridge) | Stir roasted, ground sesame into water, add rice, and boil | The time to cook the porridge |
| Kkaegangjeong (sesame brittle) | Mix roasted sesame into jocheong (grain syrup) and let it set | The time to boil the jocheong |
| Ground into broth | Add ground sesame to soup or dressed dishes | None |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;If there is no tool for pressing oil, grinding the seeds and eating them as they are is the most efficient. Nothing is lost in a pressing process, and you eat the protein and fiber together. However, if swallowed whole the hulls are not digested, so the seeds must be ground.

### 16-8. Cooking Perilla Leaves

| Method | Details |
|---|---|
| Ssam (wraps) | Wash and use as they are |
| Jangajji | Layer in soy sauce or doenjang. Doubles as storage |
| Jeon (pan-fried battered dish) | Coat in flour batter and pan-fry |
| Jjim (steamed dish) | Spread seasoning between the layers and steam |
| Guk and jjigae (soups and stews) | Add at the very end. Boiling for long drives off the aroma |
| Dried leaves | Soak in water and put into soup |

## 17. Annual Work Calendar

### 17-1. Monthly Table

| Month (South Korea) | Sesame | Perilla | Other Work |
|---|---|---|---|
| January-February | Rest | Rest | Check the condition of the seed. Check how the stored oil is keeping |
| March | Prepare and disinfect the seed | Prepare the seed | Prepare compost. Decide where to plant sesame this year |
| April | Till the field, build ridges, apply base fertilizer | Sow leaf perilla in late April | Confirm the rotation order |
| May | Sow from mid-May. Build the ridges high | Finish sowing by early May, thin | Soak the seed in water and sort it, then give it the hot-water treatment |
| June | Thin, weed, hill | Start the leaf harvest (from late June) | Maintain the furrows and drainage ditches |
| July | Flowering begins. Concentrate on drainage management | Leaf harvest and top-dressing | It is the monsoon season (jangma, late June to July in South Korea). Watch for late blight |
| August | Pinch 35-40 days after the first flower | The leaf harvest continues | Watch for stink bugs and moths |
| September | Ripening. From late September, watch the lower capsules | Finish the leaf harvest in late September | Put bags over the stems for seed saving |
| Late September to early October | Harvest. Cut on overcast days, in the morning or evening | Prepare to harvest oil perilla | Tie into bundles and stand them to dry |
| October | Dry, shake out, clean and sort | Harvest and shake out oil perilla | Set aside the seed-saving stock separately |
| November | Store, press oil | Store | Ferment the oil cake and turn it into fertilizer |
| December | Rest | Rest | Check the condition of storage |

### 17-2. The Thread Running Through the Year

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;A sesame year in South Korea has three critical points: building the ridges in May, the monsoon in July, and the harvest timing in late September.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Building the ridges in May is an invisible critical point. At planting time the ridge height seems to make no difference at all, and the difference shows only two months later, in the monsoon. If you skimp on one shovelful and build the ridges low, you lose the whole field in July.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The July monsoon is when this crop's fate is decided. All that can be done is to clear the furrows and remove diseased plants, and the rest was already settled in May.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The late-September harvest is a critical point decided within a matter of days. The moment when two or three lower capsules split open is the criterion, and if you miss those few days, you spill a whole year's farming onto the field. The most valuable habit is to look at the lower capsules once every three or four days from mid-September.

> **Growing sesame and perilla comes down to raising the ridges in May and harvesting by eye in September. How high the ridges were built is half of it, and noticing the day the lower capsules split open is the other half.**
