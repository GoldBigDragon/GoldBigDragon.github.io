## Introduction

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Kkae is a single Korean name that covers two different plants. Sesame (Sesamum indicum) is an annual herb of the sesame family (Pedaliaceae) whose seeds yield sesame oil, while perilla (Perilla frutescens) is an annual herb of the mint family (Lamiaceae) whose seeds yield perilla oil and whose leaves are eaten as kkaennip (perilla leaves). They differ in appearance and in use, but they share one trait: nearly half of the seed is oil. From the disaster-preparedness standpoint taken in this document, the two also hold almost the same place.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In the evaluation table, this crop received an Excluded verdict. Its Calories per area grade is E; it reaches less than 15% of the self-sufficiency line, which means it does not qualify as a food crop. The verdict itself is accurate: no calculation supports a plan to fill your stomach by growing sesame or perilla. But if you look only at this verdict and strike these crops from the field, you lose something of great importance in a disaster at the same time. The value of sesame and perilla lies not in calories but in oil, and oil proves its worth less as calories than in cooking with oil, in lamps, and in soap-making.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Oil makes up 45-55% of sesame seed. Pressing 1kg of seed yields around 400g of oil. Why these figures matter becomes clear if you picture a household without oil. You cannot stir-fry, you cannot deep-fry, and the fat-soluble vitamins A, D, E and K, which are absorbed only together with fat, no longer reach the body. In a diet made up of grains and vegetables alone, fat is the nutrient that runs out first, and when fat is short, the body wastes away even when the same amount of food is eaten. Where there are no livestock, or where livestock cannot be slaughtered, the only way to produce oil is through oilseed crops.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The uses of oil do not end with eating. On a night when the power is out, it is oil that lights the dark. Sesame oil has been used as lamp oil since ancient times; it climbs the wick well and gives off little soot. And when oil meets ash lye, it becomes soap. What soap means in a disaster needs no explanation. Diseases that spread because people cannot wash their hands often kill before hunger does. A handful of sesame or perilla seeds divides into oil to eat, oil to burn, and soap to wash with.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;What is left after the oil is pressed is not thrown away either. The residue, known as kkaenmuk (sesame and perilla oil cake), still holds 35-40% protein. It is top-grade livestock feed, and worked into the field it becomes a nitrogen-rich fertilizer. When chemical fertilizer is cut off, oil cake is one of the best organic fertilizers within reach. Pressing oil is not a matter of taking one thing and throwing the rest away; it is a matter of dividing a sack of seeds into three: oil, feed and fertilizer.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;As a crop to grow, sesame is a plant of extremes. It is very strong against drought. Having grown for thousands of years in the dry lands of Africa and India, it endures dry soil better than the cereals do. But it is extremely weak where water stands. If a field is submerged for even a day or two, the roots are damaged, late blight spreads, and the entire field is lost. The biggest obstacle to growing sesame is the fact that summer in South Korea is the monsoon season (jangma, late June to July), and so for this crop, how high the ridges are raised and how well the drainage channels are cut matter more than any other task.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Harvesting is no easy matter either. When sesame fruits ripen, the top of each one splits open by itself and spills the seeds. Moreover, they ripen one after another from the bottom up, so by the time the upper ones have ripened, the lower ones have already spilled everything. For that reason, the method used is to cut the plants when the lowest two or three capsules begin to split open, tie them into bundles, stand them upright to dry, and then shake out the seeds.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In sum, sesame and perilla are not crops that fill the grain store but crops that fill the kitchen's oil bottle, the lamp and the soap dish. They are not crops to which a whole field should be given over, but a few ridges will supply a year's oil. Whether or not those few ridges exist makes a large difference to how a household fares when a disaster drags on.
