## 9. Pests and Diseases — Control Without Chemicals

### 9-1. Pests Confirmed in South Korea

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The evaluation table's grade for pest and disease pressure is C, with an estimated yield loss of 50.6%. That figure is drawn from a class-level anchor rather than a direct measurement on amaranth itself, but field surveys of amaranth in South Korea have confirmed pests from 7 families and 18 species, and the damage has not been minor.

| Pest group | Species | Timing | Damage |
|---|---|---|---|
| Aphids | Bean aphid, cotton aphid, green peach aphid, and 3 species in all | Late April to early June | 51.9% reduction in seed yield |
| Stink bugs | Small brown stink bug, Nysius plebeius, and 12 species in all | Mid-May to late October | 69.8% reduction in seed yield |
| Moths | White-striped grass moth, beet armyworm, and 4 species in all | Mid-August to mid-October | 72.5% reduction in leaf biomass |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The heaviest of these is the stink bugs. A figure of 69.8% means that, left unchecked, they take seven-tenths of the seed. Stink bugs do not chew the leaves; they insert their mouthpart into the ripening seed and suck out the contents. A seed head that looks fine from the outside ends up hollow inside. With as many as 12 species confirmed, appearing continuously from May through October, this is the pest group that demands the most attention in growing amaranth.

| Pest | Response without chemicals | Principle |
|---|---|---|
| Aphids | Wash them off with a strong stream of water. Spray soapy water. Protect ladybugs and lacewings | Soap dissolves the insect's oily film and blocks its breathing pores |
| Stink bugs | Catch and remove them by hand early in the morning. Clear weeds around the field | In the cool early morning they move sluggishly and are easy to catch. Weeds serve as their habitat |
| Moth caterpillars | Catch them by hand at dusk. Remove egg clusters together with the leaf | Caterpillars hide by day and come out in the evening |
| Adult moths | Use a light trap that lures them in at night | Takes advantage of their attraction to light |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;A light trap works even without electricity. Set a wide bowl of water above an oil lamp or a bonfire, and moths drawn to the light fall into the water. Floating a little oil on the surface catches them even better. That said, this method traps not only harmful moths but other insects active at night as well, so it is better not to leave it burning every single night.

### 9-2. Major Diseases

| Disease | Symptoms | When it strikes | Response without chemicals |
|---|---|---|---|
| Damping-off | The base of a young sprout pinches thin and the plant collapses | Seedling stage, when sown too thickly | Avoid sowing too thickly. Avoid under-composted manure. Give seed a warm-water treatment |
| Leaf blight | Brown spots appear on leaves and spread | During the monsoon season | Remove diseased leaves from the field. Ensure airflow |
| Root rot | Roots turn brown and the plant wilts | When water stands | Fix the drainage. Raise the beds |
| Powdery mildew | White powder settles on the leaves | In dry weather in late summer | Ensure airflow. Remove diseased leaves |
| Seed-head rot | The seed head rots black during ripening | When rain continues during ripening | Do not plant too thickly. Stand up any lodged plants |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Seed-head rot, the last row, is one of the main reasons behind the D grade for yield stability. Amaranth's seed head is a dense cluster of tiny flowers, so the inside does not dry out easily. If rain continues for several days during ripening, moisture gets trapped inside the seed head and mold spreads. This risk can only be managed through plant spacing and harvest timing. The wide 40-50cm plant spacing lowers this risk at the cost of some yield.

### 9-3. Pest-Control Materials Made Without Chemicals

| Material | How to make it | What it is used for |
|---|---|---|
| Soapy water | Dissolve 20g of unscented soap in 1L of water | Aphids, mites |
| Wood ash | Sift and sprinkle over leaves and ground | Crawling pests; also supplies potassium |
| Garlic-steeped water | Grind 100g of garlic, soak in 1L of water, then strain | Repels pests |
| Wood vinegar | Dilute 500 to 1,000-fold | Repels pests, suppresses mold |
| Bordeaux mixture | Made by dissolving copper sulfate and quicklime in water | Prevents fungal disease |
| Tobacco-steeped water | Soak tobacco leaves in water and steep | Aphids. Do not use right before harvest |

### 9-4. Preventing Trouble Before Planting

| Method | Content |
|---|---|
| Wide spacing | Keep 40-50cm between plants. Airflow blocks fungal disease |
| Crop rotation | Do not plant amaranth family crops back to back. Rest the ground for 2 years |
| Clearing the field's edges | Remove the weeds where stink bugs hide |
| Early detection | Watch for aphids from late April, and for stink bugs from mid-May |
| Take diseased material out of the field | Do not leave diseased leaves and seed heads in the field |
| Protecting natural predators | Do not kill ladybugs, lacewings, or spiders |

## 10. Nutrient Deficiency and Excess

### 10-1. Reading Deficiency Symptoms

| Symptom | Where it appears first | Cause | Why | Natural remedy |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Whole leaf yellows, plant stays small | Older leaves lower down first | Nitrogen deficiency | Nitrogen moves within the plant, pulled out of old leaves and sent to new ones | Diluted urine, fermented plant liquid fertilizer, fully matured compost |
| Leaf edges scorch brown | Lower leaves first | Potassium deficiency | Potassium is also highly mobile, so older leaves are sacrificed first | Sprinkle a small amount of wood ash |
| Leaves dark and purplish, growth slow | Whole plant | Phosphorus deficiency | Can also appear when cold temperatures reduce phosphorus uptake | Bone meal, wood ash |
| New leaves yellow with only the veins green | Upper new leaves first | Iron deficiency | Iron does not move well, so new leaves starve first. If pH is high, the plant cannot use it even if it is in the soil | Cut back on wood ash and add organic matter |
| New shoot tips dry out and twist | Growing point | Calcium deficiency | Calcium travels with the water flow and does not reach new shoots well | Charred, ground shell or eggshell |
| Seed heads fail to form, or grains are empty | Seed head | Phosphorus or potassium deficiency, or stink bugs | Either the nutrients needed for fruiting are lacking, or they have been sucked out | Wood ash, bone meal. Check for stink bugs first |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The most useful clue when reading symptoms is where they appear first. If damage starts on the older leaves, the plant is short on a nutrient that moves easily within it (nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, magnesium); if it starts on the new leaves, it is short on one that does not move easily (iron, calcium, boron).

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There is one trap with amaranth, though. In red-leaved varieties, color is hard to judge by. Red lines have naturally red leaves to begin with, so this cannot be told apart from the purplish tinge of phosphorus deficiency. For these varieties, look at leaf size and growth speed instead of color.

### 10-2. Excess Symptoms

| Symptom | Cause | Why | Response |
|---|---|---|---|
| Grows tall but the stem is soft and lodges | Excess nitrogen | The cell walls stretch thin and the tissue weakens | Stop the nitrogen. Support with hilling and staking |
| Leaves lush but seed heads weak | Excess nitrogen | Resources are pulled toward the leaves and do not reach the seed | Stop nitrogen from before the seed heads form |
| Leaves dark blue-green, aphids swarm | Excess nitrogen | The sap grows richer in amino acids, which aphids prefer | Same response |
| Leaf edges dry out, roots turn brown | Excess fertilizer concentration | Soil salt concentration rises and draws water out of the roots | Water generously to flush it out |
| Leaves yellow, growth stops, soil hardens | Alkalization from too much wood ash | pH rises and the plant cannot use iron or manganese | Stop the wood ash and add organic matter |

> **Most nutrient problems in amaranth are not deficiencies but excess nitrogen. This crop tolerates hunger well and fullness poorly.**

### 10-3. Nitrates That Build Up in the Leaves

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Leaves of amaranth given a lot of nitrogen build up nitrates. Amaranth family plants are naturally prone to accumulating nitrates, and spinach and Swiss chard share this same problem. Nitrate itself has low toxicity, but once converted to nitrite in the mouth and gut, it interferes with the blood's ability to carry oxygen. This is especially dangerous for infants.

| How to reduce it | Principle |
|---|---|
| Stop nitrogen fertilizer from 2 weeks before harvest | Gives the plant time to use up the nitrate it has accumulated on its own |
| Pick in the afternoon on a sunny day | While exposed to light, nitrate converts into protein, so it drops during the day |
| Discard the blanching water | Nitrate dissolves readily in water, and a large share leaves with it |
| Do not feed much to infants | This is the age most vulnerable to nitrite |
| Use the leaf rather than the stem | Nitrate accumulates more in the stem and petiole |

## 11. Intercropping and Crop Rotation

### 11-1. Compatibility

| Compatibility | Crop | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| Good | Legumes (cowpea, mung bean, soybean) | Some of the nitrogen fixed in their root nodules leaches into the soil for amaranth to use. Since amaranth needs little nitrogen, there is little competition |
| Good | Maize, sorghum | Both are tall and C4, so their needs are similar. More than anything, their amino acids complement each other when eaten together |
| Good | Sunflower | Similar height, so neither shades the other |
| Fair | Winter squash, sweet potato | Their ground cover suppresses weeds. However, covering amaranth early in its growth is harmful |
| Bad | Amaranth family crops (spinach, Swiss chard, sugar beet) | Being the same family, they share pathogens and pests |
| Bad | Cool-season leafy vegetables such as lettuce and napa cabbage | Cannot grow once shaded by amaranth |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The reason maize and sorghum make good partners lies less in the field than at the table. Maize and sorghum protein is short on lysine, while amaranth has plenty. Harvest them from the same field and mix them in the same bowl, and the body retains more protein than eating each one separately. When choosing crops for a disaster situation, keeping this kind of pairing in mind matters just as much as expanding the planted area.

> **Look at compatibility on the plate, not only in the field. Half the reason to plant maize and amaranth together is so they can be eaten together.**

### 11-2. Crop Rotation

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The evaluation table gives an A grade for continuous-cropping tolerance, with the basis "amaranth family, 2-year break recommended." Plant it in the same spot year after year and pathogens and pests that target amaranth family crops build up. Stink bugs in particular grow more numerous year after year when their food source stays in the same place.

| Year | What to plant | What you get |
|---|---|---|
| Year 1 | Amaranth | Seed and leaf. The deep root works the ground open |
| Year 2 | Legumes (soybean, cowpea, mung bean) | Returns the nitrogen amaranth used |
| Year 3 | Brassicas or cucurbits | A different family, so pathogens do not carry over |
| Year 4 | Back to amaranth | |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Amaranth plays one special role in a rotation. As its taproot goes down more than 2m, it breaks through any hardpan layer, and once that root decays, it leaves behind a channel for water and air to pass through. In a field packed hard from a run of shallow-rooted crops, standing amaranth for a single year loosens that lower layer. It amounts to tilling the ground with a root, a trait worth having where there is neither machinery nor fuel.

## 12. Harvest — The Fight Against Shattering

### 12-1. Leaf Harvest

| Item | Details |
|---|---|
| Timing | From around day 40 after sowing until flowering |
| What | Pick or cut the mature lower leaves by hand or with scissors |
| How much | Never more than a third of the plant at once |
| Interval | 1 to 2 weeks |
| When to stop | Once the seed heads begin to form |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Younger leaves are more tender and lower in oxalic acid. Fully grown, thickened leaves are tough and astringent, so the best time is when a leaf has grown about the size of your palm. For plants grown purely for leaves, pinch them to lower the height and encourage side branches, which multiplies the spots you can pick from.

### 12-2. Why Seed Harvest Is Difficult

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The hardest judgment call in growing amaranth is when to cut it. The cause is shattering. A wild plant drops its seed the moment it ripens in order to disperse it, and even as a cultivated crop, amaranth has kept a good deal of this trait. On top of that, a whole seed head does not ripen all at once.

| Situation | What happens |
|---|---|
| Cut too early | Many grains are underripe, so more of them are small and hollow. Germination ability also suffers |
| Cut at the right time | The lower grains have already dropped a little and the upper ones have just ripened. The point of least loss |
| Cut too late | All the ripe seed has already fallen into the field, leaving only empty seed heads |
| Left out in the rain | Grains sprout inside the seed head or go moldy and become unusable |

| Sign to judge by | What to look for |
|---|---|
| Florets | The white florets disappear all the way to the tip of the seed head and dry brown |
| Seed-head color | The green fades and the head turns brown or reddish-brown |
| Stripping by hand | The point when a light stripping pass with your palm makes the grains start falling easily |
| Leaf condition | The lower leaves start turning yellow and falling |
| Grain hardness | Fully ripe when pressing with a fingernail leaves no mark and the grain feels hard |

> **The moment a palm-stripping pass makes a few grains start to fall is the time to cut. If nothing falls at all, it is too early; if the grains pour out, it is already too late.**

### 12-3. The Order: Cutting, Drying, Threshing

| Step | Method | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Cutting | On a clear day, once the morning dew has dried, cut the flower stalks bearing the seed heads | Piling them wet lets heat and mold build up inside the seed head, and grains sprout |
| Carrying | Support them from below with a sack or cloth and move them carefully | A considerable amount falls off from the jostling during carrying |
| Drying | Hang upside down or spread on a straw mat and dry in sun and wind for 5-10 days | The inside of the seed head must dry too before the grain releases easily |
| Catching what falls | When hanging them, spread cloth or a sack underneath | Catches what drops on its own while drying |
| Threshing | Put them in a sack and beat them, or strike them with a stick on a straw mat | The same approach used for threshing sesame |
| Threshing again | One pass does not release everything, so turn them over and repeat | A good deal remains inside the seed head |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Cutting and hanging it upside down to dry beats drying it standing in the field because it lets you catch the seed that falls. Ripen it standing in the field and everything that drops simply goes into the soil. That is why volunteer sprouts come up in a thick mass the following year wherever amaranth once stood, and the sheer amount of them shows just how much was lost.

### 12-4. Cleaning — The Most Tedious Job

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;What comes out of threshing is not just seed. Broken bits of calyx, twig fragments, and pieces of husk come out along with it. The trouble is that these fragments are close enough to the seed in size and weight that the usual methods do not separate them well.

| Method | How to do it | What it filters out | Limitation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Coarse sieve | Sift through a sieve with holes bigger than the seed | Twigs and large fragments | Small fragments pass through along with the seed |
| Fine sieve | Set on a sieve with holes smaller than the seed and sift | Dust and very fine fragments | The holes need to be 1mm or smaller, which is hard to find |
| Winnowing basket | Toss it up and down in a winnowing basket to let the wind carry debris off | Light husk fragments | The seed is light too and blows away with it. Only works in light wind |
| Fanning | Pour slowly from a height while fanning from the side | Husk fragments | You have to accept some seed loss |
| Water sorting | Pour into water and skim off what floats | Hollow grains and husk | Since the seed absorbs water, it must be dried immediately afterward |
| Inclined board | Pour onto a tilted board and let it roll | Only the round seed rolls down | Getting the board and angle right takes trial and error |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The surest method is water sorting. The weight difference between the seed and the debris is small, but putting them in water reveals the difference in their nature. A well-filled seed sinks, while hollow grains and husk float. Pour them into a generously filled bowl of water, stir, skim off what floats, then scoop out what sank and spread it out to dry right away.

> **The most important thing about water sorting is how quickly you act after scooping the seed out. Once amaranth seed takes up water, it starts to sprout within hours. Fail to spread it out to dry right away and you lose the whole batch.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The inclined-board method takes advantage of the fact that the lens-shaped seed rolls well. Prop a board or straw mat up at an angle and pour slowly from the top: the seed rolls down and collects in a bowl set below, while the flat husk fragments stay stuck to the board. Needing almost no tools, it suits a disaster situation well.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;It also helps to know that perfect cleaning is not necessary. A little husk mixed in does not get in the way of eating it, and once cooked, most of it is indistinguishable anyway. Cleaning carefully only the portion you will keep for seed, and cleaning the portion you will eat just well enough, is the way to save effort.
