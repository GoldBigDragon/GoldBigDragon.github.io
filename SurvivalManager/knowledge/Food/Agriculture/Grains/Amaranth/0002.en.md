## 1. Disaster-Preparedness Evaluation

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Amaranth's overall grade is D, with a score of 36.5. Broken down by scenario, its short-term ability to get through the early stage of a disaster is E (35.1), its long-term, multi-year self-reliance ability is D (38.1), and its value as a supplementary food beyond the staple is S (56.5). The fact that the long-term score is 3 points higher than the short-term score, and that the supplementary score is 20 points higher than the overall score, sums up this crop's character in two strokes. It is not a crop to lean on in an emergency; it is a crop that pays off once it has had time to establish itself, and that payoff comes not from being a staple but from rounding one out.

### 1-3. Calories per Area's D Drags Everything Down

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The criterion that cuts most deeply into the overall score in this table is calories per area, graded D. A yield of 200kg per 1,000m² is only about a third of what rice (around 500kg) or maize (over 600kg) produce. Calories per 100g stand at 383kcal, on par with rice or wheat, but there simply isn't enough yield to multiply that calorie count by.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Why the yield is so low is answered by the size of the seed. Amaranth seed is a bit over 1mm across, and even 1,000 grains together weigh only 0.6g to 0.9g. That is in an entirely different league from rice at 22g, wheat at 40g, or soybean at 200g per 1,000 grains. A single seed head may carry several thousand grains, yet converted to weight it does not amount to much. On top of that, because the grains drop from the seed head on their own as they ripen, a considerable amount is lost again in the course of harvesting.

> **Amaranth's low score does not mean it is a bad crop — it means it loses if it has to compete with rice and maize for the staple-food role. Change the contest, and its value changes with it.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Yield stability's D and the 24.8% year-to-year variation should be read alongside this. It means the harvest swings up or down by about 25% from one year to the next — too wide a margin to stake a livelihood on. The variation generally has two causes. One is that frequent rain during flowering and grain-filling keeps the seed from ripening properly and lets it rot in the seed head. The other is that shattering losses swing widely depending on that year's weather and the timing of harvest.

### 1-4. What Builds the S in Supplementary Value

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The 20-point gap between the supplementary score of 56.5 and the overall score of 36.5 comes from protein density's S and micronutrients' A. But this crop's S in protein density is a different kind of S from other crops'. A leafy vegetable's high P:E comes from having a small denominator — few calories — but amaranth posts a P:E of 17.8% even while carrying a full grain-level 383kcal per 100g. A high ratio despite a large denominator means the absolute amount is large. 17g of protein per 100g is more than double what rice offers.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;On top of that, the "+1 grade for an essential amino acid score of 90 or more" tells you this crop's real value. Protein is not judged on quantity alone. For the human body to build protein, all nine essential amino acids must be present, and if even one falls short, no matter how much of the rest there is, only as much gets used as the shortest one allows. It is like a barrel made of staves: if one stave is short, the water only fills to that height.

| Grain | Most limiting essential amino acid | What is the problem |
|---|---|---|
| Rice | Lysine | Protein content itself is also low, a double disadvantage |
| Wheat | Lysine | Gluten protein contains almost no lysine |
| Maize | Lysine, tryptophan | One cause of pellagra in diets based on maize alone |
| Sorghum | Lysine | Much the same situation as maize |
| Soybean | Methionine | Lysine is plentiful, so it pairs well with grain |
| Amaranth | No clearly limiting amino acid | Especially rich in lysine, filling the gap grains leave |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Summed up in one line, this table says amaranth is a grain that behaves like a bean. Mixing amaranth into corn porridge raises how efficiently the body can use the corn's protein. Eat the same weight of food, and a different amount of protein ends up staying in the body. When meat, fish, and eggs disappear in a disaster and the diet turns to grain, this complementary effect is worth more than the numbers alone show.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There is also the flip side of cooking & processing's C to consider. The grade is low, but the basis line carries the words "no milling needed." Rice has to be hulled, then boiled before it is fit to eat. Wheat has to be ground. Amaranth skips this process entirely. The reason lies in the seed's structure: the starch is packed at the very center of the seed with the embryo wrapped around it like a ring, so there is no separate bran layer to strip away. Once the mill and the rice-polisher stop running, this trait is a bigger advantage than it first appears.

### 1-5. How to Read the Strengths

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The criteria that scored SS are infrastructure needs and storability, and S went to protein density, fertilizer demand, water use, taste, and seed saving.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Water use's S and fertilizer demand's S together make up this crop's real-world value. Total evapotranspiration of 250mm is remarkably low next to rice, which exceeds 1,000mm, or maize, at around 500mm. C4 photosynthesis is the reason. A C4 plant first captures and concentrates atmospheric carbon dioxide inside the leaf before using it, so it can take in what it needs while opening its stomata only a little. The less its stomata open, the less water escapes. That is why amaranth is still standing in a field where every other crop has withered in a drought year.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The "adapted to poor soil" behind fertilizer demand's S belongs to the same story. By 70 days after sowing, amaranth's root goes down more than 2m and spreads sideways as far as 1.8m. Even once the topsoil dries out and the nutrients in the shallow layer run out, the plant draws water and nutrients up from below. It is the same ability that lets weedy pigweed grow just about anywhere.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Storability's SS is this crop's clearest strength. Well-dried grain keeps for more than 3 years at room temperature. Combined with seed longevity's A, food and seed effectively sit in the same sack. Vegetables are hard to store and tuber crops (tubers/storage roots) struggle to make it through winter, but amaranth grain sealed in a jar holds up for several years. The longer a disaster drags on, the more this storability is worth.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Seed saving's S is just as practical. Because it is mainly self-pollinating, varieties do not mix easily, and because the seed is so small and so plentiful, a single plant yields thousands of grains. There is no need to worry about having enough seed for next year — if anything, there will be extra.

### 1-6. How to Read the Weaknesses

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Three criteria scored E: sunlight, cold hardiness, and non-food value. D went to calories per area, perenniality, and yield stability, and C went to cooking & processing, pest & disease pressure, pest-control self-reliance, and tillage & digging energy.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Sunlight's E is a criterion that must be kept firmly in mind. Yield dropping to 45% at 40% shade means a spot that is shaded for even half the day will produce less than half a normal harvest. C4 photosynthesis is built to maximize efficiency under strong light, so when light runs short it actually costs the plant — the energy spent concentrating carbon dioxide is simply wasted. That is the exact opposite of leafy vegetables, which hold on to 86% of their yield at 40% shade.

> **Give amaranth the sunniest spot in the field. As a crop for filling a shady odd corner, it is about the worst choice there is.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Cold hardiness' E and temperature range's B are traces of its tropical highland origin. A single frost kills it, and its growing range is a narrow 20°C to 32°C. In South Korea, there is only one growing cycle — planted in early May, harvested in September — and the cool stretches of spring and autumn cannot be used. Perenniality's D, "1 harvest per year," is tied directly to this.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;It is worth keeping in mind, though, that perenniality's D is scored on the grain alone. The seed is harvested only once a year, but the leaves are different. Starting 40 days after sowing, the lower leaves can be picked, and this can be repeated three or four times before flowering. This double harvest, which no single box on the evaluation table captures, is half the real reason for planting amaranth.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Cooking & processing's C is a grade it earns even while carrying the advantage, noted earlier, of needing no milling. The reason it is low is cooking time. The seed needs 20 to 30 minutes of boiling to cook through, and where fuel is scarce, that is a real cost. Using the toast-and-pop method instead finishes the job in seconds, though, so choosing the right cooking method can offset a good part of this. Section 16 covers this.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There is a separate practical burden that the evaluation table does not show at all: threshing and cleaning the seed after harvest. The seed is too small to sieve out easily, and the bits of calyx that fall along with it are close enough in weight to the seed that even winnowing does not separate them well. This is the most time-consuming part of growing amaranth, and section 12 covers it.
