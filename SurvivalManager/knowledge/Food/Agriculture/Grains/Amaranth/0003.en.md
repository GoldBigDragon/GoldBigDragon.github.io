## 2. What Kind of Plant Is This?

### 2-1. Taxonomy and Origin

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Amaranth is an annual herb in the amaranth family (Amaranthaceae), genus Amaranthus. Worldwide there are just over sixty species, and only a handful of these are grown for food. The same family includes spinach, Swiss chard, sugar beet, and quinoa. Knowing this lineage explains on its own why oxalic acid and nitrates build up in amaranth leaves — spinach and Swiss chard share the same trait for the same reason.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The weedy pigweed and hairy pigweed commonly seen in South Korea's fields and roadsides belong to the same genus. They look so similar that telling them apart at the seedling stage is hard, and if wild pigweed grows mixed into an amaranth field, it is easy to confuse the two when thinning. Choosing a variety with clearly red or patterned leaves cuts down on this problem. Wild pigweed leaves can also be eaten as namul (a seasoned vegetable side dish), so a mix-in is not without use — but it does become a problem when saving seed.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The plant is native to Central America and the Andean highlands. Among the Aztecs it was one of three staple crops alongside maize and beans, and it was also used in religious rites. Cultivation was nearly wiped out after the Spanish conquest, then revived in the late 20th century once its nutritional value was rediscovered. This origin shapes its growing requirements. First, as a plant from the tropical highlands, it has never known frost and tolerates heat well. Second, as a plant from land with a sharply defined dry and rainy season, it withstands drought, but its roots rot if water stands. Third, having evolved under strong equatorial sunlight, it developed the C4 pathway, which is why it is weak in shade.

### 2-2. What Does "C4" Mean?

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;That amaranth is one of the rare dicots that use C4 photosynthesis ties into nearly every trait this crop has, so it deserves its own explanation.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Ordinary plants capture the carbon dioxide that enters through the leaf's stomata directly, using a single enzyme. That enzyme, however, does not tell carbon dioxide and oxygen apart very well. When the day turns hot and the sun grows strong, oxygen concentration inside the leaf rises, and the enzyme increasingly grabs oxygen instead of carbon dioxide. Processing what that produces costs energy, so the plant ends up spending on the spot what photosynthesis just earned. This is called photorespiration, and it worsens the hotter it gets. It is one reason lettuce and napa cabbage stop growing in the height of summer.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;C4 plants avoid this problem by changing the structure of the leaf. The outer cells first capture carbon dioxide into a four-carbon compound and ferry it to the inner cells, where it is released again. Because carbon dioxide concentration is higher in the inner cells, the enzyme has little chance to grab oxygen by mistake. Photorespiration nearly disappears.

| Result | What changes |
|---|---|
| Advantage in heat | Above 25°C, photosynthesis actually speeds up. Summer is its peak season |
| Saves water | Because it concentrates carbon dioxide, it can open its stomata less. This is the basis for the 250mm evapotranspiration figure |
| Saves nitrogen | It needs less enzyme, so its leaves require less nitrogen. This is the basis for its adaptation to poor soil |
| Disadvantage in shade | Running the concentrating mechanism costs energy. When light is scarce, only that cost is paid, at a loss |

> **Amaranth's strengths and weaknesses all come from this one same structure. The reason it thrives in heat and drought is the same reason it collapses in shade.**

### 2-3. Appearance

| Organ | Characteristics |
|---|---|
| Leaves | Arranged alternately. Broadly egg-shaped, 5-15cm long. There are green, red, and mixed-color lines |
| Stem | Some lines grow upright, others creep. Upright ones grow up to 2m tall and can exceed 3cm in thickness |
| Roots | Centered on a straight taproot. By 70 days after sowing it reaches 2.4m deep and spreads 1.8m wide |
| Flowers | Very small flowers cluster densely into a spike shape. A single plant usually bears both female and male flowers together, and pollen travels by wind |
| Seed | A lens shape 1-1.5mm across. 1,000 seeds weigh 0.6-0.9g, and there are white, yellow, and black lines |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;That the root goes down more than 2m has real practical meaning. Even when the topsoil is completely dry, the plant draws on water from deep below. In a drought year you actually see amaranth standing green next to maize whose leaves are curling up. That said, putting down this deep a root requires soil that is not compacted; if there is a hardpan layer underneath, the ability is cut in half.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The seed's structure is nothing like a cereal grain's. In rice and wheat, the starchy endosperm fills the center of the seed while the germ is a small attachment off to one side. So once the hull and bran are removed, only starch remains, and the protein, fat, and vitamins go with what was stripped away — which is why white rice has less nutrition than brown rice. In amaranth, the starch is bunched at the very center of the seed and the germ wraps around it like a ring. There is no bran to strip away, and no reason to strip anything.

| Comparison | Rice/Wheat | Amaranth |
|---|---|---|
| Milling | Needed | Not needed |
| Milling loss | 20-30% | None |
| Milling equipment | A rice mill or mortar is needed | Not needed |
| Nutrient distribution | Concentrated in the outer layer, lost when milled | Even throughout the seed |
| Starch granule size | 10-30μm | 1-3μm, very small |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The small starch granules in the last row mean two things. One is that it digests easily, and the other is that when dropped into a hot pan, the water inside the granule turns to steam almost instantly and it pops like popcorn. This trait is put to decisive use in cooking, and section 16 covers it.

### 2-4. Growth Stages

| Stage | Timing | Key physiology | Key nutrient |
|---|---|---|---|
| Germination and seedling stage | Sowing to 3 weeks | The sprout emerges very small and grows slowly. This is when it is most easily overtaken by weeds | Phosphorus, a small amount of nitrogen |
| Vegetative growth stage | 3 to 8 weeks | Stems and leaves increase rapidly. Leaf harvest begins | Nitrogen |
| Flowering stage | 8 to 12 weeks | Seed heads form and pollen travels by wind | Phosphorus, potassium |
| Ripening stage | 12 to 16 weeks | The seed ripens. The ripest grains begin to shatter first | Potassium |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The riskiest box in this table is the first one. Because amaranth's seed is small, the sprout that first emerges is small too. The cotyledons are thread-thin, and even after 3 weeks the plant often has not grown past the length of a finger joint. Weeds, over that same span, grow far faster. How well the crop gets through these 3 weeks decides much of whether the planting succeeds, and section 8 deals with this problem.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The nature of the ripening stage is also worth knowing in advance. Amaranth's seed heads do not ripen evenly from top to bottom or bottom to top. Ripening spreads out even within a single seed head, and the grains that ripen first do not wait — they shatter and fall. This trait is called shattering, and it is the normal way a wild plant disperses its seed. Cultivated amaranth has not fully lost this trait either. All of the harvest techniques in section 12 follow from this one fact.

### 2-5. Nutrition per 100g

| Nutrient | Amount | Notes |
|---|:---:|---|
| Calories | 383kcal | Based on the Korean Food Composition Table's dried-seed figure. On par with cereal grains |
| Protein | 17.09g | More than double that of rice |
| Lysine | Abundant | Makes up for cereals' limiting amino acid |
| Carbohydrates | 60-65g | The starch granules are very small |
| Fat | 6-7g | High for a grain, mostly unsaturated fat |
| Dietary fiber | 6-7g | On the high side |
| Sugars | 1.65g | Almost no sweetness |
| Calcium | 150-160mg | Among the highest of any grain |
| Iron | 7-8mg | On the high side among grains |
| Magnesium | 240-250mg | Very high |
| Total phenolic compounds | 994-1,732mg/kg | Acts as an antioxidant |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The A grade for micronutrients on the evaluation table comes from this data. Eating 548g — the amount equal to 2,100kcal a day — covers more than 10% of the recommended daily allowance for 14 nutrients. Calcium, magnesium, and iron stand out in particular. In a diet where milk and dried anchovies have disappeared, sources of calcium shrink sharply, yet amaranth seed, though a grain, still carries a meaningful amount of calcium.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;One qualification applies here, though. Amaranth also contains oxalic acid and phytic acid together, so part of its calcium and iron is not absorbed and simply passes through. That means the body does not actually take in as much as the table's numbers suggest. There are ways to reduce this loss, covered in sections 14 and 16.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The leaves' nutrition has a different character from the seed's. 100g of leaf runs only about 20kcal, but in exchange it is loaded with beta-carotene, vitamin C, iron, and calcium — much the same role spinach plays. This is the real substance behind saying that one plant gives you both a grain and a leafy vegetable.

## 3. Growing Conditions

### 3-1. Temperature

| Category | Limit | Optimum | Notes |
|---|:---:|:---:|---|
| Germination temperature | 15°C or above | 20-25°C | Below 15°C it barely sprouts at all |
| Growing temperature | 15-35°C | 20-32°C | The B grade for temperature range on the evaluation table comes from this 12°C span |
| Photosynthesis optimum | 25°C or above | - | Being a C4 crop, efficiency rises the hotter it gets |
| Heat limit | 35°C or above | - | It holds up as long as there is water. Among the toughest of any leafy vegetable |
| Cold limit | 10°C | - | Growth stops |
| Lethal temperature | 0°C | - | A single frost kills it |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;What sets amaranth's sowing date is not air temperature but soil temperature. The soil must exceed 15°C before it sprouts, and 20°C before it comes up evenly. Sow too early out of impatience and the tiny seed simply rots in the soil. In central South Korea, this condition is met from late April to early May.

### 3-2. Light

| Category | Standard | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Sunlight needed | 6-8 hours a day | Below this, growth clearly worsens |
| Minimum sunlight | 6 hours a day | Under 6 hours it grows leggy and the seed heads turn out weak |
| At 40% shade | Relative yield 45% | A figure common to C4 crops, and the basis for the E grade on the evaluation table |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Being weak in shade decides where amaranth goes in the field. It is not a crop for the scrap of ground under a wall, in the shade of a tree, or squeezed between buildings. It needs a spot that gets sun all day, and since it itself grows up to 2m tall, it should be placed on the north side so it does not shade other crops.

### 3-3. Water and Soil

| Item | Standard | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Total evapotranspiration | About 250mm per season | Basis for the S grade for water use on the evaluation table |
| Early moisture | Keep from drying out from sowing until 2-3 true leaves | Once this stage is past, it tolerates drought well |
| Waterlogging | Damages the roots | More dangerous than drought |
| Soil texture | Well-drained sandy loam to loam | The first condition is that water must not stand |
| Soil depth | 30cm or more; the deeper the better | The taproot goes down more than 2m |
| pH | 5.0-7.0 | Tolerates 4.3 to 7.5. Relatively good acid tolerance |
| Raised-bed height | 20cm or more | For drainage |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;A crop that tolerates drought well but is weak to waterlogging is a common combination. A deep, thick taproot is good for finding water in dry soil, but it cannot breathe once submerged. This is why the standard calls for raising beds at least 20cm and letting furrows drain the water away. A spot where water stands for days at a time during the monsoon season (jangma, late June to July in South Korea) does not suit amaranth.

## 4. Varieties

### 4-1. Grain Types and Leaf Types

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Amaranth includes many species, and how each is used differs by species. Broadly, it splits into grain-type lines grown for the seed and vegetable-type lines grown for the leaf.

| Species | Characteristics | Main use | Use in a disaster situation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Amaranthus cruentus | The leading grain type. High-yielding with a wide range of adaptation | Grain and leaf | The safest, most versatile choice |
| Amaranthus hypochondriacus | A grain type with relatively large seeds | Grain and leaf | When seed is the main goal |
| Amaranthus caudatus | Its seed head droops long. An Andean line | Grain and ornamental | Relatively well suited to cool highlands |
| Amaranthus tricolor | A vegetable type. Leaves are tender with varied color | Leafy vegetable | When leaf is the main goal |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;No variety has been bred in South Korea; nearly all are introduced from abroad. This is the basis for the B grade for prevalence in South Korea on the evaluation table — meaning it is grown in only three or four provinces. Since obtaining new seed is difficult once distribution networks break down, securing it once and then carrying it forward through seed saving is the only path. Fortunately, as the S grade for seed saving shows, that task itself is easy.

### 4-2. Major Varieties

| Variety | Yield | Characteristics |
|---|:---:|---|
| RRC 1027 | 465kg per 1,000m² | The line that produced the highest yield in domestic South Korean trial plantings |
| Golden Giant | 400g or more per plant | Large seeds and large seed heads. Suited to grain production |
| Kerala Red | No data | At 0.42g per 1,000 seeds, especially light, with high antioxidant content |
| Chinese Multicolor | No data | Patterned leaves, used as a vegetable type |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;It helps to set two facts side by side: the D grade for calories per area on the evaluation table is calculated on a basis of 200kg per 1,000m², while domestic South Korean trials ranged all the way from 125kg to 465kg. That is a three- to four-fold spread depending on variety and growing conditions, and it is also the real substance behind the D grade for yield stability. For anyone in a position to choose seed, picking a line with a proven yield alone changes the outcome enormously.

### 4-3. What to Choose for Each Situation

| Situation | Suitable line | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Adding to grain self-sufficiency | Amaranthus cruentus, Amaranthus hypochondriacus | High-yielding with large seeds, which at least makes threshing and cleaning somewhat easier |
| Summer leafy greens are the goal | Amaranthus tricolor | Tender leaves that hold up well to repeated harvesting |
| Using seed and leaf together | Amaranthus cruentus | Leaf quality is also decent, suiting dual use |
| Cool mountain regions | Amaranthus caudatus | An Andean highland line, so relatively suited to cooler places |
| When large seeds matter | The Golden Giant line | Reduces the burden of cleaning |

> **Choosing a large-seeded line is not only about yield. The hardest part of growing amaranth is not raising it but threshing and cleaning it after harvest, and the larger the seed, the easier that job becomes.**
