## Introduction

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Amaranth (Amaranthus spp.) is an annual herb of the amaranth family (Amaranthaceae), genus Amaranthus. The weedy pigweed that South Korean farmers routinely pull from their fields and discard belongs to the same genus, and spinach, Swiss chard, and sugar beet belong to the same family. Amaranth is native to Central America and the Andes, where it stood alongside maize as a staple crop for the Aztecs and Incas. Cultivation nearly died out after the Spanish conquest, then drew fresh attention in the late 20th century, and today it is grouped with buckwheat and quinoa under the name pseudocereal — a crop that is not a true grain but is used like one.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Looked at through the lens of disaster preparedness, the first thing to grasp about this crop is that one plant yields two different things. The leaves are a vegetable, and the seed is a grain. Leaves can be picked and eaten starting 40 days after sowing, and past 100 days the seed heads pour out grains smaller than millet. The plant supplies fresh greens all summer, then hands you a sack of grain in autumn, and not many crops carry this double duty. When field space is limited, having one plot do the work of two is valuable in itself.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The seed itself is unusual. Amaranth grain has more than 17g of protein per 100g, and what matters even more is how that protein is put together. The protein in rice, wheat, and maize is critically short of the essential amino acid lysine. That is why eating grain alone can fill the stomach while leaving the body unable to put the protein to full use. Amaranth has plenty of that lysine. Stirring a handful of amaranth into corn porridge is not simply adding bulk — it makes the corn's own protein worth its weight. The evaluation table's bonus for an essential amino acid score of 90 or above points to exactly this fact.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;And yet the evaluation table gives amaranth an overall grade of D, a score of 36.5. Short-term is E. Understanding exactly what lies behind this low score is the key to understanding the crop. The problem is not the quality of the seed but the quantity. A 1,000m² plot yields only around 200kg of grain — 766kcal/m², just 0.29× the self-sufficiency line. That is 20-25% of what rice or maize produce. A plan to replace rice with amaranth simply does not work.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Two things cut into the yield. One is that the seed is extremely small: even 1,000 grains together weigh less than 1g. This tiny size makes sowing finicky, slows early growth, and above all makes threshing and cleaning after harvest a real chore. The other is shattering. Amaranth keeps a good deal of its wild nature, so once the seed ripens it drops from the seed head on its own. Wait for it to ripen and it falls; cut before it falls and unripe grain gets mixed in. Half of the harvest comes down to this balancing act.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In return, amaranth has a clear strength: heat and drought tolerance. It is one of the rare dicots that photosynthesize by the C4 pathway, so the hotter and brighter it gets, the more efficient it becomes. Total evapotranspiration of 250mm earned it an S on the evaluation table's water use, and because it also grows on poor soil, it scores S on fertilizer demand too. In the height of summer, when heat and drought wilt every other leafy vegetable, amaranth thrives instead. The same C4 trait, however, works in reverse in the shade: even 40% shading drops the yield to less than half.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In short, amaranth is not a crop that fills the storehouse — it is a crop that makes the grain already in the storehouse worth more. Set aside a few rows in a sunny, well-drained corner of the field, and summer gives you fresh greens while autumn gives you a sack of grain that fixes the shape of your protein. That one sack raises the value of ten sacks of corn.
