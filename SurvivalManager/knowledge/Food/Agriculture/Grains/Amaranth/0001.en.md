## Introduction
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Amaranth (Amaranthus spp.) is an annual plant of the genus Amaranthus in the amaranth family, the same genus as the weed pigweed. It comes from Central America and the Andes and, together with buckwheat and quinoa, is grouped as a pseudocereal, a crop that is not a grain but is used like one. A single plant gives both leaves (as a vegetable) and seeds (as a grain), so you can pick leaves from 40 days after sowing and harvest the seed in autumn. The seed protein is over 17 g per 100 g and is rich in lysine, which grains lack, so eaten mixed with maize, rice, or wheat it fills the gap in the protein of grains. However, the seeds are extremely small and shatter by themselves when ripe, so yields are low and harvesting is difficult. Amaranth is a crop not for filling the storehouse but for correcting the nutrition of the grain.

## 2. Plant Overview
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;It uses C4 photosynthesis, which is rare in a dicot. The hotter and sunnier it is, the higher the efficiency, so it tolerates heat and drought and uses little nitrogen, but in shade it only pays the cost and its yield drops sharply. The leaves are alternate and 5-15 cm long, and the stem stands upright and grows to 2 m. The straight taproot reaches a depth of 2.4 m by 70 days after sowing. The seed is 1-1.5 mm in diameter and 1,000 seeds weigh 0.6-0.9 g; it needs no milling, and its nutrients are spread evenly through the whole seed.

| Stage | Timing | Key points |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Germination and seedling stage | 3 weeks after sowing | The most dangerous period: seedlings are thread-thin and slow, so weeds easily beat them |
| Vegetative growth stage | Weeks 3-8 | Leaf harvest begins |
| Flowering stage | Weeks 8-12 | Spikes rise and pollen is carried by the wind |
| Grain-filling stage | Weeks 12-16 | The ripe seeds begin to fall |

| Nutrition per 100 g (dry seed) | Content |
|:---|:---|
| Calories | 383 kcal |
| Protein | 17.09 g (more than twice that of rice) |
| Carbohydrate / fat / dietary fiber | 60-65 g / 6-7 g / 6-7 g |
| Calcium / iron / magnesium | 150-160 mg / 7-8 mg / 240-250 mg |

## 3. Growing Environment and Varieties
| Condition | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Germination temperature | 15℃ or more (optimum 20-25℃). Sowing time is set by soil temperature, not air temperature |
| Growing temperature | 15-35℃ (optimum 20-32℃). Growth stops at 10℃ and a single frost kills it |
| Light | 6-8 hours or more a day. Plant in the sunniest spot and avoid shaded ground |
| Water | About 250 mm per season. Early moisture is important, and excess moisture is more dangerous than drought |
| Soil | Well-drained sandy loam to loam, depth of 30 cm or more, pH 5.0-7.0, ridges of 20 cm or more |

| Species | Characteristics and use |
|:---|:---|
| Amaranthus cruentus | The representative grain type. High yield, and the leaves can also be used, so the most dependable |
| Amaranthus hypochondriacus | A grain type with large seeds |
| Amaranthus caudatus | Long drooping spikes. Suited to cool highlands |
| Amaranthus tricolor | For leaf vegetable use |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Choose a large-seeded line (RRC 1027 gave 465 kg per 1,000㎡ in Korean trials, and Golden Giant is another). The hardest work with amaranth is not growing it but threshing and cleaning it after harvest, and the larger the seed, the easier this is. There are no varieties bred in Korea, and most are introduced from abroad.

## 4. Field Preparation and Sowing
| Item | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Preparing the soil | Till to 20-30 cm, dig drainage channels, break the soil fine and level it, and firm it down before and after sowing |
| Nitrogen N / phosphorus P / potassium K | 8 kg / 12 kg / 12 kg per 1,000㎡. All the phosphorus and potassium as basal fertilizer |
| Compost | 1,000 kg of fully matured compost |
| Substitutes | Wood ash (a handful per ㎡), urine diluted 10-20 times (only until the spikes rise), bone meal, fermented grass liquid (diluted 5-10 times after 2 weeks of fermentation, early stage only) |

> **Deciding to give amaranth plenty of nitrogen is almost always a mistake. The plants grow tall and leafy, but the spikes become light and one storm lays the whole field flat.**

| Sowing | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Timing | Direct sowing from late April to early May. Soil of 15℃ or more and after frost has completely ended |
| Depth | 0.5-1 cm. Deeper and the seedlings cannot emerge. After sowing, always press the soil with a board or your feet to bring it in contact with the seed |
| Spacing | 20-30 cm between rows, 40-50 cm between plants. Row sowing or spot sowing |
| Seed quantity | 200-400 g per 1,000㎡. No soaking needed |
| Seed treatment | 50-55℃ water for 10-15 minutes, or coating with fine ash. Discard seed that floats in water |
| Thinning | At 2-3 true leaves, 20 cm between plants; at 4-5 true leaves, 40-50 cm. The thinned young plants are eaten as greens |

## 5. Water, Cultivation Management, and Pests and Diseases
| Stage | Water management |
|:---|:---|
| Sowing to seedling stage | Moisten once or twice a day so the surface soil does not dry. A thin straw mulch (thin enough for the soil to show through) |
| Vegetative growth stage | Leave it to natural rainfall |
| Flowering and grain-filling stage | Supplement only in severe drought |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Most failures come in the 3 weeks after sowing. Till the field once before sowing to sprout the weed seeds, then scrape them off shallowly; sow in rows and pull everything outside the row. Once those 3 weeks are past, the amaranth makes its own shade and the weeds cannot win.

| Management | Content |
|:---|:---|
| Preventing lodging | Hill at 30-50 cm, stake at 50 cm, and string between posts at 1 m. Reduce nitrogen |
| Pinching | Cutting the tip at 60-80 cm increases side shoots and favors leaf harvest; without pinching one large spike forms, which favors seed harvest |
| Leaf and seed dual use | From 40 days after sowing until before flowering, pick the lower leaves, no more than one third of the plant at a time. When the spikes rise, stop picking leaves and stop supplying nitrogen |

| Pest or disease | Timing and damage | Response without pesticides |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Stink bugs | Mid-May to October. Seed yield down 69.8%, the largest loss | Hand-pick in the early morning and clear weeds around the field |
| Aphids | Late April to early June. Seed down 51.9% | A strong jet of water, soapy water (20 g of soap per 1 L of water), protect natural enemies |
| Moths | Mid-August to mid-October. Leaves down 72.5% | Hand-pick at dusk, remove egg masses, light traps |
| Damping-off, root rot, leaf blight, powdery mildew, ear rot | Seedling stage and rainy season, poor drainage | Do not sow thickly, drainage and airflow, remove diseased plants from the field |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Wood ash, garlic infusion, wood vinegar (diluted 500-1,000 times), and Bordeaux mixture can also be used. Do not plant amaranth family crops in succession; rest the land for 2 years.

| Deficiency or excess | Symptoms and remedy |
|:---|:---|
| Nitrogen shortage | Yellowing from the lower leaves. Diluted urine, fully matured compost |
| Potassium shortage | Lower leaf edges scorch brown. A little wood ash |
| Phosphorus shortage | Leaves purplish, slow growth. Bone meal, wood ash |
| Iron shortage | New leaves yellow with green veins, when pH is high. Reduce wood ash and add organic matter |
| Nitrogen excess | Most of amaranth's nutrient problems. Plants grow tall, lie down, have poor spikes, and attract aphids. Cut off nitrogen |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Leaves given a lot of nitrogen accumulate nitrate, so stop nitrogen 2 weeks before harvest, pick in the afternoon of a sunny day, and discard the blanching water. Do not feed much to infants.

## 6. Companion Planting and Rotation
| Compatibility | Crops |
|:---|:---|
| Good | Legumes (share nitrogen), maize and sorghum (amino acids complement each other when eaten together), sunflower |
| Poor | Amaranth family crops (spinach, chard, sugar beet) share pests and diseases; cool leafy vegetables such as lettuce and Chinese cabbage cannot grow in amaranth's shade |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Plant amaranth in year 1 and a legume (soybean, cowpea, mung bean) in year 2 to return the nitrogen the amaranth used. Look at compatibility not only in the field but also in the bowl.

## 7. Harvest, Cleaning, and Seed Saving
| Type | Method |
|:---|:---|
| Leaves | From 40 days after sowing until before flowering, pick the fully grown lower leaves at 1-2 week intervals. The younger the leaf, the softer and the lower in oxalic acid |
| When to cut for seed | The moment when stroking the spike with the palm makes a few seeds begin to fall. If none fall it is early; if they pour out it is late |
| Supporting signs | The stigmas dry to brown, the spike turns brown or reddish brown, the lower leaves yellow and fall, and the seed is hard enough that a fingernail does not mark it |
| Cutting | On a clear morning after the dew has dried, cut the stalk with the spike. Carry it over a sack or cloth so nothing spills |
| Drying | Hang upside down or spread on a mat for 5-10 days. Spread a cloth underneath to catch the seed that falls by itself |
| Threshing | Put in a sack and beat it, or beat it with a stick on a mat. Once is not enough, so turn it and repeat |

| Cleaning | Method and limits |
|:---|:---|
| Coarse sieve, fine sieve | Removes twigs and debris. The sieve holes must be 1 mm or less |
| Winnowing, fanning | Blows away light husks. The seed is light too, so there is some loss |
| Water separation | Pour into water and skim off the floating chaff. The surest, but it must be dried at once (the seed sprouts within a few hours) |
| Inclined board | Pour onto a slanted board and only the round seeds roll down |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;It does not have to be cleaned perfectly. For seed saving, mark disease-free, large-spiked, unlodged plants in advance at flowering, and leave them until the spikes dry completely to brown. Covering the spike with a cloth bag catches falling seed and keeps birds off. Dry in the shade to 10% moisture or less and seal.

## 8. Storage
| Item | Method |
|:---|:---|
| Seed | Dry to 10% moisture or less (it should crack with a snap when bitten), cool in the shade, then seal in an earthenware jar, glass jar, or metal tin. Put in roasted and cooled salt or charcoal wrapped in cloth, and keep in a cool dark place. Split between several containers |
| Storage period | 3-5 years if completely dry and sealed, 1-2 years left at room temperature, under 1 year if moisture can get in |
| Leaves | As they are, 3-5 days. Blanched and dried, 6 months to 1 year; salted at 3-5% of the weight, 2-3 months; pickled, several months; blanched and frozen, several months |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;With storability SS and seed longevity together, it is a rare crop whose food and seed can be kept in the same sack. The most common failure in storage is grain pests.

## 9. Cooking and Cautions
| Cooking | Content |
|:---|:---|
| Roast-popping | In a few dozen seconds it pops white like popcorn and can be eaten without further cooking. Uses the least fuel |
| Boiling | Use two to three times the seed volume of water and cook 20-30 minutes |
| Cooking mixed | Cook with rice, barley, or maize at grain to amaranth of 4:1 to 10:1. The most practical |
| Flour | Has no gluten, so it cannot replace wheat flour |
| Leaves | Blanch in boiling water for 30 seconds to 1 minute, rinse in cold water, squeeze out the water, and use in dressed dishes, soups, stir-fries, and porridge. Discard the blanching water |

| Compound | Problem and response |
|:---|:---|
| Oxalic acid in leaves | Blocks calcium absorption and causes stones. Blanch and discard the water, use young leaves, and do not eat much raw. Avoid if there is a history of stones |
| Nitrate in leaves | The measures in chapter 5. Higher in stems and leaf stalks |
| Saponins and phytic acid in seed | Wash with water, soak or ferment. Rinse sprouted seed several times and cook briefly |
| Lectins and trypsin inhibitors in seed | Destroyed by heat. Do not eat raw |
| Others | Beware of pollen allergy; high in dietary fiber, so eat mixed with other grains |

> **The basic rule for amaranth leaves is to blanch them and discard the water. Seed does not cause problems if cooked and not eaten raw.**

## 10. Annual Work Schedule
| Month | Work |
|:---|:---|
| January-February | Rest. Check the seed storage (moisture, insects) |
| March | Add compost and wood ash and till the field |
| April | Till to sprout weed seeds, then scrape them off shallowly. Sow from late April to early May (shallow, and always press down) |
| May | Keep the surface soil from drying. First thinning. The first 3 weeks are the crisis |
| June | Second thinning, hilling, staking, start leaf harvest |
| July | Stringing, weeding, watch for aphids and stink bugs |
| Late July-August | When spikes rise, stop leaf harvest and nitrogen. Stink bugs are the greatest threat |
| Late August-mid-September | Harvest the seed. Cut when a few seeds start to fall |
| September-October | Drying, threshing, cleaning, selecting seed stock, sealed storage after complete drying |
| November-December | Cut the stalks for compost or fuel. Check the storage |

> **Growing amaranth is easy, and harvesting it is hard. All the trouble of this crop comes from the single fact that the seed is small.**
