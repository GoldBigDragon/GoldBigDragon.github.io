## 13. Seed Saving and Seed Storage

### 13-1. Seed Saving

| Step | Method | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Choosing plants | Choose plants that are disease-free, have large seed heads, and have not lodged | Seed takes after its parent |
| Marking | Mark them in advance during flowering | By harvest time it is hard to tell which plants were the good ones |
| Letting it ripen fully | Leave it until the seed head has dried fully brown | Underripe seed has poor germination ability |
| Bagging | Cover the seed head with a cloth bag and tie it | Catches falling seed and keeps birds from taking it |
| Threshing and cleaning | Use the same method as section 12, but more thoroughly | For seed stock, mixed-in hollow grains lower the germination rate |
| Drying | Dry in the shade down to 10% moisture or less | A fingernail pressed into it should leave no mark |
| Storage | Put it in an airtight container and keep it somewhere cool and dark | Moisture, light, and heat all reduce germination ability |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The S grade for seed saving on the evaluation table rests on two things. One is that, since self-pollination predominates, varieties do not cross easily. The other is that the seed is so abundant that securing a large quantity is easy. A single plant produces anywhere from thousands to tens of thousands of grains, so setting aside just a few plants is enough to fill next year's whole field.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There is one caution, though. If weedy pigweed is growing around the field, wind-borne pollen can mix in. Being the same genus, cross-pollination occurs, and seed obtained this way starts showing the traits of weedy pigweed: the grain gets smaller and darker, and shattering gets worse. Any weedy pigweed around plants kept for seed must be pulled before it flowers.

### 13-2. Storage

| Condition | How long germination ability lasts |
|---|:---:|
| Fully dried, sealed airtight, cool and dark | 3-5 years |
| Just left at room temperature | 1-2 years |
| Where moisture can get in | Under 1 year |

| Storage method | Content |
|---|---|
| Container | Something airtight, such as a glass jar, earthenware jar, or metal tin |
| Desiccant | Wrap roasted and cooled salt, charcoal, or roasted rice in cloth and add it in |
| Location | Somewhere cool with little temperature swing, such as a cellar or a north-facing room |
| Split it up | Divide it among several containers. Losing one container does not mean losing everything |
| Labeling | Write down the variety and the year it was collected |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;With the SS grade for storability layered on top of the A grade for seed longevity, amaranth is one of the rare crops where food and seed stock can sit in the same sack. Germination does not drop much even after 3 years, so even after a year of bad harvest, you can start over with old seed. This trait carries real value for any self-sufficiency plan that looks several years ahead.

## 14. Toxicity and Compounds of Concern

### 14-1. Oxalic Acid in the Leaves

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Amaranth leaves contain oxalic acid. Spinach and Swiss chard, in the same family, share this same issue, because plants in this family manage surplus calcium in their bodies by binding it to oxalic acid and locking it away as crystals.

| What is the problem | Content |
|---|---|
| Blocks calcium absorption | Once oxalic acid binds calcium into crystals, the body cannot absorb it and simply passes it through. This is why amaranth, despite being high in calcium, does not let you use all of it |
| Kidney stones | Calcium oxalate is the main component of kidney stones. Anyone who has had kidney stones is better off not eating much of it |
| Astringent taste | High oxalic acid leaves the mouth feeling rough |

| How to reduce it | Principle |
|---|---|
| Blanch in boiling water and discard the water | Oxalic acid dissolves readily in water, and a large share leaves with it. The surest method |
| Use young leaves | Oxalic acid increases as the leaf matures |
| Eat it together with something containing calcium | If oxalic acid binds calcium in the bowl first, it interferes less with absorption in the gut |
| Do not eat much of it raw | Unblanched leaves still hold all their oxalic acid |

> **The standard way to eat amaranth leaves is to blanch them and discard the water. This is different from other namul greens where the blanching water is kept and used as broth.**

### 14-2. Nitrates in the Leaves

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The nitrate issue covered in section 10 comes up again here under toxicity. Amaranth family plants are naturally prone to accumulating nitrates, and the more nitrogen fertilizer a field has received, the more builds up in its leaves. Once converted to nitrite, it interferes with the blood's ability to carry oxygen, and is especially dangerous for infants. Three things reduce it: cutting back on nitrogen fertilizer, discarding the blanching water, and picking in the afternoon on a sunny day.

### 14-3. Saponins and Other Compounds in the Seed

| Compound | Where it is found | What the problem is | What to do |
|---|---|---|---|
| Saponins | Trace amounts near the seed coat | Tastes bitter, and eating a lot can upset the stomach. Far lower than in quinoa, so it is not a major practical problem | Wash it off in water. Saponin dissolves readily in water and foams like soap |
| Saponin increase during sprouting | Seed right after it sprouts | Sprouting raises the level of defensive compounds. The bitterness gets stronger when eaten as sprouts | When eating as sprouts, rinse several times and cook briefly |
| Phytic acid | Throughout the seed | Binds to iron, zinc, and calcium and interferes with their absorption | Reduced by soaking in water or by fermentation |
| Lectins, trypsin inhibitors | Trace amounts in the seed | Act only when raw; destroyed by heating | Always cook before eating |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In short, amaranth seed poses no problem as long as it is cooked rather than eaten raw. It has nothing like the toxicity level of a bean that needs eight hours of soaking and an hour of boiling. Washing it and boiling for 20 to 30 minutes, or toasting it in a dry pan until it pops, is enough.

### 14-4. Other Cautions

| Item | Details |
|---|---|
| Pollen | The seed heads release a lot of pollen, so anyone with allergies is better off staying away during flowering |
| Confusion with weedy pigweed | Weedy pigweed is also edible, but mature plants carry more oxalic acid and nitrate |
| Proportion of seed in the diet | Filling the diet with seed alone can bring on too much fiber and stomach discomfort. Mix it with other grains |
| Children and infants | Do not feed them much leaf, because of its nitrate. Seed is fine once cooked |

## 15. Storage Without Refrigeration

### 15-1. Storing Seed

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The evaluation table's SS grade for storability comes from this section. Well-dried seed lasts more than 3 years at room temperature. This is one of the biggest reasons this crop earns its keep as a disaster-preparedness variety.

| Step | Method | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Drying fully | Dry down to 10% moisture or less. It is ready when biting a seed makes a sharp crack and it breaks apart | Any leftover moisture lets mold and heat build up during storage |
| Cooling | After sun-drying, let it cool completely in the shade before putting it away | Packing it away still warm forms condensation inside the container |
| Sealing | Store it closed in a jar, glass bottle, or metal tin | Keeps out moisture and insects |
| Desiccant | Wrap roasted and cooled salt or charcoal in cloth and add it in | Absorbs any remaining moisture |
| Location | Keep it somewhere cool, dark, with little temperature swing | A cellar or a north-facing room suits it well |
| Split it up | Divide it among several containers | If insects get into one container, you do not lose everything |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The most common failure during storage is grain insects. Once something like a rice weevil gets in, it can empty a sack within a few months. Sealing is the first line of defense; smell comes second. Adding dried chili, garlic, Sichuan pepper, bay leaves, or ash into the container keeps insects out better. If insects have already gotten in, spread the grain out in the sun to drive them off, then dry it again before putting it back into storage.

### 15-2. Storing Leaves

| Method | How | Duration |
|---|---|:---:|
| Keeping as is | Unwashed, in a cool place | 3-5 days |
| Drying | Blanch, wring out, and dry thoroughly in the shade | 6 months to 1 year |
| Salting | Salt at 3-5% of the weight and weigh it down | 2-3 months |
| Jangajji (pickled vegetables in soy sauce or paste) | Blanch and bury it in soy sauce or doenjang (Korean soybean paste) | Several months |
| Freezing | Blanch, wring out, and freeze | Several months |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Blanching must always be part of drying the leaf. It serves two purposes. One is killing the enzymes that would otherwise change its color and flavor, and the other is drawing out the oxalic acid. Amaranth leaf dried raw keeps all its oxalic acid, and with the moisture gone, the concentration actually rises.

## 16. Cooking and Processing

### 16-1. Three Ways to Cook the Seed

| Method | Time | Fuel | Result |
|---|:---:|:---:|---|
| Boiling | 20-30 minutes | Uses a lot | The seed takes up water and turns into a smooth porridge. Use two to three times as much water as seed |
| Toasting and popping | Tens of seconds | Uses the least | Pops white like popcorn. Becomes nutty and gains in volume |
| Cooking mixed with other grains | Same as the cooking time for the grain | No extra fuel | Mixed and cooked together with rice or barley. The most practical option |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;When fuel is scarce, toasting and popping is the best option. Add the seed a spoonful at a time to a hot dry pot or griddle, and within seconds it pops and puffs up white. This is because amaranth's starch granules are extraordinarily small, at 1-3μm, so heat reaches all the way inside almost instantly. Adding too much at once drops the temperature, so instead of popping it just scorches — the trick is to do it a little at a time, in several batches.

> **Popped amaranth can be eaten just as it is, with no further cooking. A few seconds over the fire — that is this crop's single most practical advantage when fuel has run out.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Popped seed mixed with honey or grain syrup and pressed together sets into a bar. This is a method long eaten in Mexico, and it makes a portable emergency food. Ground into flour, it stirs easily into soup or porridge, and mixed with water alone it becomes a thin gruel.

### 16-2. Using It in Place of Wheat Flour

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Amaranth flour has no gluten. On its own, then, bread will not rise and noodles fall apart. Mixed into wheat flour up to about 30% it works without much trouble; beyond that, the dough crumbles. In flatbreads, pancakes, porridge, and dumpling dough that need no gluten, it can be used without any such limit.

### 16-3. Cooking the Leaf

| Method | Content |
|---|---|
| Blanch and dress | Blanch in boiling water for 30 seconds to 1 minute, rinse in cold water, wring out, and dress it. Discard the blanching water |
| Soup | Add to doenjang soup. It turns mushy if boiled long, so add it last |
| Stir-fry | Blanch, then stir-fry in oil. Stir-frying it without blanching first leaves the oxalic acid behind |
| Porridge | Chop it fine and add to grain porridge |
| Dried leaf | Soak in water and use in soup or as namul (a seasoned vegetable side dish) |

### 16-4. Why Mix It With Other Grains

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Mixing amaranth with other grains is not a way of stretching the quantity of food — it is a way of fixing the structure of the protein.

| Combination | What it makes up for |
|---|---|
| Maize + amaranth | Amaranth supplies the lysine and tryptophan maize lacks |
| Rice + amaranth | Fills rice's lysine gap and also raises total protein |
| Sorghum + amaranth | Makes up for sorghum's lysine shortfall |
| Wheat + amaranth | Fills the lysine gap. However, the dough's properties suffer, so the ratio must be controlled |
| Soybean + amaranth | Both already have plenty of lysine, so the complementary effect is small. Total amount does increase |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;A ratio of roughly 4:1 to 10:1, grain to amaranth, is enough. Adding more amaranth does not make the effect grow proportionally. Once the missing amino acid is supplied, anything beyond that is no different from just adding more of an ordinary grain.

## 17. Annual Work Calendar

### 17-1. Monthly Table

| Month | Task | What to watch for |
|---|---|---|
| Jan-Feb | Rest | Check on stored seed. Watch for moisture and insects |
| March | Start preparing the field | Work in compost and wood ash and till. If raising seedlings, sow early this month |
| Early-mid April | Till the field to sprout weeds, then scrape them out shallowly | This one step greatly eases the early weed burden |
| Late April-early May | Sow. Sow shallow and always press it down | Soil temperature above 15 degrees, after frost has fully ended |
| May | Keep the topsoil from drying out. 1st thinning | The first 3 weeks are the critical stretch. This is the fight against weeds |
| June | 2nd thinning, hilling, staking | 50cm tall is when to put up stakes |
| June | Begin harvesting leaves | From around day 40 after sowing |
| July | Stringing, weed cleanup, continue harvesting leaves | Watch for aphids and stink bugs |
| Late July-August | Seed heads form. Stop harvesting leaves | Stop giving nitrogen from this point |
| August | Flowering and ripening. Moths appear | Stink bugs are the biggest threat |
| Late August-mid-September | Seed harvest | Cut once a stripping pass makes a few grains start to fall |
| September-October | Drying, threshing, cleaning | The most labor-intensive period |
| October | Sorting seed stock, storage | Dry fully and seal airtight |
| November | Clear the field | Cut the stalks and add them to the compost pile or use as fuel |
| December | Rest | Check on storage condition |

### 17-2. The Axis Running Through the Year

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Amaranth's year has two critical stretches. One is 3 weeks in May, the other is ten days in September.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The 3 weeks in May are a crisis created by the sheer smallness of the seed. Keep to four things during this stretch — sow shallow, press it down, keep the topsoil from drying out, and suppress the weeds — and the field needs almost no attention after that. Fail these 3 weeks and that year's field turns into a weed patch.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The ten days in September are a crisis created by shattering. A few days too early and you harvest unripe grain; a few days too late and it all spills into the field. What guides this judgment is not the calendar but your palm. Running your hand once over the seed heads every single day is the single most valuable habit in growing this crop.

> **Growing amaranth is easy; harvesting it is hard. The one fact that the seed is small is where every bit of this crop's labor comes from.**
