## 7. Water Management

### 7-1. Standards by Growth Stage

| Stage | Management | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Sowing to germination | Keep the soil from drying out | The grain is small and dies immediately if it dries |
| Seedling stage | Do not water | Encourages the roots to grow down |
| Tillering & vegetative stage | Rely on natural rainfall | The period of greatest drought tolerance |
| Heading & ripening stage | Supplement water if it turns dry | The period that determines yield |
| Maturity stage | Stop watering completely | Too much water makes drying after harvest difficult |
| Throughout the monsoon season | Draining water is the whole of management | Foxtail millet is the grain most vulnerable to excess moisture |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Water management for foxtail millet centers on draining water away rather than adding it. For a crop whose water requirement is 8.7% of rice's, a situation calling for irrigation is extremely rare, and most failures instead come from too much water. Fixing the drainage channels before the monsoon starts, and finding the spots where water pools after rain and cutting a channel for it, is nearly the entirety of water management in a foxtail millet field.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The exception is the stretch from heading to ripening. If severe drought coincides with this period, the head emerges but the grain fails to set. Saying foxtail millet is drought-tolerant means it will not die, not that the harvest is guaranteed. If you have any water left, use it during this period.

### 7-2. Symptoms of Water Shortage and Excess

| Symptom | Cause | Assessment and Action |
|---|---|---|
| Leaves curl at midday and unfurl by evening | Temporary moisture shortage | A normal response. No action needed |
| Leaves are still curled in the morning | Real drought | If it is heading stage, water it |
| Drying starts from the lower leaves and moves up | Prolonged drought | The plant is shedding lower leaves to survive |
| Short heads with sparse grain | Moisture shortage at heading | Does not recover |
| Whole leaves turn pale yellow and growth stops | Excess moisture, oxygen-starved roots | Immediately dig the furrow deeper to drain the water |
| The base turns black and soft, and the plant collapses | Root rot from flooding | Hard to recover. Fix the drainage at its root cause |
| Pulled up, the roots are black and smell bad | Rot from flooding | Give up on that plant and check the drainage around it |

> **In a foxtail millet field, far more failures come from water not draining than from a shortage of water.**

## 8. Crop Management

### 8-1. Thinning

| Round | Timing | Task |
|---|---|---|
| 1st | 2-3 true leaves | Thin out the crowded spots |
| 2nd | 4-5 true leaves | Thin to leave 2 plants per hill |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;If thinning is put off, the plants compete with each other for light and nutrients and all end up thin, and a thin stem cannot bear the weight of its own head and lodges. Since this is a crop rated E for light response, even the shade between plants affects yield. When choosing which plants to keep, pick the ones with a thick base and broad leaves, not the tallest ones.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Because foxtail millet tillers, it fills in on its own even if there are somewhat too few plants. When thinning, thinning boldly rather than leaving extra plants out of reluctance results in a larger harvest in the end.

### 8-2. Weeding - Where Foxtail Millet Farming Is Won or Lost

| Timing | Task | Key Point |
|---|---|---|
| 2 weeks after emergence | 1st weeding | Pull weeds while they are finger-length |
| 4 weeks after emergence | 2nd weeding and hilling | Mound soil up around the base |
| After knee height | As needed | Foxtail millet starts to overtop the weeds |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The task that takes the most labor in growing foxtail millet, and that most decides the outcome, is the early weeding. The reason is that foxtail millet's early growth is slow. For a bit over three weeks after sprouting, it gets by on two or three thread-thin leaves, and in that time crabgrass and green foxtail grow far faster and smother it. Miss this window, and no amount of work afterward brings it back.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;What makes the problem harder is green foxtail. Being in the same genus as foxtail millet, it is nearly indistinguishable while young. There are a few tricks for telling them apart. Foxtail millet has broader, paler leaves, with fewer fine hairs near the leaf sheath. Green foxtail has narrower, darker green leaves, with more hairs on the sheath. If you are not sure, the safest approach is to keep what is growing on the sown row and pull what comes up between the rows. This is the practical reason row sowing is recommended.

### 8-3. Hilling and Lodging Management

| Task | Timing | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Hilling | Together with the 2nd weeding | New roots grow from the base, increasing support and reducing lodging |
| 2nd hilling | Just before heading | Prepares the plant to bear the weight of the head |
| After lodging | Gather three or four plants together and tie them upright | A head touching the ground rots |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Foxtail millet is prone to lodging because its roots are shallow. Hilling is the task that directly compensates for that weakness. Mounding soil around the base lets new roots grow from the nodes there and anchor the plant. It needs no fertilizer or tools and can be done together with weeding, making it one of the highest-value tasks for the effort in a disaster.

### 8-4. Scaring Off Birds and Other Care

| Item | Details |
|---|---|
| Bird damage | The drooping head makes it hard for birds to perch, but flocks of sparrows still swarm in. String up nets or thread |
| Side shoots | Tillering is normal physiology, so they are not removed |
| Mulching | Laying straw or dry grass on the ridges reduces weeds and evaporation |
| Residue handling | After harvest, till the stubble back in as organic matter |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Straw mulching is especially effective in a foxtail millet field. It reduces the weeding burden, keeps the soil over the shallow roots from drying out, and also cuts down on rain splashing soil onto the leaves and spreading disease. If it is relay-sown foxtail millet planted where barley was just cut, the barley straw is already right there, so there is no need to source it separately.

## 9. Pests and Diseases

### 9-1. Major Diseases

| Disease | Conditions | Symptoms | Response Without Chemicals |
|---|---|---|---|
| Millet blast | Late May-late August, humid conditions | Spindle-shaped brown lesions appear on leaves and spread | Resistant varieties, avoiding excess nitrogen, removing diseased plants early |
| Downy mildew | Monsoon season | Leaves develop yellow streaks with white mold on the underside | Improving airflow and drainage, removing and burning infected plants |
| Stripe leaf blight | During the growing season, spread by an insect vector | Yellow stripes appear on leaves and they wither | Resistant varieties, managing the vector insect, clearing nearby weeds |
| Smut | Seed-borne | The seed head turns into black powder | Salt water test, wood ash dusting, burning diseased heads |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Foxtail millet is a crop with few diseases. Its S rating for pest-control self-reliance means diseases that pose a real problem even without chemicals are rare. The known diseases number no more than six kinds in total, and even those are narrowly concentrated in the period from late May to late August. In a disaster, being unable to obtain pesticides is barely a constraint for foxtail millet.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The one worth watching among them is millet blast. It is a fungus in the same lineage as rice blast, and it spreads under humid, nitrogen-rich conditions. Prevention comes down to two things: not giving more nitrogen than the standard amount, and ensuring good drainage and airflow. If symptoms appear, pull that plant and burn it outside the field.

### 9-2. Major Pests

| Pest | Timing | Damage | Response Without Chemicals |
|---|---|---|---|
| Stem borer moths | July-August | Larvae bore into and feed inside the stem | Removing and burning affected stems, clearing stubble after harvest |
| Aphids | During the growing season | Suck sap from leaves and transmit viruses | Spraying soapy water, protecting natural predators, avoiding excess nitrogen |
| Armyworms | Sudden outbreaks | Strip leaves almost instantly | Hand-picking, and early harvest if severe |
| Stink bugs | Ripening stage | Suck the grain, turning it into empty husks | Hand-picking early in the morning, clearing weeds nearby |
| Sparrows and other birds | Ripening stage | Eat the grain | Nets, strung thread, noise-making devices |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The biggest loss in growing foxtail millet comes from neither disease nor insects, but from weeds and birds. Weeds are stopped by weeding in the first month, birds by physically blocking them during ripening. Since both are problems solved by human hands and time rather than by chemicals, foxtail millet becomes an especially advantageous crop when supplies have run out.

### 9-3. Natural Pest-Control Preparations

| Material | How to Make It | Target | Caution |
|---|---|---|---|
| Soapy water | 5-10g of pure soap in 1L of water | Aphids, mites | Spraying at midday scorches the leaves |
| Wood ash | Dust it on the leaves in the morning while dew is on them | Young larvae | Reapply after it washes off in rain |
| Wood vinegar | Diluted 1:500 to 1:1,000 | Preventing fungal disease, repelling pests | Too concentrated causes phytotoxicity |
| Garlic-and-chili infusion | Chop finely, soak for a day, and strain | Aphids, moth larvae | Irritating to human eyes too |
| Diluted milk | Diluted 1:5 to 1:10 with water | Powdery mildew types | Spray on a clear morning |

### 9-4. Five Principles of Pest Control Without Chemicals

| Principle | Details | Application to Foxtail Millet |
|---|---|---|
| Drainage | Keep water from pooling | Most of foxtail millet's diseases start with excess moisture |
| Healthy seed | Do not save seed from diseased heads | Smut is seed-borne |
| Proper fertilization | Do not exceed the standard nitrogen rate | Excess nitrogen brings on both blast and lodging |
| Airflow | Keep proper planting spacing and thinning | Dense planting encourages disease |
| Sanitation | Remove diseased debris from the field | Reduces the source of infection the following year |

## 10. Nutrient Deficiency and Excess

| Symptom | Cause | Natural Remedy |
|---|---|---|
| The whole plant turns pale yellow and stays short | Nitrogen deficiency | Well-rotted compost, or a very small amount of urine diluted 1:10 |
| Leaf edges scorch | Potassium deficiency | Scatter wood ash between the plants |
| Growth is slow and leaves are dark bluish-green | Phosphate deficiency | Mix bone meal into the compost |
| Only the tissue between the veins turns yellow | Magnesium deficiency | Dolomite powder, diversifying compost materials |
| New leaves turn pale white | Iron deficiency, usually from excess alkalinity | Stop applying wood ash, add leaf mold |
| Grows tall and dark green but lodges | Nitrogen excess | Stop nitrogen and rely on hilling to prop it up |
| Leaves turn reddish | Phosphate deficiency or low temperature | If it is low temperature, it recovers on its own |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The more common problem in foxtail millet is excess, not deficiency. Because it is a crop that lives on so little nutrition, fertilizing it by the usual sense of normal rates will always make it grow too tall. An overgrown foxtail millet plant lodges, and a lodged head touches the ground, where it rots or gets eaten by birds. Being greedy with fertilizer in a foxtail millet field comes back as a loss.

## 11. Intercropping and Crop Rotation

### 11-1. Compatibility

| Compatibility | Partner | Effect |
|:---:|---|---|
| Excellent | Soybeans, adzuki beans, cowpeas | Fix nitrogen and share it, and round out the amino acid profile |
| Good | Peanuts | Cover the ground, reducing weeds and evaporation |
| Good | Barley, wheat (as preceding crop) | Relay-sown as foxtail millet where they were cut in June |
| Good | Potato (as preceding crop) | Fills the field left empty after the June harvest |
| Fair | Sorghum, proso millet | Similar cultivation methods make them easy to manage together, but they share diseases |
| Poor | Maize | Being in the same grass family but taller, it shades foxtail millet |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The combination of foxtail millet and soybeans is especially valuable in disaster farming. Foxtail millet provides calories, calcium, and iron; soybeans provide lysine-rich protein; and together they fill each other's gaps exactly. In the field too, some of the nitrogen the soybeans fix flows to the foxtail millet, and because foxtail millet's shallow roots and the soybean's deep roots use different soil layers, competition between them is low. A layout of about two rows of foxtail millet to one row of soybeans works well, and choosing a short-statured soybean variety keeps it from shading the foxtail millet.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The link with the preceding crop is also a major strength of foxtail millet. Barley and wheat are harvested in early June, and potatoes are also dug up by June. That land then sits empty for a bit over three months until autumn, and foxtail millet is the grain that can complete its full cycle within that window. This creates a structure where the same land yields grain twice in one year, which is the most realistic way to draw more calories out of a small plot. Double-cropping partially offsets foxtail millet's weakness of a C grade for calories per area.

### 11-2. Rotation Sequence

| Year | Crop | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| Year 1 | Foxtail millet | Turns poor land into grain |
| Year 2 | Legumes (soybean, adzuki bean, cowpea) | Restores nitrogen |
| Year 3 | Root vegetables or leafy vegetables | Uses nutrients from a different soil layer |
| Year 4 | Back to foxtail millet | Disease pressure has dropped |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Foxtail millet has an SS rating for continuous-cropping tolerance. Planting it in the same field year after year causes no major problem, and in mountain slash-and-burn fields it has in fact been planted for two or three consecutive years. Still, rotation is recommended, less for yield than for pest and disease pressure: millet blast spores and the insect vector of stripe leaf blight both build up a little more each year.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Working double-cropping into a single year is also a form of rotation. If you plant barley, harvest it in June, plant foxtail millet in its place, and harvest that in September, the grass family continues, but the crop and the season are different. It is not as good as true rotation, but it is better than simple continuous cropping, and above all, it harvests twice from the same land.
