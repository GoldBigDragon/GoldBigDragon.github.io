## Introduction
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Foxtail millet (Setaria italica) comes from the Yellow River basin of China and, before wheat and rice arrived, was the grain East Asians lived on for thousands of years. It was the famine-relief crop planted in lean years, on poor fields, and in droughts. Its water requirement is 8.7% that of rice, it sets seed with 100-200 mm of total evapotranspiration, it needs only 12 g of nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium combined per ㎡, one third that of maize, and it can be harvested 65-80 days after planting. Its weaknesses are yield (250 kg per 1,000㎡, 932 kcal per ㎡, so about 280㎡ are needed for one person's yearly calories), waterlogging, and shade. Foxtail millet is not a crop that feeds you generously but something closer to insurance: it fills the gap when other grains fail and turns poor scraps of land into grain. The grain is small and hard to handle, and most of that difficulty appears after harvest.

## 2. Plant Overview
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;An annual grass of the genus Setaria in the grass family, native to the middle Yellow River in China. It is closely related to green foxtail, so young plants cannot be told apart in the field and they occasionally cross. The stem stands upright at 80-160 cm, and the roots spread shallow and dense, so it depletes little soil fertility but is prone to lodging. The flowers form a panicle whose spike droops, and self-pollination is the rule. The fruit is a caryopsis, and there are waxy and non-waxy types.

| Stage | Timing | Key points |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Germination and seedling stage | 3-4 weeks after sowing | Slow growth and the weakest period. If smothered by weeds or waterlogged, the year's crop is decided |
| Tillering and vegetative growth stage | Weeks 4-8 | Side stems branch off. Even if the plant count is a little short, it fills in by itself |
| Heading stage | Weeks 8-10 | The spike comes out of the leaf sheath |
| Grain-filling and maturity | Weeks 10-16 | Starch fills and the spike droops |

| Nutrition per 100 g | Content |
|:---|:---|
| Calories | 366-386 kcal (dry grain) |
| Protein | 11.4-11.5 g (P:E 12.3%, high among grains) |
| Carbohydrate / fat | 71.1 g / 3-4 g |
| Dietary fiber | 11.4 g (7 times that of white rice) |
| Calcium / iron / potassium | 100 mg / 7.2 mg / 300-350 mg |
| B vitamins | About 3 times that of white rice |
| Lacking | Lysine |

## 3. Growing Environment and Varieties
| Condition | Optimum | Limits and effects |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Growing temperature | 25-35℃ | 15-44℃. Growth stops below 15℃ and frost kills the plant |
| Germination | 30-31℃ | Minimum 4-6℃. If low, germination is late and the seed rots |
| Grain-filling stage | 20-32℃ | Excessive heat leaves the grain unfilled |
| Light | Direct sun all day | 6 hours or more. Yield is 45% under 40% shading |
| Water | 100-200 mm in total | 80-350 mm. The least of any grain |
| Soil | Sandy to loam, well-drained soil | Even gravelly ground and slash-and-burn plots. Roots are damaged in clay. pH 5.5-7.5 |
| Water table | 1 m or deeper | If high, the roots cannot breathe |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;South-facing hills and slopes (water drains by itself), poor gravelly ground, and mountain slash-and-burn plots are good. On flat fields, raise the ridges high, and avoid converted paddies, low ground, shaded spots, and north-facing sites.

| Variety | Maturity | Characteristics |
|:---|:---:|:---|
| Waxy foxtail millet (chajo) | - | Sticky, so used for mixed rice, rice cakes, malt syrup, and liquor |
| Non-waxy foxtail millet (mejo) | - | Used for rice, porridge, and flour. Easy to grind into flour and stores well |
| Samdacham | Mid | 224 kg per 1,000㎡. A short, high-yielding type, 12% higher than Hwanggeumjo |
| Hwanggeumjo | Early | Resistant to lodging. Suited to regions with early frost and to sowing in late June |
| Johwangme (Miryang 5) | Mid | 27% higher yield than Hwanggeumjo. Resistant to blast |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;What grows more important the longer the disaster lasts is not the bred varieties but the landraces that have long been planted in the region. Foxtail millet is self-pollinating, so seed saving is easy.

## 4. Field Preparation and Sowing
| Item | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Preparing the soil | Till to 20-30 cm, ridges 20-30 cm high, and drainage channels around the field and in each furrow. Because the grain is small, break up clods fine and press the soil down after sowing |
| Fertilization | Per 1,000㎡: 5-8 kg each of nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium, and fully matured compost 1,000 kg (all as basal fertilizer). More fertilizer does not increase the harvest and makes the plants fall |
| Substitutes | Wood ash 15-30 kg, rotted compost, urine diluted 10 times (a little), legume green manure (sow 2-3 weeks after plowing under), slash-and-burn ash |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Yield retention without inputs is 45%.

| Type | Sowing | Harvest |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Spring millet | Early to mid-May | Early September. The most common and highest yielding |
| Stubble millet | Mid to late June | Late September to October. Where barley or wheat was cut |
| Late sowing | By the end of June | Early October. The limit, and only early varieties work |

| Sowing | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Seed selection | Salt-water selection: put the seed in 43 g of salt per 1 L of water (specific gravity about 1.04), stir, skim off what floats, and collect only what sinks. Rinse in clean water two or three times and dry completely in the shade. If salt is scarce, use plain water |
| Seed disinfection | Dusting with wood ash (suits it best), 50-55℃ water for 10 minutes (above 60℃ the embryo dies), or 2-3 hours in the sun 2-3 days before sowing |
| Planting | 1-2 kg per 1,000㎡, 60 cm between rows, 5-10 cm spot spacing, 2-3 cm deep. After covering, press with feet or a board |

## 5. Water, Cultivation Management, and Pests and Diseases
| Stage | Water management |
|:---|:---|
| Sowing to germination | Keep the soil from drying |
| Seedling stage | Give no water |
| Tillering and vegetative growth stage | Leave it to natural rainfall |
| Heading and grain-filling stage | Supplement if dry. The period that decides yield |
| Maturity | Cut off completely |
| Rainy season | Draining the water is the whole of management |

> **In a millet field, failure from water that will not drain is far more common than failure from too little water. Foxtail millet is the grain that is strongest against drought and weakest against excess moisture.**

| Management | Content |
|:---|:---|
| Thinning | Thin loosely at 2-3 true leaves, then leave 2 plants per hill at 4-5 leaves. If delayed, plants become thin and fall |
| Weeding | The decisive work. First weeding 2 weeks after emergence (when weeds are finger length), second weeding with hilling at 4 weeks. It is hard to tell from green foxtail, so keep to row sowing |
| Hilling | Together with the second weeding, and once more just before heading. If lodged, tie three or four plants together and stand them up |
| Other | Do not remove tillers. As the spikes droop, hang nets or string to keep off birds (flocks of sparrows). A straw mulch reduces weeds and evaporation |

| Pest or disease | Response without pesticides |
|:---|:---|
| Blast (late May to late August, humid) | Resistant varieties, curb excess nitrogen, remove diseased plants early |
| Downy mildew, stripe leaf blight | Airflow and drainage, burn diseased plants, manage vector insects and weeds around |
| Smut (seed-borne) | Salt-water selection, wood ash dusting, burn diseased ears |
| Corn borers (July-August), armyworms | Remove and burn damaged stalks, hand-pick, early harvest if severe |
| Aphids, stink bugs (grain filling) | Soapy water, protect natural enemies, hand-pick in the early morning, clear weeds around |
| Birds | Nets, strung lines, noisemakers |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The greatest losses in foxtail millet are neither disease nor insects but weeds and birds. The control principles are drainage, healthy seed, proper fertilization, airflow (plant spacing and thinning), and sanitation (clear diseased residue). Wood ash, garlic and chili infusions, and milk diluted 5-10 times are also used.

| Symptom | Cause and remedy |
|:---|:---|
| Pale yellow overall and short | Nitrogen shortage. Rotted compost, a very small amount of diluted urine |
| Slow growth and dark blue-green leaves | Phosphorus shortage. Bone meal |
| Yellow only between the veins | Magnesium shortage. Dolomite powder |
| New leaves turn white | Iron shortage (usually too much alkali). Stop wood ash |
| Tall, deep green, and falling | Nitrogen excess (more common than shortage). Cut off nitrogen and hill up |

## 6. Companion Planting and Rotation
| Compatibility | Crops |
|:---|:---|
| Very good | Soybean, adzuki bean, cowpea (fix nitrogen and complement the amino acid composition) |
| Good | Peanut, and stubble millet after barley, wheat, or potato |
| Fair | Sorghum, proso millet (similar cultivation but share pests and diseases) |
| Poor | Maize (grows tall and shades the millet) |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Plant foxtail millet in year 1 and a legume (soybean, adzuki bean, cowpea) in year 2 to restore nitrogen, then root or leafy vegetables in year 3, and return to foxtail millet in year 4. Continuous-cropping tolerance is SS.

## 7. Harvest, Threshing, and Seed Saving
| Step | Content |
|:---|:---|
| Timing | 45 days after heading for early varieties and 55 days for late ones. When the spike turns yellow-brown, bows deeply, and a fingernail leaves no mark. If shaking the spike drops a few grains, you have just passed the best time |
| Cutting | On a clear morning after the dew has dried, cut with a sickle 20-30 cm below the spike. Hold the spike pointing down and lay it on a cloth or mat. Separate diseased spikes |
| Drying | Preliminary drying for 2-3 days (18-20%) → tie five or six spikes together and hang upside down under the eaves for 1-2 weeks (15-16%) → thresh → final sun drying for 2-3 days (13-14%) → rest in the shade for a day to even out |
| Judging moisture | A bitten grain cracks with a snap and leaves powder, the salt test, a handful opened out slides apart, shaking in both hands sounds like running sand, and sealed in a jar for a day it shows no fog |
| Threshing | Beating inside a sack (best for foxtail millet), flailing on a mat, trampling, rubbing in a tub, rubbing on a grooved board, or stripping by hand (for seed) |
| Chaff | Pour in a gentle wind of 2-3 m/s, winnow, fan, or use a fine sieve. The grain is light, so adjusting the wind is tricky |

| Seed saving | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Selection | Mark in advance during grain filling. The main spike of disease-free, unlodged plants with large, even spikes. Exclude plants that showed smut or that are unusually early or late. Leave them a little longer than for ordinary harvest to ripen fully |
| Quantity | Twice the amount needed (1-2 kg per 1,000㎡, so the burden is small) |
| Storage | In the husk, moisture 12-13%, 10℃ or less, sealed container, light blocked. 2-3 years sealed at room temperature |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Renew the seed every 4-5 years. Always keep seed separate from grain for eating.

## 8. Storage
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Storability is SS. Store in the husk and mill only as much as you will eat. Milled foxtail millet loses more than half of its storage life.

| Item | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Conditions | Moisture 13-14%, temperature 15℃ or less (above 25℃ insect generations turn over quickly), surrounding humidity 65% or less, checked every two months (smell, warmth, condensation) |
| Containers | Glass jar or metal drum 3 years or more / earthenware jar 2-3 years / thick plastic 1-2 years / sack 6 months to 1 year (the small grains leak through gaps) |
| Insect control | Complete sealing (fill to the brim), wood ash at 2-5%, diatomaceous earth 1-3 g per 1 kg of grain, mugwort, sweet flag, and pagoda tree leaves, roasted salt, charcoal, fine sand or yellow clay |

## 9. Cooking and Cautions
| Milling (removing the husk) | Method |
|:---|:---|
| Pounding in a mortar | Put in dry grain, pound lightly several times, and take it out every 5 minutes to winnow. The key is not to pound hard |
| Moistening before pounding | Sprinkle a little water and leave 20-30 minutes before pounding. The husk comes off easily and fewer kernels break |
| Others | Millstone (raise the upper stone and turn slowly), rubbing in a stone basin, treadle pestle (far less effort) |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Do not throw the bran away; use it for livestock feed or fertilizer. For grinding flour, pounding after roasting is less work and gives a fragrant result, and flour spoils much faster than grain, so make only a month's worth.

| Cooking | Fuel and tips |
|:---|:---|
| Flour porridge | Stir flour into water and cook 5-10 minutes. Uses the least fuel |
| Millet porridge | Use plenty of water and cook 20 minutes. Easy to digest, suited to children and the sick |
| Millet rice, mixed rice | Soak 30 minutes or more and use a little more water than for rice. Mixing 20-30% into rice or barley has little resistance and complements nutrition |
| Residual-heat cooking | Boil 10 minutes, wrap in straw or a blanket, and leave 40-60 minutes. Cuts fuel by more than half |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Processing that also serves storage includes roasted flour (sealed, 6 months), malt syrup and taffy (1 year in a cool place), millet liquor, millet rice cake, popped millet, and fermented porridge.

| Caution | Content |
|:---|:---|
| Toxicity | None at ordinary intake |
| Moldy grain | Mycotoxins are not destroyed by heat. Discard it all, and do not give it to livestock |
| Dietary fiber | Very high at 11.4 g per 100 g. For children and the elderly, mix with other grains |
| Sole diet | Lysine is short, so eat it with legumes |
| Thyroid | There are reports of trace goitrogens in millets, so avoid eating large amounts over a long time when iodine intake is extremely low |
| Undercooked | The starch is not digested, so soak well and cook thoroughly |

## 10. Annual Work Schedule
| Month | Work |
|:---|:---|
| January-February | Check stored grain, check seed, turn compost, germination test |
| March | Collect wood ash, check drainage channels |
| April | Plowing, making ridges, basal fertilizer, breaking the soil fine |
| May | Salt-water selection, wood ash dusting, sow spring millet (early to mid-month). Always press down after sowing |
| June | First thinning and weeding, sow stubble millet (mid to late month) |
| July | Second thinning, hilling, maintaining drainage channels, heading |
| August | Grain filling, keeping birds off, watching for disease. Put up nets when the spikes droop |
| September | Harvest spring millet, preliminary drying, hang to dry. Cut within three days once the grains begin to fall |
| October | Harvest stubble millet, thresh, final drying, store (moisture 13-14%) |
| November-December | Select seed and store it separately, plow in the stubble, milling, check storage |

> **Five things not to forget: drainage, early weeding, restraint with fertilizer, harvesting a little early, and storing in the husk.**
