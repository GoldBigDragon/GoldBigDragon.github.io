## Introduction

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Foxtail millet (粟, Setaria italica) came from the Yellow River basin in China. Before wheat and rice arrived, it was the staple grain East Asians lived on for thousands of years. In South Korea too it goes back far enough that foxtail millet grains turn up at Neolithic sites, and in the years when rice was scarce, it was foxtail millet that fed people in the mountains and on slash-and-burn fields. When the harvest failed, people planted foxtail millet; when a field was poor, they planted foxtail millet; when the rains did not come, they planted foxtail millet. It is the grain the term "famine relief crop" fits most exactly.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The reasons show up in the numbers. Foxtail millet's water requirement is 8.7% of rice's, and about half of soybean's. In terms of total evapotranspiration, it sets seed on just 100 to 200mm. That is less than South Korea's rainfall for a single midsummer month. For fertilizer, 12g/m² of nitrogen, phosphate and potash combined is enough — a third of what maize needs. It can be harvested 65-80 days after sowing, among the fastest of all the grains. It is one of the few options that lets you sow even in early summer, after another crop has failed to drought or disease, and still salvage something within the same year.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The weaknesses are just as clear. The biggest is yield. 250kg from 1,000m², which works out to 932kcal per m². Covering one person's entire yearly calories with foxtail millet alone would take about 280m² — nearly 85 pyeong — almost three times the area maize would need. Foxtail millet is not a crop that feeds you well; it is a crop that keeps you from starving. Next is waterlogging. A crop this drought-tolerant collapses within days once water pools on the ground. In a climate like South Korea's, where summer rain comes in a rush, whether a foxtail millet crop succeeds or fails comes down to the drainage channels. Third is shade. Because it is a C4 crop, even 40% shading drops the yield below half.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Its overall grade is B, and within that, its long-term self-reliance score is A. That combination sums up where foxtail millet stands. It does not yield enough to be a staple's centerpiece, but it is the best crop for filling the gap when another grain fails, turning poor scraps of land into grain, and covering the two months left over between cutting barley and autumn. It is less the star of a grain self-sufficiency plan than an insurance policy for one. On that premise, this document focuses especially on threshing and hulling foxtail millet without tools, on drying it down to 13-14% moisture, and on keeping storage pests down without chemicals. Foxtail millet's grains are small and fussy to handle, and most of that fussiness shows up after harvest.
