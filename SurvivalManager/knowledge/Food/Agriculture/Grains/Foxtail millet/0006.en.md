## 15. Storage Without Refrigeration

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Foxtail millet's storability is SS. Across 915 storage-stage observations in the FAO Food Loss Index, the median loss rate for millets is 1.18%, among the lowest of all grains. Stored with the hull on, it lasts more than three years at room temperature, and even longer under good conditions. This storability is the second reason foxtail millet became established as a famine relief crop: getting through a bad year requires being able to stockpile the surplus from a good one for a long time.

### 15-1. Three Enemies of Storage

| Enemy | Conditions | Result | Main Countermeasure |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mold | 15%+ moisture | Spoilage, toxin production, total loss | Drying |
| Insects | Temperature 20°C+, moisture 12%+ | Eat out the kernel, leaving only powder and droppings | Sealing, low temperature, control |
| Rodents | An accessible container | Eat and contaminate it with droppings | Physical barriers |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;These three are connected. When insects are active, the heat and moisture from their respiration raises the local temperature and humidity, and mold starts there. When a rodent chews through a sack, that hole lets in moisture and insects. If one line of defense fails, the others follow it down.

### 15-2. Storage Conditions

| Condition | Target | Outside That Range |
|---|---|---|
| Moisture | 13-14%; 12-13% for seed | Above 15%, mold grows |
| Temperature | 15°C or below is ideal, as low as possible | Above 25°C, insect generations turn over quickly |
| Humidity | Surrounding air 65% or below | If higher, dry grain absorbs moisture back |
| Light | Fully blocked | Heat builds up and quality declines |
| Form | With the hull on | Milled foxtail millet has less than half the storage life |
| Inspection | Once every two months | Catching it early prevents loss |

> **Store grain with the hull on, and mill only as much as you will eat. The hull is packaging nature already gave you.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The principle of storing with the hull on applies especially strongly to foxtail millet. Once milled, the grain has its germ exposed, so it oxidizes fast, and being small, insects can bore into it easily too. With the hull on, it lasts three years, but milled it is hard to keep past six months. There is a temptation to mill it all at once, but the habit of milling only what you will eat in a month is what ultimately protects the grain.

### 15-3. Storage Containers

| Container | Sealing | Duration | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|:---:|---|---|---|
| Earthenware jar (onggi) | Good | 2-3 years | Temperature changes slowly, and rodents cannot chew through it | Heavy and breakable |
| Glass jar | Very good | 3+ years | You can see the condition by eye | Small capacity and does not block light |
| Metal tin | Very good | 3+ years | Completely blocks rodents and insects | If condensation forms, that spot spoils |
| Thick plastic | Fair | 1-2 years | Light and cheap | Rodents chew through it, and it tears |
| Straw sack, burlap sack | Poor | 6 months-1 year | Breathable, so it does not turn damp | Foxtail millet's small grain leaks out through the gaps |
| Underground storage pit | Good | 2-3+ years | Steady, low temperature | If water seeps in, you lose all of it |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Foxtail millet's small grain makes choosing a container trickier than for other grains. It leaks out through the loose weave of a burlap sack or the seams of a bag, and that same gap becomes a passage for insects and rodents. A seamless container like an earthenware jar or a metal tin suits foxtail millet best.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The earthenware jar is the balance point Korean ancestors found. Thick clay walls slow outside temperature changes, its microscopic pores allow just enough slow respiration that condensation does not form, and rodents cannot get through it. Covering the mouth with hanji (traditional mulberry paper) or cloth, setting the lid on top, and sealing the rim with clay makes it essentially airtight. Gathering up any jars you still have on hand is a genuinely worthwhile preparation.

### 15-4. Controlling Insects During Storage - Without Chemicals

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The real primary enemy of grain storage is insects. Pests like the Angoumois grain moth, Indian meal moth, rice weevil, and confused flour beetle are often already present as eggs in the grain right after harvest, and they hatch inside storage. One insect becomes dozens within two months, and within half a year a single jar can be filled with powder and droppings. Because foxtail millet's grain is small, a single insect ruins several grains, so the loss ratio is even higher. Here are the methods available without insecticide.

| Method | Principle | Application | Effect |
|---|---|---|---|
| Full sealing | Cuts off oxygen to suffocate insects | Fill glass jars or metal tins completely and seal | Most reliable. The less empty space, the better |
| Mixing in wood ash | Fine ash particles scratch the insect's exoskeleton and dehydrate it | Mix in evenly at 2-5% of the grain's volume | A long-proven method. Wash it off before eating |
| Diatomaceous earth | Its microscopic silica shells damage the exoskeleton | Mix in 1-3g per kg of grain | More effective than ash. Only works when dry |
| Fine sand or loess | The grains fill the gaps, blocking insect movement | Pack it in as alternating layers | Because foxtail millet's grain is small, sifting it back out later is a hassle |
| Dried chili peppers, Sichuan pepper | The pungent compounds act as a repellent | Put a few in the container | A supplementary measure |
| Mugwort, sweet flag, or Chinese scholar tree leaves | Essential oils suppress egg-laying | Dry them and layer them among the grain | Widely used traditionally |
| Roasted salt | Absorbs moisture, removing the conditions insects need to survive | Wrap in cloth and place at the bottom and top of the container | Doubles as a desiccant |
| Charcoal | Absorbs moisture and odor | Wrap dried, fired charcoal in cloth and place it inside | Also helps prevent mold |
| Cold treatment | Eggs and larvae die below freezing | Set it outside for a few days in the depth of winter, then bring it back in | Watch for condensation when bringing it back inside |
| Heat treatment | Eggs and larvae die at 60 degrees | Spread thinly in the sun for a few hours | Do not use this on seed grain |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The most important one is the first. Full sealing is the most powerful tool available even without chemicals. Because insects breathe too, when the grain and insects inside a sealed container consume oxygen, carbon dioxide builds up and oxygen drops, killing the insects. The more you minimize empty space by filling the container completely, the faster this effect works. A container filled all the way lasts far longer than one filled only halfway. Dividing the grain fully into several small jars is better than filling one large container only halfway.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Wood ash and diatomaceous earth work on the same principle. Neither kills chemically; both kill physically. Fine, sharp particles scrape away the waxy layer covering an insect's body, and the insect loses moisture and dries out. This approach has two advantages: insects cannot develop resistance to it, and it is harmless to people. Washing it off with water before eating is enough, and eating a little of it causes no problem. The drawback is that it does not work in the presence of moisture. If the grain turns damp, both ash and diatomaceous earth lose their effect, so drying always has to come first.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;When using ash, sift it and use only the fine part, like flour. Mix in about 2-5% of the grain's volume — roughly one doe (about 1.8 L) of ash per mal (about 18 L) of foxtail millet — and stir by hand so it coats the surface of every grain in a thin layer. Because foxtail millet's grain is small, it has a large surface area relative to its volume, so the ash sticks well and works effectively. It looks dusted grey on the outside, but rinsing it three or four times in water makes it clean again. This is a method still used today in Africa, India, and northern China.

### 15-5. Regular Inspection

| Timing | What to Check | Action If Abnormal |
|---|---|---|
| Every two months | Condensation on the outside of the container, moldy smell | Open it immediately and spread it out to dry |
| Every two months | Powder or web-like threads on the surface | This is Indian meal moth. Sun-dry the whole batch and sift it out |
| Every two months | Warmth when you grab a handful of grain | Heat is a sign of excess moisture. Take it out immediately |
| Spring and autumn | Open the whole container and check smell and color | If there is a strange smell, go through it in batches |
| Before the monsoon | Recheck the seal | If it has loosened, reseal it |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The most sensitive sense for inspection is smell. Mold reveals itself through smell long before it becomes visible. If, on opening the container, you notice a stale, damp, basement-like smell, it has already begun. At that stage, taking everything out and sun-drying it can still save most of it.

## 16. Cooking and Processing

### 16-1. Milling - Removing the Hull Without Tools

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Foxtail millet's outer hull is stuck tightly to the grain, so it must be milled. Left with the hull on, it is not digestible and catches in the mouth. What makes milling foxtail millet difficult is that the grain is small, making it tricky to control how much force you use.

| Method | Tools | Procedure | Efficiency |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pounding in a mortar | Mortar and pestle | Put in dry grain and pound lightly, many times. Take it out and winnow every 5 minutes | Most common |
| Moistening before pounding | Mortar | Sprinkle a little water before pounding and let it sit 20-30 minutes | The hull comes off well and fewer grains break |
| Hulling with a millstone | Millstone | Raise the upper stone slightly and turn it slowly | Controlling the gap is the key |
| Rubbing in a stone basin | A hollowed stone and a round stone | Rub by hand to remove the hull | Suited to small amounts |
| Foot-pedal mortar | Treadle pounder (didilbanga) | Step on the pedal to raise the pestle, then release it | Takes much less effort |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The key to milling foxtail millet is not to pound it hard. Being small-grained, a strong impact breaks the kernel before the hull comes off. Rather than lifting the pestle high and slamming it down, you should lift it low and pound with a rubbing motion. Pound for 5 minutes, take it out, winnow away the bran, then put it back in and repeat pounding four or five times. Clearing away the bran in between is what lets the force reach the remaining grain properly.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Lightly moistening the grain also helps, because only the hull swells, loosening its bond with the kernel. Since foxtail millet's grain is small, sprinkling too much water makes it clump, so it is best to wet your hand and flick water on, no more than that. After moistening, you must finish pounding and drying it all within the same day.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;A treadle pounder is well worth building. Its structure is simple: rest the middle of a long log on a support, fix a pestle head into one end, and step on the other end with your foot, then release it. Because your body weight supplies the force, it is several times more efficient than pounding with your arms, and, above all, the force stays consistent, so fewer grains break.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Do not throw away the bran. Being rich in dietary fiber and minerals, it makes good livestock feed, and where there is no livestock, it goes into the compost.

### 16-2. Milling into Flour

| Method | Tools | Characteristics |
|---|---|---|
| Millstone | Two matching stones, upper and lower | Produces the finest flour |
| Mortar | Mortar and pestle | Produces coarse flour. Sift it and pound again |
| Grinding on a flat stone | A flat stone and a rolling stone | Suited to small amounts |
| Roasting, then grinding | Any of the above | Roasting first makes grinding far easier and gives a nutty flavor |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Roasting before grinding is worth remembering. If you roast dry grain over low heat until it smells nutty, the starch structure breaks down and it grinds much more easily. What's more, roasted flour can be mixed straight into water and eaten, requiring no separate cooking fuel. This is the principle behind misugaru (roasted grain powder mixed into a drink). It is the most sensible form of food when you have to move.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Flour spoils much faster than whole grain. Its greater surface area speeds up oxidation, and insects can bore into it easily too. Grind only as much as you will eat in a month or two, and keep the rest as whole grain.

### 16-3. Fuel-Saving Cooking

| Method | Fuel | Procedure | Notes |
|---|:---:|---|---|
| Foxtail millet rice | Medium | Soak for 30+ minutes, then cook with plenty of water | Use a bit more water than for rice |
| Mixed rice | Medium | Mix in 20-30% with rice or barley | Not off-putting, and it rounds out the nutrition |
| Foxtail millet porridge | Low | Cook with plenty of water. 20 minutes is enough | Easy to digest, suited to children and the sick |
| Flour porridge | Very low | Mix the flour into water and boil. 5-10 minutes | Uses the least fuel of all |
| Residual-heat cooking | Very low | Boil for 10 minutes, then turn off the heat, wrap it in straw or a blanket, and let it sit 40-60 minutes | Cuts fuel use by more than half |
| Roasted flour | Almost none | Mix flour that has already been roasted into water | Needs no cooking fuel |
| Pan-frying | Low | Fry a thin batter of the flour | If too thick, the inside stays raw |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Foxtail millet is among the grains best suited to making porridge. Its small grain disperses quickly, it digests easily, and using plenty of water lets you stretch the quantity. In a situation where grain is scarce enough that even a single kernel must be shared among many people, porridge is the most sensible form, and foxtail millet is the grain best suited to it. This is why foxtail millet gruel (miyeom) has traditionally been food for children and the sick.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Heat-retention cooking is especially effective for grain. Soak foxtail millet, boil it for just 10 minutes, take it off the heat, and wrap the whole pot in a box packed with straw or rice husks, or in a thick blanket; the internal temperature stays above 80°C for more than an hour, and cooking continues on its own. A single wooden box packed with straw is equipment enough, and once you have made one, fuel use drops by half.

### 16-4. Processing That Doubles as Storage

| Processing | Procedure | Storage | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Roasted flour | Roast, then grind | Sealed, 6 months | Mix into water and eat right away |
| Grain syrup (jocheong), yeot | Ferment with malt (yeotgireum) and simmer down for a long time | Cool place, 1 year | A means of getting sweetness |
| Foxtail millet liquor | Mix steamed foxtail millet with nuruk (fermentation starter) and ferment | Store sealed | Doubles as calorie preservation and water disinfection |
| Foxtail millet tteok | Steam waxy foxtail millet flour and pound it | A few days | Lasts longer if dried |
| Puffed grain | Put it in a heated pot to pop it | Sealed, 1-2 months | Ready to eat right away |
| Fermented porridge | Mix flour with water and let it sit 2-3 days | A few days | Improves nutrient absorption and turns the taste sour |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Grain syrup turns out to be more valuable than expected in a disaster. When sugar disappears, it is not just sweetness that is missed — a way to get concentrated calories disappears too. Fermenting foxtail millet with malt and simmering it down for a long time greatly reduces its volume and turns it into grain syrup with concentrated calories; this keeps for a long time at room temperature and can be eaten as is or added to other food. The drawback is that simmering it down takes a lot of fuel, but sharing the hearth with other cooking reduces the waste.

## 17. Annual Work Calendar

| Month | Task | Key Point |
|---|---|---|
| January | Check stored grain, check seed condition, maintain tools | Check for condensation and moldy smell |
| February | Plan the field, turn the compost, germination test | Always check the germination rate of old seed |
| March | Collect wood ash, inspect drainage channels | Sift the ash accumulated over winter and store it |
| April | Tilling, making raised beds, base fertilizer, finely leveling the soil | Raising the beds is the first step in growing foxtail millet |
| May | Salt water test, wood ash dusting, spring sowing (early-mid month) | Always firm the soil after sowing |
| June | 1st thinning and weeding, relay-sowing (mid-late month) | Early weeding determines the yield |
| July | 2nd thinning, hilling, fixing drainage channels, heading | Draining water during the monsoon is the whole of management |
| August | Ripening, scaring off birds, watching for disease | Put up nets once the heads start to droop |
| September | Harvesting spring-sown foxtail millet, preliminary drying, hang-drying | Once grain starts falling, cut it within three days |
| October | Harvesting relay-sown foxtail millet, threshing, final drying, storage | Seal it only after confirming 13-14% moisture |
| November | Seed selection and separate storage, clearing the field | Till the stubble back in as organic matter |
| December | Milling and grinding, storage inspection, planning for next year | Mill only what you will eat in a month |

### 17-1. Five Things to Remember

| Item | Details |
|---|---|
| Drainage | Foxtail millet is the most drought-tolerant and the most vulnerable to excess moisture. Raised beds and furrows are your insurance |
| Early weeding | Managing weeds in the month after emergence determines the entire harvest |
| Restraint with fertilizer | Its nutrient uptake is strong, so a little is enough. Too much makes it lodge |
| Harvest timing | Once the grain starts falling, it is already late. Cut a little early |
| With the hull on | Store with the hull on and mill only what you will eat. Milling cuts its storage life to less than half |
