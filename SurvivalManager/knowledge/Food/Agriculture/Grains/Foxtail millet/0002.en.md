## 1. Disaster-Preparedness Evaluation

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Foxtail millet's overall grade is B, a score of 47.2 points. Broken down by scenario, its short-term ability to get through the early stage of a disaster is B (45.9), its long-term self-reliance across multiple years is A (48.5), and its supplementary value as a side-dish resource beyond the staple is SS (60.5). Long-term scores higher than short-term, and the supplementary value is unusually high. This means foxtail millet is not a crop that feeds a person on its own, but one that fills out the table alongside other crops and comes back reliably year after year.

### 1-3. How to Read the Strengths

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Seven criteria are rated S or higher: speed to harvest S, fertilizer demand S, water use SS, infrastructure needs SS, pest-control self-reliance S, storability SS, continuous-cropping tolerance SS, seed saving S. The only one missing from this list is yield. Foxtail millet asks for the minimum in everything it needs put into it, while giving back little in return.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Water use SS puts it at effectively the very top among grains. Saying its water requirement is 8.7% of rice's means that with the water it takes to grow one rice plant, you could grow eleven foxtail millet plants. When the groundwater pump stops, the irrigation channels run dry, and you are getting by on a single rain barrel, that difference becomes decisive. Even in a year when drought cracks the rice paddies and withers the maize, a foxtail millet field still yields something.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Fertilizer demand S should be read alongside this. Foxtail millet's roots are thin and shallow but dense, so they thoroughly sweep up the nutrients in the topsoil. On top of that, its own requirement is low, so it still performs even on land short of fertilizer. The stubble and roots left after harvest return to the soil as organic matter, so it drains the soil's fertility little. If maize is a crop that drains a field, foxtail millet is one that places almost no burden on it. That is where continuous-cropping tolerance SS comes from.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Speed to harvest S carries a separate meaning in a disaster. Being harvestable in 65-80 days means there is room to recover even if the plan goes wrong. When a spring crop dies to a late frost, when a field floods in the monsoon season (jangma, late June to July in South Korea), when disease forces you to plow one field under — as long as you sow foxtail millet by the end of June, you can still harvest within that same year. This is the first reason foxtail millet is called a famine relief crop.

> **The value of foxtail millet lies not in the harvest when things go well, but in remaining an option even after everything else has failed.**

### 1-4. How to Read the Weaknesses

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The criteria rated E are three: light response, cold hardiness, and non-food value. Above that, D goes to perenniality, cooking & processing, and yield stability, and calories per area is C.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Calories per area C is, in practice, the heaviest constraint. 932kcal per m² is half of potato's and less than half of maize's. Being 0.36× the self-sufficiency line means the field has to be that much larger if you want to cover one person's 2,100kcal a day with foxtail millet alone. That is not a problem if you have plenty of land, but surviving on foxtail millet alone in a small suburban garden plot is hard. Foxtail millet shows its real worth where there is a large stretch of poor land.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Light response E is for the same reason as with sorghum. C4 photosynthesis is most efficient in strong light, but collapses sharply in shade. A foxtail millet field must be placed where the sun reaches it all day, and if only shaded ground is available, a C3 cereal should be considered in its place.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Cooking & processing D comes from how small the grain is. Foxtail millet's grains are only about 2mm across, small even among grains. Get the force wrong while milling and the grain breaks before the hull comes off, and during winnowing it easily blows away along with the chaff. A mortar and pestle is enough to do the job, but it takes technique, and that technique is covered in Section 16.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There is one more weakness not in the table but essential to note: waterlogging. Foxtail millet's roots rot within days on ground where water pools. It is foxtail millet's paradox that a crop this drought-tolerant is among the most vulnerable to excess moisture. Given South Korea's summer rainfall pattern, this is in practice the most common cause of failure. When choosing a field, favor a spot with a low water table that drains well, and raising the beds is the first step in growing foxtail millet.

## 2. What Kind of Plant Is This?

### 2-1. Taxonomy and Origin

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Foxtail millet is an annual herb in the grass family (Poaceae), genus Setaria (the bristlegrass genus). It belongs to the same genus as the green foxtail common along roadsides, and it is in fact the accepted view that foxtail millet is a crop domesticated from green foxtail. Because its drooping seed head resembles a fox's tail, it is called "foxtail millet" in English.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Its center of origin is considered the middle reaches of the Yellow River in China. Cultivation traces already appear at sites from the 6000s BCE. That region has low rainfall, severe spring drought, and thick deposits of loess. This is where foxtail millet's tolerance for drought and poor soil comes from, along with its habit of spreading its roots shallow and wide as the soil dries. Conversely, the one condition foxtail millet has never had to face there is flooding during the monsoon season, and so it has no way of coping with it.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Its close kinship with green foxtail becomes a real problem in the field. Green foxtail growing in a foxtail millet field is nearly indistinguishable from the crop while young, so it survives weeding, then robs the crop of nutrients as it grows, and occasionally cross-pollinates to produce weedy individuals. That is why telling the weed apart is especially important in managing a foxtail millet field.

### 2-2. Appearance

| Part | Characteristics |
|---|---|
| Leaf | Linear-lanceolate. 10-30cm long, 2-3cm wide, with fine serrations along the edges |
| Stem | Stands upright, 80-160cm tall. Solid inside, with distinct nodes |
| Root | Fibrous roots spread shallow and dense. Because the roots are thin, they drain little from the soil's fertility |
| Flower | A panicle whose spike droops downward. Self-pollination is the norm |
| Fruit | A caryopsis. Yellow, orange, and other colors; divided into glutinous and non-glutinous foxtail millet |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The shallow roots cut both ways. On the good side, the field does not need deep tillage, fertility drains less, and the roots quickly take up even the moisture from a shower that only wets the shallow topsoil. On the bad side, they cannot draw up moisture from deeper layers, so in a prolonged drought foxtail millet is more vulnerable than sorghum, and being shallow-rooted, it lodges easily in wind. That is why hilling is an important task in growing foxtail millet.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The drooping shape of the seed head also has a practical meaning. Because birds find it hard to perch on a drooping head, bird damage is less than with sorghum, and because rain does not pool on it, the head is less prone to mold. On the other hand, the heavier the head gets, the more the stem bends, and the greater the risk of lodging.

### 2-3. Growth Stages

| Stage | Timing | Key Physiology | Key Nutrients |
|---|---|---|---|
| Germination & seedling stage | 3-4 weeks from sowing | Germinates and puts down initial roots. Growth is slow | Phosphate, nitrogen |
| Tillering & vegetative stage | 4-8 weeks | Side shoots branch out and leaves increase | Nitrogen |
| Heading stage | 8-10 weeks | The seed head emerges from the leaf sheath | Phosphate, potassium |
| Ripening & maturity stage | 10-16 weeks | Starch fills the grains and the head droops | Potassium |

> **Foxtail millet is at its weakest in the three weeks right after germination. If it gets smothered by weeds or submerged in water during this time, that decides the year's harvest.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Foxtail millet's early growth is noticeably slow, because its seed is small and carries little stored nutrition. For about three weeks after sprouting it gets by on two or three thread-thin leaves, and if green foxtail or crabgrass grows faster in that window and smothers it, foxtail millet is simply crowded out. Protecting these three weeks is the single most important labor in growing foxtail millet; after that, it grows on its own.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Tillering — the tendency to branch into side shoots — is also worth knowing. Under good conditions, a single plant grown from one seed splits into three or four stems, each producing its own head. This is why the sowing rate can be as low as 1-2kg per 1,000m². When there are too few plants, foxtail millet tillers on its own to fill the gap, and when it is too crowded, it does not tiller.

| Nutrient | Amount | Notes |
|---|:---:|---|

| Carbohydrates | 71.1g | Mostly starch |
| Protein | 11.4-11.5g | At 12.3% of calories, among the highest of grains |

| B vitamins | Thiamine, riboflavin, niacin, B6 | About 3 times the level in white rice |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;What stands out in foxtail millet's nutrition is calcium and iron. 100mg of calcium per 100g is a figure grains rarely reach. In a disaster where milk and anchovies disappear and fish eaten bones-and-all run out, sources of calcium become severely scarce, and foxtail millet fills part of that gap. Its 7.2mg of iron works the same way, forming a line of defense against the anemia that creeps in gradually once meat is no longer available.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Dietary fiber at 11.4g is seven times that of white rice. This figure cuts both ways. It has the benefit of aiding bowel movement and keeping you full longer, but it also means a large share of it is indigestible right when calories are most urgently needed. Feeding children or the elderly nothing but foxtail millet rice leads to a situation where they feel full but still lose weight. When using foxtail millet as a staple, you must mix in some fat or other grains.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Like all grains, foxtail millet is limited by low lysine. Eating it with beans completes the amino acid profile. The old custom of mixing adzuki beans or soybeans into rice made from foxtail millet solves a nutritional problem, not a matter of taste. On its own, foxtail millet's protein is excellent at 12.3% of calories, but the quality of that protein only comes alive with beans.
