## 3. Growing Conditions

### 3-1. Limits and Optimal Values

| Condition | Optimal | Tolerable Range | Outside That Range |
|---|---|---|---|
| Growing temperature | 25-35°C | 15-44°C | Growth stops below 15°C, and frost kills the plant |
| Germination temperature | 30-31°C | Min 4-6°C, max 44-45°C | If too low, germination is delayed and seeds rot |
| Ripening temperature | 20-32°C | Range 12°C | If it gets too hot during ripening, the grain does not fill out |
| Light | Full direct sun all day | 6+ hours | At 40% shade, yield drops to 45% |
| Total water requirement | 100-200mm | 80-350mm | The lowest of all grains |
| Soil | Sandy to clay loam, well-drained soil | Even gravelly fields and slash-and-burn land | Roots rot in clayey soil |
| Soil pH | pH 5.5-7.5 | pH 5.0-8.0 | Wide tolerance range |
| Water table | 1m or deeper | Deeper is better | If too high, the roots cannot breathe |
| Frost | Must be absent | None | A single frost kills the above-ground parts |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The only item in this table that is truly strict is the water table. A condition that would be little more than a note for other grains is decisive for foxtail millet. Its tolerance for drought and its tolerance for excess moisture are extremely asymmetric: it survives a month of dryness but dies after three days underwater.

### 3-2. Choosing a Site in a Disaster

| Site | Suitability | Reason |
|---|:---:|---|
| South-facing hills, slopes | Best | Water drains on its own and sunlight lasts long |
| Poor land mixed with gravel | Very good | Foxtail millet bears grain where other crops fail |
| Mountain slash-and-burn land | Good | Traditionally where foxtail millet has been planted |
| Flat field | Fair | Beds must be raised high |
| Field converted from a paddy | Poor | Water table is high and water pools at the bottom |
| Valley floor, low ground | Unsuitable | Water collects and frost comes early |
| Tree shade, north-facing | Unsuitable | Shade damage is far greater than for other crops |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The rule for choosing a site to plant foxtail millet is simple. Avoid any spot where water still stands the day after a heavy rain, and choose a spot that gets sun all day. Keep to just these two things, and how poor the soil is matters far less. In fact, gravelly fields, slopes, and long-fallow land, where other crops struggle, are well-suited to foxtail millet. In a disaster, giving the good land to potatoes or beans and turning the remaining land over to foxtail millet is the arrangement that maximizes the total harvest.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Converting a rice paddy to a dry field needs special care. A paddy floor has a compacted layer meant to keep water from draining, and water pools on top of that layer. To plant foxtail millet there, you must till deep enough to break up that layer, raise the beds more than 30cm high, and cut deep drainage channels around the field. Even after doing all that, a long monsoon can still cause failure.

## 4. Varieties

### 4-1. Glutinous and Non-Glutinous Foxtail Millet

| Type | Characteristics | Uses | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Waxy foxtail millet (chajo) | Sticky, chewy | Mixed rice, tteok (rice cake), yeot (traditional grain syrup), alcohol | Blends well into rice, so it is not off-putting |
| Non-glutinous foxtail millet (mejo) | Not sticky, crumbly | Rice, porridge, flour | Better for grinding into flour, and stores better |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In South Korea, waxy foxtail millet has traditionally been preferred, because it does not sit apart when mixed into rice. Still, on the assumption of a disaster, there is reason to keep some non-glutinous foxtail millet on hand as well. It is better ground into flour for porridge or pan-fried cakes, and its relatively lower fat content gives it a slight edge for long-term storage.

### 4-2. Comparing the Main Varieties

| Variety | Maturity | Yield (kg/1,000m²) | Characteristics |
|---|:---:|:---:|---|
| Samdachal | Mid-season | 224 | Released 2011. Short-stemmed, high-yielding; 12% higher yield than Hwanggeumjo |
| Hwanggeumjo | Early-season | - | Released 2004 by the Gangwon Province Agricultural Research and Extension Services. An early-maturing, lodging-resistant non-glutinous variety |
| Johwangme (Miryang 5) | Mid-season | - | 27% higher yield than Hwanggeumjo. Resistant to millet blast |
| Gyeonggwan 2 | Mid-season | - | A new variety in the golden waxy foxtail millet line |

### 4-3. Selection Criteria by Situation

| Situation | Recommended | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Maximizing yield | Samdachal | At 224kg per 1,000m², among the highest in South Korea |
| Areas with early frost | Hwanggeumjo | Being early-maturing, it finishes ripening early |
| When the preceding crop finishes late | Hwanggeumjo | Even planted in late June, it is harvested before frost |
| Windy areas | Samdachal, Hwanggeumjo | Short-stemmed and lodging-resistant |
| Fields with frequent disease | Johwangme | Resistant to millet blast |
| Securing seed is the top priority | Landraces | A locally adapted line is already there |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The longer a disaster drags on, the more what matters shifts from the yield of a bred variety to the landrace that has long been grown in that particular area. A landrace yields less, but it is already adapted to the local climate and diseases, and its traits do not drift even after repeated seed-saving. On the assumption that commercial seed supply will be cut off, securing one landrace line is a practical form of preparedness.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Because foxtail millet is a self-pollinating crop, saving its own seed is very easy. Still, there is a recommendation to renew seed every 4-5 years to maintain a variety's traits. This refers less to cross-pollination with other varieties than to the gradual decline caused by variation accumulating within the field and immature seed getting mixed in. Strictly selecting the plants you save seed from can extend that cycle considerably.

## 5. Field Preparation and Fertilizer

### 5-1. Preparing the Ground

| Item | Details | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Tillage depth | 20-30cm | The roots are shallow, so very deep tillage is not necessary |
| Raised bed | Height 20-30cm | Preventing waterlogging is the first condition of growing foxtail millet |
| Drainage channel | Around the field and along every furrow | Must serve as a waterway during the monsoon season |
| Leveling | Break up soil clods finely | The grain is so small that if it falls between clods, it will not germinate |
| Organic matter | Well-rotted compost, 1,000kg/1,000m² | No need to add a lot |
| Firming | Press down after sowing | The smaller the seed, the more contact with the soil matters |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;What gets especially emphasized in preparing a field for foxtail millet is breaking the soil finely. Foxtail millet's grains are only about 2mm across, and the sowing depth is a mere 2-3cm. If clods remain, a seed can drop deep into a gap between them and never come up, or, the opposite, sit perched on top of a clod and dry out and die. Half a day spent smoothing the surface finely with a rake or a board after tilling makes a big difference to the germination rate.

### 5-2. Standard Fertilizer Rate (per 1,000m²)

| Fertilizer | Rate | Timing |
|---|:---:|---|
| Nitrogen (N) | 5-8kg | Part as base fertilizer, a small top-dressing early in growth |
| Phosphate (P) | 5-8kg | All as base fertilizer |
| Potassium (K) | 5-8kg | All as base fertilizer |
| Compost | 1,000kg | All as base fertilizer, well-rotted |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Foxtail millet is a crop with strong nutrient-uptake efficiency. Because it thoroughly draws up whatever nutrients remain in the soil, it does not need heavy fertilizing, and in fact too much nitrogen makes the stems grow tall and lodge. When a shallow-rooted crop grows tall for nothing else, the outcome is predictable. Even when fertilizer is plentiful, it is better not to exceed the standard rate for foxtail millet.

> **Giving foxtail millet more fertilizer does not increase the harvest — it makes the plant lodge. Directing surplus fertilizer to maize or leafy vegetables instead grows the total harvest more.**

### 5-3. When There Is No Chemical Fertilizer - Disaster Substitutes

| Substitute | Nutrients Supplied | How to Make and Use It | Caution |
|---|---|---|---|
| Wood ash | Potassium, phosphate, calcium | Scatter 15-30kg over 1,000m² and rake it in | Alkaline; overuse raises the soil pH |
| Well-rotted compost | All-around | Layer fallen leaves, grass, and manure and let it rot for 3-6 months | Under-rotted compost burns the roots |
| Diluted urine | Nitrogen, potassium | Dilute 1:10 with water and pour along the furrows | Use only a little for foxtail millet especially; too much causes lodging |
| Bone meal | Phosphate, calcium | Burn and crush it, mix into compost, and let it sit for two months | Breaks down slowly, so prepare it in advance |
| Legume green manure | Nitrogen | Plow under soybeans or Chinese milk vetch as the preceding crop | Sow 2-3 weeks after plowing it under |
| Slash-and-burn ash | All-around | Use the ash left where grass and shrubs were burned, as is | Burning the soil itself destroys its organic matter |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Foxtail millet has a zero-input yield retention of 45%. Even with no fertilizer at all, it still yields close to half of what it would under normal conditions. On top of that, its baseline requirement is already low at 12g per m², so in practice compost alone can cover a large share of it. In a situation where a lack of fertilizer makes farming impossible, foxtail millet is the grain you can keep planting until the very end.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The slash-and-burn method long used in the mountains is also worth recording. Burning land thick with grass and shrubs leaves ash behind that supplies potassium and phosphate, temporarily raises the soil's pH, and reduces weed seeds and pathogens. Planting foxtail millet there does well even without fertilizer. However, once two or three years pass, the nutrients are depleted and you must move to a new site, and it carries the risk of wildfire and soil erosion. It is a means that can be used temporarily in a disaster, but not a method to repeat.

## 6. Sowing

### 6-1. Timing

| Cropping Type | Sowing Time | Harvest | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Spring-sown | Early-mid May | Early September | Most common, and gives the highest yield |
| Relay-sown (after another crop) | Mid-late June | Late September-October | Planted right after cutting barley or wheat |
| Late sowing | By end of June | Early October | The cutoff line. Only early-maturing varieties are possible |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Between spring-sown and relay-sown, spring-sown is better. It is more drought-tolerant, and being early-maturing, it is not pressed by frost. In a disaster, however, foxtail millet's role leans closer to the relay-sown side. That is because its strength lies in being sown into a June field left empty after cutting barley, a field plowed under after a spring crop failed, or a field that flooded in the monsoon and then dried out, and still being harvested within that same year.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The sowing deadline is the end of June. Since foxtail millet's growing period is 65-80 days, sowing at the end of June lets you harvest by mid-September, leaving margin before the first frost. Once July arrives, the ripening period turns cool and the grain fails to fill out.

### 6-2. Seed Selection - Salt Water Test

| Step | Details | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Dissolve 43g of salt in 1L of water | Specific gravity about 1.04 |
| 2 | Add the seeds and stir well by hand | This releases air clinging to the surface |
| 3 | Skim off the ones that float and keep only the ones that sink | The ones that sink are the sound seeds |
| 4 | Rinse two or three times in clean water to remove the salt | Leftover salt harms germination |
| 5 | Spread in the shade and dry completely | Drying in direct sun damages the embryo |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Foxtail millet's grains are too small to tell chaff from a sound seed by eye, which is why the salt water test is especially useful. Without a scale for the salt, you can gauge it by floating a raw egg. When a raw egg placed in the salt water floats to about the size of a 500-won coin above the surface, the specific gravity is roughly 1.03-1.04.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;If salt is scarce, use the plain-water method instead: put the seeds in plain water, stir, and skim off only the ones that float. It is not as precise as the salt water test, but it does filter out completely empty seeds. Do not throw away used salt water — save it and use it again.

### 6-3. Seed Disinfection - Without Chemicals

| Method | Procedure | Effect | Caution |
|---|---|---|---|
| Wood ash dusting | Coat dried seed with fine ash | Makes the surface alkaline and dry, suppressing fungi and insects | Coarse ash makes sowing uneven |
| Hot water soak | Soak in 50-55°C water for 10 minutes, then cool in cold water | Kills pathogens on the surface | Above 60°C, the embryo dies |
| Wood vinegar soak | Soak in a 1:500 dilution for 6-12 hours | Disinfects the surface and promotes germination | Undiluted, it kills the seed |
| Sun disinfection | Spread in the sun for 2-3 hours, 2-3 days before sowing | Removes surface moisture and reduces contaminating microbes | Do not leave it too long in scorching sun |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;What suits foxtail millet best is wood ash dusting. Because the grain is small, the ash coats it evenly, and even after sowing, that ash briefly shields the seed from microbes in the soil. It also helps that ash is a material always on hand in a disaster. Sift it and use only the fine part; lightly misting the seed with water before dusting on the ash makes it stick well.

### 6-4. Planting

| Item | Standard | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Sowing rate | 1-2kg per 1,000m² | The grain is small and it tillers, so a small amount is enough |
| Row spacing | 60cm | Wide enough for weeding and hilling |
| Plant spacing | Spot-sown at 5-10cm | Sow thickly, then thin |
| Sowing depth | 2-3cm | Too shallow and it dries out; too deep and it cannot emerge |
| Plants per hill | 2 per hill | Adjusted by thinning |
| After covering with soil | Press down with your foot or a board | The smaller the seed, the greater the effect |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Foxtail millet's grains are too small to scatter evenly by hand. Mixing in three or four times as much dry sand or fine soil spreads it far more evenly, and you can also see by eye how far you have already sown. Mixing in ash lets you disinfect at the same time.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Firming is especially important for foxtail millet. The smaller the seed, the less stored nutrition it has, so it needs to reach the moisture in the soil quickly, but if there is a gap between the soil and the seed, water cannot reach it. After covering the seed, laying down a board and stepping on it, or rolling a log over it to press it down, draws moisture up from the layer below through capillary action, making germination even. When sowing in a dry spring with no rain, this one step alone can decide the outcome.
