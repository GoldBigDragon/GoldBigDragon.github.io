## 12. Harvest and Threshing

### 12-1. Judging the Timing

| Criterion | Details | Assessment |
|---|---|---|
| Early-maturing varieties | 45 days after heading | A rough guideline |
| Late-maturing varieties | 55 days after heading | Check the variety's description |
| Head color | The head turns yellow-brown and droops deeply | Maturity is near |
| Grain firmness | Pressing with a fingernail leaves no mark | Fully ripe |
| Grain cross-section | Split in half, the inside is white and firm | Ripening is complete |
| Shattering | Shaking the head drops a few grains | The right time has just passed. You must hurry |
| Weather | After 2-3 clear days in a row | Moisture is low, making drying easy |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Foxtail millet's harvest window is narrow. Too early, and the grain still holds too much moisture, so it shrivels as it dries and breaks apart during milling. Too late, and the grain drops from the head on its own and scatters on the ground. Because the grain is so small, once it falls to the ground it cannot be picked back up, so the loss is final. The trick is to walk the field every day, shake the heads by hand, and cut them within three days of the point when a few grains first start falling.

> **Grain cut a little early can still be dried indoors, but grain that falls because it was cut too late can never be recovered.**

### 12-2. Harvesting Technique

| Step | Details | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Start on a clear morning, after the dew has dried | A wet head is where mold gets its start |
| 2 | Cut with a sickle 20-30cm below the head | A stalk length makes it easy to bundle and hang |
| 3 | While cutting, hold the head pointing downward | Shaking makes the grain fall |
| 4 | Lay cut heads down right there on a cloth or straw mat (meongseok) | Lets you recover grain that scatters on the ground |
| 5 | Separate diseased heads from healthy ones | Mixing them spreads mold |
| 6 | Bundle five or six heads together and hang them upside down under the eaves | A position that allows airflow determines how well it dries |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;When cutting foxtail millet, it helps to spread cloth over the field and work on top of it. Because the grain is so small, a considerable amount falls with even a single stroke of the sickle, and spreading cloth lets you recover it. It seems like a small difference, but it amounts to 5% to 10% of the harvest. Even old clothing or a scrap of plastic sheeting will do.

### 12-3. Drying the Grain - Down to 13-14% Moisture

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Everything about storing grain comes down to moisture. Above 15% moisture, mold grows; below 13%, both mold and insects nearly stop being active. Freshly cut foxtail millet has 20-25% moisture. Bringing that down to 13-14% is the one and most important task right after harvest.

| Stage | Method | Duration | Moisture Reached |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Preliminary drying | Stand cut bundles of heads up in the field or yard | 2-3 days | 18-20% |
| 2. Hang-drying | Hang upside down under the eaves or in a shed | 1-2 weeks | 15-16% |
| 3. Threshing | Once fully dry, thresh it out | 1 day | - |
| 4. Final drying | Spread on a straw mat or cloth and sun-dry | 2-3 days | 13-14% |
| 5. Tempering | Let it sit a day in the shade to even out the moisture | 1 day | Evening out |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The principles for sun-drying are the same for every grain. First, do not spread it directly on the ground; moisture rising from the soil re-wets the layer underneath. Lay down one layer of a straw mat, cloth, or board, whichever you have. Second, do not pile it more than 3-5cm deep, and turn it three or four times a day. Third, once the sun starts to set, bring it in or cover it; exposure to night dew brings back the moisture removed during the day. Fourth, do not rush the drying. If only the outside dries while moisture stays trapped inside, mold appears later.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Foxtail millet's grain is small and easily blown away by wind. If the wind is strong while sun-drying, cover it with a loosely woven cloth or choose a spot sheltered from wind. Grain that blows away cannot be recovered.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There are ways to gauge moisture without a meter. Once you get used to them, they are fairly accurate.

| Method | Procedure | Assessment |
|---|---|---|
| Biting | Bite a grain with your molars | If it snaps cleanly and powders, it is 13-14%. If it just squashes, it is still damp |
| Salt test | Put a spoonful of dry salt and a handful of grain in a jar, shake, and let sit for 15 minutes | If the salt sticks to the jar wall, moisture is high |
| Feel by hand | Grab a handful and open your hand | If it slides apart freely, it is dry; if it clumps, it is damp |
| Sound | Hold it in both hands and shake | If it sounds like flowing sand, it is dry |
| Jar test | Fill a glass jar halfway, seal it, and leave it for a day | If condensation fogs the inside of the jar, it needs more drying |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The jar test is the most reliable final check before putting grain into storage. Inside a sealed jar, the grain and the air reach a moisture equilibrium, and if moisture exceeds 14%, the humidity inside the jar rises above 70%, causing condensation to form at dawn when the temperature drops. It shows up even more clearly in autumn, when nights turn cool.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Some years bring continuous rain and no sun to use. In that case, spread the grain thinly in a loft above a firebox or stove, or on the cooler part of a heated ondol floor. It dries over several days, but the temperature must not exceed 50°C. Above that, it becomes unusable as seed and the cooked taste also suffers. It should feel just warm to the touch; if it feels hot, it is already too high.

### 12-4. Threshing - Without Tools

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Foxtail millet is a grain that is relatively easy to thresh, because once it is dry enough, the grain comes off the head readily. The catch is that the grain is small and scatters in every direction, and a lot of chaff and debris mixes in. The trick is to enclose the work area.

| Method | Procedure | Scale | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Stripping by hand | Hold the head's stalk and strip it down with a gloved hand | Small amounts | Least loss of all. Used for seed |
| Beating inside a sack | Put the heads in a sturdy sack and slam the whole sack on the ground | Medium | Best suited to foxtail millet. The grain does not scatter |
| Beating with a flail | Spread the heads on a straw mat and beat them with a flail or stick | Large | Enclose the area with cloth on every side |
| Treading | Spread on a straw mat and tread on it with a rubbing motion | Large | Takes less effort |
| Rubbing in a barrel | Put the heads in a large barrel and stir and rub them with a stick | Medium | The grain does not scatter |
| Stripping on a washboard | Press the heads against a ridged board and rub | Small amounts | Can be made by carving grooves into a wooden board |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Beating inside a sack suits foxtail millet especially well. Being small-grained, it is the grain that scatters the most when beaten, and the sack catches all of it. Fill a sturdy burlap or thick plastic sack about a third full of dry heads, tie the opening, then lift the whole sack and slam it against a log or a flat stone. After about twenty strikes, open it up, and most of the grain will have come loose.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;You can make a flail yourself. Just loosely tie a short stick to the end of a long handle with a leather strap or tough cord. When you swing the handle, the stick at the end spins around and strikes the grain. It is far more efficient and far less tiring than beating with the strength of your arms alone.

### 12-5. Winnowing Away the Chaff - Winnowing Fan and Basket

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Threshed foxtail millet is mixed with bits of the head, leaf fragments, dust, and chaff. Storing it as is lets the moisture clinging to the chaff and any insect eggs ruin the entire stock. Winnowing with wind is essential.

| Method | Procedure | Conditions |
|---|---|---|
| Pouring in the wind | On a windy day, slowly pour it out from a height | 2-3m/sec. Foxtail millet's grain is light, so strong wind blows the grain away too |
| Winnowing basket | Load it into the basket and toss it into the air with a push-and-pull motion | Works even without wind |
| Fanning | One person pours slowly while another fans a breeze | The wind strength can be controlled, which suits foxtail millet |
| Sieving | A coarse sieve for large chaff, a fine sieve for dust | Foxtail millet's grain is small, so the sieve mesh must be fine |
| Water sorting | Pour into water and skim off the chaff that floats | Must be dried again immediately afterward |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Foxtail millet's grain is light, which makes controlling the wind tricky. Pouring it into strong wind the way you would with sorghum or maize blows the grain away along with everything else. For that reason, a method where you can control the strength, like fanning, is better than natural wind, and the winnowing basket is the most reliable. The trick with the basket is not to shake it up and down, but to push it forward and pull it back, briefly tossing the grain into the air. While it is airborne, the lighter material lags behind and collects toward the front, and you sweep that away by hand. It takes half a day to learn and a lifetime to use.

## 13. Seed Saving

### 13-1. Selecting Plants for Seed

| Criterion | Details | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Selection timing | Walk the field during ripening and mark plants in advance | After harvest, you cannot tell which plant it came from |
| Plant condition | No disease symptoms, not lodged, with a large, even head | Traits pass on to the next generation |
| Head position | The thickest main head on the plant | Side heads ripen unevenly |
| Harvest timing | Leave a little longer than the regular harvest, to fully ripen | Immature seed has lower germination |
| To exclude | Plants that showed smut, plants that were unusually late or early | Prevents seed-borne infection and drift in traits |
| Quantity | Twice the amount you need | Since only 1-2kg per 1,000m² is needed, this is not a heavy burden |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Because foxtail millet is a self-pollinating crop, saving its seed is very easy. Even if another variety is growing nearby, the odds of mixing are low, and the seed you collect generally shows the same traits as its parent. From a disaster-preparedness standpoint, this is a major advantage. It does not collapse in a single generation the way maize does, nor does it need to be kept alive through the winter the way potatoes do.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Still, the recommendation to renew seed every 4-5 years has a reason behind it. If you save seed carelessly, you end up using seed collected at random from the whole field, and mixed into that are seeds from plants that were late, disease-prone, or small-headed. As that accumulates year after year, the variety's traits blur within a few years. Strictly selecting plants for seed and managing them separately can slow this decline considerably.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Cross-pollination with green foxtail also happens occasionally. Green foxtail at the edge of the field must be pulled before it heads out. A cross-pollinated plant inherits the tendency for its grain to shatter easily, leading to greater loss at harvest.

### 13-2. Storing Seed

| Condition | Standard | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Moisture | 12-13%. A bit drier than for eating | The lower the moisture, the longer seed lasts |
| Temperature | 10°C or below is ideal | If higher, respiration wears down the embryo |
| Container | Glass jar, metal tin, earthenware jar | Blocks both moisture and insects |
| Light | Fully blocked | Heat and light reduce germination capacity |
| Form | Store with the hull still on | Milled grain lasts much less time |
| Longevity | 2-3 years sealed at room temperature | Lasts longer under good conditions |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Seed must always be kept separate from grain for eating. As winter deepens and food runs low, hands reach for the seed, and once that seed is touched, there is no crop next year. Keeping it physically in a different place, in a different container, and labeled, is more reliable than trusting human restraint.

### 13-3. Germination Test

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Before sowing old seed, check its germination capacity. Wrap fifty grains in a damp cloth, leave them somewhere warm, and wait five days. Foxtail millet's grain is small, and twenty seeds give too much error, so fifty are used. If forty or more out of fifty germinate, use the seed as is; if it is thirty to forty, increase the sowing rate; if it is below thirty, you need to get new seed.

## 14. Toxicity and Precautions

### 14-1. Toxicity of the Grain - None

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Foxtail millet has no toxicity at normal intake levels. Unlike sorghum, its leaves do not contain cyanogenic glycosides at a level that matters, and the grain itself has no toxic component that needs to be removed. Milling it, washing it, and cooking it into rice makes it safe as is. Not needing any special detoxification step before cooking is a real practical advantage in a disaster.

### 14-2. Cases That Need Caution

| Item | Details | Action |
|---|---|---|
| Moldy grain | Mycotoxins do not disappear even with heating | Discard all of it. Do not feed it to livestock either |
| Excess dietary fiber | Very high at 11.4g per 100g | Feed it mixed with other grains to children and the elderly |
| A single-food diet | Low in lysine | Eat it together with legumes |
| Thyroid | There are reports of trace goitrogens in millets | Not a problem at normal intake levels |
| Undercooked grain | The starch is not digested | Soak thoroughly and cook well |

> **Moldy grain does not become safe by washing, boiling, or roasting it. Throwing it away is the only way.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The dietary fiber issue actually becomes important in a disaster. Rice made from foxtail millet is very filling, but that comes with a large share of indigestible material, which can work against someone whose calorie needs are urgent. Young children especially have small stomachs, and by the time they feel full, they have not taken in all the calories they need. When feeding foxtail millet, it is better to mix it with other grains and add at least a little fat.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Goitrogens are a compound known to exist in trace amounts across millets generally, and there are reports that eating large amounts of them over a long period, under conditions of extremely inadequate iodine intake, can affect thyroid function. In an environment like South Korea, where iodine can be obtained from seaweed and salt, this is not a realistic risk. Still, if a diet relying solely on foxtail millet continues for several years, keeping a stock of seaweed such as miyeok (sea mustard) or gim (laver) is advisable for several reasons.
