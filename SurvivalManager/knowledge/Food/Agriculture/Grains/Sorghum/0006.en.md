## 15. Storage Without Refrigeration

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Sorghum's storability grade is SS. It sits at the very top even among grains, and under the right conditions it keeps for more than 3 years at room temperature. Its fat content is low, at 2-3%, so unlike oats it is little at risk of going rancid, and its tough husk also gives physical protection. But this grade holds only when the conditions are met. The saying that grain is lost in the storehouse, not in the field, is no exaggeration.

### 15-1. The Three Enemies of Storage

| Enemy | Conditions | Result | Main countermeasure |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mold | Moisture of 15% or more | Spoilage, toxin production, the whole batch discarded | Drying |
| Insects | Temperature of 20°C or higher, moisture of 12% or more | They eat out the kernels and leave only empty husks | Airtight sealing, low temperature, pest control |
| Rodents | A container they can reach | They eat the grain and contaminate it with droppings | Physical barrier |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;These three are linked. When insects are active, their respiration heat and moisture raise the temperature and humidity locally, and mold starts there. When a rodent gnaws through a sack, moisture and insects come in through the hole. Because the failure of one drags the others down with it, all three must be blocked at the same time.

### 15-2. Storage Conditions

| Condition | Target | If it is missed |
|---|---|---|
| Moisture | 13-14%; 12-13% for seed | Above 15%, mold grows |
| Temperature | 15°C or lower is ideal; as low as possible | Above 25°C, insects breed faster |
| Humidity | 65% or lower in the surrounding air | If higher, dry grain takes moisture back up |
| Light | Completely blocked | Heat builds up and quality declines |
| Form | In the husk | Milled grain has half the storage life or less |
| Inspection | Once every two months | Problems found early can be stopped before losses occur |

> **Store grain in the husk and mill only as much as you will eat. The husk is the packaging that nature provides.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The principle of storing grain in the husk matters especially in a disaster. The husk of sorghum is both a physical barrier against insects and a shield that slows oxidation. Milled sorghum is hard to keep beyond 6 months, but in the husk it lasts 3 years. There is a temptation to mill it all at once and have it ready, but the habit of milling only a month's worth at a time is what in the end protects the grain.

### 15-3. Storage Containers

| Container | Airtightness | Duration | Advantages | Drawbacks |
|---|:---:|---|---|---|
| Earthenware jar (onggi) | Good | 2-3 years | Temperature changes slowly, and rodents cannot gnaw through it | Heavy and breakable |
| Glass jar | Very good | 3 years or more | The condition of the grain can be checked by eye | Small capacity, and it does not block light |
| Metal tin | Very good | 3 years or more | Completely blocks rodents and insects | Spoils at any spot where condensation forms |
| Thick plastic bag | Fair | 1-2 years | Light and cheap | Rodents chew through it, and it tears |
| Straw sacks (gamani) or burlap sacks | Poor | 6 months-1 year | Breathable, so the grain does not turn damp | Defenseless against insects and rodents |
| Underground storage pit | Good | 2-3 years or more | Temperature is constant and low | If water seeps in, everything is lost |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The earthenware jar is a balance point that Korean ancestors found. Its thick clay walls slow the effect of outside temperature changes, its microscopic pores allow very slow breathing so that no condensation forms, and rodents cannot gnaw through it. If you cover the mouth with hanji (traditional Korean paper) or cloth, set a lid on top, and seal the rim with clay, it becomes effectively airtight. Making new ones is hard in a disaster, but gathering up the jars that remain is a thoroughly worthwhile preparation.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;An underground storage pit is the method to use when quantities are large. Choose a high spot where water does not seep in, dig 1-1.5m deep, line the floor and walls thickly with dry straw, put in the grain, and cover it again with straw and soil. It is a method that has been used in Africa and the Middle East for thousands of years, and in a well-made pit the oxygen falls and the insects die on their own. However, once opened it is hard to seal again, so make several pits, each sized for a portion that will be taken out all at once.

### 15-4. Controlling Insects in Storage - Without Chemicals

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The real main enemy in grain storage is insects. Pests such as the Angoumois grain moth, the Indian meal moth, the rice weevil, and the red flour beetle are already present as eggs in freshly harvested grain and hatch inside the storehouse. One insect becomes dozens within two months, and in half a year a single sack is filled with empty husks and droppings. The methods that can be used where no insecticides are available are as follows.

| Method | Principle | How to apply | Effect |
|---|---|---|---|
| Full airtight sealing | Cuts off oxygen and suffocates the insects | Fill glass jars or metal tins to the brim and seal them | The most reliable. The less empty space, the better |
| Mixing in wood ash | Fine ash particles scratch the insects' exoskeleton and dehydrate them | Mix in 2-5% of the grain volume evenly | A method proven over a long time. Wash it off before eating |
| Diatomaceous earth | Tiny silica shells damage the exoskeleton | Mix 1-3g into 1kg of grain | More effective than ash. Works only when dry |
| Fine sand or yellow clay | The particles fill the gaps and stop insects from moving | Put it in as alternating layers | The drawback is that it increases the bulk |
| Dried chili peppers or sancho (Korean prickly ash) | The pungent compounds act as a repellent | Place a few in the container | A supplementary measure |
| Leaves of mugwort, sweet flag, or pagoda tree | The essential oils suppress egg-laying | Dry them and layer them between the grain | Widely used traditionally |
| Dry salt | Binds moisture and removes the conditions insects need to survive | Wrap roasted salt in cloth and put it in | Doubles as a desiccant |
| Charcoal | Absorbs moisture and odors | Put in charcoal that has been baked and dried | Also helps prevent mold |
| Cold treatment | Eggs and larvae die below freezing | Put the grain outside for a few days in midwinter, then bring it back in | Watch for condensation when bringing it back in |
| Heat treatment | Eggs and larvae die at 60°C | Spread it thinly in the sun for several hours | Not used for seed |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The most important of these is the first. Full airtight sealing is the most powerful measure available even when no chemicals are at hand. Insects breathe too, so when the grain and insects use up the oxygen inside a sealed container, the carbon dioxide concentration rises, the oxygen falls, and the insects die. The more completely the container is filled and the less empty space it holds, the faster this effect works. A fully packed tin lasts longer than a half-filled one.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Wood ash and diatomaceous earth work on the same principle. Both kill insects physically rather than chemically. When fine, sharp particles scrape off the wax layer covering an insect's body, the insect loses water and dries out. This approach has two advantages. First, insects cannot develop resistance. Second, it is harmless to people. You only need to wash it off with water before eating, and eating a little of it is not a problem. The drawback is that it does not work when there is moisture. If the grain turns damp, neither ash nor diatomaceous earth is effective, so in the end drying comes first.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;When using ash, choose fine ash. Sift it to remove coarse pieces of charcoal and use only the fraction as fine as wheat flour. Mix in 2-5% of the grain volume, that is, about one doe (about 1.8 L) of ash for one mal (about 18 L) of grain, and turn it by hand so that every kernel gets a thin coating. The grain looks cloudy gray on the surface, but rinsing it three or four times with water when you eat it makes it clean. It is a method still widely used in Africa and India today, and it has passed thousands of years of practical testing.

### 15-5. Regular Inspection

| When | What to check | Action if something is wrong |
|---|---|---|
| Every two months | Condensation on the outside of the container; moldy smell | Open it immediately, spread the grain out, and dry it |
| Every two months | Powder or cobweb-like threads on the grain surface | This is the Indian meal moth. Dry all the grain in the sun and sift out the pests |
| Every two months | Warmth when you hold the grain in your hand | Heat is a sign of excess moisture. Take the grain out immediately |
| Spring and autumn | Open the whole container and check smell and color | If there is an off smell, divide the grain and examine each portion |
| Before the monsoon season (jangma, late June to July in South Korea) | Recheck that the container is sealed | If the seal has loosened, reseal it |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The most important sense in inspection is smell. Mold shows itself by smell well before it can be seen. If you open the container and notice a musty, damp smell, something like the smell of a cellar, it has already started. If you take everything out and dry it in the sun at that stage, you can save most of it, but once the mold becomes visible it is too late.

## 16. Cooking and Processing

### 16-1. Milling - Removing the Husk Without Special Equipment

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Sorghum is hard to eat unless it is milled. The husk is tough and tannin is concentrated in the seed coat, so if you cook it as is, it turns out astringent and coarse. Where there is no electric mill, milling is work done by human strength.

| Method | Tool | Procedure | Efficiency |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pounding in a mortar | Mortar and pestle | Put in dry grain and pound up and down. Every 5-10 minutes, take it out and winnow away the bran | The most common method. Pound in three batches and repeat the pounding and winnowing |
| Dampened pounding | Mortar | Sprinkle a little water on the grain before pounding and leave it for 30 minutes | The husk comes off more easily and fewer kernels break |
| Millstone dehusking | Hand millstone | Raise the upper stone slightly and turn it so that only the husk is stripped off | Adjusting the gap of the upper stone is the key |
| Rubbing in a stone basin | A hollow stone (dolhwak) and a round stone | Rub by hand to strip off the husk | Suited to small amounts |
| Foot mortar | Lever-type treadle pounder (didilbang-a) | Step on it with the foot to raise and drop the pestle | Takes far less effort. Can be built yourself |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The knack of milling is not to try to finish in one go. If you pound hard for a long time, the kernels break before the husk comes off. Pound for 5-10 minutes, take the grain out, winnow away the stripped bran, and put it back in to pound again, repeating this three or four times. Only if you clear away the bran at intervals does the force reach the remaining kernels properly. Slightly dampening the grain helps as well, because only the husk swells and its bond with the kernel loosens. However, once dampened, the grain must always be pounded and dried within the same day.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The treadle pounder is a tool well worth building in advance. A long log is rested at its midpoint on a support, a pestle is fixed at one end, and the other end is stepped on and released. Because the person's body weight supplies the force, it is several times more efficient than pounding with the arms, and with two people taking turns it can process a considerable amount in a day. If the disaster looks likely to be prolonged, it is a piece of equipment to build even before a mortar.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Do not throw the bran away. It is rich in dietary fiber and minerals, so it makes good livestock feed, and if you have no livestock, put it in the compost. People can eat it too, but the tannin is concentrated in it, making it hard to digest and interfering with nutrient absorption, so think of it only as a last resort in extreme hunger.

### 16-2. Removing Tannin

| Step | Procedure | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Put the milled kernels in plenty of water and wash them by rubbing with your hands | Removes the remaining bran and the tannin on the surface |
| 2 | Discard the cloudy water and repeat with fresh water, three or four times | Continue until the water runs clear |
| 3 | Finally, soak for 30 minutes to overnight | The tannin inside leaches out into the water |
| 4 | Always discard the soaking water | The tannin is dissolved in it |
| 5 | Rinse and cook | The astringent taste almost disappears |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Where water is scarce, this process is a burden. In that case, do not throw the soaking water away; collect it and reuse it for washing laundry or for watering the field. Water containing tannin is astringent to people, but it does no harm to the soil.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Fermentation is also a way to reduce tannin. If you soak milled sorghum in water and leave it for two or three days, lactic acid fermentation takes place, the tannin and phytic acid are broken down considerably, and the absorption rate of iron and zinc rises. African sorghum porridge and fermented batter are foods that use this principle. A sour smell means success; if you notice a rotten smell or see mold, discard it.

### 16-3. Grinding - Making Flour

| Method | Tool | Characteristics |
|---|---|---|
| Millstone | Two stones, upper and lower | Gives the finest flour. Goes quickly with two people |
| Mortar | Mortar and pestle | Gives coarse flour. Sift it and pound the coarse part again |
| Grinding on a stone slab | A flat stone and a rolling-pin-shaped stone | Suited to small amounts. Same principle as the Mexican metate |
| Wooden mortar | A mortar hollowed out of a log | Easier to make than stone. The flour is less fine |
| Roast first, then pound | Any of the above | Roasting makes the tissue brittle, so it takes much less effort |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Remembering the roast-then-pound method saves a great deal of effort. If you roast dry grain over low heat until it gives off a nutty aroma, the starch structure breaks down and it grinds much more easily. Moreover, roasted flour can be eaten simply stirred into water, so no cooking fuel is needed. Misutgaru (Korean roasted grain powder mixed into water) works on this principle. If you are preparing food to eat on the move in a disaster, roasted sorghum flour is the most sensible form.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Flour spoils much faster than whole grain. The larger surface area speeds oxidation, and insects burrow in easily. Grind only one to two months' worth and leave the rest as whole grain. Always put ground flour in an airtight container and keep it in a cool place.

### 16-4. Cooking That Saves Fuel

| Method | Fuel | Procedure | Notes |
|---|:---:|---|---|
| Cooked sorghum | Medium | Soak in water for at least 30 minutes, then cook it like rice | If it is not soaked, the inside does not cook and only fuel is wasted |
| Mixed-grain rice (honban) | Medium | Mix sorghum into rice or other grains at 2-3 parts in 10 (20-30%) | Little resistance to the taste, and it supplements the nutrition |
| Porridge | Low | Stir flour into water and boil. 10 minutes is enough | Uses a lot of water but the least fuel |
| Residual-heat cooking | Very low | Boil for 10 minutes, remove from the heat, wrap in straw or a blanket, and leave for 40-60 minutes | Cuts fuel use by half or more |
| Roasted flour | Almost none | Stir previously roasted flour into water | No cooking fuel needed |
| Pan-fried cakes | Low | Fry a flour batter thinly in a pan | If thick, the inside does not cook |
| Baking in ashes | Very low | Shape the dough into lumps and bury them in hot ashes | Uses the leftover heat of a hearth after other cooking is done |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Heat-retention cooking is particularly effective with grains. Soak the sorghum in water, boil it for only 10 minutes, take it off the heat, and wrap the whole pot in a box filled with straw or rice husks, or in a thick quilt; the internal temperature stays above 80°C for over an hour while cooking continues. One wooden box filled with straw is enough equipment, and once you have made it, fuel consumption is halved.

### 16-5. Processing That Doubles as Preservation

| Processing | Procedure | Storage | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Roasted flour (misutgaru) | Roast and pound | Airtight, 6 months | Stir into water and eat right away |
| Sorghum yeot (grain taffy) and jocheong (grain syrup) | Saccharify with malt (yeotgireum) and boil down for a long time | 1 year in a cool place | One of the few ways to get sweetness |
| Fermented batter | Mix flour with water and ferment for 2-3 days | A few days | Tannin falls and nutrient absorption improves |
| Sorghum liquor | Mix steamed sorghum with nuruk (Korean fermentation starter) and ferment | Stored sealed | Preserves calories and also serves to disinfect water |
| Puffed grain (tuibap) | Put it in a heated pot and pop it | Airtight, 1-2 months | Increases in volume and can be eaten right away |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;If you have also planted sweet sorghum, you can obtain sweet juice from the stalks. Cut ripe stalks, strip the leaves, crush the joints and squeeze out the juice, and boil it down for a long time to get a molasses-like syrup. It is almost the only field-crop means of producing your own sweetener when sugar has disappeared, and the syrup you make is itself a calorie reserve. The drawback is that boiling it down takes a lot of fuel, but if you use the hearth together with other cooking, waste is reduced.

## 17. Annual Work Calendar

| Month | Work | Key points |
|---|---|---|
| January | Inspect stored grain, check seed condition, maintain tools | Check for condensation and moldy smell. Repair the flail and the mortar |
| February | Plan the field, turn the compost, run germination tests | Always check the germination rate of old seed |
| March | Collect wood ash, finish the compost | Sift the ash produced over the winter and set it aside |
| April | Plow the field, dig drainage ditches, apply base fertilizer | Dig the furrows deep with the monsoon in mind |
| May | Salt-water seed selection, seed disinfection, sowing (from mid-May in central South Korea) | Sowing in batches 2 weeks apart spreads out the harvest period |
| June | Remaining sowing, first thinning, weeding | Weed control in the first month decides the yield |
| July | Second thinning, top-dressing, hilling, start of panicle emergence | Always irrigate if drought strikes at this time |
| August | Flowering and grain filling, bird scaring, disease watch | When the heads begin to ripen, stretch netting or string over them |
| September | Harvest begins, preliminary drying, hanging to dry | If rain is forecast, do not wait for full ripeness |
| October | Finish the harvest, thresh, final drying, store | Seal airtight after confirming moisture of 13-14% |
| November | Sort seed and keep it separately, clean up the field, dispose of residue | Clear away stubble to remove the overwintering sites of moths |
| December | Milling and grinding, storage inspection, plan for next year | Mill only a month's worth |

### 17-1. Five Things Not to Forget

| Item | Details |
|---|---|
| Sunlight | In shade, sorghum yield collapses to half. Give it the sunniest spot |
| Drainage | It tolerates drought but spoils within days if waterlogged. The furrow is the insurance |
| Nitrogen | Giving more because there seems to be plenty only makes the plants grow tall and fall over. Do not exceed the standard rate |
| Drying | Moisture of 13-14% is everything in storage. Check by biting a kernel to see whether it cracks crisply |
| In the husk | Store in the husk and mill only as much as you will eat. Milling cuts the storage life to half or less |
