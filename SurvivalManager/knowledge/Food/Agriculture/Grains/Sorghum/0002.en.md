## 1. Disaster-Preparedness Evaluation

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Sorghum's overall grade is A, with a score of 50.2. Broken down by scenario, its short-term ability to get through the early phase of a disaster is B (48.1), its long-term self-reliance over many years is S (52.5), and its supplementary value as a side-dish resource beyond the staple is SS (57.8). The fact that the long-term score is more than 4 points higher than the short-term score sums up the character of sorghum. It has to be planted and then waited on for four months, but once it has taken hold, it holds up better than other crops as the years go by.

### 1-3. How to Read the Strengths

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Five criteria are graded S or higher: water use, infrastructure needs, storability, continuous-cropping tolerance and seed saving. It is worth thinking about what it means when these five come together in one crop. It means that you can grow it with almost no irrigation, put up no facilities, plant it in the same field every year, save your own seed, and keep the harvest for more than three years. It is a structure in which the cycle does not break even when nothing at all comes in from outside. In disaster preparedness, this deserves to be called a self-reliance loop.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The S for water use is especially important. A total evapotranspiration of 250mm is less than 20% of South Korea's annual precipitation. When pumped wells fail, irrigation channels are blocked and you have to get by on a single rain barrel, sorghum sets grain on nothing but the water that falls from the sky. Compared with maize, which demands 550mm, and rice, which demands even more, the difference is plain. Even in a drought year when every other field is wiped out, a sorghum field can still salvage half a crop.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The A for fertilizer demand and the SS for continuous-cropping tolerance should be read as a pair. Sorghum sends its roots down deep and dense, drawing up nutrients and water even from layers that other grains cannot reach. So the same amount of fertilizer goes further. On top of that, it has the continuous-cropping tolerance typical of the grass family, so it can be grown on the same spot year after year even when there is no room to rotate fields. In disaster conditions where land is limited and hands are few, these two traits give more freedom than one might expect.

> **The strength of sorghum lies not in the size of its harvest but in the fact that its yield does not drop sharply even when conditions worsen.**

### 1-4. How to Read the Weaknesses

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Two criteria carry an E: sunlight response and cold hardiness. Below those, a D is attached to cooking & processing, yield stability and non-food value.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The E for sunlight is sorghum's most painful vulnerability. C4 photosynthesis delivers overwhelming efficiency in strong light and high temperatures, but in exchange it breaks down faster than other crops when the light weakens. A relative yield of 45% at 40% shade means that in a field half covered by shade, the harvest falls to half or less. Under the same conditions, C3 cereals such as oat keep 88%. A sorghum field must therefore always be placed in the sunniest spot available, and places shaded by walls or large trees, as well as north-facing slopes, should be avoided. In a disaster of the kind that leaves the sky overcast for a long time, such as a volcanic eruption or a major wildfire, a plan that leans on sorghum monoculture needs to be reconsidered from the ground up.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The E for cold hardiness narrows the growing period. Sorghum dies outright after a single frost. It needs 120-150 days, and that many days have to be secured within the frost-free period, so if sowing is delayed, the grain-filling stage runs into frost. This is why, in central South Korea, late June is treated as the latest sowing limit.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The D for cooking & processing is the labor needed before the grain can be eaten. Sorghum grain is wrapped in a hard hull and an astringent seed coat, so it cannot be eaten as it is. Whether with a mortar or a millstone, a dehulling and polishing step is unavoidable, and even after polishing, the grain has to be washed several times, changing the water, to remove the tannins. Where electric polishing machines have stopped, this work takes up a major part of the day. That is why Section 16 covers dehulling, polishing and milling without tools in detail.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The D for yield stability also needs to be taken into account. A year-to-year yield variation of 24.8% means the harvest swings by about 25% from one year to the next. Saying that sorghum is strong against drought means it does not die out completely even in a lean year; it does not mean it gives a steady harvest every year. Whatever is left over in a good year must be put into reserve, and this is how the SS for storability comes to offset this weakness.

## 2. What Kind of Plant Is This?

### 2-1. Classification and Origin

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Sorghum is an annual plant of the genus Sorghum in the grass family (Poaceae). It belongs to the same family as rice, barley, wheat, maize and foxtail millet. This fact has two implications. One is that it shares a large part of its pests and diseases with them; the other is that it also has the continuous-cropping tolerance and the fibrous root structure typical of the grass family.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Its origin is the savanna zone of northeastern Africa, an environment where short rainy seasons and long dry seasons alternate, temperatures are high, and the soil is shallow and low in nutrients. Sorghum's ability to withstand heat above 40°C, to adapt to a wide soil pH range of 5.5-8.5, and to stop growing in drought and revive when rain comes all come from there. Conversely, waterlogged ground and cool shade are conditions sorghum never experienced, and so it has no means of coping with them.

### 2-2. Appearance

| Organ | Characteristics |
|---|---|
| Leaf | Narrow, long and linear. The leaf sheath wraps around the stem, and the wax layer on the surface reduces water loss |
| Stem | Stands upright, 80-120cm tall. Depending on the variety, it can exceed 3m. The nodes are distinct and the stem is solid inside |
| Root | Fibrous roots spread deep and dense. Even within the grass family, its root density is especially high |
| Flower | Borne in a panicle at the tip of the stem. A single head sets 1,000-3,000 grains |
| Fruit | A caryopsis. Color differs by variety, such as reddish brown, white and black, and varieties are divided into glutinous (waxy) and non-glutinous types |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The wax layer on the leaves is the key structure that explains sorghum's drought tolerance. As drought deepens, sorghum rolls its leaves inward to reduce the area over which the stomata are exposed, and the wax covering them holds on to what little moisture remains. People often judge a field of rolled-up sorghum to be dead and plow it under, but once rain falls, the leaves flatten out again within two days.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;It is also worth knowing the classification by use. Sorghum is divided into grain sorghum, whose grain is eaten; sweet sorghum (sorgo), whose stem sap yields a sweet juice; and broom-corn (also called jangmok sorghum), whose panicle branches are bound into brooms. In disaster preparedness, grain sorghum is the basic choice, but sweet sorghum is close to the only field crop that yields a sweetener once sugar has disappeared, and broom-corn lets you supply yourself with an everyday necessity, the broom. If you have room, it is reasonable to plant mainly grain sorghum and put sweet sorghum in one corner.

### 2-3. Growth Stages

| Stage | Timing | Key physiology | Important nutrients |
|---|---|---|---|
| Germination and seedling stage | 3 weeks from sowing | The seed germinates and puts down its first roots | Phosphorus, nitrogen |
| Vegetative growth stage | Weeks 3-6 | Stem and leaves grow quickly | Nitrogen |
| Heading stage | Weeks 6-8 | The panicle emerges from the leaf sheath | Phosphorus, potassium |
| Flowering and grain-setting stage | Weeks 8-10 | Pollination takes place and the grains set | Potassium |
| Grain-filling and maturation stage | Weeks 10-14 | Starch fills the grains and they harden | Potassium |

> **Even drought-tolerant sorghum is different during heading and flowering. If water is cut off during these 2 weeks, the panicle emerges but sets no grain and ends up empty.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Sorghum's drought tolerance differs from stage to stage. During vegetative growth it holds out through a month without rain, but from just before the panicle emerges through flowering, it demands water just like any other grain. This period usually falls between late July and early August in South Korea, so in years when the monsoon season (jangma, late June to July in South Korea) ends early and a heat wave follows, even sorghum fields have to be irrigated. Conversely, rain after the grain-filling stage does harm. It causes grain mold in the heads and makes drying after harvest difficult.

### 2-4. Nutrition per 100g

| Nutrient | Content | Notes |
|---|:---:|---|
| Energy | 358-374kcal | Based on dry grain. High among grains |
| Carbohydrate | 74-79.3g | Mostly starch |
| Protein | 11-11.8g | 12.5% of calories, among the highest of the grains |
| Fat | 2-3.5g | Less than oat, so the risk of rancidity is low |
| Dietary fiber | 6.6g | Especially abundant in the outer hull |
| Iron | 4.4mg | High among grains |
| Magnesium | 165mg | Involved in nerve and muscle function |
| Polyphenols and tannins | Varies by variety | Antioxidants, but in excess they interfere with absorption |
| B vitamins | Thiamin, riboflavin, B6, pantothenic acid, folate | Losses depend on how far the grain is polished |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Protein at 11.67g is noticeably higher than in rice. Converted to a share of calories, it is 12.5%, above the 10% minimum a person needs. However, as with grains in general, lysine is deficient, so the amino acid profile is completed only when sorghum is eaten together with beans. The old custom of mixing adzuki beans or soybeans into susubap (cooked sorghum) is a way of solving a nutritional problem, not a matter of taste.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The iron content is also worth noting. At more than 4mg per 100g, filling two meals a day with sorghum comes close to the recommended intake for an adult man. In a disaster, when meat is gone, anemia is a real threat that creeps up slowly, and sorghum becomes one pillar of the response. However, tannins interfere with iron absorption, so this advantage is realized only if dehulling, polishing and washing are done thoroughly.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;It is also worth noting that sorghum contains no gluten. For people who cannot eat wheat, sorghum is one of the few alternative grains. In return, the dough does not develop viscosity, so it is hard to make into bread or noodles. What can be made with sorghum is covered in Section 16.
