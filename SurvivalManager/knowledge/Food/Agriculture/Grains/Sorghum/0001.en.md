## Introduction
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Sorghum (Sorghum bicolor) is a grain that came from northeastern Africa, around present-day Ethiopia and Sudan. It has fed people for more than five thousand years on land with a long dry season, shallow and nutrient-poor soil, and midday temperatures that hover around 40℃, so when water runs short it rolls its leaves to cut transpiration, and when it runs shorter it stops growing and resumes when rain comes. It needs only 250 mm of total evapotranspiration, less than half of maize, and only 18 g per ㎡ of nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium combined, a little over half of maize. As a grass it does not collapse under continuous cropping, and dried grain keeps for more than 3 years at room temperature. It holds 45% of normal yield without fertilizer, so when logistics are cut, fertilizer plants have stopped and fields cannot be irrigated, sorghum shows its worth from the second year. It has four weak points. Calories per area are 1,309 kcal per ㎡, so about 200㎡ is needed for one person's annual calories; it is so weak in shade that blocking just 40% of the light cuts yield to under half; it is killed by a single frost; and it must be milled and its tannin washed out before it can be eaten. Grain is usually lost not in the field but in the storehouse, so sorghum is harder to put away than to grow.

## 2. Plant Overview
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;An annual of the grass family (genus Sorghum). The leaves are long, narrow and linear, and a wax layer on the surface reduces water loss. The stem stands upright, is solid, and is 80-120 cm tall (over 3 m in some varieties). Fibrous roots spread deep and dense. The flowers form a panicle at the tip of the stem, and one head carries 1,000-3,000 kernels. The fruit is a caryopsis, and sorghum is divided into waxy and non-waxy types.

| Stage | Timing | Key point |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Germination and seedling | 3 weeks after sowing | Phosphorus, nitrogen |
| Vegetative growth | 3-6 weeks | Nitrogen |
| Heading | 6-8 weeks | Phosphorus, potassium |
| Flowering and fruiting | 8-10 weeks | Potassium |
| Grain filling and maturity | 10-14 weeks | Potassium |

> **Even drought-hardy sorghum is different at heading and flowering. If water stops during these 2 weeks, heads emerge but no kernels set, leaving empty heads.**

| Nutrition per 100 g | Content |
|:---|:---|
| Calories | 358-374 kcal (dry grain) |
| Carbohydrates | 74-79.3 g (mostly starch) |
| Protein | 11-11.8 g (clearly higher than rice) |
| Fat | 2-3.5 g (less than oat, so low rancidity risk) |
| Others | Dietary fiber 6.6 g, iron 4.4 mg, magnesium 165 mg, B vitamins, polyphenols and tannin. Gluten-free |

## 3. Growing Environment and Varieties
| Condition | Optimum | Limit and effect |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Growing temperature | 25-30℃ | 12-48℃. Growth stops below 12℃ and the plant dies at 0℃ |
| Germination | 21-35℃ | Minimum 15℃. Below that, germination is delayed and the seed rots |
| Light | Strong midday sun | A C4 crop. Relative yield 45% under 40% shading (88% for C3 cereals such as oat) |
| Water | About 250 mm total | 150-400 mm. In severe drought it stops growing and endures |
| Soil | Sandy loam to clay loam | Almost any soil texture. pH 5.5-7.5 (5.0-8.5). Roots are damaged in clay where water pools |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Look at sun before water when choosing a spot. A gentle south-facing slope is best, and a flat field works if the beds are raised. It also sets grain on gravelly poor ground. A field converted from a paddy needs the drainage layer broken and the beds raised high, and the shade of buildings or large trees, north-facing slopes and valley bottoms (short sunlight, early frost) are unsuitable.

| Type | Characteristics | Disaster perspective |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Waxy sorghum | Endosperm starch is 90-100% amylopectin. Sticky and soft. Mixed rice, rice cake, liquor | Easy to mix into rice, so little aversion. Most varieties spread in Korea |
| Non-waxy sorghum | 22-24% amylose. Crumbly. Rice, porridge, flour | Better for grinding into flour |

| Variety | Height | Yield (kg/1,000㎡) | Characteristics |
|:---|:---|:---:|:---|
| Miryang 15 | Medium | 484 | Highest yield in Korea. Plant form suited to machine harvest |
| Goeunchal | About 100 cm | 374 | Resists lodging and helps avoid head mold |
| Sodamchal | 91-117 cm | 296 | Reddish-brown grain. High antioxidant activity, resists lodging |
| Jungmo waxy sorghum | 88 cm | - | Shortest dwarf type. White grain. Little wind damage |
| Namphungchal (Miryang 4) | Medium | - | Mid-maturing waxy sorghum. Lodging resistant, disaster resistant, high yielding |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;If yield is the aim, Miryang 15; if labor is short, Sodamchal and Goeunchal; for windy places, Jungmo waxy sorghum and Sodamchal; where frost comes early, early-maturing lines. The longer a disaster lasts, the more a landrace that can be carried on by seed saving matters over yield.

## 4. Field Preparation and Sowing
| Item | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Soil preparation | Plow 20-30 cm deep and break up the clods to level the soil (the seeds are small, and if they fall between clods they will not germinate). Drainage ditches around the field and in the furrows, bed height 15-20 cm |
| Fertilizing (per 1,000㎡) | Compost 1,000-1,500 kg, nitrogen 10-15 kg (50% basal, 50% top dressing), phosphate 5 kg, potassium 4.5 kg. Compost, phosphate and potassium entirely as basal fertilizer |
| Timing | Basal fertilizer 1-2 weeks before sowing; top dressing at the 5-10 leaf stage together with thinning and hilling |
| Substitute resources | Wood ash 20-40 kg (hardwood ash), rotted compost (3-6 months), urine diluted 10 times (poured into the furrows, stopped 3 weeks before harvest), bone meal, legume green manure (sow 2-3 weeks after plowing under) |

> **More nitrogen only makes sorghum taller, and it falls over. Even if fertilizer is plentiful, it is better for the harvest not to exceed the standard amount.**

| Region and cropping | Sowing |
|:---|:---|
| Central region | Mid May to early June |
| Southern region | Early to mid May |
| After barley or wheat | Mid to late June |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In a disaster, do not sow everything at once; sow in batches 2 weeks apart.

| Sowing | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Seed selection | Stir seed into salt water (51 g of salt per 1 L of water, specific gravity 1.035) and use only what sinks. Without a scale, use a concentration at which a raw egg floats |
| Disinfection | 50-55℃ water for 10-15 minutes (the embryo dies above 60℃), coating with fine wood ash, wood vinegar diluted 500 times for 6-12 hours, 2-3 hours in the sun 2-3 days before sowing |
| Planting | 1-2 kg per 1,000㎡, depth 2-5 cm. Single-row: row spacing 60 cm, plant spacing 20 cm, 7-8 seeds per hill. Double-row: bed width 110 cm, row spacing 30 cm, plant spacing 15 cm |
| Transplanting | 2-3 seeds per cell in a 128-cell tray, raise seedlings for 15-20 days. When the preceding crop finishes late |
| Finishing | After covering, tread it down with the feet or press with a board (germination rises greatly; especially effective when drought is expected) |

## 5. Water, Cultivation Management, and Pests and Diseases
| Period | Water management |
|:---|:---|
| Sowing to germination | Keep the soil from drying out |
| Seedling stage | No irrigation needed (encourages deep roots) |
| Vegetative growth | Leave it to natural rainfall. It does not die even after a dry month |
| Heading to flowering | Always water in dry weather. These 2 weeks decide the yield |
| Grain filling | Taper off gradually |
| Maturity | Cut off completely. Too much water makes drying after harvest difficult |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Leaves curling at midday are a normal defensive response; if they are still curled in the morning, it is real drought. Leaves yellowing and drying from the bottom up mean long drought, and heads that emerged with empty kernels mean water shortage at heading and flowering, which cannot be recovered. Leaves turning pale yellow with black roots mean waterlogging, so dig a drainage ditch at once, and a soft base that topples means root rot.

| Management | Details |
|:---|:---|
| Thinning | Pull weak plants at 3-4 true leaves, and at the 5-10 leaf stage leave only the 1-2 strongest. Use the pulled seedlings as feed or compost, but because they are rich in cyanogenic glycoside, do not feed large amounts |
| Weeding | Start when the weeds are as long as a finger and finish when the crop is knee high. The 30-45 days after sowing are critical; after that the sorghum covers the weeds |
| Hilling | Together with thinning and once more afterwards. Encourages new roots and prevents lodging |
| Lodging | Do not give more than the standard nitrogen, hill up thoroughly and choose short varieties. After lodging, tie three or four plants together and stand them up |
| Ratooning | If you cut leaving 10-15 cm of stubble at the first harvest, new stems rise and give a second harvest of about half. Only in the south, where grain filling can finish before frost, and only when the first harvest ends within August |
| Birds | From about 3 weeks before ripening, nets, paper bags or old cloth over the heads, strings stretched over the field. If not guarded, as much as a third of the harvest can be lost |

| Pest or disease | Response without chemicals |
|:---|:---|
| Head mold (frequent rain at grain filling, heads black or pink) | Early-maturing varieties, early harvest, immediate drying, discarding diseased heads |
| Covered kernel smut (seed-borne) | Salt water selection, coating with wood ash, hot water treatment, burning diseased heads |
| Anthracnose, sheath blight, stalk rot | Ventilation and plant spacing, rotation, better drainage, removing diseased leaves, avoiding excess nitrogen, wood vinegar |
| Aphids, mites | Soapy water (5-10 g of pure soap per 1 L of water, morning or evening), protect ladybugs and lacewings |
| Asian corn borer and other moths, armyworm | Remove and burn damaged stems, hand picking, treating stubble after harvest, early harvest if severe |
| Stink bugs | Hand picking in the early morning, removing weeds around the field |

> **Moldy grain does not become safe by washing, boiling or roasting. Throwing it away is the only way.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The most dangerous disease is head mold, and prevention depends entirely on drying. Do not take seed from diseased plants, plant and harvest so as to avoid the season when disease spreads, and clear diseased residue and stubble from the field. Wood ash (on a dewy morning), wood vinegar diluted 500-1,000 times, and tobacco leaf or chili infusion are also used.

| Symptom | Cause and response |
|:---|:---|
| Yellowing from the lower leaves and small plants | Nitrogen deficiency. Rotted compost, urine diluted 10 times |
| Leaf margins scorched brown | Potassium deficiency. Wood ash |
| Slow growth and dark blue-green leaves | Phosphorus deficiency. Mix bone meal into compost |
| Yellow between the veins, veins stay green | Magnesium deficiency. Dolomite powder |
| New leaves turn white | Iron deficiency (excess alkalinity). Stop wood ash, add leaf mold |
| Tall, dark leaves and lodging | Excess nitrogen. Cut off nitrogen and hill up to hold the plants |

## 6. Intercropping and Crop Rotation
| Compatibility | Crop |
|:---|:---|
| Very good | Soybean, adzuki bean, cowpea (nitrogen fixed by root nodule bacteria is shared; allow generous spacing when mixed) |
| Good | Peanut and pumpkin (cover the ground and reduce evaporation and weeds), potato (growth periods are offset) |
| Good as a following crop | Barley, wheat (fields where sorghum's allelopathic compounds have reduced the weeds) |
| Poor | Maize, foxtail millet, proso millet (the same grass family, so they share pests and diseases and compete heavily for nutrients) |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Rotate sorghum in year 1, a legume in year 2, a leaf or root vegetable in year 3, and return to sorghum in year 4. Sorghum tolerates continuous cropping, but disease density builds up.

## 7. Harvest, Drying, Threshing, and Seed Saving
| Step | Details |
|:---|:---|
| Timing | 120-150 days after sowing. When the heads darken to the variety's own color, a split kernel is white and firm inside, a black dot (black layer) forms at the base of the kernel, and the lower leaves dry and the stem turns yellow. If rain continues during grain filling, cut without waiting for full ripeness |
| Cutting | On a clear day after the dew has dried, cut with a sickle 20-30 cm below the head. Sort diseased and sound heads on the spot. Bind about six or seven heads into a bundle and hang them upside down where rain will not reach them |
| Drying | Preliminary drying 2-3 days (about 20%) → hang under the eaves 1-2 weeks (15-16%) → spread on straw mats in the sun 2-4 days → 13-14% |
| Checking moisture | A kernel that snaps crisply when bitten and leaves flour is 13-14%. It also passes if dry grain shaken in both hands knocks together like pebbles and a half-filled sealed jar left for a day or two does not fog |
| Threshing | Strip by hand (seed grain), beat with a stick or flail on a straw mat, strike inside a sack, tread on a mat spread with heads, rub on a grooved board, rub inside a tub |
| Winnowing | Let the chaff blow away in a steady wind of 2-4 m/s, winnow in a basket, fan it, sieve it (coarse sieve for chaff, fine sieve for dust and small grains) |

| Seed saving | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Selection | Walk the field at grain filling and mark plants. The thick, even main head of a plant that is disease-free, not lodged and has a well-filled head. Exclude plants that showed smut |
| Isolation | Cross-pollination is 5-10%, so keep it away from other varieties. If the field is small, plant so that flowering times are offset |
| Storage | In the husk, moisture 12-13%, 10℃ or below, light completely blocked, airtight container. 2-3 years airtight at room temperature. Keep apart from food grain |
| Germination test | Wrap 20 kernels in wet cloth and leave for five days. Use as is if 16 or more sprout, raise the sowing rate if 12-15, and get new seed if fewer than 10 |

## 8. Storage
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Storability is SS, but there are three enemies: mold (moisture 15% or more, spoilage and toxins, discard it all), insects (20℃ or more, moisture 12% or more) and rats (accessible containers). If one fails the others follow, so all three must be blocked at once.

| Item | Standard |
|:---|:---|
| Conditions | Moisture 13-14%, temperature 15℃ or below (insects multiply fast above 25℃), surrounding humidity 65% or below, light blocked, check once every two months |
| Form | In the husk (3 years). Milled sorghum rice hardly lasts more than 6 months. Pound only a month's worth at a time |
| Container | Glass jar or metal tin 3 years or more, earthenware jar 2-3 years (cover the mouth with paper or cloth, set the lid and seal the rim with clay), underground storage pit 2-3 years or more, thick plastic 1-2 years, straw or burlap sack 6 months to 1 year |

> **Store grain in the husk and mill only what you will eat. The husk is the packaging that nature gave.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;For insect control, completely airtight storage filled to the top is the surest. Also used are wood ash (fine ash at 2-5% of the grain volume), diatomaceous earth (1-3 g per 1 kg of grain), fine sand or yellow clay, dry salt, charcoal, dried chili and Korean pepper, and mugwort and sweet flag leaves. You can also put the grain outside for a few days in midwinter (cold) or spread it thin in the sun for a few hours (heat, 60℃; not for seed). Ash and diatomaceous earth stop working if the grain is damp, so drying comes first. If powder or webbing-like threads appear on the grain surface, it is Indian meal moth, so dry all of it in the sun and sieve it out.

## 9. Cooking and Precautions
| Preparation | Method |
|:---|:---|
| Milling | Put dry grain in a mortar and pound, taking it out every 5-10 minutes to winnow off the bran. Sprinkling a little water and leaving it for 30 minutes before pounding makes the husk come off easily and breaks fewer kernels. With a millstone, raise the upper stone slightly and turn it. A foot-operated treadle mill takes far less effort. Do not throw away the bran |
| Removing tannin | Rub the milled grain in plenty of water and discard the cloudy water, three or four times, then soak for 30 minutes to overnight at the end and discard that water. The astringency almost disappears |
| Flour milling | A millstone gives the finest flour and a mortar a coarse one. Pounding after roasting is much less work. Flour spoils much faster than grain |

| Cooking | Fuel and tips |
|:---|:---|
| Sorghum rice | Soak in water for 30 minutes or more before cooking (unsoaked, the inside stays raw and only fuel is used) |
| Mixed rice | Mix 20-30% into rice or other grain. Little aversion |
| Porridge | Stir flour into water and cook for 10 minutes. Uses the least fuel |
| Baking in ashes | Bury a lump of dough in the hot ashes of a hearth that has finished other cooking |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Processing that also serves as storage includes roasted flour (misutgaru, airtight 6 months), sorghum malt syrup (saccharified with malt and boiled down, in a cool place 1 year), fermented dough (2-3 days, which lowers tannin and improves absorption), sorghum liquor, and puffed grain (airtight 1-2 months). If sweet sorghum was planted too, crush the stalks, press out the juice and boil it down for a long time to get syrup.

| Precaution | Details |
|:---|:---|
| Cyanogenic glycoside (dhurrin) | Almost none in dry grain, which is safe. It is high in young leaves and shoots, leaves that regrew after drought, leaves right after frost (not for 1 week), and young stems that regrew after ratooning (use once past knee height), so do not feed them to people or livestock. Fully grown stalks and leaves are safer dried or made into silage |
| Tannin | Concentrated in the hull and seed coat, higher in reddish-brown types. It causes astringency and hinders protein and iron absorption, though it also has antioxidant action and reduces damage from birds and mold |
| Mycotoxin | A good part remains even above 200℃, so boiling, roasting or steaming cannot remove it. Discard grain that shows mold or smells of it, and put it in the compost pile to break down |

## 10. Annual Work Schedule
| Month | Work |
|:---|:---|
| Jan-Feb | Check stored grain (condensation, moldy smell), check seed and run a germination test, turn the compost, maintain tools |
| Mar-Apr | Collect wood ash, finish the compost, plow, dig drainage ditches and deep furrows, apply basal fertilizer |
| May | Salt water selection, seed disinfection, sowing (from mid month in the central region). Sowing in batches 2 weeks apart spreads out the harvest |
| June | Remaining sowing, first thinning, weeding. Weed control in the first month decides the yield |
| July | Second thinning, top dressing, hilling, heading begins. Always irrigate in a drought |
| August | Flowering and grain filling, scaring birds, watching for disease |
| September | Harvest begins, preliminary drying, hanging to dry. Do not wait for full ripeness if rain is forecast |
| October | Finish harvest, thresh, finishing drying, storage (seal after confirming moisture of 13-14%) |
| Nov-Dec | Select and store seed separately, clear stubble to remove overwintering sites for moths, mill and grind only a month's worth at a time |

> **Five things not to forget: the sunniest spot, furrows and drainage, nitrogen not above the standard amount, drying to 13-14% moisture, and storage in the husk.**
