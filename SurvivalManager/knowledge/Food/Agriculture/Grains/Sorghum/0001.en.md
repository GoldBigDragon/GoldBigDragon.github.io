## Introduction

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Sorghum (Sorghum bicolor) comes from northeastern Africa, the region that is now Ethiopia and Sudan. It is a land of short rainy seasons and long dry seasons, shallow soil poor in nutrients, and midday temperatures that hover around 40°C or higher. The fact that sorghum has fed people in such places for more than five thousand years explains almost everything about its character. When water runs short, it rolls its leaves to reduce transpiration; when water runs shorter still, it stops growing altogether and resumes growing once rain comes. In conditions where any other grain would wither and die on the spot, sorghum waits. It is the fifth most widely grown grain in the world, yet in South Korea it is treated as a minor grain for one reason only: until now, the Korean Peninsula has never been short of water.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Evaluated under disaster conditions, the picture changes. Its overall grade is A, and its long-term self-reliance over many years is rated S. It needs only 250mm of water in total evapotranspiration. That is half or less of what maize needs, and nowhere near what rice requires. Fertilizer is modest too: nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium together come to just 18g per m², a little over half of maize's requirement. Being a grass-family crop, it does not collapse under continuous cropping; on the contrary, substances released from its roots suppress weeds in the following crop. Dried grain keeps for more than 3 years at room temperature. When supply chains are cut, fertilizer plants stop and fields can no longer be irrigated, few grains offer this whole combination.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Its weaknesses are just as clear. First, its calories per area, at 1,309kcal per m², fall short of potato or maize. Covering one person's calories for a year with sorghum alone takes about 200m², or 60 pyeong (1 pyeong = 3.3m²).

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Second, it is extremely sensitive to shade. As a C4 crop, it reaches its peak photosynthetic efficiency in strong light, but in exchange, even 40% shade cuts its yield to less than half. In a disaster in which volcanic ash or large-scale fires darken the sky, it belongs to the group of crops that fail first.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Third, a single frost kills it. Fourth, it takes a lot of work to get it from the field to the table. The grain cannot be eaten in its hull, so it must always be dehulled and polished, and the tannins in the hull and seed coat leave it astringent and even interfere with nutrient absorption unless they are washed out thoroughly.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In short, sorghum is not the crop that saves you in the first year of a disaster but one that proves its worth from the second year on. When potato and maize lose strength in fields without fertilizer, sorghum keeps 45% of its normal-times yield under the same conditions. Depleted soil, reduced water and vanished pesticides are all conditions that sorghum can put up with. This document is written on that premise. In particular, it devotes a great deal of space to how to thresh, pound and grind grain without tools, to drying methods that bring moisture down to 13-14%, and to ways of keeping out, without chemicals, the insects that swarm in storage. Sorghum is a grain that is harder to put away than to grow, and in a disaster, the place where grain is lost is usually not the field but the storehouse.
