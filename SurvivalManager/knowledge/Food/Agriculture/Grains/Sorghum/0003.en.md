## 3. Growing Conditions

### 3-1. Limits and Optimal Values

| Condition | Optimal value | Tolerated range | When outside the range |
|---|---|---|---|
| Growing temperature | 25-30°C | 12-48°C | Growth stops below 12°C, and the plant dies at 0°C |
| Germination temperature | 21-35°C | Minimum 15°C | Below 15°C, germination is delayed and the seeds rot |
| Grain-filling temperature | 20-32°C | Range 12°C | If the heat during grain filling is excessive, the grains do not fill out |
| Light | 8-10 hours of direct sunlight a day | 6 hours or more | At 40% shade, the yield falls to 45% |
| Total water requirement | About 250mm | 150-400mm | Even in extreme drought, it stops growing and endures |
| Soil | Sandy loam to clay loam | Almost any soil texture | In heavy clay where water collects, the roots are damaged |
| Soil acidity | pH 5.5-7.5 | pH 5.0-8.5 | Its range of adaptation to acidity is among the widest of the grains |
| Frost | Must be absent | None | A single frost kills the aboveground parts |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;What stands out in this table is that the tolerated ranges are unusually wide. Few grains can cope with a soil pH of 5.0-8.5 and temperatures of 12-48°C. In a disaster, when there is no luxury of choosing a field, this breadth is itself an asset. Even on land that has turned acidic, on alkaline land rich in lime, or on gravelly slopes, sorghum sets grain to some extent.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Conversely, only two items are narrow: light and frost. As long as these two are secured, the rest is generally flexible.

### 3-2. Choosing a Site in a Disaster

| Site | Suitability | Reason |
|---|:---:|---|
| Gently sloping south-facing land | Best | Long hours of sun, and water does not collect |
| Flat field | Good | Raising the beds solves the drainage problem |
| Poor, gravelly land | Usable | Sorghum sets grain where other crops fail |
| Field converted from a paddy | Caution | The layer that blocks drainage must be broken up and the beds built high |
| Shade of buildings or large trees | Unsuitable | Damage from shading is much greater than for other crops |
| North-facing slopes, valley floors | Unsuitable | Sunshine is short and frost comes early |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;When choosing a spot for sorghum, look at sunlight before water. Sorghum can cope on its own in a spot short of water, but in a spot short of sunlight there is nothing it can do. If good land is limited, giving the sunny places to sorghum and putting the shaded places where water is plentiful to leafy vegetables or C3 cereals will increase the total harvest.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The frost problem is managed through terrain. Cold air is heavy, so it flows down to low places and pools there. Even within the same village, frost falls 1 to 2 weeks earlier on a valley floor than halfway up a slope. In areas where harvest time and frost come uncomfortably close to each other, simply moving the field to somewhat higher ground creates a safety margin.

## 4. Varieties

### 4-1. Glutinous and Non-Glutinous Sorghum

| Type | Endosperm starch | Texture | Main uses | Disaster viewpoint |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Glutinous sorghum | Amylopectin 90-100% | Sticky and soft | Honban (mixed-grain rice), tteok (rice cake), alcoholic drinks | Mixes well into rice, so people object less |
| Non-glutinous sorghum | Amylose 22-24% | Dry and crumbly | Cooked grain (rice-style), porridge, flour | Better suited to grinding into flour |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Most varieties distributed in South Korea belong to the glutinous line. This is because Korean food culture has been built around honban and tteok. In a disaster, too, this choice remains largely valid. Glutinous sorghum cooks well with little water, which saves fuel, and when it is cooked together with rice or other grains, it feels less out of place.

### 4-2. Comparison of Major Varieties

| Variety | Height | Yield (kg/1,000m²) | Characteristics |
|---|:---:|:---:|---|
| Miryang 15 | Medium | 484 | Highest yield in South Korea. A plant type suited to machine harvesting |
| Goeunchal | About 100cm | 374 | Resistant to lodging and advantageous for avoiding grain mold |
| Sodamchal | 91-117cm | 296 | Reddish-brown grain. High antioxidant activity and resistant to lodging |
| Jungmochalsusu | 88cm | - | The shortest dwarf variety. White grain. Little wind damage |
| Nampungchal (Miryang 4) | Medium | - | Mid-maturing glutinous sorghum. Resistant to lodging and to weather damage, and high-yielding |

### 4-3. Selection Criteria by Situation

| Situation | Recommendation | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Maximum yield | Miryang 15 | At 484kg per 1,000m², the highest level in South Korea |
| When labor is short | Sodamchal, Goeunchal | They do not lodge, so no stakes or tying are needed |
| Windy areas | Jungmochalsusu, Sodamchal | Being short, they have a fundamentally low risk of lodging |
| Areas with early frost | Early-maturing lines | Finishing grain filling before frost comes first |
| When securing seed is the top priority | Landraces | The line can be continued by saving your own seed |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The longer a disaster lasts, the more a criterion other than yield comes to matter: whether you can save your own seed. Sorghum is mainly self-pollinating, so seed saving is easy, and whether it is a landrace or a bred variety, the same traits mostly come true the following year. With an outcrossing rate of 5-10%, it is not perfect, but unlike maize it does not break down in a single generation. As long as several varieties are not planted side by side in one field, the line holds for a few years.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Height also deserves a second thought. In normal times, tall varieties produce a lot of stem and leaf and so have a high fodder value, but where there is no one to put up stakes and tie the plants, a single lodging event can take away the entire harvest. In areas that typhoons pass through, choosing a dwarf variety, even at the cost of a little yield, turns out better in the end.

## 5. Field Preparation and Fertilizer

### 5-1. Preparing the Soil

| Item | Details | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Plowing depth | 20-30cm | Opens up a layer for the deep roots to spread into |
| Drainage ditches | Always around the field and in the furrows | Root rot from excess moisture is the main cause of failure in sorghum |
| Raised beds | 15-20cm high | Keep the roots from being submerged during the monsoon season (jangma, late June to July in South Korea) |
| Organic matter | Compost 1,000-1,500kg/1,000m² | Supplies water-holding capacity and nutrients together |
| Seedbed preparation | Break up clods and level evenly | The seeds are small, and if they fall between clods they do not germinate |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Sorghum is as weak against excess moisture as it is strong against drought. It knows how to wait out a drought, but roots under water cannot breathe and are damaged within a few days. In a climate like South Korea's, where summer rain comes in concentrated bursts, success or failure in sorghum farming is decided by the drainage ditches. Half a day spent digging furrows with a single shovel protects the entire harvest.

### 5-2. Standard Fertilizer Rates (per 1,000m²)

| Fertilizer | Rate | Timing |
|---|:---:|---|
| Nitrogen (N) | 10-15kg | 50% as base fertilizer, 50% as top-dressing |
| Phosphorus (P) | 5kg | All as base fertilizer |
| Potassium (K) | 4.5kg | All as base fertilizer |
| Compost | 1,000-1,500kg | All as base fertilizer |

| Type | Timing | Details |
|---|---|---|
| Base fertilizer | 1-2 weeks before sowing | All the compost, all the phosphorus and potassium, half the nitrogen |
| Top-dressing | 5-10 leaf stage | The other half of the nitrogen. Done together with thinning and hilling |

> **If extra nitrogen is given to sorghum, it only grows taller and lodges. Even when fertilizer is plentiful, it is better for the harvest not to exceed the standard rate.**

### 5-3. When There Is No Chemical Fertilizer - Disaster Substitutes

| Substitute resource | Nutrients supplied | How to make and use it | Cautions |
|---|---|---|---|
| Wood ash | Potassium, phosphorus, calcium | Ash from broadleaf trees is better. Scatter 20-40kg per 1,000m² and rake it in | It is alkaline, so overuse raises the soil pH and locks up trace elements |
| Well-rotted compost | Nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, organic matter | Pile fallen leaves, grass and manure in layers and let them rot for 3-6 months, turning the pile | Compost that has not rotted enough burns roots and attracts pests |
| Diluted urine | Nitrogen, potassium | Dilute it 10-fold with water and pour it into the furrows between plants | It burns leaves if it touches them directly. Stop 3 weeks before harvest |
| Bone meal | Phosphorus, calcium | Burn bones, crush them, mix into compost and leave for two months | If scattered as it is, it breaks down slowly and the effect is delayed |
| Legume green manure | Nitrogen | Plant soybean or Chinese milk vetch as the preceding crop and plow it under when it flowers | Sow only 2-3 weeks after plowing it under |
| Livestock bedding | Comprehensive | Pile bedding of rice straw mixed with manure at the edge of the field and let it rot | Avoid material with a lot of salt |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;It is worth remembering the figure of 45% for sorghum's zero-input yield retention. Even with no fertilizer at all, you still harvest nearly half of what you would in normal times. Compared with potato, which collapses to 18% under the same conditions, this is a big difference. In other words, sorghum does better with fertilizer, but it is not a crop to give up on for lack of it.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Among the substitute fertilizers, the most realistic is wood ash. In a disaster, burning wood for cooking and heating keeps producing ash, and that ash contains 5-10% potassium and 1-3% phosphorus. Potassium is what sorghum demands most during grain filling, so it is a good match. However, ash is alkaline, and if it is spread continuously the soil pH rises. It helps fields that were originally acidic, but for fields close to neutral, limit it to about once every 2 years.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Nitrogen is the hardest to come by. Urine is the most immediate nitrogen source, and the urine one adult passes in a day contains 6-10g of nitrogen. Diluted 10-fold and poured into the furrows, it serves as top-dressing. People shrink from it because of the smell and hygiene, but the urea that soaks into the soil breaks down within a few days and does not persist. It is not, however, used on crops close to harvest.

## 6. Sowing

### 6-1. Timing

| Region | Best sowing time | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Central South Korea | Mid-May to early June | The most stable window |
| Southern South Korea | Early to mid-May | Sow early so the plants build vigor before the monsoon (jangma) |
| Succeeding crop after barley or wheat | Mid to late June in South Korea | Sown in succession on the spot where barley or wheat was cut |
| Sowing deadline | Late June in South Korea | Any later and the grain-filling stage is caught by frost |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There are two criteria for deciding when to sow: whether the soil temperature has passed 15°C, and whether 150 days remain until the first frost. The first condition determines germination, the second grain filling. The window that satisfies both is, in South Korea, from mid-May to late June.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In a disaster, it is better not to plant everything at once but to split the sowing at 2-week intervals. The harvest is spread out so that labor does not pile up, and it also prevents a typhoon or disease outbreak at one moment from sweeping away the whole crop. The fewer tools and people you have, the more this spreading matters.

### 6-2. Seed Selection - Salt-Water Sorting

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;A single head of sorghum carries 1,000-3,000 grains. Among them, plump grains are mixed with shriveled, empty ones. If the empty ones are sown as they are, they do not germinate, or if they do come up, they are weak and are crowded out by weeds. Salt-water sorting, which separates them with salt water, is the most effective selection method that can be done without tools.

| Step | Details | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Dissolve 51g of salt in 1L of water | Specific gravity 1.035 |
| 2 | Add the seeds and stir well by hand | Removes air stuck to the surface |
| 3 | Skim off what floats and take out only what sinks | What sinks are the sound seeds |
| 4 | Rinse two or three times in clean water to remove the salt | If salt remains, germination suffers |
| 5 | Spread in the shade and dry completely | Drying in the sun damages the embryo |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;If you have no scale to weigh the salt, estimate with a raw egg. When a raw egg placed in the salt water shows a patch above the surface about the size of a 500-won coin (a South Korean coin about 26.5mm across), the specific gravity is roughly 1.03-1.04. It is a method used since long ago, and it is still accurate enough today.

### 6-3. Seed Disinfection - When No Chemicals Are Available

| Method | Procedure | Effect | Caution |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hot-water treatment | Soak in water at 50-55°C for 10-15 minutes, take out and cool in cold water | Kills fungi on the seed surface and just beneath it, such as those causing covered smut | Above 60°C the embryo dies. Holding the temperature steady is the key |
| Wood-ash dusting | Coat dried seeds with fine ash | Makes the surface dry and alkaline, suppressing fungi and insects | Coarse ash clogs the seeder |
| Wood-vinegar soak | Soak for 6-12 hours in a solution diluted 500-fold | Sterilizes the surface and promotes germination | Using the undiluted liquid kills the seeds |
| Sun treatment | Spread in the sun for 2-3 hours, 2-3 days before sowing | Drives off surface moisture and reduces stray microbes | Do not leave them long in the scorching midsummer sun |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Without a thermometer, hot-water treatment is risky. If the water is too hot, the seeds die; if it is lukewarm, the fungi survive. Water that feels hot when you put your hand in but can still be borne for 3 seconds is roughly around 50°C. Mixing boiling water and cold water in a ratio of 2 to 3 gives a similar temperature. If you are not sure, it is safer to use wood-ash dusting instead.

### 6-4. Planting

| Item | Standard | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Seeding rate | 1-2kg per 1,000m² | The seeds are small, so a small amount is enough |
| Sowing depth | 2-5cm | If too shallow they dry out, and if too deep they cannot emerge |
| Single-row planting | Row spacing 60cm, hill spacing 20cm, 7-8 seeds per hill | The most common layout |
| Double-row planting | Ridge width 110cm, row spacing 30cm, hill spacing 15cm | High area efficiency |
| Transplanted cultivation | 2-3 seeds per cell in 128-cell trays, seedlings raised for 15-20 days | Used when the preceding crop finishes late |
| After covering with soil | Press down with your feet or a wooden board | Brings seed and soil into close contact and raises the germination rate greatly |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The last step, firming (that is, covering the seeds and pressing the soil down), is especially effective when drought is expected. If there are gaps between soil and seed, the moisture in the soil cannot reach the seed. Pressing the soil down lets water from the lower layer rise to the seed by capillary action. When sowing in a spring without rain, this one step makes a big difference to the germination rate.
