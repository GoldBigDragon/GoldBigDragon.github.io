## 7. Water Management

### 7-1. Criteria by Growth Stage

| Stage | Management | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Sowing to germination | Keep the soil from drying out | Germination absolutely requires moisture |
| Seedling stage | No special irrigation is needed | This encourages the roots to go deep |
| Vegetative growth stage | Leave it to natural rainfall | The plant does not die even after a month without water |
| Heading to flowering | Always irrigate if it turns dry | These two weeks determine the yield |
| Grain-filling stage | Reduce gradually | This is when starch fills the grain |
| Maturity stage | Cut off irrigation completely | Too much water makes drying after harvest difficult |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;When sorghum is called drought tolerant, that description refers to an average over the whole growing period. Stage by stage, the picture is different. Sorghum in the seedling and vegetative stages holds out astonishingly well, but from just before the heads emerge until the flowers fade it is no different from other cereals. If the soil dries out completely during this period, heads emerge but no grain sets, leaving empty heads, and that loss cannot be recovered no matter how much water is applied later.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;When water is extremely limited, this principle becomes an allocation strategy. Rather than spreading the little water you have across the whole growing period, you get a bigger harvest by enduring completely at first and giving it all at once just before heading. Sorghum knows how to endure, and exploiting that ability is the core of water-saving cultivation.

### 7-2. Symptoms of Too Little and Too Much Water

| Symptom | Cause | Assessment and action |
|---|---|---|
| Leaves curl inward at midday | Temporary water shortage | If they open again in the evening, this is normal. It is sorghum's normal defense response |
| Leaves are still curled in the morning | Real drought | Irrigation is needed at this stage |
| Leaves turn yellow and dry out from the bottom upward | Prolonged drought | The plant is sacrificing its lower leaves in order to survive |
| Heads have emerged but the grain is empty | Water shortage at heading and flowering | Cannot be recovered. Change the irrigation plan for next year |
| Leaves turn pale yellow and the roots are dark | Waterlogging, lack of oxygen | Dig a drainage channel immediately |
| The base turns soft and the plants fall over | Root rot from flooding | Recovery is difficult. Drainage must be fixed at its root |

> **Leaves curled at midday are not a disease. Judge by whether they are still curled in the morning.**

## 8. Crop Management

### 8-1. Thinning

| Round | Timing | Task |
|---|---|---|
| 1st | 3-4 true leaves | Pull out the weak plants, securing space between plants |
| 2nd | 5-10 leaf stage | Leave only the 1-2 sturdiest plants |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;If thinning is put off, plants compete with one another for light and all of them become spindly. Spindly stalks are blown over by the wind. Because sorghum is graded E for sunlight (relative yield at 40% shade), even the shade between plants has a large effect on yield. When choosing which plants to keep, pick not the tallest ones but those with a thick base and dark leaf color.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Do not throw away the young sorghum you pull out. The tender leaves and stems can be used as livestock feed, and if there are no animals, they go onto the compost pile. However, the leaves of young sorghum contain relatively large amounts of the cyanogenic glycoside described later, so they should not be fed to livestock in large quantities.

### 8-2. Weeding and Hilling

| Task | Timing | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Weeding | Early in growth, when weeds are as long as a finger | Sorghum grows slowly at first and is easily outcompeted by weeds |
| Hilling | At the same time as thinning, and once more after that | Soil piled around the base encourages new roots and prevents lodging |
| Final weeding | When the plants have grown to knee height | After that, sorghum smothers the weeds |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In sorghum cultivation, weeds are the largest cause of yield loss. Of the total potential loss without pest control, weeds account for a larger share than pests and diseases. Yet the period when the crop is vulnerable to weeds is short: the 30-45 days after sowing, until the sorghum reaches a person's knee. Once it gets past that critical point, sorghum suppresses weeds with its height, and a substance called sorgoleone released from its roots inhibits the germination of weeds nearby. In the end, weeding in sorghum farming is concentrated in the first month, and if you are diligent for that one month, sorghum takes care of the rest.

### 8-3. Lodging Management and Other Tasks

| Item | Details |
|---|---|
| Preventing lodging | Do not apply more than the standard amount of nitrogen, hill thoroughly, and choose short-stalked varieties |
| After lodging | Do not try to stand them all up at once; gather three or four plants at a time, tie them with string, and prop them up |
| Ratooning | Cut leaving 10-15cm of the base and the plant regrows, allowing a second harvest |
| Bird damage | From the time the heads begin to ripen, hang nets, old clothes, or shiny scraps |
| Side branches | Removing them does not increase yield, so leave them as they are |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Ratooning, that is, regrowth cultivation, is especially valuable in a disaster. If you cut the plants at the first harvest leaving the base, new stalks rise from the stubble and produce heads once more. The second harvest is about half the size of the first, but it comes without any seed or tillage. It is possible, however, only in southern South Korea, where grain filling can be completed before frost, and only when the first harvest is finished within August.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Bird damage is easy to underestimate. Sorghum heads stand exposed, so sparrows and magpies flock to them. If you cannot guard the crop during the three weeks of ripening, as much as a third of the harvest can disappear. Without nets, two methods work: covering the heads with paper bags or old cloth, and stretching several lines of string over the field so that birds cannot land.

## 9. Pests and Diseases

### 9-1. Major Diseases

| Disease | Conditions | Symptoms | Response without chemicals |
|---|---|---|---|
| Grain mold (head mold) | Frequent rain during grain filling | Heads turn black or pink and mold grows on them | Early harvest, immediate drying, discarding diseased heads. Choose early-maturing varieties |
| Covered smut | Seed-borne | The place of the grain turns into black powder | Salt-water selection of seed and dusting with wood ash, hot-water seed treatment. Burn diseased heads |
| Anthracnose | Hot, humid weather | Reddish-brown lesions form on leaves and stalks | Ensure ventilation, rotate crops, remove diseased leaves. Spray wood vinegar |
| Sheath blight | Dense planting and high humidity | Leaf sheaths soften and wither | Keep proper plant spacing, improve drainage |
| Stalk rot | Excess moisture after drought during grain filling | The inside of the stalk becomes hollow and plants fall over | Avoid excess nitrogen and reduce sudden swings in moisture |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Of these, the most dangerous in a disaster is grain mold. The reason is not yield loss but toxicity. Some of the molds that grow on the heads produce mycotoxins, and these toxins are not destroyed by heating. If you eat moldy sorghum because you cannot bear to waste it, the result is vomiting and diarrhea in the acute case and liver damage in the chronic case. The hungrier you are, the easier it is for this judgment to become clouded, so it needs to be settled in advance.

> **Moldy grain does not become safe by washing, boiling, or roasting. Throwing it away is the only option.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Prevention depends entirely on drying. If rain continues in the later part of grain filling, mold has already begun on the heads. In that case, do not wait for full ripeness; it is better to cut the crop a little early, bring it in, and dry it indoors. Grain that is slightly smaller because it was harvested a few days early can be tolerated, but moldy grain is a total loss.

### 9-2. Major Pests

| Pest | Timing | Damage | Response without chemicals |
|---|---|---|---|
| Grass aphid (gijang-teduri aphid) | Throughout the growing season | Sucks sap from the underside of leaves and invites sooty mold | Spray soapy water, protect ladybugs and lacewings, avoid excess nitrogen |
| Asian corn borer and other moths | July-September | Larvae bore into the stalks and eat the heads | Remove and burn damaged stalks, deal with the stubble after harvest |
| Stink bugs | July-September | Suck the developing grain, turning it into empty husks | Hand-pick in the early morning, remove weeds nearby |
| Oriental armyworm | Sudden outbreaks | Eats the leaves in no time | Pick by hand as soon as an outbreak is confirmed; harvest early if severe |
| Birds | Grain filling | Eat up the grain | Nets, covering the heads, stringing lines over the field |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Sorghum is rated A for pest-control self-reliance because there are still measures to take even without chemicals. Birds, the largest source of loss, can be stopped with physical barriers, and for moths, simply dealing with the stubble and crop residue after harvest greatly reduces their numbers the following year. Aphids generally find a balance once natural enemies become established. In that no disease absolutely requires a curative treatment, sorghum is in a different position from potatoes or tomatoes.

### 9-3. Natural Pest-Control Preparations

| Material | How to make it | Target | Caution |
|---|---|---|---|
| Soapy water | Dissolve 5-10g of pure soap in 1L of water | Aphids, spider mites | Spraying at midday scorches the leaves. Spray in the morning or evening |
| Wood ash | Dust onto the leaves on a dewy morning | Young larvae, slugs | Rain washes it off, so it has to be applied again |
| Wood vinegar | Dilute 500-1,000 times and spray | Preventing fungal diseases; repellent | A strong concentration injures the plants |
| Tobacco leaf and chili pepper infusion | Chop finely, soak for a day, then strain and use | Moth larvae, aphids | It also irritates people, so protective gear is needed |
| Bordeaux mixture | Made by dissolving copper sulfate and quicklime in water | Preventing fungal diseases | Possible only when the materials can be obtained |

### 9-4. Five Principles of Control Without Chemicals

| Principle | Details | Application to sorghum |
|---|---|---|
| Healthy seed | Do not save seed from diseased plants | Covered smut is spread entirely by seed |
| Crop rotation | Do not plant the same family in succession | Sorghum tolerates continuous cropping, but disease levels build up |
| Ventilation | Keep to the planting spacing | Dense planting invites sheath blight and anthracnose |
| Sanitation | Clear diseased residue from the field | Moth larvae overwinter in the stubble |
| Avoidance | Sow and harvest so as to avoid the period when disease is circulating | Grain mold is avoided by early harvest |

## 10. Nutrient Deficiency and Excess

| Symptom | Cause | Remedy with natural materials |
|---|---|---|
| Leaves turn yellow all over starting from the bottom, and plants stay short | Nitrogen deficiency | Well-rotted compost as top-dressing; urine diluted 10 times applied in the furrows |
| Leaf edges turn brown as if scorched | Potassium deficiency | Scatter wood ash between the plants |
| Growth is slow overall and the leaves are dark bluish green | Phosphorus deficiency | Mix bone meal into the compost |
| Only the tissue between the veins is yellow while the veins stay green | Magnesium deficiency | Dolomite powder; diversify the compost materials |
| New leaves turn pale and whitish | Iron deficiency, usually from excess alkalinity | Stop applying wood ash and add leaf mold |
| Plants grow tall with dark leaves and fall over | Nitrogen excess | Stop supplying nitrogen and support the plants by hilling |
| Heads have emerged but have few grains | Water shortage at flowering, or phosphorus shortage | Adjust next year's irrigation timing and base fertilizer |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The most common problem in sorghum is not deficiency but nitrogen excess. When compost and urine become plentiful in a disaster, there is a temptation to apply too much, and sorghum converts it into height. Tall sorghum falls over in wind and rain, and fallen heads touch the ground and rot. If fertilizer is left over, diverting it to maize or leafy vegetables rather than sorghum increases the overall harvest.

## 11. Intercropping and Crop Rotation

### 11-1. Compatibility

| Compatibility | Companion | Effect |
|:---:|---|---|
| Very good | Soybean, adzuki bean, cowpea | Root-nodule bacteria fix nitrogen and share it with the sorghum |
| Good | Peanut | Covers the ground, reducing moisture evaporation and weeds |
| Good | Squash | Broad leaves cover the space between the ridges, reducing weeding |
| Good | Potato | Growth periods are offset, so there is little competition |
| Good as a following crop | Barley, wheat | They use a field where sorghum's allelopathic compounds have reduced the weeds |
| Poor | Maize, foxtail millet, proso millet | As members of the same grass family, they share pests and diseases and compete heavily for nutrients |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The combination of sorghum and a legume is the basic pattern of disaster farming. Sorghum supplies carbohydrate and beans supply protein rich in lysine, so the two complement each other's amino acid composition. They help each other in the field as well. Some of the nitrogen fixed by the beans flows through the soil to the sorghum, and the tall sorghum stalks serve as stakes for climbing beans. It is an arrangement that yields calories and protein from the same field at the same time while drawing down soil fertility less.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;When sorghum and beans are mixed in the same planting, however, the spacing must be generous. Remember that sorghum's sunlight grade is E. If the beans shade the sorghum, the loss on the sorghum side outweighs the gain from the beans. A layout of about two rows of sorghum to one row of beans, in which the sorghum gets first claim on the sun, is appropriate.

### 11-2. Rotation Sequence

| Year | Crop | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| Year 1 | Sorghum | Suppresses weeds and draws up nutrients from deep layers |
| Year 2 | Legumes (soybean, adzuki bean, cowpea) | Restore nitrogen |
| Year 3 | Leafy vegetables or root vegetables | Use the leftover nutrients in the shallow layer |
| Year 4 | Return to sorghum | Disease levels have dropped sufficiently |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Sorghum tolerates repeated planting so well that it is rated SS for continuous-cropping tolerance. If land is too limited to allow rotation, planting it for two or three years in a row causes no major problem. Rotation is still recommended, not because of yield but because of pest and disease levels. Covered smut spores and moth larvae accumulate a little each year and start to become noticeable around the third year.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;It is also worth knowing that crops following sorghum grow particularly well. This is because sorgoleone released from sorghum roots remains in the soil and suppresses the germination of weed seeds. The effect lasts for several months after the sorghum is cut. Where herbicides cannot be used, planting sorghum for a year in a heavily weedy field to clear it is also a strategy.
