## 12. Harvest and Threshing

### 12-1. Judging the Right Time

| Criterion | Details | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| Days | 120-150 days after sowing | Varies with the variety and that year's temperatures |
| Head color | The whole head deepens to the variety's characteristic color | Maturity is near |
| Grain hardness | Pressing with a fingernail leaves no mark | Fully ripe |
| Grain cross-section | Split in half, the inside is white and hard | Grain filling is complete |
| Black layer | A black spot forms at the base of the grain | A sure sign of physiological maturity |
| Leaf condition | The lower leaves dry out and the stalk turns yellow | Harvest time |
| Weather | After 2-3 clear days in a row | Moisture is low, so drying is easy |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Grain filling proceeds through the milk, dough, hard-dough (yellow-ripe), and full-ripe stages, in that order. At the milk stage, pressing a grain releases a milky liquid; at the dough stage the grain is in a doughy state; and by the hard-dough stage only a firm press with a fingernail leaves a mark. The best time to harvest is from the end of the hard-dough stage to the beginning of full ripeness. If you wait longer, grains drop, bird damage increases, and the risk of grain mold grows.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The black layer at the base of the grain is the surest sign. When the grain stops receiving nutrients from the stalk, a layer of black cells forms at the point of attachment. Once it is visible, the grain will not grow any larger even if it is left in the field. All that remains is drying, and that drying is safer under the eaves than in the field.

> **If rain continues during grain filling, do not wait for full ripeness; cut the crop and bring it in. Losing a few percent of the grain is better than losing all of it to grain mold.**

### 12-2. Harvesting Tips

| Step | Details | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Start on a clear morning, after the dew has dried | Wet heads are where mold begins |
| 2 | Cut with a sickle 20-30cm below the head | Leaving a length of stalk as a handle makes it easier to tie and hang the bundles |
| 3 | In a field to be ratooned, leave 10-15cm of the base | New stalks rise from the stubble |
| 4 | Sort the cut heads into diseased and sound ones on the spot | If they are left mixed, mold spreads |
| 5 | Tie six or seven heads into one bundle | If the bundle is large, the inside does not dry |
| 6 | Hang the bundles upside down in a place sheltered from rain | Hanging them so that air can circulate determines how well they dry |

### 12-3. Drying the Grain - Down to 13-14% Moisture

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Everything about grain storage is decided by moisture. Above 15% moisture, mold grows; below 13%, both mold and insects almost stop being active. The fate of a whole year's farming hangs on these two or three percentage points. Freshly cut sorghum has a moisture content of 20-30%. Bringing this down to 13-14% is the one and most important task right after harvest.

| Step | Method | Duration | Moisture reached |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Pre-drying | Stand the cut bundles upright in the field or yard | 2-3 days | About 20% |
| 2. Hang-drying | Hang them upside down under the eaves or in a shed | 1-2 weeks | 15-16% |
| 3. Final drying | Spread on a meongseok (straw drying mat) or cloth and dry in the sun | 2-4 days | 13-14% |
| 4. Tempering | Leave in the shade for a day to even out the moisture | 1 day | Evened out |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There are a few rules to follow when drying in the sun. First, do not spread the grain directly on the ground. Moisture rising from the soil rewets the grain at the bottom. Lay down a layer of something, whether a meongseok, cloth, or boards. Second, do not pile the grain in a thick layer. Keep it to no more than two finger joints, 3-5cm, and turn it three or four times a day with a rake or by hand. Third, when the sun goes down, gather the grain in or cover it. If it is exposed to the night dew, the moisture removed during the day returns. Fourth, do not dry in a hurry under the scorching midsummer sun. Only the outside dries, moisture is trapped inside, and mold appears later.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There are ways to estimate moisture when you have no moisture meter. They require no tools and become fairly accurate with practice.

| Method | Procedure | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| Biting | Bite a grain with a molar | If it cracks with a snap and produces powder, it is at 13-14%. If it squashes or deforms, it is still wet |
| Salt test | Put a spoonful of dry salt and a handful of grain in a glass jar, shake, and leave for 15 minutes | If the salt sticks to the wall of the jar or clumps, moisture is high |
| Feel by hand | Squeeze a handful and open the hand | If the grain slides apart, it is dry. If it clumps, it is wet |
| Sound | Cup the grain in both hands and shake | A sound like pebbles knocking together means dry; a dull sound means wet |
| Jar test | Fill a glass jar halfway, seal it, and leave it for a day | If the inside of the jar fogs up, the grain still needs drying |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The jar test is especially reliable. In a sealed jar, the grain and the air come to moisture equilibrium, and if the grain's moisture is above 14%, the humidity in the jar exceeds 70% and condensation forms at dawn when the temperature drops. It is well suited as a final check before the grain goes into storage. In autumn, with its cool nights, it shows up even better.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Some years the rain keeps coming and the sun cannot be used. In that case, use fire. Spread the grain thinly in a loft above a hearth or stove, or on the far, cooler end of an ondol (floor-heating) floor, and it dries over several days. The temperature must not exceed 50°C, however. Above that, the grain can no longer be used as seed, and the starch changes so that the cooked grain tastes worse. If it feels hot to the touch, it is already too hot. Warm is about right.

### 12-4. Threshing - Methods Without Tools

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Sorghum is on the easier side to thresh compared with rice or barley. The grains sit loosely on the head, and once it is dry enough they fall off with only a light impact. The key is how dry the heads are. Heads that are not dry enough will not release their grain no matter how much you beat them; the grain only gets crushed. Before threshing, the heads must be dry enough to rustle.

| Method | Procedure | Scale | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Stripping by hand | Hold the stalk of the head and strip downward with a gloved hand | Small amounts | Least loss. Used for seed |
| Beating | Spread the heads on a meongseok and beat them with a stick or flail | Medium | The most common |
| Striking in a sack | Put the heads in a sturdy sack and strike the whole sack against the ground | Medium | The grain does not bounce and scatter |
| Treading | Spread on a meongseok and tread, rubbing with the feet | Large amounts | Takes less effort. The soles of the shoes must be clean |
| Rubbing on a washboard | Rub the heads against a grooved board | Small amounts | A corrugated board or a wooden board with grooves cut into it will do instead |
| Rubbing in a tub | Put the heads in a large tub and rub them, stirring with a stick | Medium | Easier on the hands |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;A flail can be made by hand. Simply hang a short stick loosely from the end of a long handle with a leather strap or tough cord. When the handle is swung, the stick at the end whirls around and strikes the grain. It is three to four times as efficient as beating with arm strength alone, and tires you less even when used all day. It is one of the first farm tools to make in a disaster.

### 12-5. Blowing Away the Chaff - Winnowing Fan and Basket

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The threshed grain is mixed with bits of head, broken kernels, and dust. If it is stored as it is, the moisture clinging to the chaff and the insect eggs will ruin the entire store. Separating these out with wind is essential.

| Method | Procedure | Conditions |
|---|---|---|
| Pouring in the wind | On a windy day, pour the grain slowly from a height. The heavy grain falls straight down, and the chaff blows far away | A steady wind of 2-4m per second |
| Winnowing with a basket | Put the grain in a winnowing basket, toss it upward and catch it. Repeated, the lighter material is pushed out to the front | No wind is needed |
| Fanning | One person pours slowly while another makes wind with a fan or a plank | Two people are needed |
| Sieving | Use a coarse sieve to remove the chaff and a fine sieve to remove dust and small grains | Two kinds of sieve are needed |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;A winnowing basket is a tool you can make yourself in a disaster. One woven from split bamboo or bush clover twigs works, as does one made by standing thin plywood up on three sides. The knack of winnowing is not to shake the basket up and down but to push it forward and pull it back, briefly lifting the grain into the air. While it hangs in the air, the lighter material lags behind and gathers at the front. Half a day is enough to learn, and once learned it is a skill for life.

## 13. Seed Saving

### 13-1. Selecting Plants for Seed

| Criterion | Details | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Timing of selection | Walk the field during grain filling and mark plants in advance | After harvest, you cannot tell which plant a head came from |
| Plant condition | No disease symptoms, not lodged, with well-filled heads | The traits pass on to the next generation |
| Head position | The thickest, most uniform main head on the plant | Side heads ripen unevenly |
| Harvest timing | Leave them a little longer than the general harvest so they ripen fully | Immature seed has a lower germination rate |
| Exclude | Plants that showed covered smut | It is transmitted through seed |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Sorghum is basically self-pollinating, but it has an outcrossing rate of 5-10%. That is, of 100 grains, five to ten received pollen from a neighboring plant. This is no problem if you plant only one variety, but if waxy (glutinous) and non-waxy sorghum are planted side by side, their characteristics get mixed within a few years. To keep a variety pure, plant at least 200m apart, or stagger the planting so that flowering differs by 2 weeks or more.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;If the field is small and isolation is realistically difficult, there is another way: before flowering, cover the heads of the plants chosen for seed with paper bags or loosely woven cloth. Since sorghum is basically self-pollinating, it sets seed normally even inside a bag. Managing just two or three heads this way is enough to keep a pure seed line for the following year.

### 13-2. Seed Storage

| Condition | Standard | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Moisture | 12-13%. Slightly drier than food grain | The lower the moisture, the longer the seed lives |
| Temperature | Low and constant. 10°C or below is ideal | At high temperatures the embryo is used up by respiration |
| Container | Airtight containers such as glass jars, metal tins, or earthenware jars | They keep out moisture and insects at the same time |
| Light | Block it out completely | Light and heat reduce germination ability |
| Form | Store in the husk | Milled grain has a much shorter life |
| Longevity | 2-3 years sealed at room temperature | With good conditions, seed can still germinate after 4-5 years |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Seed must always be kept separate from food grain. As winter deepens and food runs short, hands reach for the seed, and once it is touched there is no farming next year. Keeping it in a physically different place and a different container, and labeling the outside, is better than trusting human judgment. The seed needed is 1-2kg per 1,000m², so setting aside about twice the actual requirement is enough.

### 13-3. Germination Test

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Before planting old seed, check its germination ability. Wrap twenty grains in a damp cloth, put them in a warm place, and wait five days. Count how many have sprouted: if sixteen or more of the twenty did, use the seed as it is; if twelve to fifteen did, increase the sowing rate; and if fewer than ten did, you must find new seed. This one simple test prevents losing a whole year's farming.

## 14. Toxicity and Precautions

### 14-1. Cyanogenic Glycoside - Leaves and Young Stalks

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Sorghum has one toxicity you need to know about. The problem lies not in the grain but in the leaves and young stalks. Sorghum leaves contain a cyanogenic glycoside called dhurrin, and when the plant tissue is damaged, an enzyme acts on it and hydrocyanic acid (hydrogen cyanide) is produced.

| Condition | Risk | Reason | Action |
|---|:---:|---|---|
| Dry grain | None | The grain contains almost no dhurrin | Safe to eat |
| Young leaves and shoots | High | The period of highest dhurrin concentration | Do not feed to people or livestock |
| Leaves regrown after drought | Very high | Concentration surges under stress | Do not graze livestock |
| Leaves just after a frost | Very high | Cells are destroyed and cyanide is released | Do not feed for one week after frost |
| Young stalks regrown from ratooning | High | They are young tissue | Use only after they pass knee height |
| Fully grown stalks and leaves | Low | The concentration drops sharply | Safer if dried or made into silage |

> **Sorghum grain eaten by people is safe. What is dangerous is the young leaves and stalks, and the main victims are not people but grazing livestock.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In a disaster there are moments when this knowledge is needed: when food is short and you are tempted to try eating young sorghum shoots as namul (seasoned vegetable side dish), and when you want to feed sorghum leaves to livestock. The former should not be done, and the latter requires choosing the timing. Cyanide poisoning progresses quickly, and a cow can die within a few hours. However, once the leaves are completely dried, the dhurrin breaks down and the danger drops greatly. Feeding them as hay is safe.

### 14-2. Tannins

| Item | Details |
|---|---|
| Location | Concentrated in the hull and seed coat |
| Variety differences | More in reddish-brown types and less in white types |
| Problem | Causes astringency and interferes with the absorption of protein and iron |
| Benefit | Acts as an antioxidant and reduces damage from birds and mold |
| Removal | Remove the hull by milling, then wash several times, changing the water |
| Further removal | Soak overnight and discard the water. Fermentation also helps |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Tannins are less a poison than an antinutrient. Small amounts cause no problem, but the story is different when sorghum is the staple and 500g or more is eaten every day. It is a grain rich in iron, yet its tannins obstruct the absorption of that very iron. That is why thorough milling and washing are not merely a matter of texture.

### 14-3. Mold

| Hazard | Risk | Action |
|---|:---:|---|
| Normal dry grain | None | Safe |
| Heads with mold growing on them | Very high | Discard all of it. Do not give it to livestock either |
| Grain that has turned damp and smelly in storage | Very high | Discard. The toxins remain even after washing or roasting |
| Grain that has changed color and clumped together | High | Remove that part together with a generous margin around it |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Mycotoxins are extremely heat-resistant, and a considerable part remains even at 200°C or above. Boiling, roasting, and steaming cannot remove them. For grain with visible mold or a moldy smell, the only defense is not to put it in a human mouth, however much it hurts to waste it. It is not entirely useless, though: put on the compost pile, it breaks down and becomes fertilizer for the following year.
