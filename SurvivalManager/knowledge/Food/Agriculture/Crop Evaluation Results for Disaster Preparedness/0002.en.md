### 1-2. What Each Column Means

| Column | Meaning |
|:---|:---|
| **Short-term** | 0-1 year. How much food you get this year from planting now. |
| **Long-term** | More than 1 year. Whether you can keep your own seed, maintain soil fertility, and sustain production for several years. |
| **Supplementary** | Usefulness as a supplement that adds nutrition and palatability rather than serving as a staple. **Not added into the overall score.** |
| **Overall** | √(short-term × long-term) × productivity coefficient K × corroboration coefficient |
| **Corroboration coefficient** | The closer to 1.000, the stronger the evidence. A lower value means the score has already been discounted. |
| **Yield loss** | The evidence tier behind the pest/disease yield-loss-without-control figure. L1 is a direct measurement; the higher the number, the more distantly related the taxon the value was borrowed from. |
| **Light group** | Crop-group assignment from the 40%-shade trial. Crops in the same group receive the same value. |

---
### 1-3. Three Pitfalls

> **1. Looking only at the overall grade**  
> The potato is **S** short-term and **B** long-term. Sorghum is **B** short-term and **S** long-term. The two look similar overall, but **the crop to plant in the first year of a disaster and the crop to plant in year 3 are not the same.**

> **2. Mistaking the supplementary grade for a staple-food grade**  
> It is normal for as many as 87 species to be Supplementary **SS**. The supplementary scenario gives micronutrients the maximum weight (3.50) and lowers the calorie weight to 0.50, so **it is by definition of this scenario that vegetables and wild greens dominate the top**. Supplementary **SS** does not mean "you can live on this alone" but "adding it prevents nutritional deficiency."

> **3. Reading exclusion as a low score**  
> The dandelion, ranked 1st among excluded crops, has a score without the exclusion gate of **48.2 points**, which would place it in the top 20 among valid crops. Exclusion happens not because the score is low but because of **a single fatal disqualifier**. Once that one criterion is fixed, the crop immediately becomes usable.

### 2-1. What to Read from the Top Ranks

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;**Ranks 1-4 (apios, kudzu, Jerusalem artichoke, sweet potato) are all underground tubers.** The first conclusion of this table is that tuber crops, not grains, are the winners under zero-input conditions. Tubers need little fertilizer, are highly perennial, and need no storage facility because they stay in the ground.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;**The potato, by contrast, is pushed down to 35th place (B).** Its individual criteria are excellent - **A** for calories, **A** for protein, **A** for speed to harvest - but a single **E (17.6%) in yield retention without synthetic inputs** collapses its long-term score. Without fungicide, late blight shortens its growing period.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;**Maize ranks 44th (B).** It is the crop grown most in conventional farming, but it loses 55% of its yield at 40% shade (the worst of any crop group) and, with a year-to-year variation rate of 24.8%, is the most unstable of the major grains.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;**Mushrooms rank surprisingly high.** Wine cap 6th, shiitake 24th, wood ear 30th. This is because, not being photosynthetic, **they are the one axis unaffected by a sunlight-reduction disaster**. However, since substrate must be brought in from outside, their land-use efficiency is capped at A.
