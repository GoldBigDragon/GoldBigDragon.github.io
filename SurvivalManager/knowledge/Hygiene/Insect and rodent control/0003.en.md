## 2. Source Reduction and Repulsion
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Source reduction is the most effective approach because it targets the **most vulnerable and immobile stage** of an insect's life cycle. While adult insects fly away and disperse, eggs and larvae remain concentrated in fixed locations unable to flee. Thus, the same effort yields far greater population reduction.

---
### 2-1. The 1-Week Standing Water Rule
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Mosquitoes are born in water. The time required to transition from egg to adult varies with water temperature, taking roughly **7–10 days** during summer temperatures of 25–30°C. Cold water temperatures can extend this to over two weeks. From this biological timeline emerges a practical operational rule:

> **If standing water is emptied or inverted once a week, no mosquito will reach adulthood and take flight from that water.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This rule works because mosquito larvae **can survive strictly inside water.** Emptying the water causes larvae and pupae to desiccate and die instantly. No insecticides, oil films, or special chemical treatments are required. All that is needed is a watchful eye to spot every single collection of standing water.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The primary challenge is that mosquitoes breed in places humans do not naturally recognize as water bodies. *Aedes* mosquitoes can breed even in water collected within a bottle cap.

| Frequently Overlooked Breeding Sites | Reason Overlooked |
| --- | --- |
| Flowerpot saucers, empty pots | Trapped water goes unnoticed |
| Discarded tires | Water persists inside and resists freezing in winter |
| Clogged rain gutters and downspouts | Positioned out of direct line of sight |
| Folds in tarpaulins and plastic sheets | Small volumes continuously replenished by rain |
| Uncovered water barrels and buckets | Retained for utility water, so emptying is avoided |
| Abandoned cans, bottles, and packaging | Hidden under accumulated debris |
| Tree hollows, bamboo stumps | Natural features, so not perceived as breeding sites |
| Septic tanks, stagnant drainage lines | Access and inspection are difficult |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Water that cannot be emptied requires alternative controls. Water retained for drinking or utility—such as storage tanks or rain barrels—must be **sealed with tight covers to prevent egg-laying.** For expansive water surfaces that cannot be covered, creating a surface film provides a mechanical solution. Floating a very thin layer of vegetable oil or waste oil prevents larvae and pupae from extending breathing siphons to the surface, causing suffocation. However, this water can no longer be used as drinking water and pollutes surrounding soil and aquatic ecosystems; thus, oil films should be restricted strictly to abandoned stagnant pools. For fish-habitable reservoirs, introducing small freshwater fish like killifish or loaches that consume larvae offers a far cleaner solution.

### 2-2. Smoke Is Not an Insecticide
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Smudge fires are a time-honored technique used globally that genuinely reduces mosquito presence. However, understanding **the exact mechanism by which it operates** is crucial for proper deployment.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Smoke repels mosquitoes through three mechanisms. First, particulate smoke obscures the mosquito's chemosensory receptors, disrupting the olfactory plumes of carbon dioxide and body odors emitted by humans. Because mosquitoes track prey via odor plumes, breaking the plume prevents them from locating targets. Second, volatile organic compounds released from specific burning plants exert direct chemical repulsion—such as cineole and thujone in wormwood, terpenes in arborvitae and juniper, and pinene in pine needles. Third, smoke creates thermal updrafts that physically impede the weak flight mechanics of mosquitoes.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Crucially, none of these three mechanisms **exert a lethal effect.** Smoke does not kill mosquitoes; it merely disorients them or drives them elsewhere, and the moment smoke dissipates, mosquitoes return. Even a light breeze pushes smoke in one direction, leaving the windward side completely unprotected. Smudge fires must therefore be understood as a **secondary outdoor auxiliary measure**, not a replacement for mosquito netting or source reduction.

| Material | Primary Volatiles | Characteristics |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Dried Wormwood (*Mugwort*) | Cineole, thujone, camphor | Most widely used; pungent aroma, burns slowly |
| Arborvitae / Hinoki / Juniper Leaves | Terpenes | Pungent aroma, produces dense smoke |
| Pine Needles / Pinecones | Pinene | Readily available; resin content causes frequent flaring |
| Dried Citrus Peels | Limonene | Strong citrus aroma even in small quantities |
| Green Grass / Fresh Foliage | High moisture | Acts primarily via smoke volume rather than volatiles |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The technique for generating smoke is **smoldering without open flame.** Layering damp foliage or green grass over well-dried burning fuel induces incomplete combustion, producing dense, long-lasting smoke. Covering the fire lightly with soil to restrict oxygen supply operates on the same principle.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;A critical life-threatening hazard must be emphasized here. Generating dense smoke relies on **inducing incomplete combustion**, which inherently produces carbon monoxide (CO). Carbon monoxide is colorless and odorless, binding to blood hemoglobin with an affinity over 200 times stronger than oxygen, thereby blocking systemic oxygen transport. Poisoning begins with mild headaches and drowsiness, rapidly progressing to unconsciousness. Fatalities frequently occur when individuals fall asleep due to CO-induced drowsiness.

> **Lighting a smudge fire inside a closed tent or room kills humans far more reliably than it kills mosquitoes.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Smoking must be conducted strictly outdoors or in fully open spaces. No form of combustion should ever be left unattended indoors without continuous ventilation.

### 2-3. Botanical Repellents and Pyrethrum
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Among natural botanical materials, the only plant possessing **genuine lethal insecticidal power** is pyrethrum (*Chrysanthemum cinerariifolium*). The flower heads of pyrethrum contain natural pyrethrins, the chemical template for synthetic pyrethroids—a cornerstone of modern insecticides.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Pyrethrins bind to voltage-gated sodium channels in insect nerve cells, preventing the channels from closing. When sodium channels remain permanently open, nerves fire repetitively without cessation, causing immediate loss of motor coordination (knockdown) followed by death. Mammals exhibit low toxicity to pyrethrins because mammalian sodium channels are structurally less sensitive, and liver esterase enzymes rapidly metabolize and detoxify pyrethrins in the bloodstream.

| Parameter | Details |
| --- | --- |
| Active Part | Concentrated primarily in flower heads, specifically the ovaries |
| Harvest Timing | Highest pyrethrin content occurs when flowers are in full bloom with 3–4 rows of disk florets open |
| Drying | Must be dried strictly in the shade. Sunlight breaks down pyrethrins rapidly |
| Storage | Store in airtight containers in a dark location; potency degrades over time |
| Usage | Pulverize into fine powder for dusting, smolder for smoke, or extract in water, oil, or alcohol |
| Residual Effect | Photodegrades rapidly; leaves virtually zero persistent environmental residue |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Rapid photodegradation serves as both an advantage and a drawback. While it leaves no persistent environmental residue and remains safe around food, it offers **virtually zero residual protection**, requiring fresh application for each use. This rapid degradation is precisely why pyrethrum must be shade-dried and stored in darkness. Additionally, pyrethrins are highly toxic to aquatic life and felines; caution is mandatory around fish tanks and cat-inhabited areas.

| Other Botanical Plants | Compounds & Mechanism | Real-World Utility |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Neem | Azadirachtin; anti-feedant and growth regulator | Does not kill immediately; gradually suppresses population size |
| Cinnamon | Cinnamaldehyde; repulsion and larvicidal | Demonstrates proven larvicidal efficacy against mosquito larvae |
| Clove | Eugenol; repulsion | Strong aroma useful for short-term repulsion |
| Citronella / Lemongrass | Citronellal, geraniol | Clear repellent effect, but duration is short |
| Eucalyptus | Cineole | Used for repulsion; concentrated oil causes skin irritation |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Conversely, **tobacco leaf extracts are strongly discouraged.** While nicotine is an exceptionally potent insecticide, it is simultaneously a deadly neurotoxin to humans. Nicotine absorbs rapidly through mammalian skin; handling concentrated extracts with bare hands can induce nausea, dizziness, and cardiac arrhythmia. Green Tobacco Sickness (GTS) observed in tobacco harvesters is a direct result of transdermal nicotine poisoning. In disaster situations lacking medical antidotes or means to measure chemical concentration, the risks vastly outweigh any benefit.
