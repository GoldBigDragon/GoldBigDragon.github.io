## 1. Vectors and Control Priorities
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Vectors do not merely transport pathogens passively. Some carry pathogens physically on their body surfaces (mechanical transmission), while others harbor pathogens that multiply internally before being injected during feeding (biological transmission). This distinction dictates countermeasures. Mechanical transmission can be broken by preventing contact, whereas in biological transmission, a single vector represents an infection event; thus, **even a single bite must be prevented.**

---
### 1-1. Vectors
| Vector | Primary Diseases | Transmission Mode | Critical Characteristic |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Mosquito (*Anopheles*) | Malaria | Injected during blood feeding | Nocturnal blood feeding; domestically centered on *Plasmodium vivax* |
| Mosquito (*Aedes*) | Dengue, Zika, Chikungunya | Injected during blood feeding | Diurnal blood feeding; breeds in small water containers |
| Mosquito (*Culex tritaeniorhynchus*) | Japanese Encephalitis | Injected during blood feeding | Breeds in rice paddies and puddles; pigs serve as amplifying hosts |
| Fly | Diarrheal diseases, Dysentery, Typhoid, Trachoma | Transported mechanically on body | Travels between feces and food; breeds in excreta |
| Louse (Body Louse) | Epidemic Typhus, Trench Fever, Relapsing Fever | Excreta enters through scratch wounds | Lives in clothing seams; surges when hygiene breaks down |
| Flea | Plague, Murine Typhus | Blood feeding and excreta | Transfers to humans when rodent hosts die |
| Chigger Mite Larva | Scrub Typhus | Injected during tissue fluid feeding | Leaves a black eschar (scab) at the bite site |
| Hard Tick | SFTS, Lyme Disease | Injected during blood feeding | Domestic SFTS exhibits a high case-fatality rate |
| Rodents | Leptospirosis, Hantavirus (HFRS), *Salmonella* | Urine and feces | Urine via water/wounds; dry feces via airborne dust |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;What must be read from this table is that **invasion pathways differ entirely by vector.** Mosquitoes and ticks pierce the skin; lice and fleas deposit feces that enter wounds when scratched; flies contaminate food mechanically; *Leptospira* enters when contaminated water contacts skin abrasions or mucous membranes; and Hantavirus enters **via the respiratory tract** when dried rodent feces are aerosolized into dust.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Consequently, control measures split by pathway. The key for mosquitoes is netting; for ticks, clothing and exposure management; for lice, laundering and heat treatment; for *Leptospira*, avoiding bare-skin contact with floodwaters; and for Hantavirus, **never sweeping or dusting dry rodent droppings.** Sweeping dry rodent feces with a broom disperses Hantavirus into the air; droppings must first be wetted thoroughly with water or disinfectant before being wiped away.

### 1-2. Physical Barriers
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;While chemical control depends on three variables—concentration, contact, and resistance—physical barriers rely on the simple principle that **pathogens cannot enter if holes are smaller than their bodies.** Mosquito netting meshes do not shrink even when chemical resistance develops. Under disaster conditions where pesticide supplies are severed and resistance status cannot be verified, this simplicity becomes a decisive advantage.

| Target | Approx. Body Width | Mesh Size Required for Exclusion |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Mosquito | 1.5–2 mm | Approximately ≤1.2–1.5 mm |
| Housefly | 3–4 mm | Approximately ≤2 mm |
| Horsefly / Botfly | ≥5 mm | Approximately ≤3 mm |
| Biting Midges / Blackflies | 0.5–1 mm | Approximately ≤0.6 mm |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Standard mosquito netting uses a benchmark of 156 holes per square inch (~24 holes per cm²). This density blocks mosquitoes effectively while allowing adequate airflow. Further reducing mesh size can exclude biting midges, but airflow degrades so severely that occupants cannot tolerate hot nights and ultimately remove the netting.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;When commercial mosquito netting is unavailable, substitutes are evaluated by mesh size and structural durability.

| Substitute | Performance | Limitations |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Thin Cotton Gauze / Ramie / Hemp | Mesh size is small enough to block mosquitoes | Clings when wet, severely dropping airflow |
| Old Lace Curtains | Effective if hole size is uniform | Hole sizes are frequently non-uniform |
| Onion Mesh / Fruit Netting | Mesh size is generally too large; ineffective against mosquitoes | Usable as rodent or bird barriers |
| Fishing Net / Shade Cloth | Varies by mesh size | Holes ≥3 mm allow mosquitoes to pass through freely |
| Layering Fabric Two-Fold | Reduces effective mesh size | Significantly restricts airflow |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The most common point of failure in physical barriers is not the netting itself, but the **edges.** Mosquitoes are highly adept at locating small gaps; a hanging net margin failing to touch the floor or a few-millimeter gap between a window frame and screen is fully sufficient for entry. In sleeping areas, netting edges must be tucked firmly beneath mattresses or bedding, and window screens pressed tightly against frames. Overall **sealing integrity**, rather than mesh grade alone, determines real-world performance.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Physical protection against ticks centers on clothing structure. Chigger mite larvae and hard ticks cling to vegetation tips, transferring onto passing animal limbs and crawling upward. Thus, **tucking pant legs inside socks** is a simple yet highly effective countermeasure. Light-colored clothing provides no chemical kill effect, but makes attached ticks visually obvious for rapid removal. Checking body surfaces and shaking out clothing after outdoor activity serves as the practical last line of defense. Because ticks generally require several hours of attachment to transmit pathogens, prompt detection and removal significantly reduce infection risk.

### 1-3. Priorities in Control
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;When resources are limited, mismanaging priorities wastes the majority of effort. The hierarchy of intervention effectiveness generally follows this sequence:

| Priority | Intervention | Reason for High Impact | Cost |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Source Reduction | Eliminates the next generation entirely | Requires labor only |
| 2 | Physical Barriers | Breaks contact regardless of population density | Requires materials; zero recurring cost |
| 3 | Repulsion | Lowers probability of contact | Requires continuous consumption |
| 4 | Adult Eradication | Reduces only currently existing adult populations | High consumption; short-lived effect |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The difference between killing a single adult vector and eliminating a single larval habitat is a matter of scale. A single female mosquito lays hundreds of eggs in her lifetime; thus, eliminating a single stagnant puddle is equivalent to killing hundreds of adult mosquitoes.

> **Most of the effort expended on chasing or killing adult vectors would have yielded severalfold greater impact had it been directed at eliminating their breeding sources.**
