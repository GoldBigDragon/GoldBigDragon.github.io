## 1. Vectors and Priorities of Blocking
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Vectors spread disease in two ways. Mechanical transmission, in which the vector carries it on its body surface, is cut by blocking contact. Biological transmission, in which the vector multiplies the pathogen inside its body and then injects it, makes a single vector an infection event in itself, so even one bite must be prevented.

---
### 1-1. Vectors
| Vector | Main disease | Mode of transmission | Decisive characteristics |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Mosquito (Anopheles) | Malaria | Injected when sucking blood | Feeds on blood at night; in Korea mostly vivax |
| Mosquito (Aedes) | Dengue, Zika, chikungunya | Injected when sucking blood | Feeds by day, breeds in water in small containers |
| Mosquito (Culex tritaeniorhynchus) | Japanese encephalitis | Injected when sucking blood | Breeds in rice paddies and puddles; pigs are the amplifying host |
| Fly | Diarrheal diseases, dysentery, typhoid, trachoma | Carried on the body | Shuttles between feces and food, breeds in feces |
| Louse (body louse) | Epidemic typhus, trench fever, relapsing fever | Feces enter through wounds | Lives in the seams of clothing; explodes in number when hygiene collapses |
| Flea | Plague, murine typhus | Blood sucking and feces | Moves on to humans when the rats die |
| Chigger larva | Scrub typhus | Injected when ingesting body fluids | A black scab (eschar) is left at the bite site |
| Hard tick | SFTS (severe fever with thrombocytopenia syndrome), Lyme disease | Injected when sucking blood | SFTS in Korea has a fairly high fatality rate |
| Rat | Leptospirosis, hemorrhagic fever with renal syndrome (hantavirus), salmonella | Urine and droppings | Urine through water and wounds, droppings dry and become dust |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The routes of entry differ, and control also splits by route.

| Route | Key control |
| --- | --- |
| Mosquitoes and ticks: pierce the skin and inject | Nets for mosquitoes; clothing and exposure management for ticks |
| Lice and fleas: feces enter the wound when the bite is scratched | Laundering and heat treatment for lice |
| Leptospira: contaminated water touches tiny skin wounds or mucous membranes | Do not enter floodwater with bare skin |
| Hantavirus: dried rat droppings blow up as dust and enter the airways | Do not sweep or shake out places with rat droppings |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Sweeping dried rat droppings with a broom is the same as scattering hantavirus into the air. Wet them first with water or disinfectant, then wipe them away.

---
### 1-2. Physical Blocking
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Chemical control depends on concentration, contact, and resistance, but physical blocking relies on the fact that they cannot get in through a small hole. Even if insecticide resistance develops, the mesh does not shrink. This is a decisive advantage in a disaster, when supplies of chemicals are cut and resistance cannot be checked.

| Target | Body width (approx.) | Mesh needed to block |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Mosquito | 1.5-2mm | About 1.2-1.5mm or less |
| Housefly | 3-4mm | About 2mm or less |
| Horsefly, bot fly | 5mm or more | About 3mm or less |
| Midge, blackfly | 0.5-1mm | About 0.6mm or less |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;An ordinary mosquito net is based on at least 156 holes per square inch (about 24 per cm²). Mosquitoes cannot pass and air passes through well enough. Making the mesh finer would also block midges, but ventilation is poor, so people cannot bear it on hot nights and throw it off.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;When there is no manufactured mosquito net, judge substitutes by mesh size and durability.

| Substitute | Performance | Limitation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Thin cotton gauze, ramie, hemp cloth | Mesh is small enough to block mosquitoes | When wet it sticks and airflow drops sharply |
| Old lace curtain cloth | Effective if the holes are uniform | Hole sizes are often uneven |
| Onion bags, fruit netting | Holes are usually large and useless against mosquitoes | Can be used to block rats and birds |
| Fishing net, shade netting | Depends on hole size | At 3mm or more, mosquitoes pass right through |
| Layering cloth in two or more layers | Effective mesh gets smaller | Airflow suffers a lot |

- The point where blocking fails is not the net but the edge. A net hem that does not reach the floor, or a gap of a few millimeters between the window frame and the screen, is all a mosquito needs to get in. In bed, tuck the net hem fully under the mattress or bedding, and at windows press down and secure the frame. How well it is sealed determines performance more than the grade of the net.
- Tick blocking is done through the structure of clothing. Chigger larvae and hard ticks cling to grass tips, transfer to the legs of passing animals, and crawl upward. Tucking trouser hems into socks is simple but very effective.
- Light-colored clothing has no insecticidal effect but makes attached ticks easy to see. Shaking out clothes and checking the body after outdoor activity is the last line of defense. A tick usually has to stay attached for several hours or more to transmit pathogens, so finding and removing it quickly greatly reduces the risk of infection.

### 1-3. Control Priorities
| Rank | Means | Why it is effective | Cost |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Source elimination | Wipes out the next generation entirely | Only labor needed |
| 2 | Physical blocking | Cuts contact regardless of population | Materials needed; no recurring cost |
| 3 | Repellence | Lowers the chance of contact | Keeps being used up |
| 4 | Killing adults | Reduces only those already born | Large consumption, short-lived effect |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;A single female mosquito lays hundreds of eggs in her lifetime. Eliminating one puddle is the same as killing hundreds of adults. Most of the effort spent repelling or killing adults would have produced several times the effect if it had been spent eliminating the places where adults are born.
