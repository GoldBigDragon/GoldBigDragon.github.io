## 4. Rodent Control
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Rodents demand a control logic fundamentally different from insects. While individual eradication of insects yields limited impact due to their vast populations, rodents exist in smaller numbers but possess the ability to **learn, exhibit caution, and actively alter their environment.** They gnaw to widen openings, avoid novel objects, and memorize feeding locations. Consequently, rodent control is not a matter of equipment, but a matter of sequence.

---
### 4-1. Removal of Food and Shelter
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The most common failure in rodent control is setting traps first. The maximum rodent population a location can sustain is **determined by available food and shelter.** Even if traps temporarily reduce numbers, intact food and shelter cause remaining individuals to increase their reproduction rates, while new rodents migrate from surrounding areas to fill the void. Norway rats produce 6–12 pups per litter, breed multiple times a year, and offspring reach reproductive maturity in just three months. A trapping rate of a few rats per day can never catch up with this reproductive capacity.

> **Traps reduce the rodents currently present, whereas removing food sources determines the rodent population of the future. Therefore, eliminating food and shelter must take priority.**

| Target | Reality in Disaster Situations | Action |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Food Sources | Exposed grain sacks, food scraps, food waste, livestock feed | Seal in metal, glass, or heavy plastic; bury or burn scraps daily |
| Water Sources | Leaking water barrels, stagnant rainwater, wet rags | Cover water barrels; drain standing water |
| Shelter | Debris piles, stacked lumber, abandoned furniture, overgrown vegetation | Elevate at least 30 cm off the ground and keep away from walls |
| Pathways | Pipes, conduits, ivy, wall crevices | Seal with gnaw-proof materials |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Regarding water, a distinction must be made between species. Norway rats require direct access to free water, whereas house mice can survive solely on moisture contained within their food. Cutting off water sources is highly effective against Norway rats, whereas sealing food sources remains the only reliable pressure against house mice.

---
### 4-2. Gap Dimensions and Trap Structures
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The size of a gap a rodent can squeeze through is dictated by its **skull.** Their bodies are flexible enough that once the cranium passes through, the rest follows.

| Species | Minimum Traversable Gap | Reference Object |
| --- | --- | --- |
| House Mouse | Approx. 6 mm | Thickness of a pencil |
| Norway Rat | Approx. 12 mm | Thickness of an adult thumb |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;An important condition applies: rodent incisors grow continuously throughout their lives, driving a constant urge to gnaw, which they use to **enlarge small gaps into passable openings.** Sealing materials must be evaluate not only by mesh size, but also by resistance to gnawing. Openings blocked using only wood, vinyl, sponge, or spray polyurethane foam will reopen within days. Stuffing steel wool tightly into gaps and capping it with cement, using wire mesh with a grid size ≤6 mm, or applying sheet metal provides real-world durability. The traditional technique of mixing ceramic shards into mud plaster operates on the exact same logic.

| Trap Mechanism | Operating Principle | Characteristics |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Snap Trap | Spring mechanism delivers an instantaneous blow | Near-instant kill ensures reliable recovery; catches one rat per setting |
| Cage/Funnel Trap | One-way door mechanism | Captures multiple individuals; leaves live rodents requiring disposal |
| Bucket Trap | Slick incline with rotating cylinder over water | Enables repeated captures without resetting; constructible from natural materials |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Trap success depends far more on **placement** than on bait choice. Rodents travel with one side of their body pressed against walls and avoid crossing open spaces; traps must be placed flush against walls with the trigger plate facing the wall. Grease marks along walls, accumulated droppings, and gnaw marks indicate active pathways.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Another critical factor is the **neophobia** of Norway rats. Norway rats avoid unfamiliar objects in accustomed spaces for several days; moving a trap immediately because it fails to capture a rat on day one guarantees failure. The standard protocol is to place un-set traps with bait for several days to allow rodents to drop their guard before activating the mechanism. Conversely, house mice are curious and approach new objects promptly. For bait, nuts, grain powder mixed with oil, and dried fish prove far more effective than cheese.

---
### 4-3. Rodenticides
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Anticoagulant rodenticides act slowly over several days. While designed to prevent rodents from associating the bait with mortality, this delayed action causes rodents to **return to their burrows to die.** Carcasses inside walls and above ceilings cannot be recovered, releasing foul odors upon decomposition and becoming breeding grounds for blowflies and maggots. Attempting to reduce one pest creates a far larger insect infestation.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The second consequence is secondary poisoning. Rodents slowed by rodenticide ingestion become easy prey for predators, resulting in the secondary poisoning of cats, dogs, owls, and weasels. **When natural rodent predators perish first**, rodent populations recover rapidly. The risk of accidental ingestion by children and livestock is also severe, and necessary treatments—such as Vitamin K1 therapy and blood transfusions—are unobtainable during disasters.

> **Rodenticides leave unrecoverable carcasses in inaccessible locations and kill the natural predators that hunt rodents. Reliable traps remain the superior choice in disaster situations.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Dead rodents must never be handled with bare hands. Wrap the carcass in plastic or large leaves, or transfer it using a shovel; douse thoroughly with disinfectant, double-bag, and bury deeply or incinerate. As carcasses cool, fleas abandon them in search of new hosts; this is why pest outbreaks like plague often surge following large-scale rodent poisonings.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Sanitation techniques must be chosen based on **pathogen transmission routes.**

| Disease | Excreted Via | Invasion Pathway | Strictly Prohibited Action |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Leptospirosis | Urine | Contaminated water contacting wounds or mucous membranes | Entering floodwaters with bare feet or bare hands |
| Hantavirus (HFRS) | Dried feces and urine | Inhaled into the respiratory tract as airborne dust | Dry sweeping, dusting, or vacuuming dried droppings |
| Salmonellosis | Feces | Ingested orally via contaminated food and hands | Using grains or cooking surfaces traversed by rodents without processing |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;A single conclusion emerges from these transmission routes: **rodent dropping cleanup must be conducted strictly using wet methods.** Open doors and windows to ventilate for at least 30 minutes, spray droppings and nesting materials with dilute chlorine disinfectant or soapy water, allow them to soak for 10 minutes, wipe them up with paper towels or rags, double-bag, and bury or burn them. Dry sweeping or dusting aerosolizes pathogens, creating a respiratory transmission route for yourself.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;During cleanup, cover your nose and mouth with cloth, protect your hands, and wash thoroughly with soap afterward. If skin abrasions exist on hands, cover them with waterproof dressings before starting. *Leptospira* cannot penetrate intact skin, but a single minor wound is fully sufficient for entry.
