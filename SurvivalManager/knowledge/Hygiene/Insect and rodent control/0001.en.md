## Introduction
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In disaster situations, insects and rodents are not merely a nuisance; they are **vectors for infectious diseases.** While the previous waste disposal document focused on the pathways through which human excreta reach the human mouth, this document covers the pathways through which pathogens gain **wings and legs** to move on their own. These transmission routes cannot be broken by sanitation management alone. Mosquitoes breed even in clean standing water, and rodents will invade any location, no matter how clean, as long as food sources exist.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Disasters simultaneously create ideal conditions for vectors to multiply. Floods create stagnant pools that expand mosquito breeding sites; power outages halt refrigeration, causing food to spoil and attract flies and rodents; collapsed structures and accumulated debris offer shelter for rats; and the breakdown of bathing and laundry facilities leads to louse infestations. This is why vector populations normally suppressed by routine public health systems can multiply severalfold within a single season.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This document operates under the assumption that commercial insecticides and rodenticides are unavailable. Consequently, the focus is on **control priorities**: which actions yield the highest impact, which methods merely repel rather than eradicate, and how far natural remedies can actually go before hitting their limits. Understanding that smoke serves as a repellent rather than a lethal agent, that diatomaceous earth becomes useless when wet, and that removing food sources must precede setting traps is far more critical in practice than relying on chemicals.
