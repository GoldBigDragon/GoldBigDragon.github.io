## 3. Physical Eradication
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Pyrethrum in the previous section operated as a chemical neurotoxin. While fast-acting, it photodegrades rapidly, leaving zero residual protection, and repeated exposure selects for resistant individuals, rendering it increasingly ineffective over time. The mechanisms in this section function differently. **Physical eradication attacks the structural integrity of the insect body rather than its nervous system.** Insects can evolve metabolic detoxification enzymes to tolerate chemical agents, but they possess no evolutionary pathway to withstand systemic desiccation or thermal protein denaturation. Though slow and labor-intensive, **the complete absence of resistance development** serves as a decisive advantage when supplies are severed.

---
### 3-1. Diatomaceous Earth and Ash
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The outermost layer of the insect exoskeleton features a thin waxy layer called the epicuticle. Measuring less than 1 μm in thickness, this lipid film acts as the primary defense barrier preventing internal moisture evaporation. Because insects possess a high surface-area-to-volume ratio, breaching this membrane causes fluid loss at a rate incomparably faster than in mammals.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Diatomaceous earth (DE) consists of pulverized fossilized silica skeletons of diatoms, disrupting this barrier through dual mechanisms. First, razor-sharp microscopic particles **abrasively score** the wax layer as the insect crawls over them. Second, microscopic pores covering the particle surfaces **adsorb** the lipid components of the wax layer. Moisture leaks through the abraded sites and thinned membranes, accelerating systemic dehydration until the insect succumbs to body fluid loss.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The loss of efficacy upon wetting stems from the exact same physical mechanism. When water fills the particle pores, adsorption sites for lipids vanish, particles clump together, losing their ability to adhere to insect bodies, and ambient high humidity replenishes the moisture lost by the insect.

> **Diatomaceous earth acts as an insecticide strictly in a dry state. Wet diatomaceous earth is merely mud.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Understanding that DE is not an instant killer is crucial. Death takes several hours to days, and the insect must physically traverse the powder for it to function; thus, application should be focused on **unavoidable transit lines** like runways and wall edges. A common mistake is applying thick piles. Visible heaps cause insects to detour around them, so DE should be spread as a dust layer thin enough for the underlying floor surface to show through.

| Material | Mechanism | Efficacy | Cautions |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Diatomaceous Earth | Abrasion and lipid adsorption | Highest | Ineffective when wet; avoid dust inhalation |
| Wood Ash (Fine) | Abrasion, absorption, alkaline irritation | Moderate | Particles are coarse; sift through mesh before use |
| Dry Fine Soil / Red Clay | Abrasion, absorption | Low | Limited to auxiliary grain storage uses |
| Slaked Lime Powder | Alkaline corrosion and desiccation | Moderate | Highly caustic; unsuitable for living quarters |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;While amorphous silica in food-grade DE carries low risk of silicosis, fine airborne dust irritates respiratory tracts and eyes. Application must be performed wearing a wet cloth mask while standing upwind.

### 3-2. Thermal Treatment and Sunlight
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Lice and scabies mites succumb to heat because **proteins undergo irreversible thermal denaturation.** Enzymes and structural proteins begin unfolding around 50°C, and once denatured, they cannot return to their original conformation even when cooled. Eggs exhibit higher thermal tolerance than adults, but their physical threshold remains within the same limit.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The critical fact is that body lice reside in **clothing seams**, not directly on human skin. Body lice hide in fabric folds to lay eggs, migrating to skin briefly only during feeding; scabies mites also persist on bedding and clothing for several days.

> **Lice and scabies inhabit clothing and bedding rather than human skin. Bathing the body while wearing unwashed clothes results in re-infestation the very next day.**

| Treatment | Conditions | Mechanism and Effect |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Boiling | Boiling water for several minutes | Kills adults and eggs completely; most reliable |
| Hot Water Wash | ≥60°C for 30 minutes | Kills adults and eggs |
| Thermal Drying / Ironing | ≥50°C for over 10 minutes | Direct heat applied to seams; highly effective against scabies |
| Sealed Isolation | Sealed in plastic for 1–2 weeks | Body lice starve in 1–2 days; scabies mites starve in 2–3 days |
| Solar Exposure | Cover with black fabric at midday | Internal thermal elevation, not UV light, accomplishes the kill |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;When utilizing solar exposure, spreading items thinly for a mid-day flip and covering with black fabric significantly elevates internal temperatures for a reliable kill.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The most common failure in laundering is **insufficient water temperature.** Washing in cold water fails to destroy eggs glued to seams, allowing them to hatch days later. Even under fuel constraints, boiling clothing while washing the body in cold water yields far superior sanitation outcomes than the reverse.

### 3-3. Four Conditions for Preventing Grain Pests
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Rice weevils and cowpea weevils deposit eggs **inside** individual grain kernels. Because eggs remain invisible externally, incoming grain must be presumed contaminated; the operational objective is **preventing embedded eggs from developing.** Disrupting even a single environmental condition halts pest development.

| Condition | Threshold | Mechanism |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Moisture Content | Grain moisture ≤13%; development halts near ≤12% | Larvae depend on internal grain moisture for metabolic development |
| Temperature | Development halts ≤15°C; mortality under long exposure near 0°C | Ectothermic metabolic rates correlate directly with ambient temperature |
| Oxygen | Self-consumed and depleted following sealing | Suffocation occurs within weeks inside airtight containers |
| Physical Contact | Mix ash or DE at 0.5–1% of grain weight | Dehydrates pests during movement and coats oviposition surfaces |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;If moisture testing equipment is unavailable, bite a grain kernel with your teeth. If it yields softly, moisture remains elevated; **if it fractures with a sharp crack**, it has reached a safe storage threshold. Operational sequence is equally critical. Sealing incompletely dried grain traps internal moisture, fostering mold rather than suppressing insects; sealing hot grain straight from sun-drying causes condensation inside containers during cooling. Dry grains during the day, allow them to cool in the evening, and seal at night.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The traditional method of mixing ash operates through three concurrent mechanisms: ash particles dehydrate pests, fill interstitial spaces between grains to impede movement and egg-laying, and hygroscopic properties keep grains dry. Rinsing grain in water prior to cooking removes the ash. For grain already infested, submerging it in water causes hollow, consumed kernels to float for easy skimming.
