## 2. Physics of Particle Filtration
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The most common misconception regarding filtration is **explaining it purely through sieving.** This is the picture where objects larger than the mesh are blocked and smaller ones pass through. If this picture were correct, blocking 0.1 μm particles would require 0.1 μm pores, and breathing through such filter media would be impossible. Real filtration operates far more sophisticatedly through four primary physical mechanisms working together.

---
### 2-1. Four Principles
| Mechanism | How It Works | Effectively Captured Size |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sieving | Particles larger than the gaps between fibers are physically blocked | Several μm and above |
| Inertial Impaction | Heavy particles fail to follow air streamlines and collide with fibers | 1 μm and above |
| Interception | Particles following streamlines pass close enough to touch and adhere to fibers | 0.5–1 μm |
| Diffusion | Light particles wander via Brownian motion until touching fibers | 0.1 μm and below |
| Electrostatic Attraction | Electrically charged fibers pull particles in | All size ranges regardless of size |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Here comes a crucial fact. Inertial impaction and interception work better as **particles grow larger**, while diffusion works better as **particles grow smaller**. Because these two trends move in opposite directions, a valley forms somewhere in between where both principles are weak.

> **That valley is roughly around 0.3 μm. Particles larger than this are captured by collision, and smaller ones are captured by wandering, but particles of this exact size satisfy neither mechanism well and penetrate most easily.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This size is known as the Most Penetrating Particle Size (MPPS). This clarifies a widely misunderstood point: the 95% rating of an N95 mask represents **the worst-case performance measured at the most penetrating particle size**, whereas larger or smaller particles are filtered out even more effectively. This explains why the claim that viruses smaller than 0.3 μm freely pass through is false; as particles get smaller, diffusion becomes stronger, making them easier to capture.

---
### 2-2. Electrostatics and Breathing Speed
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;A significant portion of commercial mask performance comes not from the fibers themselves, but from **electrostatic charges embedded in the fibers.** Applying an electric field to fine meltblown nonwoven fibers causes passing particles to be attracted and bound electrically. This enhances filtration efficiency without shrinking pore sizes, keeping breathing resistance low.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;However, these electrostatic charges dissipate easily. Water, alcohol, detergents, high humidity, and prolonged storage all destroy the charge. This is why **washing a mask degrades its performance**, and disinfecting it with alcohol produces the same result. Furthermore, homemade cloth masks possess no electrostatic charge to begin with, relying solely on pure mechanical filtration.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Breathing speed also alters performance. Rapid airflow shortens the time small particles spend near fibers, reducing the opportunity for diffusion to capture them. In other words, **the harder you breathe, the lower the filtration efficiency becomes.** A mask worn while running or carrying heavy loads provides less filtration than when resting statically.

---
### 2-3. Does Layering Improve Performance?
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Stacking multiple layers of fabric does increase filtration efficiency. However, two phenomena occur simultaneously.

| Number of Layers | Filtration Efficiency | Breathing Resistance |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 Layer | Low | Low |
| 2 Layers | Noticeably increases | Noticeably increases |
| 3–4 Layers | Slightly increases further | Significantly increases |
| More Layers | Diminishing returns (Plateaus) | Sharply increases |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The problem is that while filtration efficiency levels off gradually, breathing resistance climbs steeply. Indiscriminately adding layers eventually leaves **only drawbacks beyond a certain point.** These drawbacks manifest in three ways:

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;First, as resistance rises, air seeks the path of least resistance. Instead of passing through the mask media, air leaks in through gaps around the edges of the mask. A paradox arises where **a superior filter fabric actually increases edge leakage.** Second, when breathing becomes difficult, people pull down or remove the mask entirely. An unworn mask is equivalent to no mask. Third, if exhaled air cannot escape and is re-inhaled, carbon monoxide and carbon dioxide accumulate, triggering headaches and dizziness. For individuals with cardiopulmonary conditions, the elderly, and young children, this in itself becomes a health hazard.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Therefore, the criterion for determining the number of layers is not peak filtration efficiency, but **whether one can breathe continuously in that state for the required duration.** The answer varies depending on whether it is for a quick walkthrough or hours of physical labor.
