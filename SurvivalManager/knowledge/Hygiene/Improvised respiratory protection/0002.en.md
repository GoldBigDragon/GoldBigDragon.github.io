## 1. What It Can and Cannot Block
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Before all other details, this single paragraph must come first.

> **Improvised masks are partially effective against particulate matter such as dust, ash, smoke soot, and respiratory droplets; however, they are virtually useless against gases and vapors like carbon monoxide, chlorine, ammonia, and hydrogen sulfide. Furthermore, in oxygen-deficient environments, no mask is of any use.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;These three sentences point to three distinctly different hazards. Because the required countermeasures are completely different, they must be clearly distinguished.

---
### 1-1. Hazards
| Hazard Type | Examples | Improvised Mask | What Is Actually Required |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Particulates | Soil dust, volcanic ash, collapse dust, soot, mold spores, droplets, dry fecal dust | Partially effective | Wearing a mask with a tight face seal |
| Gases & Vapors | Carbon monoxide, chlorine, ammonia, hydrogen sulfide, sulfur dioxide, hydrogen cyanide | **Virtually useless** | Absorbent/catalytic filter canisters for the specific gas |
| Oxygen Deficiency | Wells, septic tanks, basements, silos, storage tanks, sealed fire spaces | **Completely useless** | Air-supplying equipment, or refraining from entering |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The dividing line between particulates and gases is size. Soil dust particles measure several micrometers (μm), smoke soot is around 0.1 μm, and respiratory droplets range from several μm to tens of μm. All of these fall within size ranges that can be trapped or caught between fibers. Conversely, gas molecules measure around 0.0003 μm—one thousand to ten thousand times smaller than particulates. **No fiber structure can filter out molecules of this size.** Blocking a gas requires chemically capturing or reacting with it rather than filtering it, and the necessary chemical means vary by gas.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Oxygen deficiency is different in nature altogether. Air-purifying respirators are all designed **on the premise that breathing-grade oxygen is present in the ambient air.** Masks only filter out harmful substances; they cannot supply good substances. Wearing a mask in a space where atmospheric oxygen concentration drops below 19.5% is utterly meaningless, and falsely believing one is protected only drives one deeper into danger.

---
### 1-2. Senses Will Not Warn You
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Gas hazards are especially dangerous because the human body provides no warning. The habit of judging danger by smell works in the exact opposite direction here.

| Gas | Sensory Perception | Why It Is Dangerous |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Carbon Monoxide | Colorless, odorless, non-irritating | Gives no signal at all. Starts with headaches and drowsiness before passing out |
| Hydrogen Sulfide | Rotten egg odor at low concentrations | **Olfactory fatigue sets in at high concentrations, making the smell disappear** |
| Methane & Nitrogen | Odorless | Non-toxic in themselves, but displace oxygen and cause asphyxiation |
| Carbon Dioxide | Odorless | Accumulates in low-lying spots and enclosed spaces |
| Chlorine & Ammonia | Strong irritation | Detectable by smell, but exposure has already occurred by then |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Olfactory fatigue caused by hydrogen sulfide is a critical concept to learn. If a foul odor **suddenly disappears, it does not mean the space has become safe, but rather that concentration has spiked to a lethal level.** This is the classic progression of accidents occurring in septic tanks, sewers, manure pits, and stagnant water reservoirs.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Carbon monoxide offers no warnings whatsoever. It binds to hemoglobin over 200 times more strongly than oxygen, preventing blood from transporting oxygen, yet this process triggers no sensation of suffocation. Instead, it induces drowsiness, preventing the victim from self-evacuating.

---
### 1-3. Repeated Accidents in Confined Spaces
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Wells, septic tanks, underground storage vaults, silos, water tanks, and long-sealed basements share common traits. Lacking air exchange, **oxygen is consumed while other gases accumulate.** As organic matter decomposes, it consumes oxygen and releases carbon dioxide, hydrogen sulfide, and methane.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Accidents in these spaces almost always follow the exact same pattern: one person enters and collapses, a rescuer rushes in and collapses alongside them, and then a third person follows. Because those who collapse do not show signs of gasping or struggling for air, but **silently lose consciousness**, onlookers outside fail to realize how dangerous the space actually is.

> **Rushing into a confined space where someone has collapsed is not a rescue operation—it is increasing the body count. A towel or mask does nothing in this situation.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The only proper actions are refraining from entering, pulling victims out from the outside using hooks or ropes, and **first exchanging the air.** Open lids and ventilate over time, or blow fresh air in from above to displace heavy gases. The old method of lowering a candle or match to see if the flame goes out is known, but it only detects oxygen deficiency, fails to detect carbon monoxide, and serves as an ignition source if methane is present. It is not a reliable method of judgment.
