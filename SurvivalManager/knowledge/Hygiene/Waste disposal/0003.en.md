## 2. Principles of Improvised Latrines
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The primary function of a latrine is not to destroy excreta, but to **isolate** them. Among improvised latrines in disaster situations, virtually none actually destroy pathogens; most simply buy time and space without killing the pathogens. Understanding this distinction is essential to avoid false peace of mind, such as believing "it is safe because I sprinkled ash."

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;For isolation to be effective, three conditions must be met simultaneously: pathogens must not leak into waterways, flies must not reach them, and human hands and feet must not come into contact with them. Any type of latrine should be evaluated based on how well it satisfies these three conditions.

---
### 2-1. Pit Latrines
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The operating principle of a pit latrine is simple: liquid waste infiltrates the soil and is filtered, while solid waste remains in the pit, undergoing anaerobic decomposition to reduce in volume.

| Parameter | Value | Rationale |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Distance from Water Source | Minimum 30 m | Required filtration distance for bacteria to be adsorbed and killed while passing through soil |
| Pit Bottom to Water Table | Minimum 1.5 m above | An unsaturated soil layer must remain for filtration to function |
| Topographic Relation to Water Source | Strictly downhill from water source | Groundwater flows along terrain slopes |
| Distance from Living Quarters | Within 50 m | If too far, people revert to open defecation at night or during rain |
| Depth | Generally 2–3 m | Deeper pits last longer, but risk of pit collapse increases |
| User Ratio | Initially 1 per 50 persons; stabilized 1 per 20 persons | Long waiting times drive users back to open defecation |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The 30 m distance is not an absolute safety line, but a **baseline parameter for average soil.** In sand, gravel, or heavily fractured rock formations, water travels much further without being filtered, making 30 m insufficient, whereas in clay soil, a much shorter distance can be safe. The criterion for judgment is not distance itself, but **how rapidly water moves through the ground**; soil that drains quickly after rain requires a greater distance.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The requirement of 1.5 m above the water table is even more critical. If the bottom of the pit contacts groundwater, the filtration zone vanishes, turning the pit from a waste storage area into a **direct injection site for groundwater contamination.** In regions where the water table rises during wet seasons, judgment must be based on the peak wet-season water table level rather than dry-season levels.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Lifespan is calculated by volume. Accumulation rates vary according to conditions, but a standard baseline estimate is **0.05–0.09 m³ per person per year.** Wet pits decompose faster and accumulate less, whereas dumping anal cleansing materials other than paper increases accumulation significantly.

| Pit Dimensions | Effective Volume | Lifespan for a 5-Person Family |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Diameter 1 m, Depth 2 m | ~$1.6\text{ m}^3$ | ~4–6 years |
| 1 m × 1 m, Depth 3 m | ~$3.0\text{ m}^3$ | ~7–12 years |
| 1 m × 1 m, Depth 1 m | ~$1.0\text{ m}^3$ | ~2–4 years |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;When calculating lifespan, the top 0.5 m must be left empty. If the pit fills too close to the surface, flies and rainwater gain access, raising the risk of overflow. Once a pit reaches the end of its lifespan, it must be sealed with at least 30 cm of soil and its location clearly marked. Pathogens inside a sealed pit die off over time, but roundworm eggs can survive for years; therefore, it is vital never to dig up that location again.

### 2-2. Trenches and Catholes
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;During the initial days when tools and time to dig deep pits are lacking, shallow structures are utilized. The principle remains identical, differing only in scale.

| Method | Dimensions | Principle and Limitations |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cathole | Diameter & depth 15–20 cm, single-use | Shallow topsoil contains the highest microbial population for rapid decomposition. Must be completely covered after each use |
| Shallow Trench | Width 30 cm, depth 30–50 cm | Dug as a single channel, used progressively from one end, covering used sections immediately |
| Deep Trench | Width 50–80 cm, depth 1.5–2 m | Used for weeks to months. Wooden planks are laid across to prevent footings from collapsing |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Success or failure hinges on **whether covering soil is pre-positioned alongside the pit.** If soil is far away, people fail to cover their waste, transforming uncovered trenches into fly-breeding sites far more dangerous than open defecation.

### 2-3. Bucket Latrines and Absorbents
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In rocky terrain, areas with high water tables, or inside buildings, collecting waste in containers for transport is the only viable method. Here, the key element is not the container itself, but the **absorbent material applied after each use.** Absorbents accomplish three goals simultaneously: they absorb moisture to prevent leakage, cover the surface to block flies and odor, and supply carbon to aid decomposition. Odor consists mostly of hydrogen sulfide and ammonia produced via anaerobic decomposition; covering the surface and reducing moisture significantly decreases both gases.

| Absorbent | Moisture Absorption | Carbon Supply | Remarks |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Sawdust / Wood Shavings | Excellent | Excellent | Best performance, but difficult to obtain instantly in nature |
| Dry Leaves / Dry Grass | Good | Good | Crushing into finer pieces increases effectiveness |
| Rice Hulls / Straw | Good | Good | Bulky, requiring frequent emptying of containers |
| Dry Soil | Moderate | None | Most readily available; provides sufficient covering functionality |
| Wood Ash | Moderate | None | Alkaline properties aid odor suppression, though decomposition slows |
| Dry Peat Moss | Excellent | Moderate | Obtainable in wetland vicinities |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The required quantity is **enough to completely conceal the feces**, typically equaling 1–2 times the volume of the waste. Skimping on absorbents immediately brings back odors and flies, meaning the available supply of absorbents dictates the capacity limit of this method.

### 2-4. Why Separating Urine and Feces Improves Outcomes
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Urine from a healthy individual is virtually sterile inside the bladder, whereas pathogens causing problems in disaster sanitation reside almost exclusively in feces. Conversely, urine accounts for the overwhelming majority of total waste volume. These two facts form the rationale for urine-diverting systems.

| Item | Urine | Feces |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Daily Output per Person | ~1–1.5 L | ~100–250 g |
| Moisture Content | ≥95% | ~70–80% |
| Pathogen Load | Generally minimal | Extremely high |
| Key Components | Urea, mineral salts | Organic matter, microorganisms |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Mixing the two causes feces to absorb moisture from urine, creating a sloppy slurry. Wet organic matter enters an anaerobic state, generating foul-smelling compounds like hydrogen sulfide, expanding total volume to shorten pit lifespan, and preventing desiccation, which delays pathogen die-off. Conversely, separating them allows feces to lose moisture rapidly for accelerated drying and decomposition, while urine can be collected in sealed containers for separate disposal.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Odor chemistry also demonstrates the advantage of separation. Fresh urine has virtually no odor, but when urease enzymes from bacteria in feces decompose urea in urine, ammonia gas is generated, producing a pungent stench. Thus, **the primary cause of urine stench is contact with feces.** Urine-diverting structures reduce odor not by masking scents, but by preventing this chemical reaction from occurring in the first place.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;However, separated urine is not entirely risk-free. Urine from individuals with urinary tract infections may contain pathogens, and **contamination by feces during excretion is common.** If applied to agricultural crops, urine should be diluted 5–10 times with water, and harvesting should be avoided for at least one month after application as a standard safety margin.

### 2-5. Ash and Quicklime
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Sprinkling ash or lime over excreta is an ancient practice that is indeed effective. However, misunderstanding the magnitude and scope of its effectiveness poses severe risks.

| Material | Chemical Properties | Real-World Effect | Limitations |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Wood Ash | Primarily potassium carbonate, pH ~10–12 | Surface covering, moisture absorption, surface pH elevation, odor suppression | Acts only on contacted surfaces; fails to reach inside waste clumps |
| Slaked Lime (Calcium Hydroxide) | pH ~12.4 | Strong pH elevation; effective in bacterial die-off | Requires moisture to react; gradually neutralized by atmospheric $CO_2$ |
| Quicklime (Calcium Oxide) | Exothermic reaction with water | Heat generation, drying, powerful sterilization | Severe chemical/thermal burn risks to skin/eyes; spatters upon contact with water |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Raising pH kills pathogens through two mechanisms: strong alkalinity directly destroys microbial cell membranes and proteins, while simultaneously converting ammonium ions ($NH_4^+$) into un-ionized ammonia ($NH_3$). Un-ionized ammonia penetrates cell membranes easily, exerting far stronger germicidal efficacy. When lime is added to excreta, the die-off of bacteria and viruses is driven largely by this **ammonia effect** rather than pH alone.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Limitations are clear. Sprinkled ash and lime cover only the surface, leaving lower layers virtually unaffected; thus, if complete sterilization is intended, the mixture must be thoroughly blended. Roundworm eggs survive in high alkalinity for considerable periods. Overusing lime raises pH so high that decomposer microorganisms are killed as well, causing the pit contents to solidify without decomposing or reducing in volume. Quicklime reacts violently with water, generating intense heat with very real risks of severe burns.

> **Ash and lime serve as coverings and odor suppressants; sprinkling them alone should never be interpreted as complete disinfection.**
