## 1. Mechanisms Through Which Excreta Kill
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;A healthy adult excretes approximately 100–250 g of feces and 1–1.5 L of urine daily. Diarrhea patients discharge far greater volumes of fluid, with cholera cases sometimes exceeding 10 L per day. The fundamental danger lies not in volume, but in pathogen density. A single gram of feces from an infected person can harbor millions to billions of pathogens, whereas the dose required to establish an infection is only a minute fraction of that amount.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Under normal conditions, a four-tier barrier—flush toilets, sewer lines, wastewater treatment plants, and water purification facilities—dilutes and destroys this density. A disaster collapses all four tiers simultaneously. Power outages stop pumps, causing sewage to back up, while chlorine dosing at purification plants halts. At this point, the only barriers remaining are those erected directly by humans.

---
### 1-1. The Five Fs
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In sanitation science, the pathways through which fecal pathogens reach a new host's mouth are summarized in the **F-diagram**, named because each primary pathway begins with the letter F.

| Pathway | Description | Real-World Manifestation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Fluids | Drinking contaminated water or rinsing with it | Sewage-seeped wells, flooded rivers, submerged water tanks |
| Fingers | Traces on hands transfer to mouths or food | Post-defecation, post-childcare, or food preparation |
| Flies | Insects travel between feces and food | Flies landing on exposed excreta then alighting on dining plates |
| Fields | Soil becomes contaminated and contacts humans | Barefoot walking, children playing in dirt, unwashed soil-laden produce |
| Food | Pathways converge at food where pathogens multiply | Cooked food left sitting at room temperature |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Crucially, these pathways operate in **parallel.** Blocking one pathway while leaving others open allows disease outbreaks to persist. This explains why field interventions implementing only safe water supply, only latrines, or only handwashing typically achieve a diarrheal reduction rate plateauing around 30%. Implementing a single measure blocks only one-third of total transmission.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;However, all pathways share a **single point of origin**: exposed feces. Sanitation science therefore divides barriers into two distinct categories.

| Category | Location | Examples | Characteristics |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Primary Barrier | Point of origin | Latrines, safe containment, and burial of feces | Reduces transmission across all pathways simultaneously |
| Secondary Barrier | Immediately before destination | Water disinfection, handwashing, covering food, footwear | Must be erected separately for each individual pathway |

> **A single primary barrier accomplishes the work of multiple secondary barriers. This is why constructing latrines must take priority when resources are scarce.**

### 1-2. Waterborne Diseases
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The infectious doses in the table below represent approximate values for healthy adults. Individuals with reduced gastric acidity, malnourished persons, children, and the elderly become infected at far lower thresholds. Disaster settings typically combine all these vulnerability factors.

| Disease | Pathogen | Approx. Infectious Dose | Incubation Period | Characteristics |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Cholera | *Vibrio cholerae* | $10^6$–$10^8$ cells (far lower on empty stomach / low acid) | Hours to 5 days | Rice-water diarrhea; fatal dehydration within hours |
| Bacillary Dysentery | *Shigella* | 10–100 cells | 1–3 days | Bloody stool; strong direct person-to-person transmission |
| Typhoid Fever | *Salmonella* Typhi | ~$10^5$ cells | 6–30 days | Characterized by high fever; chronic carrier state post-recovery |
| Hepatitis A | Hepatitis A virus | 10–100 particles | 15–50 days | Jaundice; long incubation period complicates source tracing |
| Rota / Norovirus | Rotavirus, Norovirus | 10–100 particles | 12 hours to 3 days | Major contributor to child diarrhea mortality; vomit is infectious |
| Cryptosporidiosis | *Cryptosporidium* | 10–30 oocysts | 2–10 days | Oocysts exhibit extreme resistance to chlorine disinfection |
| Ascariasis | *Ascaris lumbricoides* | Few eggs | Several weeks | Eggs survive in soil for years |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;What should be gathered from this table is not mere disease names, but **two structural axes.** First, pathogens with short incubation periods (cholera, dysentery) spread explosively, whereas those with long incubation periods (typhoid, hepatitis A) manifest only after widespread dissemination has already occurred; waiting for symptoms to appear means intervention is already late. Second, certain pathogens—such as *Cryptosporidium* oocysts and roundworm eggs—are **highly resistant to chemical disinfection.** Chlorine disinfection effectively eliminates bacteria and most viruses but is weak against protozoan oocysts, while roundworm eggs withstand chlorine, desiccation, and low temperatures alike. For these pathogens, physical containment, filtration, and boiling are the only effective solutions.

### 1-3. Interrupting Transmission Pathways
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The five pathways converge in practice into three main channels: **waterways, hands, and flies.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;**Waterways** involve gravity and soil permeability. Pathogens originating from feces migrate across the surface or infiltrate soil layers. Soil acts as an excellent natural filter, binding and killing most bacteria within a few meters of travel, provided the flow passes through an **unsaturated soil layer.** Within a fully saturated layer—below the water table—filtration largely fails, allowing pathogens to travel tens of meters unhindered. In gravel beds or fractured limestone/granite formations, migration distances extend far further. This physical reality forms the rationale for setback distance regulations.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;**Hands** involve contact and persistence. Fecal contamination on hands dries quickly and becomes invisible to the naked eye, yet pathogens persist. The undersides of fingernails, spaces between fingers, and the backs of hands are most frequently missed during hasty washing. The hand pathway is especially dangerous because a single food handler's hands can contaminate food consumed by dozens of people.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;**Flies** involve mobility and amplification. Houseflies fly hundreds of meters between feces and food, carrying pathogens on their legs and body hairs. Furthermore, exposed feces serves as a breeding site for maggots, allowing a single pile of exposed excreta to multiply fly populations hundredfold within days. **Covering feces** is incomparably more effective than attempting to chase away flies.

| Pathway | Physical Characteristics | Most Effective Intervention |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Waterways | Gravity, permeability, filtration distance | Burial lower than and far from water sources; separation from water table |
| Hands | Contact, surface persistence | Running water and physical friction (soap, ash, or sand) |
| Flies | Flight mobility, rapid breeding | Covering feces with soil/ash; placing lids over latrine pits |

### 1-4. Why Dead Bodies Are Not the Primary Cause of Epidemics
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;One of the most widespread misconceptions in disaster reporting is that "cadavers cause epidemics." The World Health Organization (WHO), Pan American Health Organization (PAHO), and International Committee of the Red Cross (ICRC) have repeatedly disproved the notion that bodies resulting from natural disasters trigger disease outbreaks. The pathological reason is straightforward:

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Epidemics are driven by **living infected individuals** shedding pathogens into the environment. Victims of earthquakes or floods die predominantly from physical trauma or drowning, not infectious disease; their bodies generally do not harbor epidemic pathogens to begin with. Furthermore, most human pathogens perish rapidly following host death due to falling body temperature and cessation of nutrient supply. The malodorous gases released during decomposition are unpleasant, but they do not constitute infectious biohazards.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This misconception is harmful because panic leads to hasty mass burials or cremations, **sacrificing victim identification.** Unidentified fatalities impose lifelong distress on surviving families and result in severe practical complications regarding inheritance, insurance, and legal status. Forcing hasty disposal when no actual public health necessity exists is the greatest harm caused by this myth.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This does not mean cadaver handling carries zero risk. The actual remaining risks are specific and manageable:

| Actual Risk | Description | Required Countermeasure |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Water source contamination by body fluids | Feces or body fluids leaking from cadavers entering rivers or wells | Burial far from water sources and strictly above the water table |
| Contact infection among handlers | Tuberculosis, bloodborne viruses, gastrointestinal pathogens | Gloves or plastic barriers; mandatory handwashing after handling; covering open cuts |
| Epidemic fatalities | Deaths caused specifically by cholera, Ebola, Lassa fever, etc. | These specific cases require quarantine and specialized disinfection protocols |
| Psychological impact | Unrecovered or exposed cadavers severe mental distress and collapse of community functioning | Dignified recovery and handling serves as a primary psychosocial intervention |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Burial site selection follows the exact same logic as latrine placement for the living: a minimum distance of 30 m from water sources, situated well above the water table, and topographically lower than living quarters and water supplies. Burial depth must ensure at least 1 m of soil covers the body to prevent scavengers from disturbing the site. Individual burials should be performed whenever possible, with locations, physical descriptions, and personal belongings carefully documented.
