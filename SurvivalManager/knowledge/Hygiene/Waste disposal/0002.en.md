## 1. How Human Waste Kills People
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;A healthy adult excretes about 100-250 g of feces and about 1-1.5 L of urine per day. A patient with diarrhea excretes far more fluid, and with cholera it can exceed 10 L a day. The problem is not the volume but the density. 1 g of feces from an infected person contains millions to billions of pathogens, and the amount needed for infection is only a tiny fraction of that.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In ordinary times, four layers of barriers — the flush toilet, the sewer pipe, the sewage treatment plant, and the water purification plant — dilute and destroy this density. A disaster brings all four layers down at once. When the electricity is cut, pumps stop, sewage flows back, and chlorine injection at the water plant also stops. What remains is only the barrier people build themselves.

---
### 1-1. The Five F's
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The routes by which pathogens in feces reach another person's mouth are organized in the F-diagram, in which every route begins with the letter F in English.

| Route | Description | What it looks like in real situations |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Fluids (water) | Drinking contaminated water or rinsing with it | Wells seeped with sewage, flooded rivers, inundated cisterns |
| Fingers (hands) | A trace on the hands is carried to the mouth or food | Hands after using the toilet, hands after cleaning a child, cooking hands |
| Flies | Insects travel back and forth between feces and food | A fly that sat on exposed feces lands on a dish |
| Fields (soil) | The soil becomes contaminated and people come into contact with it | Bare feet, children playing in the dirt, vegetables with soil on them |
| Food | The above routes merge in food and multiply | Cooked food left at room temperature |

- The routes are parallel. Even if one route is blocked, the disease continues as long as the others remain alive. This is why the reduction in diarrheal disease stays at around 30% when only safe drinking water supply, only latrines, or only handwashing is introduced. Doing just one blocks only a third.
- The routes share a single starting point: exposed feces. This is why barriers are divided into two grades.

| Grade | Position | Example | Nature |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Primary barrier | Starting point | Latrines, safe isolation and burial of feces | Reduces all routes at once |
| Secondary barrier | Just before the destination | Water disinfection, handwashing, covering food, footwear | Must be built separately for each route |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;One primary barrier does the share of several secondary barriers. The scarcer the resources, the more the latrine comes first.

### 1-2. Waterborne Diseases
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The infectious doses are rough values for a healthy adult. People with weak stomach acid, people in poor nutritional condition, children, and the elderly are infected by far smaller amounts, and disaster situations usually have all of these conditions.

| Disease | Pathogen | Approximate infectious dose | Incubation period | Features |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Cholera | Vibrio cholerae | 10⁶-10⁸ organisms (much fewer on an empty stomach or with antacids) | A few hours-5 days | Rice-water diarrhea, death from dehydration possible within hours |
| Bacillary dysentery | Shigella | 10-100 organisms | 1-3 days | Bloody stool, strong direct person-to-person transmission |
| Typhoid fever | Salmonella Typhi | About 10⁵ organisms | 6-30 days | Mainly high fever, carriers arise after recovery |
| Hepatitis A | Hepatitis A virus | 10-100 organisms | 15-50 days | Jaundice, long incubation makes tracing the cause hard |
| Rotavirus and norovirus | Rotavirus, Norovirus | 10-100 organisms | 12 hours-3 days | A major share of child diarrhea deaths, vomit is also a source of transmission |
| Cryptosporidium | Cryptosporidium | 10-30 organisms | 2-10 days | Cysts strongly resist chlorine disinfection |
| Roundworm | Ascaris lumbricoides | A few eggs | A few weeks | Eggs survive in soil for years |

- Those with short incubation periods (cholera, dysentery) spread explosively, and those with long ones (typhoid, hepatitis A) show up only after they have already spread. By the time symptoms appear, it is already too late.
- Cryptosporidium cysts and roundworm eggs are not easily killed by disinfection. Chlorine disinfection is strong against bacteria and most viruses but weak against protozoan cysts, and roundworm eggs withstand chlorine, drying, and cold. For these pathogens the answer is not chemical disinfection but physical isolation, filtration, and boiling.

---
### 1-3. Blocking the Infection Routes
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The five routes actually converge into three branches: water paths, hands, and flies.

- Water paths: Pathogens in feces travel flowing over the surface or passing through the soil. Soil is an excellent filter, so bacteria are usually adsorbed and die while passing a few meters, but this filtering works only by passing through an unsaturated soil layer. In a layer full of water (below the groundwater table), almost no filtering occurs and they travel tens of meters intact. In gravel layers or in limestone and granite regions with developed cracks, they travel much farther. This is the basis of the separation distance rules.
- Hands: Feces on the hands soon dry and become invisible, but the pathogens remain. Under the fingernails, between the fingers, and the backs of the hands are the places most often missed in a casual wash. It is dangerous because one cook's hands lead to the food of dozens of people.
- Flies: Houseflies fly hundreds of meters from feces to food, carrying pathogens on their legs and body hairs. The feces itself is a breeding ground for maggots, so a single pile of exposed feces multiplies the fly population hundreds of times in a few days. Covering the feces is overwhelmingly more effective than chasing off flies.

| Route | Physical property | Most effective block |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Water paths | Gravity, permeability, filtration distance | Burial lower than and far from the water source, separation from the groundwater table |
| Hands | Contact, residue | Running water and friction (soap, or ash or sand) |
| Flies | Flight, breeding | Cover the feces with soil or ash and put a lid on the hole |

### 1-4. Corpses and Epidemics
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The WHO, PAHO, and the International Committee of the Red Cross have repeatedly denied the common belief that corpses from natural disasters cause epidemics.

- Epidemics spread when living infected people spread pathogens. Most people who die in earthquakes or floods died of trauma or drowning, and their bodies have no pathogens that would cause an epidemic in the first place.
- Most pathogens die quickly once the host dies, because body temperature falls and the nutrient supply stops. The substances that cause the smell of decay are unpleasant, but they are not infectious pathogens in themselves.
- The harm of this misconception: out of fear, corpses are hastily buried in mass graves or burned, and identification is given up. Unidentified dead leave a lifelong problem for the bereaved family and lead to real harm such as inheritance, insurance, and legal status. The greatest harm is that it makes people rush when there is no reason to rush.

| Actual risk | Content | Response |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Water source contamination by body fluids | Feces and fluids leaking from the corpse flow into rivers or wells | Bury it far from water sources and above the groundwater table |
| Contact infection of handlers | Tuberculosis, bloodborne viruses, digestive pathogens | Gloves or plastic bags, always wash hands after handling, cover wounds |
| Deaths from infectious disease | Death from high-risk infectious diseases such as cholera, Ebola, and Lassa fever | Exceptionally, isolation and disinfection are needed |
| Psychological impact | Abandoned corpses destroy survivors' mental health and community function | Dignified recovery is itself the response |

- The burial location is the same as the latrine standard. At least 30 m from water sources, well above the groundwater table, and on the topographically lower side than dwellings and water sources.
- Cover the body with at least 1 m of soil so that animals cannot dig it up.
- If possible, bury individually and record the location, physical appearance, and belongings.
