## Introduction
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In large-scale disasters, human mortality occurs in two distinct waves. The first is immediate mortality caused by earthquakes, floods, or explosions themselves. The second is the wave of diarrheal diseases arriving days to weeks later. This second curve arrives later than the first but persists much longer; in uncontrolled refugee scenarios, historical records repeatedly document that it claims more lives than the initial disaster itself. Furthermore, this second curve stems almost entirely from **the problem of human feces finding its way back into human mouths.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This issue is exceptionally dangerous because the required infectious dose is extremely small. Ingestion of a mere 10–100 *Shigella* bacteria, 10–100 rotaviruses, or a few dozen Hepatitis A viruses is sufficient to establish an infection. Visible contamination is not required; an **amount completely invisible to the naked eye** is fully sufficient. Conversely, this means that properly erecting even a physically thin barrier can break the transmission chain. Disaster sanitation ultimately concerns where and by what principles to erect these barriers.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This document is not intended for memorizing the steps to construct a latrine, but rather for understanding **why the distance must be 30 meters, why the pit must be 1.5 meters above the water table, and why sprinkling ash does not kill all pathogens.** Understanding these underlying principles enables sound judgment even when available materials and terrain differ from textbooks. Assuming scenarios where electricity and municipal water/sewerage systems are cut off, this document outlines barriers that can be constructed using only materials obtainable from nature.
