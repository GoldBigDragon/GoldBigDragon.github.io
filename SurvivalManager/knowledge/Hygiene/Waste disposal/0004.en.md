## 3. Stabilization and Remaining Transmission Pathways
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;While the preceding chapter focused on isolating excreta, this chapter covers rendering contained waste harmless and addressing transmission pathways that latrines alone cannot block.

---
### 3-1. Composting
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Composting latrines mix excreta with carbon sources to foster decomposition by aerobic microorganisms. During this process, metabolic heat generated by microorganisms raises the internal temperature of the compost pile, and it is this heat that destroys pathogens. In essence, the agent of sterilization in composting is **temperature, not the microorganisms themselves.**

| Pathogen | Approximate Inactivation Conditions |
| --- | --- |
| Bacteria (*E. coli*, *Salmonella*, etc.) | 55°C for several hours |
| Enteric Viruses | 55–60°C for several hours |
| Protozoan Oocysts | 55°C for several hours |
| Roundworm (*Ascaris*) Eggs | 55°C for ~1 hour, >60°C for several minutes; survives for months below 45°C |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Roundworm eggs serve as the practical benchmark: if roundworm eggs are destroyed, virtually all other pathogens have perished; if roundworm eggs survive, composting has failed. WHO guidelines recommend maintaining temperatures **above 50°C for at least one week** for thermal composting. Any compost heap that fails to meet this condition remains raw excreta, not usable compost.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The operational challenge in disaster scenarios is that achieving these temperatures is difficult. For temperatures to rise, the heap must be large enough to insulate itself (at least ~$1\text{ m}^3$), moisture content must be kept at 50–60%, the carbon-to-nitrogen ratio must be roughly 25–30:1, and the pile must be turned regularly to supply oxygen. Failing any single factor—cold winter ambient temperatures, small containers, excess moisture, or carbon deficiency—prevents the temperature from rising.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;If high temperatures cannot be secured, the remaining alternative is time: sealing the waste and storing it long-term. Storage for over one year at ambient temperatures of 20–35°C eliminates most viruses and protozoa, yet **roundworm eggs may still survive.** Therefore, excreta treated via low-temperature storage should be applied safely only to low-contact vegetation—such as fruit trees, fodder crops, or firewood timber—rather than crops intended for direct human consumption.

### 3-2. Mechanics of Handwashing
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Removing pathogens from hands relies on three physical forces: **friction** to detach pathogens from skin, **surfactant action** to dislodge grease-bound particles, and **running water** to flush detached pathogens away from the hands. Omitting any one of these three drastically reduces overall efficacy.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The third force is most commonly omitted. Dipping and rubbing hands in a basin of standing water utilizes friction and surfactant action, but detached pathogens remain suspended in the water and reattach upon lifting the hands. When multiple individuals share the same basin, the basin itself becomes a vector for cross-contamination.

> **In handwashing, what matters more than water volume is whether water flows unidirectional away from the hands. Submerging hands in standing water is not handwashing; it is sharing pathogens.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The **tippy tap** represents a low-resource mechanism implementing this principle with minimal water. It consists of a suspended water container tilted via a foot pedal to dispense a fine stream of water. Though simple in structure, it solves three challenges simultaneously: running water prevents recontamination, foot operation eliminates touching faucets to avoid contact contamination, and the fine stream uses only ~100 mL per wash. Compared to using >500 mL in a basin, water consumption drops to a fraction.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;When soap is unavailable, abrasive materials serve as substitutes. While they cannot replace the chemical surfactant action of soap, coarse particles increase physical friction, which, combined with running water, compensates significantly.

| Substitute | Mechanism | Efficacy and Cautions |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Wood Ash | Alkaline; fine particles provide high friction. Creates mild lye with water for slight surfactant action | Rated as the closest substitute to soap. Use fine ash and rinse thoroughly |
| Fine Sand | Provides friction only | Use sand fine enough to avoid skin abrasion |
| Soil | Friction and particle adsorption | Avoid surface soil prone to fecal contamination; use subsoil |
| Lye Solution | Strong surfactant action | Intended for laundry and dishwashing; must be heavily diluted for skin use |
| Saponin Plants (Soapberry, Soapwort, etc.) | Natural surfactants | Highly useful if available, though local availability varies |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Optimal handwashing timing is determined by physical principles: transmission is blocked most efficiently **immediately after pathogens contaminate hands** and **immediately before entry into the mouth.** Post-defecation, post-childcare, and post-patient care belong to the former, while pre-cooking, pre-eating, and pre-wound care belong to the latter.

### 3-3. Techniques for Fly Control
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Effective fly control focuses on **breaking the reproductive cycle**, not killing adult flies. Housefly eggs hatch within a day under favorable conditions, and maggots mature into adult flies in about a week. A single exposed pile of feces or food waste can spawn hundreds of flies within days.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Hence, three primary physical intervention points exist: covering feces immediately to prevent egg-laying, placing lids over latrine openings to block entry and exit, and burying or burning food scraps daily. Installing a simple lid is vastly more effective than applying insecticides. Chasing adult flies remains merely a secondary measure after these three primary controls are established.

### 3-4. Menstrual Hygiene
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Menstruation does not halt during disasters, yet hygiene supplies are among the first to be disrupted. Neglecting this domain leads to infections, mobility restrictions, and latrine avoidance, causing all preceding sanitation barriers to collapse together.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;When utilizing reusable cloths, the critical requirement is **complete drying.** Folding cloths while damp fosters rapid bacterial and fungal proliferation, presenting direct infection risks. Washing must begin with a cold-water rinse to remove blood proteins; using hot water first coagulates proteins, binding them permanently to fabric fibers. Subsequently, wash with soap or mild lye solution and dry thoroughly under direct sunlight. Sunlight provides simultaneous drying and UV sterilization, outperforming shade drying.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Natural absorbent materials include boiled and dried cotton fabric, clean dry moss, and soft dry grass; however, because all carry risks of irritation and contamination, boiled cotton fabric should be prioritized for direct skin contact. Used disposable items must be handled with the same safety protocol as feces and buried or incinerated.

### 3-5. Solid Waste Management
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Solid waste management is less immediately urgent than excreta disposal, yet neglected waste creates breeding grounds for flies and rodents, neutralizing primary sanitation barriers. The two primary disposal options are incineration and burial, with the core decision resting on what can be safely burned.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Open campfire temperatures generally range between 600–800°C with uneven oxygen supply, representing **incomplete combustion.** Under these conditions, certain synthetic materials transform into compounds far more toxic than their original state. Industrial incinerators maintain high temperatures and long residence times specifically to prevent these hazardous transformations.

| Category | Items | Rationale |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Burnable | Paper, dry wood, dry grass, natural fibers (cotton, linen) | Composed primarily of carbon, hydrogen, and oxygen, producing simple combustion byproducts |
| Non-Burnable | PVC (wire insulation, vinyl flooring, certain pipes/packaging) | Contains chlorine; low-temperature incomplete combustion generates highly toxic, bioaccumulative dioxins and furans |
| Non-Burnable | Foamed Plastics (polyurethane sponge, mattresses, insulation) | Contains nitrogen; combustion releases hydrogen cyanide, a primary fatal gas in fires |
| Non-Burnable | Styrofoam, tires, painted/treated timber | Releases aromatic hydrocarbons, heavy metals, arsenic, and chromium compounds in smoke and ash |
| Non-Burnable | Batteries, fluorescent tubes, paint cans, sealed containers, spray cans | Poses heavy metal contamination or explosion hazards |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;When classification is uncertain, apply a simple rule of thumb: **materials originating directly from nature are generally burnable, whereas human-synthesized materials are non-burnable.** Non-burnable waste must be buried following the same setback criteria as pit latrines: at least 30 m from water sources, above the water table, and topographically lower than water supplies. Items containing heavy metals, such as batteries and paint, must be segregated and clearly marked. Because non-biodegradable substances persist in soil, unrecorded burial sites present severe long-term environmental hazards once conditions recover.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Food waste is better buried than burned due to high moisture content causing excessive smoke; soil microorganisms decompose organic waste effectively. Covering food waste with at least 30 cm of soil simultaneously prevents fly oviposition and rodent access.
