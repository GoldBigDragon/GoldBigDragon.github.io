## 3. Stabilization and the Remaining Routes
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This covers making contained feces harmless, and the remaining routes a latrine cannot block (hands, flies, menstruation, garbage).

---
### 3-1. Composting
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;A composting latrine mixes feces with a carbon source and lets aerobic microorganisms break it down. The metabolic heat of the microorganisms raises the temperature inside the pile, and that heat kills pathogens. What disinfects is not the microorganisms but the temperature.

| Pathogen | Kill conditions (approximate) |
| --- | --- |
| Bacteria such as E. coli and Salmonella | A few hours at 55 °C |
| Enteric viruses | A few hours at 55-60 °C |
| Protozoan cysts | A few hours at 55 °C |
| Roundworm eggs | About 1 hour at 55 °C, a few minutes at 60 °C and above, survive for months at 45 °C and below |

- Roundworm eggs are effectively the standard. If the roundworm eggs are dead, nearly everything else is dead, and if they are alive, the composting has failed.
- WHO recommendation: thermal composting maintains 50 °C or more for at least one week. A pile that fails to meet this condition is not compost but still feces.
- For the temperature to rise, the pile must be big enough to insulate itself (at least about 1 m³), moisture must be 50-60%, the carbon to nitrogen ratio must be roughly 25-30 to 1, and it must be turned to supply oxygen. Any single one of winter, a small container, excess moisture, or a shortage of carbon source keeps the temperature from rising.
- If you cannot secure temperature, use time. That means sealing it and leaving it for a long time. Stored for one year or more at room temperature of 20-35 °C, most viruses and protozoa die but roundworm eggs may remain. Feces treated by low-temperature storage should be used not on vegetables eaten raw but where contact is low, such as fruit trees, fodder crops, and trees for firewood.

---
### 3-2. Handwashing
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There are three forces that remove pathogens from hands. Friction detaches them from the skin, surfactant action pulls off what is stuck to the oil film, and running water carries what was detached off the hands. If even one is missing, the effect drops greatly.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The one most often missing is running water. If you fill a basin, dip your hands in, and rub them, friction and surfactant action work, but the detached pathogens float in that water and reattach when you take your hands out. When several people use the same basin, the basin itself becomes a source of transmission.

> **Caution:** In handwashing, what matters more than the amount of water is whether the water flows away in one direction. Dipping your hands in standing water is not handwashing but sharing pathogens.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The structure that implements this principle with minimal water is the tippy tap. A container of water is hung by a string, and when you step on a pedal it tilts and a thin stream of water comes out.

- Because the water is running, there is no recontamination.
- There is no need to touch a faucet with your hands, so there is no contact contamination.
- The stream is thin, so around 100 ml is enough for one wash (a basin needs 500 ml or more).

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;When there is no soap, the substitute is an abrasive. It cannot completely replace soap's surfactant function, but if you increase friction with coarse particles and rinse off with running water, much of it is made up.

| Substitute | Principle | Effect and cautions |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Wood ash | Alkaline, with high friction from fine particles. On meeting water it becomes a weak lye, and some surfactant action also appears | The closest substitute for soap. Use fine ash and rinse completely |
| Fine sand | Provides friction only | Use sand fine enough not to damage the hands |
| Soil | Friction and particle adsorption | Avoid surface soil that may be contaminated with feces, and use soil from below the surface layer |
| Lye | Strong surfactant action | For laundry and dishes. For hands, use it greatly diluted |
| Saponin plants (honey locust pods, soapwort, etc.) | Natural surfactant | Useful if available, but hard to obtain in some regions |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The most efficient times to wash are right after pathogens get onto the hands and right before they enter the mouth.

- Right after they get on: after using the toilet, after cleaning a child, after contact with a patient
- Right before they go in: before cooking, before eating, before treating a wound

---
### 3-3. Blocking Flies
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Fly control is not about killing adults but about breaking the breeding chain. Housefly eggs hatch within a day under good conditions, and maggots become adults in about a week. A single pile of exposed feces or food waste produces hundreds in a few days.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There are three blocking points. Cover feces immediately to prevent egg-laying, put a lid on the latrine hole to block entry and exit, and bury or burn food scraps every day. One lid is more effective than insecticide. Chasing off adults is a supplementary measure after all three of these are done.

---
### 3-4. Menstrual Hygiene
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Menstruation does not stop in a disaster, but the supplies to deal with it are the first to run out. Left unaddressed, it leads to infection and restricted activity and makes people avoid using the latrine itself, so the barriers described above collapse along with it.

- The key to reusable cloth is complete drying. If it is folded while wet, bacteria and mold multiply and it becomes an infection risk.
- Washing order: first rinse in cold water to remove blood proteins (hot water used first coagulates the proteins and fixes them into the fibers). Wash with soap or a weak lye. Dry completely in direct sunlight. Sunlight dries and disinfects with ultraviolet light at the same time, so it is better than shade drying.
- Natural materials that can be used as absorbents are boiled and dried cotton fabric, clean dry moss, and soft dry grass. All carry a possibility of irritation and contamination, so for the surface that touches the body, use boiled cotton fabric whenever possible.
- Treat used items at the same grade as feces and bury or burn them.

---
### 3-5. General Waste
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Garbage is less urgent than human waste, but if left alone it becomes a breeding ground for flies and rats and neutralizes the barriers above. The disposal methods are incineration and burial, and the key is what can be burned.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The combustion temperature of a household bonfire is generally 600-800 °C, and the oxygen supply is also uneven. This means incomplete combustion, and some substances are turned into substances far more dangerous than the originals.

| Category | Items | Reason |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Can be burned | Paper, dry wood, dry grass, natural fibers (cotton, linen) | Mainly carbon, hydrogen, and oxygen, so combustion products are relatively simple |
| Must not be burned | PVC (wire insulation, vinyl flooring, some pipes and packaging) | Contains chlorine, so incomplete low-temperature combustion produces dioxins and furans. Highly toxic in trace amounts and accumulates in the body |
| Must not be burned | Foamed plastics (polyurethane sponge, mattresses, insulation) | Contains nitrogen, so combustion releases hydrogen cyanide. A leading cause of fire deaths |
| Must not be burned | Styrofoam, tires, painted wood, preservative-treated wood | Aromatic compounds, heavy metals, and arsenic and chromium compounds are released in smoke and ash |
| Must not be burned | Batteries, fluorescent lamps, paint cans, sealed containers, spray cans | Heavy metal release or explosion risk |

- A rule of thumb when it is hard to tell: what was originally in nature can be burned, and what people synthesized generally must not be burned.
- What cannot be burned is buried. The burial location uses the same criteria as the latrine pit (at least 30 m from water sources, above the groundwater table, on the topographically lower side than water sources).
- Items containing heavy metals, such as batteries and paint, are collected separately and marked. They do not break down in soil, and the worst case is that after the situation recovers, nobody knows what was buried in that spot.
- Bury food waste rather than burning it. It has a lot of moisture, so it burns poorly and only smokes, and when buried, soil microorganisms break it down. Covering it with at least 30 cm of soil blocks fly egg-laying and rat access at the same time.
