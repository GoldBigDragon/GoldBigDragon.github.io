## 3. Material Dictates the Response
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The most practical branching point in mold remediation is not which chemical agent is selected, but **whether the surface absorbs water.** Because the main body of mold consists of hyphae extending deep into the substrate, whether the material allows hyphal penetration determines if the issue can be resolved by wiping or requires complete disposal.

---
### 3-1. Non-porous vs. Porous
| Category | Materials | Hyphal Penetration | Countermeasure |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Non-porous | Glass, metal, glazed tile, hard plastic, ceramics | Surface attachment only | Scrub clean and dry completely |
| Semi-porous | Wood, concrete, brick, mortar | Shallow penetration beneath surface | Scrape or plane away, followed by thorough drying |
| Porous | Drywall (gypsum board), insulation, ceiling tiles, carpet, fabric, paper, books | Deep internal penetration | Removal and disposal is the rule |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Wiping porous materials is futile due to three overlapping factors. First, because hyphae reside deep inside the material, they survive no matter how thoroughly the surface is wiped. Second, cleaning solutions absorb into the material, **introducing additional moisture into the core.** Third, even if the mold is killed, dead spores and hyphal fragments remain as potent allergens. Sterilization does not eliminate allergic reactions.

> **Mold on drywall and insulation is not something to be wiped, but cut out and removed. Applying cleaning liquid merely introduces moisture into it once more.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Fabrics and clothing fall in between. Cotton and linen that can be boiled can often be salvaged through boiling water, alkaline washing, and thorough sun-drying, though stains may persist. Conversely, stuffed bedding, mattresses, cushions, and leather rarely recover because their interior cannot dry completely. Books and paper will not spread further if completely dried and brushed with a soft brush, though existing stains and weakened fibers cannot be restored.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;When cutting out porous materials, set the removal boundary **wider than the visible mold area.** Hyphae extend far beyond visible spots, and the scope of moisture penetration is invariably larger than the discolored zone. Cut sections should be wrapped in plastic or cloth immediately at the site to prevent airborne spores from contaminating the rest of the dwelling during transport.

---
### 3-2. Cleaning and Inhibition
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;**Killing mold is fundamentally different from physically removing it.** Mechanically scrubbing away surface hyphae and spores with a brush and water remains the most reliable primary measure. The agents listed below serve as secondary aids to reduce residual populations and delay recurrence.

| Material | Operating Principle | Real-World Scope of Effect | Limitations |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Vinegar (5% Acetic Acid) | Disrupts cell membranes and enzymes through acidity | Effective against certain species; suited for non-porous surfaces | Ineffective against resistant species; fails to reach deep porous layers |
| Saltwater (Saturated) | Lowers water activity via osmotic pressure | Inhibition and preservation | Does not kill; salt residue remains upon drying |
| Lye (Potassium Carbonate) | Strong alkali denatures proteins and lipids | Strong degreasing; removes grease along with mold | Corrodes skin and metal; requires thorough rinsing |
| Boric Acid / Borax | Disrupts metabolism and leaves protective residue | Penetrates wood for long-term inhibition | Toxic; prohibited around food and children |
| Alcohol (70%) | Denatures proteins and dissolves cell membranes | Fast-acting surface sterilization | Rapidly evaporates; leaves no residual effect |
| Sunlight (UV) | Damages DNA of surface spores | Surfaces of hung clothing and dried objects | Cannot reach shaded or internal layers |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;While vinegar is widely used, it is not a universal cure. Acetic acid is effective against certain mold species but almost useless against others, and its efficacy drops rapidly when diluted with water, meaning it must be used near full strength to be meaningful. However, because it leaves virtually no toxic residue and poses minimal harm beyond odor, it is a reasonable choice for inhabited spaces.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Sunlight represents the cheapest remedy in disaster situations. However, **drying and elevated temperature actually accomplish far more than UV rays.** Because UV radiation cannot penetrate thick materials, clothing must be turned inside out, books stood open with separated pages, and, above all, left exposed until completely dry.

---
### 3-3. Principles of Bleach
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Sodium hypochlorite bleach is a powerful disinfectant on non-porous surfaces. However, on porous materials, water absorbs inward while chlorine remains on the surface, causing efficacy to plummet while introducing unwanted moisture into the core. This explains why the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency discourages routine use of biocides in mold cleanup. Scrubbing with detergent and water followed by complete drying remains the standard baseline, while bleach should be restricted to smooth, impermeable surfaces like tiles, toilets, and countertops.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Furthermore, there is a crucial taboo that must never be broken:

> **Mixing bleach with ammonia-containing detergents produces chloramine gas, while mixing it with vinegar or acidic cleaners produces chlorine gas. Both gases can be fatal in confined spaces.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;These accidents frequently occur without any intentional mixing. Applying a second detergent immediately onto a floor cleaned with bleach, reusing a bucket without rinsing, or using two products sequentially in a bathroom is fully sufficient to trigger a reaction. Because urine also contains ammonia, pouring bleach into stagnant urine causes the exact same chemical hazard. **Using one cleaning product at a time and rinsing thoroughly with water before applying another** is the sole safe rule.

---
### 3-4. Respiratory Protection During Work
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Mold remediation exposes individuals to vastly higher spore concentrations than merely occupying a moldy space. Scrubbing, tearing, and cutting release massive quantities of spores into the air.

| Protection Measure | Rationale |
| --- | --- |
| Wet Surfaces First | Dampening surfaces with water or cleaning solution prevents spores from becoming airborne. This is the single most effective measure |
| Ventilation | Open doors and windows to direct airflow outward. Directing airflow inward spreads contamination throughout the house |
| Respiratory Protection | Cover nose and mouth with multiple layers of cloth at minimum, while understanding its physical limits |
| Eye & Skin Protection | Spores, alkali, and chlorine irritate eyes and mucous membranes; wear goggles and gloves |
| Post-Work Hygiene | Strip work clothing immediately on site for separate washing, and wash the body thoroughly |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The extent to which improvised cloth masks filter out spores, and **why they are useless against gaseous compounds** responsible for mold odors, are separate technical topics. Rather than relying solely on upgrading mask filtration, **dampening surfaces to prevent spores from launching into the air initially** remains far more effective.
