## 1. Conditions for Mold Growth
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Mold is a fungus, not a plant. Because it cannot perform photosynthesis, it must decompose organic matter to obtain nutrients, and that decomposition strictly requires water. Spores wait in a dormant, dry state for years, germinating once suitable conditions are met. Therefore, dealing with mold **is not a fight to eliminate spores, but a fight to deny them the necessary conditions.** Eliminating spores altogether is impossible to begin with.

---
### 1-1. Conditions Required for Germination
| Condition | Threshold | Real-World Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Moisture | Surface Relative Humidity ≥ 70% | Proliferation accelerates exponentially above 80% |
| Temperature | Generally 0–50°C, Optimal 20–30°C | Temperatures comfortable for humans are optimal for mold |
| Nutrients | Cellulose, starch, protein, lipids | Paper, wood, paste, dust, sebum, and food residue are fully sufficient |
| Time | 24–48 hours | Beyond this window, visible colonies begin to take form |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In reality, temperature and nutrients are practically uncontrollable variables. Temperatures tolerable to humans suit mold equally well, and a single layer of dust provides ample nutrients. The reason mold blooms even on inert surfaces like cement or glass is that it feeds on the dust and sebum settled on those surfaces. **Ultimately, moisture is the single variable humans can actually manipulate.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Regarding moisture, what matters is **surface relative humidity**, not air humidity. Mold does not feed on water suspended in the air; it feeds on moisture clinging to surfaces. Thus, even if a hygrometer in the center of a room reads 55%, the surface relative humidity on a cold, north-facing wall can easily exceed 90%. This explains why peeling back wallpaper often reveals a single pitch-black wall while the rest of the room appears pristine.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Another critical metric is **water activity ($a_w$)**. Represented on a scale from 0 to 1, it measures how freely available water within a material is for biological use. Most molds generally require a water activity of 0.8 or higher, though xerophilic molds like *Aspergillus* can grow around 0.7. Foods preserved with salt or sugar resist spoilage because those substances bind water molecules to lower the water activity, not because salt directly kills mold.

---
### 1-2. The 24 to 48 Hour Window
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;If wet materials are **dried within 24 to 48 hours following flooding or leaks, mold will fail to grow in most cases.** This time frame is not arbitrary; it derives directly from the time required for a spore to absorb moisture, germinate, and extend hyphae.

| Elapsed Time | Occurring Events |
| --- | --- |
| 0–24 hours | Spores absorb water and prepare to germinate. Nothing is visible yet |
| 24–48 hours | Germination and hyphal elongation begin. Still imperceptible to the naked eye |
| 3–7 days | Colonies form, displaying distinct coloration and odors |
| 1–2 weeks and beyond | Spores are produced and airborne, spreading to new areas |

> **When mold becomes visible to the naked eye, it has already reached the third stage. The outcome of mold control is decided during the first two days when nothing appears.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The immediate priority in a flooded structure is not hunting for mold, but **draining water and circulating air** while mold has not yet taken root. Wiping surface tiles while leaving wet carpets, mattresses, and water-logged wall insulation untouched wastes those crucial two days.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Another easily overlooked factor is **hidden moisture**. Inside wall cavities, beneath flooring, behind furniture, and within insulation layers, moisture persists for weeks after outer surfaces appear dry. Mold growing in these concealed spaces continuously releases spores and odors without ever being seen. If surfaces feel dry but a persistent musty smell remains, that odor is your signal.

---
### 1-3. Humidity Control Over Surface Removal
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Wiping away mold versus eliminating moisture yields entirely different durations of effectiveness. Wiping removes **what has already grown**, whereas eliminating moisture prevents **everything that would grow in the future.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Furthermore, surface cleaning alone can never achieve complete removal. Visible dark stains represent only a fraction of the mold organism. The main body of mold consists of hyphae extending deep into the material, while surface spots are merely spore-producing structures pushed outward. Wiping away spores leaves the internal hyphae intact, and the moment moisture returns, the colony rebounds within days.

| Approach | Scope of Effect | Duration |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Surface Cleaning | Visible colonies and surface spores | Days to weeks if moisture persists |
| Disinfectant Application | Surviving hyphae on the surface | Ends once the agent dries; virtually no residual effect |
| Porous Material Removal | Deeply embedded internal hyphae | Permanent |
| Drying & Humidity Control | All species across all locations | Permanent as long as conditions are maintained |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Therefore, the sequence remains inviolable: **cut off the moisture source, dry the area, and then wipe or discard.** Attempting to clean without stopping the water supply is an implicit declaration to repeat the same chore endlessly. In disaster situations where time and physical energy are the most precious resources, adhering strictly to this sequence is paramount.
