## 4. Ventilation and Dehumidification
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Preventing mold is a matter of where to send the water. When there is no electric dehumidifier or blower, the forces you can use are three: airflow, temperature difference, and substances that hold water.

---
### 4-1. Cross Ventilation
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Air can leave only as much as comes in, so without an exit the entrance does not work either. If you open only one window, only the air around that window moves in and out a little, and the air deep in the room stays nearly as it was.

| Condition | Effect |
| --- | --- |
| Only one window open | Only the area near the window is exchanged and indoor air is nearly stagnant |
| Two facing openings open | A flow through the room forms and all the air is exchanged |
| Narrow the inlet and widen the outlet | The incoming speed rises so wind reaches the corners |
| Create a height difference | Warm air escapes upward and pulls cool air in below |

- A height difference is the strongest means usable without fuel. Warm air rises, and if there is an outlet high up, it leaves on its own, drawing outside air in through the inlet low down. It happens even on windless days as long as there is a temperature difference between indoors and outdoors. Put one vent low and one high.
- Winter ventilation: cold air can hold very little water vapor in itself, so even if the outside air seems damp, its absolute water vapor content is far less than indoors. When this air is warmed indoors, relative humidity drops sharply, so ventilation on cold days dries the interior.
- Ventilation on a damp midsummer day can bring moisture in. Opening a basement window on a rainy day does not help.

---
### 4-2. Condensation
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The amount of water vapor air can hold decreases as temperature falls. The temperature at which air cools until it can no longer hold the water vapor is the dew point, and when it touches a surface colder than the dew point, water forms on the spot. Condensation is a matter of where humidity and surface temperature meet. Condensation and mold always begin on the coldest surface in a space.

| Frequent places | Reason |
| --- | --- |
| North exterior walls, corners | No sunlight and heat escapes through two faces, so they are coldest |
| Window frames and glass | The weakest point in insulation |
| Between furniture and the wall | No air passes, so the wall surface temperature falls further |
| The line where wall meets floor | Cold and moisture rise from the ground |
| Inside built-in closets | Closed, so there is no air exchange |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Moving furniture 5-10 cm off the wall is simple but very effective. When air flows between the wall and the furniture, the wall surface temperature approaches the temperature of the surrounding air and does not fall below the dew point. Leaving closet doors open occasionally and lifting bedding instead of leaving it on the floor work on the same principle.

---
### 4-3. Dehumidifying with Natural Materials
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There are two ways to hold water from the air. One reacts chemically with water and holds it irreversibly; the other physically adsorbs on the surface and can be used again once dried.

| Material | Method | Approximate capacity | Properties |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Quicklime (calcium oxide) | Becomes slaked lime by chemical reaction | Up to about one third of its own weight | Irreversible. Gives off heat on reaction, with a burn risk |
| Calcium chloride | Absorbs by deliquescence and becomes a liquid | More than its own weight | The runoff liquid damages metal and cloth |
| Charcoal | Physical adsorption on the porous surface | A few percent of weight | Can be reused repeatedly by drying in the sun, and also takes up odor |
| Wood ash | Moisture absorption | Low | Available anywhere and used together in grain storage |
| Salt | Moisture absorption | Low | A supplementary means that works only when relative humidity is high |
| Dry rice, beans | Moisture absorption | Low | Used to dry small items, and avoid eating them afterward |

- Quicklime: the strongest of the natural materials. It is made by burning limestone or shells at high temperature, and on meeting water it gives off heat and turns into slaked lime. This reaction is irreversible, so once used it is finished, and the reaction heat and strong alkali cause severe burns to skin and eyes. Keep it in a sack or a lidded container so that people do not touch it directly. Spent slaked lime should not be discarded but can be reused to disinfect latrines and livestock sheds.
- Charcoal: weak in performance but the most practical in a disaster. Because it is surface adsorption rather than a chemical reaction, once saturated it can be dried in the sun and reused many times, it is not dangerous to touch, and it is also used for water filtration and odor removal. Keeping a sack of charcoal beside the wardrobe, bookshelf, and grain sacks and taking it out to dry on every fine day is a dehumidification cycle that can be sustained without electricity.

---
### 4-4. Storing Grain, Seeds, Clothes, and Books
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There is one common principle. Seal after drying completely, and if you could not dry it, do not seal it. If you seal something not fully dry, the trapped moisture circulates inside and feeds mold.

| Target | Key point | Cause of failure |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Grain, seeds | Dry to 13% moisture or less and then seal. Put charcoal or ash in together | Sealed while hot, and condensation forms inside as it cools |
| Clothes, bedding | Store after complete drying but leave airflow. Keep off the floor | Folded with the creases and the inside of seams not fully dry |
| Books, paper | Stand upright so air passes between pages. Keep off the wall | Stacked lying down, the lower ones are pressed and do not dry |
| Seeds | Cool, dry, dark. In a paper bag inside a sealed container | Put directly in plastic, and water droplets form inside |
| Leather, wood | Keep airflow and avoid direct sunlight | Fully sealed, and mold spreads on the surface |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Take stored items out and check them periodically. Mold takes days to become visible, and the smell comes first. On every fine day, opening the sack and smelling it, putting a hand in to check for warmth or dampness, and drying the charcoal and putting it back is the cheapest inspection. The mistake in dealing with mold is always getting through the winter without inspection.
