## 4. Ventilation and Dehumidification
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;All the preceding chapters converge toward a single conclusion: preventing mold is ultimately a question of **where to direct the moisture.** In the absence of electric dehumidifiers and blowers, the only forces available are airflow, temperature differentials, and moisture-binding materials.

---
### 4-1. Cross Ventilation
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Opening a single window versus opening two facing windows yields a drastic difference. Air can only enter as much as it can exit; thus, **without an outlet, an inlet cannot function.** Opening only one window merely moves the air surrounding that window slightly, while air deep inside the room remains stagnant.

| Condition | Effect |
| --- | --- |
| Single Window Open | Air exchange occurs only near the window; indoor air remains stagnant |
| Two Facing Windows Open | Creates a flow penetrating the room, exchanging the entire air mass |
| Narrow Inlet, Wide Outlet | Increases incoming air velocity, allowing breeze to reach deep corners |
| Height Differential | Warm air escapes upward, drawing cool air in from below |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Creating a height differential is the most powerful method available without fuel. Warm air rises because it is lighter; if an outlet exists at a high point, warm air escapes naturally, suctioning outside air through a low inlet. Even on windless days, this flow occurs as long as an indoor-outdoor temperature differential exists. Therefore, as a rule, ventilation openings should be placed **one at a low point and one at a high point.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;A common misconception regarding winter ventilation also warrants clarification. Cold air holds inherently less water vapor, so even if outdoor air appears humid, its absolute water vapor content is far lower than indoor air. When this air warms up indoors, its relative humidity drops significantly; thus, **ventilating on cold days actually dries out the indoor space.** Conversely, ventilating on humid summer days introduces moisture, so opening basement windows on rainy days is unhelpful.

---
### 4-2. Condensation
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The amount of water vapor air can hold decreases as temperature drops. The temperature at which air cools and can no longer hold water vapor is called the dew point; **when air contacts a surface colder than the dew point, water condenses on that spot.** Condensation is not merely a problem of humidity, but a problem of where humidity meets surface temperature.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Hence, condensation and mold invariably begin on the **coldest surface** in a given space.

| Common Vulnerable Spots | Reason |
| --- | --- |
| North-facing exterior walls, corners | Deprived of sunlight and losing heat through two surfaces, making them the coldest |
| Window frames and glass | The weakest points in thermal insulation |
| Gaps between furniture and walls | Lack of air circulation drops wall surface temperature further |
| Lines where walls meet floors | Coldness and moisture rise from the ground |
| Inside built-in closets | Enclosed space with no air exchange |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;A simple yet highly effective measure is **moving furniture 5–10 cm away from walls.** Allowing air to flow between walls and furniture brings the wall surface temperature closer to the ambient air temperature, preventing it from dropping below the dew point. Occasionally leaving closet doors open and stowing bedding away rather than leaving it flat on the floor follow this exact same principle.

---
### 4-3. Dehumidification Using Natural Materials
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Methods to capture airborne moisture fall into two categories: capturing it **irreversibly** through chemical reaction with water, or **adsorbing** it physically on surfaces for reuse after drying.

| Material | Mechanism | Approx. Capacity | Properties |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Quicklime (Calcium Oxide) | Converts to slaked lime via chemical reaction | Up to ~1/3 of its own weight | Irreversible; exothermic reaction poses burn risks |
| Calcium Chloride | Absorbs moisture via deliquescence into liquid | Exceeds its own weight | Runoff liquid damages metals and fabrics |
| Charcoal | Physical adsorption on porous surface | Several % of its weight | Reusable after sun-drying; also adsorbs odors |
| Wood Ash | Hygroscopic absorption | Low | Readily available everywhere; co-used in grain storage |
| Salt | Hygroscopic absorption | Low | Auxiliary tool operating only at high relative humidity |
| Dry Rice / Beans | Hygroscopic absorption | Low | Used for drying small items; avoid consuming afterward |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Quicklime is the most powerful among natural materials. Made by calcining limestone or seashells at high temperatures, it reacts with water to generate heat and convert into slaked lime. Because this reaction is irreversible, it is single-use; **its reaction heat and strong alkalinity pose severe burn risks to skin and eyes.** It must be placed in sacks or capped containers to prevent direct human contact. Spent slaked lime should not be discarded, as it can be repurposed for disinfecting latrines and livestock pens.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Charcoal is weaker in performance but the most practical in disaster situations. Operating through physical surface adsorption rather than chemical reaction, it can be dried in the sun and reused repeatedly once saturated. It is safe to touch, and the same material is used for water filtration and odor removal. Placing sacks of charcoal beside closets, bookshelves, and grain bags and **taking them out to dry on every clear day** forms a sustainable dehumidification cycle without electricity.

---
### 4-4. Storage of Grains, Seeds, Clothing, and Books
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Storage is where all preceding principles converge. A single common rule governs: **dry completely before sealing, and do not seal if not completely dry.** Sealing incompletely dried items causes trapped moisture to circulate internally, fostering mold growth.

| Target | Key Point | Cause of Failure |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Grains & Seeds | Seal after drying to ≤13% moisture; add charcoal or ash | Sealing while warm causes condensation inside as it cools |
| Clothing & Bedding | Store after complete drying while maintaining ventilation; elevate off floors | Folded while inner seams and padding remain damp |
| Books & Paper | Stand upright with space between pages for airflow; space from walls | Stacking flat compresses lower layers, preventing drying |
| Seeds | Low temp, low humidity, dark place; paper bags inside sealed containers | Storing directly in plastic causes water droplets to condense inside |
| Leather & Wood | Maintain ventilation while avoiding direct sunlight | Complete sealing causes surface mold proliferation |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Finally, stored items **must be inspected periodically.** Mold takes days to become visible, but odor manifests first. Opening sacks on clear days to smell them, inserting hands to check for heat or dampness, and re-inserting dried charcoal represent the cheapest inspection routine. The ultimate mistake in mold management is **passing the winter without inspection.**
