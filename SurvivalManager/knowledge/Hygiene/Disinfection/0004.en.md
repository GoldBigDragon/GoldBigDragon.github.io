## 3. Chemical Disinfection and Limitations on Wounds
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Chemical disinfectants complement physical methods by allowing mass processing and leaving residual protective effects. Conversely, strict adherence to concentration and contact time is required; chemical strength degrades during storage; and improper usage causes harm to human tissue. Here, four chemical agents operating on distinct mechanisms are examined.

---
### 3-1. Chlorine
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;When chlorine-based disinfectants enter water, hypochlorous acid is formed. Because this molecule is electrically neutral, it penetrates microbial cell membranes to oxidize internal enzymes and nucleic acids. Hypochlorous acid is the active agent; **if water shifts toward alkalinity, this active form decreases, causing germicidal power to drop rapidly.** This is why disinfecting dishes washed with alkaline lye water using chlorine without rinsing them first reduces effectiveness.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The efficacy of chlorine disinfection is determined not by concentration alone, but by **the product of concentration and contact time ($C \times T$).** If concentration is halved, contact time must be doubled to achieve the same result. While urgency prompts raising concentrations, waiting longer is actually safer and more economical.

| Application | Approx. Free Chlorine Concentration | Contact Time |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Drinking Water | ~2 mg/L dosage | 30+ minutes |
| Tableware & Cooking Surfaces | 0.05% (1:100 dilution) | A few minutes while wet |
| Floors & Toilet Surfaces | 0.2% | 10+ minutes |
| Feces, Vomit, Cadavers, High-Risk Waste | 0.5% (1:10 dilution) | 10+ minutes |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Household liquid bleach generally contains 5–6% available chlorine. Emergency guidelines from the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) specify adding 8 drops of 6% bleach per 3.8 L of water—roughly **2 drops per 1 L of water**—and waiting 30 minutes. If water is turbid or cold, double the dosage or extend the time. A faint chlorine odor should be present after 30 minutes; if no smell remains, chlorine has been completely consumed, requiring the same dosage to be added again followed by another 15-minute wait.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Understanding the standard of **0.2–0.5 mg/L residual chlorine** is essential. This figure represents not the amount needed for initial disinfection, but the **amount that must remain after disinfection is complete.** Residual chlorine confirms two things: First, it proves that chemical agent remained even after processing all organic matter and microorganisms in the water. Second, it serves as an insurance policy against recontamination during transport and storage. Thus, verifying residual chlorine at distribution points is the primary metric of water quality management.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Chlorine also bears clear limitations. Cryptosporidium oocysts withstand conventional chlorine concentrations for extended periods, requiring filtration or boiling; high organic loads deplete chlorine prematurely; and liquid bleach itself decomposes under light, heat, and time, experiencing noticeable concentration drops in just a few months. It must be stored tightly sealed in a cool, dark location.

### 3-2. Iodine, Hydrogen Peroxide, and Alcohol
| Disinfectant | Principle | Strengths | Limitations |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Iodine (2% Tincture) | Iodinates proteins and nucleic acids to inactivate them | Superior to chlorine against protozoan cysts | Leaves taste and color; unsuitable for pregnant women and thyroid patients |
| Hydrogen Peroxide (3%) | Oxidizes cellular components via free radicals | Decomposes leaving only water and oxygen | Rapidly decomposes under light; requires high concentrations for spores |
| Alcohol (60–90%) | Denatures proteins and dissolves lipid membranes | Fast-acting; leaves no chemical residue | Ineffective against spores; evaporates leaving no residual protection |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;When using iodine for drinking water, add 5 drops of 2% tincture per 1 L of clear water (10 drops for turbid water) and let sit for at least 30 minutes. However, because it is unsuitable for long-term daily consumption, chlorine takes priority whenever available.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Alcohol exhibits a property that defies common intuition: **water-diluted alcohol is more potent than pure absolute alcohol.** The primary mechanism by which alcohol kills microbes is protein denaturation, a process that **requires water.** Without water, proteins are dehydrated and hardened instead; specifically, surface proteins coagulate first, forming a protective barrier that prevents alcohol from penetrating into the cell interior. Thus, the 60–90% concentration range is optimal, with 70% used as standard practice. This is covered in greater detail in the "Disinfectant Alcohol" document.

### 3-3. What NOT to Do in Wound Disinfection
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Extreme caution is required when selecting disinfectants for wound care for a simple reason: **disinfectants do not distinguish between microbial cells and human cells.** The same mechanism that destroys cell membranes and denatures proteins works identically on fibroblasts healing the wound and newly formed granulation tissue.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Hydrogen peroxide is subject to frequent misunderstanding. The foaming observed upon pouring it onto a wound is caused by tissue enzymes (catalase) decomposing hydrogen peroxide into oxygen—not the sound of bacteria dying. Although the visual reaction appears dramatic, actual germicidal efficacy is modest while newly regenerating tissue suffers chemical damage. Alcohol and concentrated iodine similarly delay healing if repeatedly poured directly into open wounds.

> **Repeatedly pouring hydrogen peroxide and alcohol into open wounds kills newly regenerated tissue far more reliably than it kills bacteria.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The single most effective intervention for reducing wound infection is not chemical disinfection, but **physical irrigation and cleansing.**

| Action | Reason |
| --- | --- |
| Flush thoroughly with clean water | Physically washes away bacteria and foreign matter. Volume and pressure are key |
| Remove soil, sand, and debris | Remaining foreign matter acts as a culture medium for infection and tetanus |
| Apply disinfectant to surrounding skin | Use to clean healthy skin around the wound perimeter, not inside the wound bed |
| Cover and maintain moist environment | A moist environment facilitates epithelial regeneration |
| Monitor at least once daily | Redness, heat, swelling, pus, or red streaks indicate developing infection |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;If clean water for irrigation is scarce, use water disinfected to drinking standards; if unavailable, use boiled and cooled water. The standard is that water used to flush wounds **must be safe to drink.** Irrigation with floodwater or stagnant water directly introduces Leptospira bacteria and tetanus spores into the wound.
