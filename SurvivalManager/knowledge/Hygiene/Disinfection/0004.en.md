## 3. Chemical Disinfection and Its Limits on Wounds
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Chemical disinfectants can treat large quantities and leave a residual effect, so they supplement physical methods. You must keep to concentration and time, they weaken in storage, and if used wrongly they hurt people.

---
### 3-1. Chlorine
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;When a chlorine-based disinfectant goes into water, hypochlorous acid forms. This molecule is electrically neutral, so it passes through the microbe's cell membrane and oxidizes the enzymes and nucleic acids inside. What actually acts is hypochlorous acid, and when water tilts alkaline this form decreases and germicidal power falls sharply.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Effectiveness is determined not by concentration alone but by the product of concentration and contact time. At half the concentration you must give twice the time to reach the same result. The more urgent it is, the more one tries to raise the concentration, but waiting is safer and more economical.

| Use | Approximate available chlorine concentration | Contact time |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Drinking water | Dose of around 2 mg/L | 30 minutes or more |
| Tableware and cooking surfaces | 0.05% (1:100 dilution) | A few minutes, kept wet |
| Floor and toilet surfaces | 0.2% | 10 minutes or more |
| Feces, vomit, corpses, high-risk contamination | 0.5% (1:10 dilution) | 10 minutes or more |

- Household bleach is generally 5-6% available chlorine. The US Environmental Protection Agency's emergency guidance is to put 8 drops of 6% bleach in 3.8 L of water, roughly two drops per 1 L, and wait 30 minutes. If the water is turbid or cold, double the amount or lengthen the time.
- After 30 minutes there should be a faint smell of chlorine. If there is none at all, the chlorine has been entirely used up, so add the same amount again and wait another 15 minutes.
- A residual chlorine of 0.2-0.5 mg/L is not the amount needed for disinfection but the amount that must remain after disinfection is finished. It is evidence that agent was left over after dealing with the organic matter and microbes in the water, and insurance against new contamination entering during transport and storage. Checking residual chlorine at the point where water is distributed is the key indicator in water quality management.
- Limitations: Cryptosporidium oocysts withstand ordinary concentrations of chlorine for a long time, so filtration or boiling is needed. With much organic matter the agent is consumed first. Bleach decomposes with light, heat, and time, and its concentration drops noticeably after only a few months. Store it with the lid closed in a dark, cool place.

### 3-2. Iodine, Hydrogen Peroxide, Alcohol
| Disinfectant | Principle | Strength | Limitation |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Iodine (2% tincture) | Iodinates and inactivates proteins and nucleic acids | Better than chlorine against protozoan cysts | Leaves taste and color; unsuitable for pregnant women and people with thyroid disease |
| Hydrogen peroxide (3%) | Oxidizes cell components with free radicals | Only water and oxygen remain when it decomposes | Decomposes quickly in light, and needs high concentrations against spores |
| Alcohol (60-90%) | Denatures proteins and dissolves lipid membranes | Fast and leaves no residue | Ineffective against spores and evaporates so has no lasting effect |

- When using iodine for drinking water, put 5 drops of 2% tincture in 1 L of water (10 drops for turbid water) and leave it for 30 minutes or more. It is not suited to long-term daily drinking, so if you can use chlorine, chlorine takes priority.
- Alcohol mixed with water is stronger than pure alcohol. Water is needed for protein denaturation, its main action. Without water, proteins dehydrate and harden, and especially if cell-surface proteins harden first, a layer forms that alcohol cannot get through. 60-90% is optimal, and 70% is conventionally used. Details are in the Distilling alcohol document.

### 3-3. What Not to Do When Disinfecting Wounds
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Disinfectants do not distinguish between microbes and human cells. The actions of destroying cell membranes and denaturing proteins act in just the same way on the fibroblasts that heal a wound and on newly grown granulation tissue.

- Hydrogen peroxide: the foaming that occurs when it is poured on a wound is tissue enzymes breaking down the hydrogen peroxide and releasing oxygen, not the sound of bacteria dying. It has little real germicidal power while damaging newly grown tissue.
- Alcohol and concentrated iodine: repeatedly pouring them on an open wound slows healing.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The most powerful means of reducing infection in a wound is not disinfectant but physical washing.

| Action | Reason |
| --- | --- |
| Wash thoroughly under flowing clean water | Physically washes out bacteria and foreign matter. Volume and pressure are the key |
| Remove soil, sand, and foreign matter | Remaining foreign matter is itself a culture medium for infection and tetanus |
| Disinfectant on the surrounding skin | Use it to wipe the skin at the edge, not inside the wound |
| Cover and keep it from drying | A moist state helps epithelial regeneration |
| Observe at least once a day | Redness, warmth, swelling, pus, and red streaks are signs of infection |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The water used to wash a wound must be water you could drink. If disinfected water is hard to get, use water that has been boiled and cooled. Washing a wound with floodwater or standing water puts leptospira and tetanus directly into the wound.
