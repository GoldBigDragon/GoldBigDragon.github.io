## 2. Physical Disinfection
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The advantages of physical disinfection lie at the exact opposite end of chemical disinfection. It leaves no chemical residue, imparts no taste or odor, induces no microbial resistance, and does not degrade past an expiration date. What is required are resources that remain available even in disaster situations, such as fuel or sunlight. On the flip side, processing in large quantities is difficult, requiring time and labor.

---
### 2-1. Heat
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Heat kills microorganisms through protein denaturation, nucleic acid damage, and structural collapse of cell membranes. What is crucial here is that **moist heat is far more efficient than dry heat.** Because water allows proteins to unfold at much lower temperatures, 100°C in a wet state achieves a similar effect to 160°C in a dry state. Thus, boiling serves as the fundamental baseline for disinfection.

| Condition | Duration | Achieved Level |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 100°C (Boiling Water) | 1 minute | Kills most waterborne pathogens; spores survive |
| Altitude > 1,000m | 3 minutes | Competing lower boiling point requires longer time |
| 72°C | 15 seconds | High-Temperature Short-Time (HTST) pasteurization |
| 63°C | 30 minutes | Standard pasteurization; pathogenic microbes are killed |
| 121°C (Pressure Cooker) | 15+ minutes | Kills endospores; achieves sterilization |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The most practical fact here is **63°C for 30 minutes.** Water does not strictly need to reach a rolling boil. Cholera, Shigella, Typhoid bacteria, Hepatitis A virus, and cysts of Giardia and Cryptosporidium are all killed given sufficient time in the 60°C range. In fuel-scarce scenarios, this makes a massive difference, as a large portion of energy spent boiling goes toward raising temperature from 60°C to 100°C.

> **Maintaining water at a temperature too hot to hold your hand in for 30 minutes kills most waterborne pathogens without boiling.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;However, this method bears the weakness of temperature verification. Without a thermometer, one must err on the side of safety, which is why official guidelines set a **rolling boil**—an easily verifiable metric—as the baseline standard. Water vigorously bubbling with large bubbles is visual proof of 100°C recognizable by anyone. At high altitudes, the boiling point itself drops, making a visual boil insufficient on its own and requiring extended duration.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;One must also clearly understand what boiling cannot solve. Heat only kills microorganisms and **cannot eliminate chemical contamination.** Boiling water containing heavy metals, pesticides, or high salinity actually increases chemical concentration as water evaporates. Mycotoxins also remain intact. Water with chemical contamination requires distillation or complete avoidance, not boiling.

---
### 2-2. Solar Disinfection (SODIS)
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Placing water in transparent PET bottles under direct sunlight—known as solar disinfection—fits disaster scenarios well because it requires zero fuel. It operates on two overlapping mechanisms: longer-wavelength UV-A damages microbial DNA and cellular structures, while rising water temperature inside the bottle applies thermal stress. When both actions overlap, the combined effect exceeds the sum of its parts.

| Condition | Standard | Reason |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Container | Transparent PET bottle, ≤2–3L | Glass blocks a significant portion of UV radiation |
| Turbidity | ≤30 NTU | Suspended solids block UV rays from penetrating inner water |
| Sunny Day | Minimum 6 hours, including midday | Peak UV intensity hours are required |
| Cloudy Day | 2 consecutive days | Total UV radiation is significantly reduced |
| Water Temp | If ≥50°C, duration shortens to ~1 hour | Synergistic heat accelerates the process |
| Orientation | Lay flat, paint back half black | Thinner water layer aids light penetration and heat absorption |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The rationale behind the turbidity limit illustrates the essence of this method. Because UV light **must penetrate to work**, water so muddy that light cannot travel a few centimeters will only be disinfected on the surface while remaining contaminated inside. Thus, turbid water must first be settled or filtered through cloth. A practical rule of thumb in the field is checking whether newspaper print can be read through the bottle.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Disadvantages include difficulty in mass processing, total reliance on weather conditions, persistence of chemical contaminants, and the **absence of residual disinfectant capacity**, leaving water vulnerable to recontamination. Therefore, water treated by solar disinfection should ideally be consumed directly from the bottle without transferring it to other containers.

### 2-3. Drying and Freezing
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Drying deprives microorganisms of the water required for survival. Most bacteria slowly die on dry surfaces, and sun-drying enhances this effect through added UV exposure and heat. This is why **sun-drying laundry and cookware completely** acts as a self-contained disinfection measure during disasters.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;However, certain organisms resist desiccation. Bacterial endospores endure for years; roundworm (Ascaris) eggs retain infectivity in soil for years; and protozoan cysts persist for extended periods. Hantavirus in dried rodent droppings becoming airborne dust to infect humans follows this exact same mechanism. **Being dry does not equate to being safe.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Freezing is not a method of disinfection at all. This common misconception must be clearly dispelled.

> **Freezing does not kill microorganisms; it merely puts them to sleep. Upon thawing, they resume multiplication; in fact, freezing is a method used to preserve microorganisms long-term.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;A vast majority of bacteria and viruses survive long-term at low temperatures with halted metabolism. Preserving laboratory strains at tens of degrees below zero leverages this exact property. While ice crystal formation may rupture cell walls and kill a fraction of microbes, the mortality rate is inconsistent and cannot be relied upon as a disinfection measure. An exception exists for certain parasites, such as Anisakis in fish, which die given sufficient duration at low enough temperatures. However, this is limited to specific parasites and does not apply to bacteria or viruses.
