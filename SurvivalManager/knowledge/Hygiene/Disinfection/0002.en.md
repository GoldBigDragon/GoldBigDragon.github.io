## 1. Levels of Disinfection and Cleaning
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Before discussing disinfection, terminology must first be clarified. The four terms often used interchangeably in daily life actually **reach completely different levels.** Failing to define the necessary level leads to either wasting resources through excessive measures or gaining false peace of mind through insufficient ones.

---
### 1-1. Differences in Terminology
| Term | Reached Level | Representative Method |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cleaning | Physically **removing** contaminants and microbes | Water, soap, brush, running water |
| Sanitation | Reducing microbial counts to a level safe for public health | Hot water rinsing, diluted chlorine |
| Disinfection | Killing and inactivating pathogenic microbes, excluding spores | Boiling, chlorine, alcohol, iodine |
| Sterilization | Removing **all** microbes, including spores | Autoclaving at 121°C for 15+ min, dry heat at 160°C+ for prolonged duration |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The dividing point here is **endospores (spores)**. When environmental conditions deteriorate, certain bacteria transform into a dormant state wrapped in a thick shell; this state withstands boiling water, chlorine, and alcohol alike. Tetanus, botulinum, and anthrax bacteria fall into this category. To reliably kill spores, temperatures exceeding the boiling point of water—meaning pressurization to reach 121°C or higher—are required.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;What level is actually needed in disaster situations? To state the conclusion first, **disinfection is sufficient in most cases, while sterilization is practically impossible and generally unnecessary.** What kills people in disasters are diarrheal diseases and wound infections, most of which are caused by non-spore-forming bacteria, viruses, and protozoa. Boiling is fully sufficient to eliminate them.

| Pathogen | Resistance Order | What It Withstands |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Enveloped Viruses | Weakest | Easily destroyed by soap and alcohol |
| Vegetative Bacteria | Weak | Easily killed by boiling and chlorine |
| Non-enveloped Viruses | Intermediate | Relatively resistant to alcohol |
| Mycobacteria | Above Intermediate | High resistance due to lipid cell wall |
| Protozoan Cysts | Strong | Resistant to chlorine; must be boiled or filtered |
| Bacterial Spores | Strongest | Withstands boiling water and most chemical agents |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;If Cryptosporidium in water is a concern, one should not increase chlorine, but rather **boil or filter** it; if soil enters a wound, **removing foreign matter** takes priority over applying antiseptic solution.

---
### 1-2. Why Cleaning Precedes Disinfection
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The most common failure in disinfection is reversing the sequence. Spraying disinfectant on a dirty surface results in most of the chemical efficacy being consumed by **organic matter** rather than microorganisms. Organic compounds in blood, feces, food residue, grease, and soil react immediately with oxidative disinfectants like chlorine and hydrogen peroxide, depleting them. This is precisely the chlorine demand discussed in water quality management.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In addition, organic matter acts as a physical shield. Bacteria trapped inside dried blood or grease films remain untouched by disinfectants and survive. This creates a state where the outer surface is disinfected while the interior remains contaminated, leading to the false illusion that disinfection was achieved.

> **Disinfection works only on clean surfaces. Disinfecting without prior cleaning wastes chemical agents without achieving results.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Cleaning itself creates a significant reduction in microbial load. Simply washing away surface microbes using running water and friction reduces counts substantially, and using soap destroys enveloped microbes in the process. This means that **in disaster situations lacking disinfectants, cleaning alone can accomplish a substantial portion of the task.**

---
### 1-3. Variables Determining Efficacy
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The reason results vary even when using the same disinfectant is that the four variables below act together.

| Variable | Details | Failure Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Concentration | Is the active ingredient sufficient? | Bleach degraded by prolonged storage, overly diluted solution |
| Contact Time | Has it been in contact long enough? | Wiping off immediately after spraying |
| Organic Load | Is the surface clean? | Disinfecting with blood and soil remaining |
| Temperature & pH | Are reaction conditions suitable? | Chlorine in cold water, chlorine in alkaline water |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Among these, the variable that fails most frequently in disaster situations is **contact time**. Because disinfection is a chemical reaction, it requires time. Water treated with chlorine must sit for 30 minutes, and disinfectant applied to surfaces must be left wet until it dries naturally. Skipping this time due to urgency is equivalent to nothing having happened at all.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Temperature and pH cannot be ignored either. Since chemical reactions generally accelerate at higher temperatures, cold water requires a longer wait even at the same concentration. Chlorine is especially sensitive to pH; if water shifts toward alkalinity, the most potent germicidal form decreases, causing efficacy to drop noticeably. Disinfecting dishes washed with lye water using chlorine without rinsing them first degrades effectiveness for this exact reason.
