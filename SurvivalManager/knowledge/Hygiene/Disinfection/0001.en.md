## Introduction
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Disinfection is one of the most frequently mentioned yet most frequently misunderstood concepts in disaster sanitation. The core of this misconception lies in regarding disinfection as a single binary state. Believing that there are only two states—disinfected and non-disinfected—leads to the assumption that any method produces identical results. In reality, disinfection exists in distinct levels; certain pathogens withstand standard disinfection; and even the same disinfectant yields completely different outcomes depending on concentration, contact time, and surface condition.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The three most common failure modes are as follows: First, spraying disinfectant on dirty surfaces. Organic matter consumes the disinfectant prematurely, preventing it from ever reaching the microorganisms.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Second, failing to allow sufficient contact time. Spraying and immediately wiping it off causes most disinfectants to dissipate before they even begin to work.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Third, applying a method to targets against which it is ineffective. Chlorine is powerful against bacteria but weak against Cryptosporidium oocysts, and alcohol cannot kill bacterial endospores.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This document covers the principles of disinfection rather than a mere list of chemical agents. It outlines why 100°C for 1 minute is sufficient when purifying water, why 63°C for 30 minutes achieves the same result, why solar disinfection in PET bottles works only on clear days, why alcohol is more potent at 70% than at 95%, and the mechanisms by which wood ash and lime disinfect. Assuming scenarios where electricity and commercial chemicals are cut off, this document focuses on disinfection methods that can be created using fire, sunlight, and ash.
