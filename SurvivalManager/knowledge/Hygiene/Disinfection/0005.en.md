## 4. Disinfection with Natural Materials and Application by Target
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Disinfection does not stop even when commercial disinfectants are cut off. There are methods that humans have used long before obtaining chlorine, most of which stand on two physical principles: **strong alkalinity** and **low water activity.** However, one must know exactly where each method is effective and where it reaches its limit to avoid false peace of mind.

---
### 4-1. Lye (Wood Ash Water)
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Pouring water over hardwood ash and leaching it produces a solution composed mainly of potassium carbonate. The pH of this solution generally ranges between 11 and 12, and this level of alkalinity performs three actions simultaneously: it breaks down the lipids forming microbial cell membranes, denatures proteins, and saponifies surface grease so it washes away with water. The greatest advantage of lye is that **cleaning and disinfection occur at the same time.**

| Item | Details |
| --- | --- |
| Raw Material | Hardwood ash is suitable due to higher potassium content than softwood ash |
| Extraction | Pour water over ash, let it settle, and decant the clear supernatant liquid |
| Concentration Check | Dip a feather; if the tip softens within dozens of seconds, concentration is sufficient |
| Suitable Uses | Tableware, cooking tools, laundry, floors, hands (diluted thinly) |
| Limitations | Weak against spores; high concentrations damage skin and metals |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;What must strictly be observed when using lye is **rinsing**. Remaining alkali irritates the skin and reduces the efficacy of subsequent chlorine disinfection. Furthermore, fine particles in the ash itself act as an abrasive rather than a chemical disinfectant; thus, using raw ash instead of soap for handwashing is closer to physical scrubbing with friction rather than chemical disinfection. Still, it is distinctly better than using water alone.

---
### 4-2. Quicklime and Slaked Lime
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Calcining limestone or seashells above 900°C drives off carbon dioxide, leaving calcium oxide, or quicklime. Adding water to quicklime generates intense heat and yields calcium hydroxide, or slaked lime, creating a solution with a pH exceeding 12. **This is precisely the disinfectant used for targets heavy with organic matter—such as excreta, cadavers, and livestock pens—where chlorine struggles.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;There is a clear reason why lime is powerful in organic-heavy environments. While chlorine is consumed and depleted by organic matter, alkali is not consumed; instead, it **raises and maintains the pH of the environment itself.** Spreading lime over latrine pits maintains an elevated pH state where pathogens cannot survive, while simultaneously reducing odors and flies.

| Application | Method | Cautions |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Latrine Pits | Spread thinly over excreta to cover | Excessive use kills decomposer microbes, slowing stabilization |
| Pens & Floors | Mix slaked lime into paste or slurry to coat or spray | Prevent dry dust from becoming airborne and entering eyes/airways |
| Flooded Soil | Broadcast over soil and plow under | Wear gloves and eye protection during work |
| Around Cadavers | Include during burial | Does not prevent decomposition; purpose is disinfection and odor suppression |

> **Quicklime generates intense heat upon contact with water. Contact with wet skin or eyes causes chemical alkali burns and thermal burns simultaneously, and tissue damage continues even after rinsing.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;When handling quicklime, store it dry. When mixing with water, **add quicklime slowly to water, rather than pouring water onto quicklime.** Reversing the sequence causes rapid exothermic boiling and violent spattering. This follows the same principle established for handling strong bases in "Soap."

### 4-3. Real Roles of Salt, Vinegar, and Charcoal
| Material | What It Actually Does | Common Misconception |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Salt | Lowers water activity via osmotic pressure to inhibit multiplication | Stops growth without killing; microbes revive when dissolved in water |
| Vinegar | Inhibits or kills certain microbes through acidity | Must be used near full strength; weak against spores and many bacteria |
| Charcoal | Adsorbs odors, certain dissolved substances, and residual chlorine | **Fails to filter out microbes; bacteria can multiply on its surface** |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Misconceptions about charcoal are particularly dangerous. Charcoal is an adsorbent, not a disinfectant. Improving the taste and smell of water easily creates the **illusion that it has been purified**, yet bacteria and viruses remain intact. Furthermore, the porous, wet surface of charcoal is an ideal breeding ground for bacteria, turning old charcoal filter layers into sources of contamination. Charcoal should be used strictly as a final polishing step after disinfection to improve taste, never as a substitute for disinfection.

### 4-4. Disinfection by Target
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Finally, the concepts discussed are synthesized into a target-by-target reference table. What should be read from each cell is not the name of the method, but **why that specific method is chosen.**

| Target | Preferred Method | Alternative Method | What to Avoid |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Drinking Water | Boiling for 1 min (3 min at high altitude) | Chlorine 2 mg/L for 30 min, solar disinfection for 6 hours on clear days | Drinking water passed through charcoal alone; solar disinfection of turbid water |
| Cookware & Tableware | Boiling water after cleaning | Lye washing followed by rinsing and sun-drying | Spraying disinfectant without prior cleaning |
| Wounds | Irrigation with clean water | Boiled and cooled water; apply disinfectant to surrounding skin only | Repeated use of hydrogen peroxide or alcohol inside the wound bed |
| Hands | Soap, running water, and friction | Rubbing with ash/sand followed by rinsing; 70% alcohol | Rough rinsing with water alone |
| Clothing & Bedding | Washing at ≥60°C or boiling | Complete sun-drying after washing | Folding while damp |
| Latrines & Excreta | Covering with lime or ash | 0.5% chlorine | Spraying weak chlorine directly over organic waste |
| Surfaces & Floors | 0.05–0.2% chlorine after cleaning | Lye water, soapy water | Mixing bleach with other detergents or using sequentially |
| Handling Cadavers | Contact barriers and handwashing | Lime, 0.5% chlorine | Rushing to burn or skipping identification |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Two core principles govern this matrix: First, **in almost all scenarios, cleaning comes first and disinfection comes second.** Second, under no circumstances does disinfection replace isolation and containment. As outlined in the waste disposal documentation, erecting primary barriers always comes first, while disinfection is a secondary measure to reduce what leaks past those barriers. When resources are scarce, this hierarchy determines the ultimate outcome.
