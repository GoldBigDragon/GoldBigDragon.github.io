## 4. Disinfection with Natural Materials and by Target
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Disinfection is possible even without manufactured disinfectants. Most of it rests on two principles: strong alkali and low water activity. You need to know how far each works and where it stops to avoid false reassurance.

---
### 4-1. Lye
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Pouring water over the ash of burned hardwood and steeping it yields a solution whose main component is potassium carbonate. Its pH is generally 11-12, and this alkali does three things at once. It breaks down the lipids of microbial cell membranes, denatures proteins, and saponifies grease on surfaces so that it washes away. The advantage is that cleaning and disinfection happen in one go.

| Item | Content |
| --- | --- |
| Raw material | Hardwood ash is suitable because it has a higher potassium content than softwood ash |
| Extraction | Pour water over the ash, let it settle, and pour off and use the clear liquid on top |
| Judging strength | If you dip a feather and its tip goes limp within a few tens of seconds, the strength is sufficient |
| Suitable uses | Tableware, cooking utensils, laundry, floors, hands (diluted lightly) |
| Limitation | Weak against spores, and strong concentrations damage skin and metal |

- Always rinse. Remaining alkali irritates the skin, and when you then disinfect with chlorine, it reduces the chlorine's effect.
- The fine particles in ash act as an abrasive, not a disinfectant. Using ash instead of soap to wash hands is closer to washing off by friction than to chemical disinfection. Still, it is better than water alone.

---
### 4-2. Quicklime and Slaked Lime
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Burning limestone or shells above 900 °C drives off carbon dioxide and leaves calcium oxide (quicklime). Pouring water on quicklime gives off intense heat and makes calcium hydroxide (slaked lime), and the pH of this solution exceeds 12. It is used on targets that are rich in organic matter and hard to treat with chlorine, such as manure, corpses, and livestock sheds.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Chlorine is consumed by organic matter and disappears, but alkali is not consumed and raises and maintains the pH of the environment itself. Sprinkling lime on a manure pit keeps the pH high so that pathogens cannot survive and odor and flies are reduced.

| Use | Method | Caution |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Manure pit | Sprinkle thinly over the excrement to cover | Too much kills even the decomposing bacteria and slows stabilization |
| Livestock sheds and floors | Mix slaked lime with water and apply or sprinkle | Keep dry powder from blowing into eyes and airways |
| Flooded soil floors | Sprinkle then turn over | Gloves and eye protection needed while working |
| Around corpses | Put in together when burying | Does not stop decomposition. The purpose is disinfection and odor control |

> **Caution:** Quicklime gives off heat on meeting water. On wet skin or eyes, alkali burns and thermal burns occur together, and damage keeps progressing even after washing it off.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Store quicklime dry. When mixing with water, do not pour water onto quicklime; add the quicklime to the water a little at a time. If you do not keep to the order, rapid heating makes it boil up and splatter. It is the same as the principle of handling strong bases in the Soap document.

---
### 4-3. Salt, Vinegar, Charcoal
| Material | What it actually does | Misconception |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Salt | Lowers water activity through osmotic pressure to stop growth | It stops, not kills, and microbes revive when it dissolves in water |
| Vinegar | Inhibits or kills some microbes by acidity | Must be used close to undiluted, and weak against spores and many bacteria |
| Charcoal | Adsorbs odor, some dissolved substances, and residual chlorine | Cannot filter out microbes and may even multiply on its surface |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Charcoal is not a disinfectant but an adsorbent. It improves the taste and smell of water and creates the illusion that it has become clean, but bacteria and viruses remain. The porous surface of wet charcoal is a good place for bacteria to grow, so a charcoal filter bed used for a long time can become a source of contamination. Use charcoal only as the last step to refine the taste after disinfection, and do not let it replace disinfection.

---
### 4-4. Disinfection by Target
| Target | Preferred method | Alternative | What to avoid |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Drinking water | Boiling 1 minute (3 minutes at high altitude) | Chlorine 2 mg/L for 30 minutes, 6 hours of solar disinfection on a clear day | Drinking after passing only through charcoal, solar disinfection of turbid water |
| Cooking utensils and tableware | Wash, then boiling water | Wash with lye, then rinse and sun-dry | Spraying only disinfectant without washing |
| Wounds | Thorough washing with clean water | Boiled and cooled water, disinfectant only on the surrounding skin | Repeated use of hydrogen peroxide or alcohol inside the wound |
| Hands | Soap and running water, friction | Rub with ash or sand then rinse, 70% alcohol | Rinsing carelessly with water only |
| Clothing and bedding | Wash at 60 degrees or more, or boil | Full sun-drying after washing | Folding away while not fully dry |
| Latrines and excrement | Cover with lime or ash | 0.5% chlorine | Spraying only dilute chlorine over organic matter |
| Surfaces and floors | 0.05-0.2% chlorine after cleaning | Lye, soapy water | Mixing bleach with other detergents or using them in succession |
| Handling corpses | Block contact and wash hands | Lime, 0.5% chlorine | Burning in haste or skipping identification |

- In almost every situation, washing comes first and disinfection after.
- Disinfection cannot replace isolation and barriers. As seen in the Waste disposal document, setting up the primary barrier comes first, and disinfection is the secondary means of reducing what has gotten past the barrier. The scarcer the resources, the more this order decides the outcome.
