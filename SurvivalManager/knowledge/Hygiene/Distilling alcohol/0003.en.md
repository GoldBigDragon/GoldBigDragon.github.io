## 2. Limits of Fermentation - Raw Materials and the ABV Ceiling
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;It is yeast, not humans, that produces ethanol. Yeast consumes sugars and releases ethanol and carbon dioxide; this process occurs under anaerobic (oxygen-free) conditions. The human role is merely providing yeast with sugars and an appropriate temperature while preventing wild microbes. However, there is an insurmountable ceiling in this process.

---
### 2-1. Yeast Is Killed by the Alcohol It Produces
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Ethanol is toxic to yeast itself. Because it disrupts cell membrane lipids and interferes with enzymes, as the alcohol concentration in the fermentation wash increases, yeast activity slows down and eventually halts. Standard brewing yeasts generally reach their limit around 12–15%, and even strains specially selected for high alcohol tolerance cap out around 18%.

> **Fermentation alone can never reach the concentration required for disinfection. The ceiling of fermentation is roughly 12–18%, while the floor of disinfection is 60%. The gap in between must be bridged by concentration.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Adding more sugar might seem like it would raise the ABV further, but it does not. Because yeast halts first, sugar beyond the limit simply remains as sweetness. Unfermented residual sugar becomes food for contaminating microbes, spoiling the wash. As a rule, **sugar should be added only up to the amount yeast can fully consume**.

---
### 2-2. Raw Materials - Fermentable Sugars and Starches
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Yeast can consume only monosaccharides and disaccharides. Long-chain sugars like starches cannot be consumed as-is, so they must be broken down first. This process is called **saccharification**, dividing raw materials into two main branches.

| Raw Material | Saccharification Required? | Characteristics |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Fruits (grapes, apples, persimmons, pears) | Not required | Wild yeasts on the skin may trigger spontaneous fermentation |
| Honey | Not required | Must be diluted with water; raw honey's high osmotic concentration prevents yeast growth |
| Sap (maple, birch), Sugar Cane | Not required | Low sugar concentration leads to low yield |
| Grains (rice, barley, wheat, corn) | **Required** | Starches must be broken down into sugars |
| Potatoes, Sweet Potatoes | **Required** | High starch content yields good output |
| Wild Starches (acorns, kudzu, etc.) | **Required** | Astringent tannin compounds must be leached out first |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The primary tools for saccharification are **malted barley (malt) and nuruk (starter culture)**. Their operating principles differ. Malted barley is germinated and dried barley that utilizes amylase enzymes naturally produced by the seed during germination. From the seed's perspective, it breaks down stored starch to feed itself, but humans borrow those enzymes. Nuruk is created by culturing fungi on grains; enzymes secreted by the fungi break down starches. Nuruk also contains yeast, so saccharification and fermentation proceed simultaneously.

| Stage | Conditions | Reason |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Gelatinization (Cooking) | Boil grain thoroughly with water to create a mash | Starch granules must absorb water and swell for enzymes to access them |
| Saccharification | 60–65°C for 1–2 hours | The optimal temperature range for amylase activity |
| Cooling | Cool down to 20–30°C | Adding yeast while hot will kill the yeast |
| Fermentation | 20–30°C, oxygen excluded | In the presence of oxygen, acetic acid is produced instead of alcohol |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Saccharification temperature is crucial because enzymes are proteins. If temperature is too low, the reaction is too slow; if it exceeds 75°C, the enzyme itself denatures and stops working. Protein denaturation, which was used for disinfection earlier, appears here as **a phenomenon that must be prevented**.

---
### 2-3. What Ruins Fermentation
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Most fermentation failures stem from contamination by wild microbes. The most common among them are acetic acid bacteria, which consume ethanol in the presence of oxygen and convert it into acetic acid. Hard-earned alcohol turns into vinegar.

| Problem | Cause | Result |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Vinegar smell | Acetic acid bacteria, oxygen exposure | Ethanol is converted into acetic acid |
| Fermentation fails to start | Low temperature or dead yeast | Sugars remain unfermented and rot |
| Fermentation halts midway | Sudden temp changes, nutrient shortage, ABV limit reached | Ends with unfermented sugar remaining |
| Putrid odor | Putrefactive bacterial contamination | Must be discarded |
| Moldy film / Pellicle | Film yeast, air contact | Even if skimmed off, flavor and yield decline |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Therefore, what a fermentation vessel needs is **not complete airtight sealing, but a one-way outlet**. Carbon dioxide is constantly released during fermentation, so completely sealing it builds up pressure and causes the container to explode. Conversely, leaving it open allows oxygen and acetic acid bacteria to enter. A structure where a tube is submerged in a container of water (airlock)—allowing gas to escape while preventing outside air from entering—resolves this problem.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;When producing alcohol for disinfection, there is no need to worry about flavor and aroma, making management much simpler. However, **not starting with contaminated water or rotten raw materials** remains critical. Fermentation is not a sterilization process, and many pathogens present in raw materials survive until distillation.
