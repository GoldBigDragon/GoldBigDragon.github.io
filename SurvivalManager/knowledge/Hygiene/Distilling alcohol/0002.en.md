## 1. Principles and Concentrations of Alcohol Microbe Killing
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Alcohol disinfection performs two actions simultaneously. First, it dissolves the lipids that form the cell membranes of microorganisms, collapsing the membrane structure. Second, it denatures proteins inside and outside the cells, rendering enzymes and structural elements useless. Because these two mechanisms overlap, alcohol achieves results within dozens of seconds. This speed is alcohol's greatest advantage.

---
### 1-1. Why Is It Effective Only at 60–90%?
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The most common intuition that fails in alcohol disinfection is this: **one assumes higher concentrations mean stronger killing power, but in reality, that is not the case.** The U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) specifies the optimal bactericidal concentration range of alcohol mixed with water as 60–90%, explicitly stating that absolute alcohol holds lower bactericidal efficacy than alcohol-water mixtures. The reason lies in the fundamental nature of protein denaturation.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Protein denaturation occurs when folded polypeptide chains unfold and lose function. This unfolding **requires water.** Water molecules penetrate between protein chains, replacing original bonds and breaking down the structure. In the absence of water, alcohol acts merely as a dehydrating agent rather than a denaturant, causing dehydrated proteins to harden instead.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;A second problem compounds this. When absolute alcohol touches microorganisms, surface proteins coagulate first, forming a shell-like barrier. This layer blocks alcohol from penetrating into the interior of the cell. **Cells with hardened exteriors but undamaged interiors** remain behind, reviving after the alcohol evaporates.

| Concentration | Effect | Reason |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 95%+ | Low | Lack of water prevents denaturation; only the surface hardens |
| 70–90% | Highest | Water required for denaturation and penetration capability are both secured |
| 60–70% | Sufficient | A practically widely used range |
| 50% or below | Rapidly decreases | Fails to produce enough solvent power to destroy cell membranes |
| 40% or below | Unsuitable as a disinfectant | Can be used for preservation, but unreliable for disinfection |

> **Diluting disinfectant alcohol with water is not a cost-saving trick, but an essential process to adjust it to 70%. Conversely, using 95% alcohol as-is actually weakens its effect.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Water used for dilution must be sterile. While alcohol evaporates rapidly, water remains; if contaminated water is used for dilution, microorganisms present in that water will survive inside the solution. Use boiled and cooled water or distilled water.

---
### 1-2. What Alcohol Cannot Do
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;While fast, alcohol is not omnipotent. It has clear limitations, and using it without understanding these boundaries is a common cause of disinfection failure.

| Limitation | Details |
| --- | --- |
| Ineffective against spores | Spores of Tetanus and Botulinum bacteria are not killed by alcohol |
| No residual effect | Protection ends the moment it evaporates, leaving it defenseless against recontamination |
| Vulnerable to organic matter | Ineffective if blood or grease is present, so it must be washed off first |
| Weak against certain viruses | Relatively less effective against non-enveloped viruses |
| Weak against protozoan cysts | Cannot be expected to work against organisms like Cryptosporidium |
| Cannot disinfect drinking water | Disinfection is impossible at concentrations safe for drinking |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The bottom line is subject to frequent misunderstanding. The idea that mixing liquor into water sanitizes the water is incorrect. Disinfection requires a concentration of 60% or higher, and liquids at that concentration are not drinkable water. **Alcohol is not a means for sanitizing water, but a means for disinfecting surfaces, hands, and tools.**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Alcohol disinfection also requires contact time. Applying it to hands and wiping it off immediately fails to secure sufficient time. The duration during which the surface remains wet constitutes the actual disinfection time, generally requiring around 30 seconds. Allowing it to air-dry naturally is the simplest baseline standard.

---
### 1-3. Which Types of Alcohol?
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Several types of alcohol are used for disinfection, each with slightly different properties. Ethanol is the only one among these obtainable through fermentation and distillation.

| Type | Disinfection Effect | Can It Be Produced? | Toxicity |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Ethanol | Strong | Possible via fermentation and distillation | Relatively low |
| Isopropanol | Strong | Cannot be produced by fermentation | Oral toxicity is higher than ethanol |
| Methanol | Disinfects, but **MUST NOT BE USED** | Comes off as a byproduct | Causes blindness and death |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;While methanol possesses inherent germicidal capability, it absorbs through the skin and causes toxicity simply by inhaling its vapor; using it as a disinfectant is strictly prohibited. This fact governs all subsequent procedures. When producing alcohol via fermentation and distillation, the actual problem being addressed is **not how to obtain ethanol**, but **how to separate and discard methanol**.
