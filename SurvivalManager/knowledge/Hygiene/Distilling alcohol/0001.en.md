## Introduction
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This document covers how to obtain disinfecting alcohol (ethanol) yourself. Alcohol is needed for wound care and disinfecting tools, and is also used as fuel, an extraction solvent, and a preservative. In a prolonged disaster it is one of the first supplies to run out.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Making it carries two risks.

- Physical hazards: Alcohol vapor ignites at low temperatures, and a sealed apparatus bursts from pressure.
- Methanol: It cannot be told apart from ethanol by taste or smell, and after drinking it there are no symptoms for half a day or more before blindness and death follow. This is why the liquid from the start of distillation (the heads) must always be discarded.

> **Caution:** Making alcoholic beverages for drinking is illegal in most countries, including South Korea. This document sets out principles for situations where medical disinfectants and fuel are unavailable, and it neither recommends nor explains production for drinking. Drinking distillate made without precise measurement is gambling on methanol poisoning.

---

## 1. How Alcohol Kills Microbes, and Concentration
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Alcohol disinfects in two ways. It dissolves the lipids that make up microbes' cell membranes, collapsing the membrane, and it denatures proteins inside and outside the cell, disabling enzymes and structures. The two actions combine to give a result within tens of seconds.

---
### 1-1. Why 60-90% Is Strongest
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;You might expect stronger to be better, but it is not. The US CDC puts the optimal germicidal concentration at 60-90% mixed with water, and states that absolute alcohol is less germicidal than a mixture of alcohol and water.

- Protein denaturation is the unfolding of folded chains, and unfolding needs water. Water molecules slip between the chains and replace the original bonds, so the structure collapses. Without water, alcohol acts not as a denaturant but as a dehydrating agent, and dehydrated protein actually hardens.
- When absolute alcohol touches a microbe, the surface proteins harden first and form a shell-like layer that blocks the alcohol from penetrating. Cells remain intact inside the hardened outside, and they revive after the alcohol evaporates.

| Concentration | Effect | Reason |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 95% or more | Low | Too little water for denaturation, so only the surface hardens |
| 70-90% | Highest | Both the water needed for denaturation and the penetrating power are secured |
| 60-70% | Sufficient | The range widely used in practice |
| 50% or less | Drops sharply | Not enough dissolving power to collapse the membrane |
| 40% or less | Unsuitable as a disinfectant | Can be used for preservation but cannot be relied on for disinfection |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Diluting disinfecting alcohol with water is not a trick for stretching it but an essential step to bring it to 70%. Using 95% alcohol as it is actually makes it weaker.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Dilute with disinfected water (boiled and cooled water, or distilled water). The alcohol evaporates at once but the water stays, so if you dilute with contaminated water, that water's microbes survive in the disinfectant.

---
### 1-2. Limits of Alcohol
| Limit | Details |
| --- | --- |
| Useless against spores | Spores of tetanus and botulinum bacteria are not killed by alcohol |
| No residual effect | Protection ends the instant it evaporates, leaving no defense against recontamination |
| Weak against organic matter | Blood and grease keep it from penetrating, so wash first |
| Weak against some viruses | Relatively less effective against viruses without a lipid envelope |
| Weak against protozoan cysts | Cannot be relied on for things like Cryptosporidium |
| Cannot disinfect drinking water | The concentration that makes water drinkable does not disinfect |

- The idea that mixing liquor into water disinfects it does not hold. The concentration needed for disinfection is 60% or more, and a liquid at that strength is not drinkable water. Alcohol is a means of disinfecting surfaces, hands, and tools.
- Contact time is needed. The time the surface stays wet is the disinfection time, roughly 30 seconds. Do not wipe it off right after applying it to your hands; leave it until it dries by itself.

---
### 1-3. Types of Alcohol
| Type | Disinfecting effect | Can you make it? | Toxicity |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Ethanol | Strong | Yes, by fermentation and distillation | Relatively low |
| Isopropanol | Strong | Cannot be made by fermentation | Oral toxicity greater than ethanol |
| Methanol | Disinfects, but must not be used | Comes out mixed in as a by-product | Causes blindness and death |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Methanol is absorbed through the skin and can poison through inhaled vapor alone, so its use as a disinfectant is prohibited. When making alcohol by fermentation and distillation, the real problem is not how to obtain ethanol but how to separate out the methanol and throw it away.

## 2. Fermentation: Raw Materials and the Strength Limit
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;It is yeast that makes ethanol. In the absence of oxygen, yeast eats sugar and releases ethanol and carbon dioxide. People give the yeast sugar and a suitable temperature and keep contaminating microbes out.

---
### 2-1. The Strength Ceiling
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Ethanol is a poison to the yeast itself. It disrupts the lipids of the cell membrane and interferes with enzymes, so as the alcohol concentration of the ferment rises, yeast activity slows and then stops. Ordinary brewing yeast generally hits its limit at 12-15%, and strongly alcohol-tolerant strains at around 18%.

> **Fermentation alone cannot reach the concentration needed for disinfection.** The ceiling for fermentation is roughly 12-18% and the floor for disinfection is 60%. Always fill the gap by concentrating.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Adding more sugar does not raise the strength. The yeast stops first, so sugar beyond the limit remains as sweetness, and the leftover sugar feeds contaminating microbes and spoils the ferment. Add only as much sugar as the yeast can finish eating.

---
### 2-2. Raw Materials
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Yeast eats only monosaccharides and disaccharides. Sugars linked in long chains, like starch, must be cut first, and this process is saccharification.

| Raw material | Saccharification needed? | Characteristics |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Fruit (grapes, apples, persimmons, pears) | Not needed | Wild yeast on the skin sometimes ferments it by itself |
| Honey | Not needed | Must be diluted with water. Undiluted, the concentration is so high that yeast cannot live |
| Sap (maple, birch), sugarcane | Not needed | Low sugar concentration, so the yield is low |
| Grains (rice, barley, wheat, corn) | Needed | Starch must be cut into sugar |
| Potatoes, sweet potatoes | Needed | High starch content gives a good yield |
| Wild starches such as acorns and kudzu | Needed | The astringent components must be leached out first |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The saccharification tools are malt and nuruk.

- Malt: barley that has been sprouted and dried. It uses the amylase enzyme that the seed makes for itself as it sprouts.
- Nuruk: mold grown on grain. The enzymes the mold releases break down starch. It also contains yeast, so saccharification and fermentation proceed at the same time.

| Stage | Conditions | Reason |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Gelatinization | Boil the grain thoroughly with water until it becomes like paste | Starch granules must absorb water and loosen for the enzymes to reach them |
| Saccharification | 60-65 °C for one to two hours | The temperature range at which amylase works best |
| Cooling | Cool to 20-30 °C | Adding yeast while hot kills the yeast |
| Fermentation | 20-30 °C, air excluded | With oxygen, acetic acid is made instead of alcohol |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Enzymes are proteins, so the reaction is slow at low temperatures, and above 75 °C they denature and stop working.

---
### 2-3. Causes of Fermentation Failure
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Most failures are contamination by other microbes. The most common, acetic acid bacteria, eat ethanol in the presence of oxygen and turn it into acetic acid. The alcohol you worked to make becomes vinegar.

| Problem | Cause | Result |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Vinegar smell | Acetic acid bacteria, exposure to air | The alcohol is converted to acetic acid |
| Fermentation does not start | Temperature too low or yeast dead | Sugar is left and rots |
| Fermentation stops midway | Sudden temperature change, lack of nutrients, reaching the alcohol limit | Ends with sugar still left |
| Rotten smell | Contamination by putrefying bacteria | Discard |
| Mold film | Film yeast, contact with air | Skimming it off still reduces flavor and yield |

- The fermentation vessel needs not a fully sealed but a one-way open passage. Fermentation keeps releasing carbon dioxide, so sealing it completely builds pressure and bursts the vessel, while leaving it open lets in oxygen and acetic acid bacteria. Put a tube into a bowl of water so that bubbles can leave but air cannot enter.
- Disinfectant alcohol needs no attention to taste or aroma, so management is simple. But do not start with contaminated water or rotten raw material. Fermentation does not kill microbes, and many pathogens from the raw material stay alive until distillation.

## 3. Concentration: Distillation, Freezing, and Desiccants
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Raising the 12-18% of the ferment to the 60% or more needed for disinfection means removing water. There are three methods, and their physical principles and the impurities that remain differ. This difference decides safety.

---
### 3-1. Distillation
| Substance | Boiling point at 1 atm |
| --- | --- |
| Methanol | About 64.7 °C |
| Ethanol | About 78.3 °C |
| Water | About 100 °C |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;When a mixed liquid is heated, the component with the lower boiling point enters the vapor in larger amounts. Collecting and cooling that vapor gives a liquid richer in alcohol than the original. It is not the case that only ethanol comes off at 78.3 °C and only water at 100 °C. In a mixture the two evaporate together at intermediate temperatures, and the proportion of water in the vapor grows as the temperature rises. Distillation is not complete separation but a shift in proportions.

> **Ethanol and water form an azeotrope at about 95.6%.** At this concentration the vapor has the same composition as the liquid, so no matter how many times you distill, it does not go higher.

- One pass through a simple apparatus gives 40-60% depending on the strength of the starting liquid, and distilling again raises it a little more. 70% usually takes two or three distillations, and each distillation greatly reduces the volume.

| Part of the apparatus | Role | What decides performance |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Heating part | Warms the ferment to make vapor | Ability to hold the temperature slowly between 78-90 °C |
| Vapor tube | Carries the vapor to the condenser | Length and slope. The longer it is, the more heavy components fall back on the way |
| Condenser | Cools the vapor back to liquid | Cooling area, temperature difference, time the vapor stays |

- If condensation efficiency is low, alcohol vapor escapes without becoming liquid, which is a loss and lets flammable vapor build up around it. Cool the tube by submerging it in cold water or running cold water over its outside. If the cooling water flows opposite to the direction of the vapor, the temperature difference is kept along the whole length and efficiency rises greatly.

> **Caution:** Never seal any point of the still completely. If the vapor outlet is blocked, the internal pressure rises sharply, the apparatus bursts, and hot alcohol pours onto the fire. The same accident happens if the vapor tube kinks and liquid pools in it, if the condenser clogs, or if a joint is sealed too tightly. The vapor must always have a way out.

- Ethanol has a low flash point and ignites at temperatures only slightly above room temperature. Its vapor is heavier than air, so it flows along the floor and ignites on a distant spark.
- Avoid direct flame and heat in a double boiler. Work outdoors or in a well-ventilated place, and keep distance between the fire and the apparatus.

---
### 3-2. Freeze Concentration
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Water freezes at 0 °C, and ethanol freezes at a much lower temperature. When you freeze the ferment, the water turns to ice first, so removing that ice raises the concentration of the remaining liquid. Neither fire nor apparatus is needed.

> **Caution:** Distillation sorts components by boiling point, but freezing only takes out water and sorts nothing. Methanol and fusel oils do not freeze with the water; they stay with the ethanol and are concentrated as they are.

- Freeze concentration concentrates the impurities along with it. In distillation, discarding the heads removes a good part of the methanol, but in freezing there is no fraction to discard. Consider it only when it is certain that it will be used for disinfection and never drunk.
- As the concentration rises, the freezing point drops and a lower temperature is needed. At realistic natural low temperatures it is hard to go beyond about 30%, so it cannot reach the concentration needed for disinfection.

---
### 3-3. Desiccants
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Because of the azeotrope, distillation is limited to 95.6%, but chemically binding the water can take it higher. In a disaster the realistic desiccant is quicklime. Quicklime reacts with water to become slaked lime, which holds the water, and it does not react with ethanol.

| Method | Concentration reached | Character |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A single simple distillation | 40-60% | Simple apparatus, and the heads can be discarded |
| Repeated distillation | 70-90% | Large volume loss and heavy fuel use |
| Freeze concentration | About 30% | Impurities are concentrated too, which is especially dangerous for drinking |
| Desiccant treatment | 95% or more | Can pass the azeotrope, but risk of contaminating the material |

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;For disinfection there is almost no reason to pass the azeotrope. 70% is optimal for disinfection and 95% is actually weaker. Desiccant concentration matters for uses that need fuel or anhydrous solvent, which usually does not apply in a disaster.

## 4. Heads and Methanol, Checking Strength, and Uses
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Distillate is not the same substance from start to finish. Compounds with different boiling points come off in order, so the composition varies greatly with when the liquid was collected. Separating these fractions and discarding the first part is the most important step in distillation.

---
### 4-1. Why the Heads Must Be Discarded
| Fraction | Main components | Handling |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Heads | Acetone, acetaldehyde, ethyl acetate, methanol | Discard all of it |
| Hearts | Mostly ethanol | Collect and use |
| Tails | Fusel oils (higher alcohols), water | Low toxicity but cause headaches, and the strength drops |

- Source of methanol: When pectin in plant cell walls is broken down by enzymes, methanol is released. It is produced in large amounts from pectin-rich raw materials, especially fruit skins and seeds and stone fruits such as apples and plums, and relatively little in grain fermentation. Fermenting skins and seeds together raises the risk.
- Mechanism of toxicity: Methanol itself is not very toxic. Liver enzymes convert methanol to formaldehyde and then to formic acid, and this formic acid paralyzes the cells' energy production, destroys the optic nerve, and makes the blood acidic.

> **Caution:** Methanol cannot be told apart from ethanol by taste or smell. Right after drinking there is nothing wrong, and 12-24 hours later vision begins to blur, by which time it is already irreversible. 10 ml can cause blindness and 30 ml can cause death.

- Drinking ethanol along with it lengthens the latent period. This is because liver enzymes deal with the ethanol first, delaying the breakdown of methanol. Mass methanol poisoning incidents repeat on this mechanism, and treatment requires an antidote and hemodialysis, which cannot be obtained in a disaster.
- Set the amount to discard conservatively. Without precise measuring equipment, discarding more than seems like a waste is the only safe judgment.
- Label the discarded heads as fuel or for cleaning, store them separately, and never put them in food containers.

---
### 4-2. Checking Strength
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Below 60% it cannot be trusted as a disinfectant, so check the concentration of the alcohol you made.

| Method | Principle | Accuracy |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Alcohol hydrometer | Ethanol is lighter than water, so concentration is read from density | Most accurate. Needs temperature correction |
| Measuring volume and weight | Compare the weight with that of the same volume of water | Usable if the scale is precise |
| Foam persistence | Estimated from how long foam lasts when shaken | A rough guess that depends on experience |
| Spoon burn test | Judged by whether it catches fire | Inaccurate and dangerous |

- The burn test is unsuitable for judging disinfection strength. Alcohol begins to catch fire at around 50%, but this varies greatly with temperature, container, and flame conditions. Catching fire only means it has passed 50%, not that it has reached the 60-70% needed for disinfection. An alcohol flame is almost invisible in bright light, which easily leads to burns and fires.
- A hydrometer assumes that the liquid consists only of ethanol and water. If sugar or other solutes remain, their added weight makes it read a lower strength than the real one. Do not use it on the ferment itself; use it on the liquid after distillation is finished.

---
### 4-3. Uses Other Than Disinfection, and Legal Issues
| Use | Details |
| --- | --- |
| Disinfection | Adjust to 60-90% and use on hands, tools, and surfaces |
| Fuel | Used in alcohol stoves. Almost no soot |
| Extraction solvent | Making tinctures that draw out the active components of herbs |
| Preservation | Preserving specimens, disinfecting seed surfaces, and so on |
| Cleaning | Dissolves grease and resin |

- As an extraction solvent, requirements are the opposite of disinfection. A low strength is better for components that dissolve well in water, and a high strength for components close to oil. For fuel, less water gives more heat, so the higher the strength the better.

> **Caution:** In most countries, including South Korea, making alcoholic beverages without a license, and distilled spirits in particular, is illegal. A disinfection purpose is not automatically an exception, and sale or transfer is treated more severely. This document sets out principles on the premise of situations where logistics are cut off and medicines and fuel cannot be obtained, and it is not knowledge to apply in ordinary times.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;You cannot confirm by measurement how much of the heads to discard, you cannot tell by the senses whether methanol remains, and if poisoning occurs you have no means of treating it. Use a liquid made this way only for disinfection and fuel, and do not drink it. It is a matter of probability before it is a matter of law.
